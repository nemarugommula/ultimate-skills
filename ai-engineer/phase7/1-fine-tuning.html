Phase 7 · Lesson 1

Fine-tuning: when it beats prompting, SFT data & LoRA

Changing a model's weights is the most powerful way to customize it, and the easiest to misuse. Learn when it pays off, what data it needs, and how LoRA makes it fit on a single GPU.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Tokens and cost: you pay per input and output token, and longer prompts are slower to start answering (Lesson 0.3).
  • Few-shot prompting: showing the model worked examples inside the prompt (Lesson 1.1).
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) puts fresh documents into the prompt at question time (Lesson 3.1).
  • A golden eval set: labelled examples you score every change against (Lesson 5.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2024 the team at Predibase fine-tuned 310 models: 10 open base models × 31 tasks, each with a small 4-bit adapter. On average the fine-tuned models beat their own base models by 34 points and beat GPT-4 by 10 points on those tasks. They then served 25 of these fine-tuned 7B models from a single 80 GB GPU, all sharing one copy of the base model.

The catch is in the words “on those tasks”. They were narrow jobs with clear right answers. This lesson is about telling those jobs apart from the ones where fine-tuning wastes a month.

Numbers first: moving the prompt into the weights

You route 1 million support tickets a month into 12 categories. The prompted version needs instructions plus 20 worked examples: about 3,000 input tokens per ticket for a 20-token answer. A fine-tuned small model has learned the examples, so it needs only the ticket: about 150 tokens.

Prices are illustrative, not quotes. Prompt caching (Lesson 1.4) shrinks the left column a lot, and the right column adds a one-off training cost and maybe a dedicated endpoint. The shape of the result holds: at high volume, the per-request savings of a small specialist add up fast.

In plain words: a prompt teaches the model again on every request; fine-tuning teaches it once. That only helps if what you're teaching is a behaviour: a format, a style, a narrow skill.

💡 Big idea Fine-tune for behaviour (format, tone, a narrow skill, a smaller and cheaper model). Retrieve for knowledge (facts, documents, anything that changes). And fine-tune only after a prompted baseline and an eval set tell you exactly what you're trying to beat.
🧠 Analogy: the expert, the sheet and the cheat card

Prompting is handing a brilliant temp a two-page instruction sheet every morning. RAG is giving them the filing cabinet. Full fine-tuning is sending them on a month-long course that rewires how they work, and they might forget other things along the way. LoRA is clipping a small laminated cheat card to their badge: the expert is unchanged, the card is tiny, and you can keep a different card for every job and swap them in seconds.

The usual ladder is: prompt → few-shot → RAG → fine-tune a smaller model → preference-tune it (Lesson 7.2). Each step costs more engineering than the one before it, so climb only when your eval shows the step below has stopped improving. Making a model an expert in a whole field (continued pretraining on domain text, domain vocabulary, fine-tuned embedding models, expert-built evals) is its own topic: Lesson 7.5.

SFT: what the training data looks like

SFT (supervised fine-tuning) means training on pairs of (prompt, ideal response). The loss is the usual next-token loss (Lesson 0.2), counted only on the response tokens, so the model learns to write the answers, not to predict your prompts. Data is usually a JSONL file of chat messages, in the same roles the model will see in production:

{"messages": [ {"role": "system", "content": "Route the ticket. Reply with one label."}, {"role": "user", "content": "I was charged twice for my March invoice."}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "billing.duplicate_charge"}]} {"messages": [ … one line per example … ]}
  • Quality beats quantity. Meta's LIMA study tuned a 65B model on just 1,000 carefully chosen examples and got strong results; the authors concluded that most knowledge comes from pre-training and fine-tuning mainly teaches format. One wrong label repeated 200 times teaches the wrong thing 200 times.
  • Typical sizes (rules of thumb, not laws): tens to a few hundred examples to fix a format or style; a few hundred to a few thousand for a narrow task; more if the task is hard or the inputs vary a lot.
  • Cover the edges: rare classes, messy inputs, and examples where the right answer is “I don't know” or “escalate to a human”.
  • Hold out an eval set before you train, and split it so it can't leak: by customer or by date, not by random line, or near-duplicates end up on both sides.
  • Use the model's own chat template. Training in one format and serving in another quietly ruins results. Libraries like TRL apply the template for you.

Full fine-tuning vs LoRA vs QLoRA

Full fine-tuning updates every weight. The expensive part isn't the weights, it's the training state. Mixed-precision training with the Adam optimizer keeps, for every parameter, a 16-bit weight (2 bytes), a 16-bit gradient (2 bytes) and three 32-bit optimizer values (a master copy, momentum and variance: 12 bytes). That's the classic 16 bytes per parameter from Microsoft's ZeRO paper, before counting activations. An 8B model needs about 128 GB just for that: several GPUs.

LoRA (low-rank adaptation; Hu et al., 2021) freezes the original weight matrix W and learns a small correction made of two thin matrices:

W′ = W + (α / r) · B · A W is d × k and frozen. B is d × r, A is r × k, and only they are trained. The rank r is small (often 8–64); α scales the update. B starts at zero, so training starts from exactly the base model.

Why can such a thin correction work? The changes fine-tuning makes tend to be “low-rank”: a few directions matter. The Math for AI course explains rank and why LoRA works.

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) goes one step further: store the frozen base model in 4-bit NF4 (NormalFloat 4, a 4-bit format designed for the bell-shaped distribution of weights), quantize the quantization constants too (“double quantization”, about 4.127 bits per weight in total), and page optimizer memory to the CPU when it spikes. Gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit weights into 16-bit LoRA adapters. The headline: fine-tuning a 65B model went from more than 780 GB of GPU memory to under 48 GB.

LoRA's count, one matrix at a time: a 4,096 × 4,096 attention projection has 16.8 M weights; at r = 16 the adapter has 16 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072, 0.78% of it. The whole adapter is 41.9 M parameters, about 84 MB on disk in 16-bit, against 16 GB for the model.

Overfitting and catastrophic forgetting

  • Overfitting: training loss keeps falling while the held-out eval loss turns up, and the model starts parroting training answers. Usual fixes: fewer epochs (1–3 is typical), a lower learning rate, more varied data, stop at the best eval checkpoint.
  • Catastrophic forgetting: the model gets better at your task and worse at everything else, such as following other instructions or staying safe. A 2024 study, LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less, found exactly that trade: LoRA learned less of a new domain than full fine-tuning, but kept more of the base model's other skills. Keep a small regression eval of general abilities next to your task eval, and mix some general instruction data into training if it drops.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Hugging Face TRL (trainers for SFT, DPO and more) with PEFT (LoRA and other adapters) is the standard open stack; Unsloth wraps the same workflow with custom kernels and claims about 2× faster training with up to 70% less GPU memory. Managed options as of 2026: Google Vertex AI (supervised tuning of Gemini models), Amazon Bedrock (supervised, reinforcement fine-tuning and distillation), and open-model platforms like Together AI (LoRA or full fine-tuning). Check before you build on any of them: OpenAI announced in May 2026 that it is winding down self-serve fine-tuning (no new jobs for active customers after 6 January 2027; existing fine-tuned models keep serving until their base models are retired).

# pip install trl peft datasets   (TRL 1.x, as of late 2026)
from datasets import load_dataset
from peft import LoraConfig
from trl import SFTConfig, SFTTrainer

# JSONL rows: {"messages": [{"role": "user", ...}, {"role": "assistant", ...}]}
data = load_dataset("json", data_files={"train": "train.jsonl", "eval": "eval.jsonl"})

peft_config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,                          # rank of the update
    lora_alpha=32,                 # update is scaled by alpha / r
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    target_modules="all-linear",   # attention + MLP projections, as in QLoRA
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)
args = SFTConfig(
    output_dir="ticket-router-lora",
    num_train_epochs=2,
    learning_rate=1e-4,             # adapters take a higher LR than full fine-tuning
    per_device_train_batch_size=4, gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    max_length=2048,
    assistant_only_loss=True,      # learn the answers, not the prompts
    eval_strategy="steps", eval_steps=100,
)
trainer = SFTTrainer(model="Qwen/Qwen3-8B", args=args, peft_config=peft_config,
                     train_dataset=data["train"], eval_dataset=data["eval"])
trainer.train()
trainer.save_model()                    # saves just the adapter: tens of MB

# QLoRA: same trainer, base model loaded in 4-bit NF4
import torch
from transformers import BitsAndBytesConfig
bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(load_in_4bit=True, bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
                         bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True, bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16)

Serving the result: merge the adapter into the weights (PEFT's merge_and_unload()) for one model, or keep adapters separate and serve many on one base model: vLLM's --enable-lora --lora-modules name=path, or LoRAX (Lesson 7.4). 🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Ship on a prompted frontier model first, log every request, and let real traffic build the dataset. Fine-tune when cost or latency starts to hurt and you have a few hundred good labelled examples: one LoRA on an open 7–14B model, rented GPUs for an afternoon, and an eval that proves it matches the big model.

Big tech

A training platform with dataset versioning, automated evals and safety regression tests on every run; dozens of task adapters served from a shared base (Apple ships its on-device features this way); and fine-tunes rebuilt whenever the base model is upgraded, which is a hidden recurring cost.

🎤 Interview angle

“Would you fine-tune for this?” A senior answer:

  • Asks whether the gap is knowledge (RAG) or behaviour (maybe fine-tune), and what the prompted baseline scores on an eval set.
  • Does the cost maths: tokens saved per request × volume vs. training, hosting and maintenance (re-tuning on every base-model upgrade).
  • Picks LoRA/QLoRA by default and says why (memory, adapters, forgetting), with full fine-tuning for large domain shifts.
  • Talks about data: quality, coverage of edge cases, a leak-proof held-out split, and a regression eval for general skills.
🏭 In the real world
310 small specialists vs GPT-4 🏢 Predibase · 2024 LoRA Land (arXiv 2405.00732)

How: 4-bit LoRA fine-tunes of 10 open base models on 31 tasks, served by LoRAX, an open-source server that shares one base model across many adapters.

Why this method: on average +34 points over the base models and +10 over GPT-4 on those tasks, with 25 fine-tuned 7B models on one A100 80 GB.

Swappable adapters on your phone 🏢 Apple · 2024 Apple Intelligence foundation models

How: one ~3B on-device model plus rank-16 LoRA adapters per feature, loaded and swapped on the fly.

Why this method: each adapter needs only tens of megabytes, so many features share one base model in limited phone memory.

A small tuned model beats a giant one 📜 History · 2022 InstructGPT (arXiv 2203.02155)

How: OpenAI fine-tuned GPT-3 on labeller-written demonstrations (SFT), then on human preferences (Lesson 7.2).

Why this method: people preferred the outputs of the 1.3B tuned model to those of the 175B base model, despite 100× fewer parameters.

Fine-tuning teaches format, not facts 🧪 Research · 2024 Does Fine-Tuning on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?

How: researchers at the Technion and Google varied how many fine-tuning examples contained facts the model didn't already know.

Why this method: new facts were learned much more slowly than known ones, and as they were learned, the model's tendency to hallucinate rose. Use RAG for facts.

🤯 Fun fact · 350 GB → 35 MB

In the original LoRA paper, adapting GPT-3 175B with rank 4 on just the query and value matrices shrank each task's checkpoint from 350 GB to about 35 MB, roughly 10,000× smaller. Storing 100 task versions took about 354 GB instead of 35 TB.

Check yourself

1. A LoRA adapter of rank 8 on one 4,096 × 4,096 weight matrix. How many trainable parameters?
B is 4,096 × 8 and A is 8 × 4,096: 8 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 65,536, about 0.4% of the 16.8 M weights in the full matrix (the third option).
2. Your assistant must answer questions about product prices that change every week. A colleague proposes fine-tuning on the price list. Best response?
Fine-tuning is a poor way to store changing facts: they're learned slowly, can raise hallucination, and are stale a week later. Retrieval keeps facts fresh and citable. Pushing harder (higher learning rate) mostly causes forgetting.
3. During fine-tuning, training loss keeps falling but held-out eval loss started rising after epoch 2, and answers now copy training examples word for word. What's happening, and what do you do?
Diverging train and eval loss is the textbook sign of memorizing. Early stopping at the best eval checkpoint is the quick fix; more diverse data and gentler training are the lasting ones.
📝 TL;DR
  • Fine-tune for behaviour (format, style, a narrow skill, a smaller cheaper model); use RAG for knowledge.
  • Start from a prompted baseline and an eval set; a few hundred to a few thousand clean, leak-proof examples beats a big noisy pile.
  • Full fine-tuning costs about 16 bytes per parameter in training state; LoRA trains ~0.1–1% of the parameters; QLoRA adds a 4-bit base so 65–70B models fit on one 48 GB GPU.
  • Watch for overfitting (train vs eval loss) and forgetting (a general-skills regression eval).
