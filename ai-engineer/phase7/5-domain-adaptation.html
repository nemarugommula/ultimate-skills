Phase 7 · Lesson 5

Domain adaptation: making an LLM an expert in your domain

“Make the model understand our domain” is the most common request an AI engineer gets. There's a ladder of answers, from a glossary in the prompt to training your own model. Each rung costs roughly ten times the one below it. Climb only as high as your evals say you must.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) = look up relevant passages from your documents and put them in the prompt (Lesson 3.1).
  • Embeddings turn text into vectors so that similar meanings land close together, which is how search finds passages (Lesson 2.1).
  • Fine-tuning with SFT (supervised fine-tuning on input → ideal output examples) and LoRA (training small add-on matrices instead of all the weights) teaches behaviour and format (Lesson 7.1).
  • Evals = a fixed set of test cases you score every change against (Lesson 5.1).
🎯 Hook

In March 2023 Bloomberg published BloombergGPT: a 50.6-billion-parameter model trained from scratch on 363 billion tokens of financial data plus 345 billion tokens of general text. Training took about 53 days on 512 A100 GPUs. It outperformed existing models on financial tasks by significant margins without losing general ability.

Six weeks later, a team of researchers ran OpenAI's general-purpose ChatGPT and GPT-4 on eight financial benchmarks. Both did better than BloombergGPT on almost every dataset except named-entity recognition; on one financial question-answering set, ChatGPT scored 59.9% to BloombergGPT's 43.4%. GPT-4 is a much bigger model, so this isn't a fair fight, and that's the point: a better general model plus your data in the prompt often beats a specialist you trained, and it keeps improving without you retraining anything.

Numbers first: what each rung costs

You want a model that's good at, say, insurance claims. Here are the five ways to get there, cheapest first. The figures are typical orders of magnitude for one team and one use case, not quotes.

Notice what changes as you climb: not just cost, but what kind of thing you're changing. Rungs 1–2 change what the model sees. Rungs 3–5 change what the model is. Seeing is cheap to update tomorrow; being is not.

💡 Big idea “Domain expertise” isn't one thing. It's at least five: vocabulary, facts, formats, reasoning conventions and risk tolerance. Each has a cheapest fix, and most of them live on the bottom two rungs. Diagnose which of the five is actually failing (with an eval) before you pick a rung.

🧠 Analogy: hiring a brilliant generalist doctor for your hospital

You've hired a doctor who aced medical school but has never worked here. First you hand them the house rules and a list of local abbreviations (rung 1). Then you give them access to the patient records and protocols so they look things up instead of guessing (rung 2). After a few supervised shifts they learn how this hospital writes notes (rung 3). If they'll work in a specialty whose language they've never read, they do a residency (rung 4). Raising your own doctor from childhood (rung 5) is something almost no hospital does. And notice: you'd never ask a doctor to memorise today's bed list. That's what the records system is for.

Rungs 1–2: most domain problems are context problems

Three results to keep in your pocket when someone proposes training a domain model:

  • Prompting can beat domain training. Microsoft's Medprompt (2023) used only general prompting techniques (picking similar solved examples, chain-of-thought, shuffled-answer ensembles) and took GPT-4 past 90% on MedQA (US medical licensing exam questions), beating Google's medically fine-tuned Med-PaLM 2 (86.5%).
  • RAG beats fine-tuning for facts. A 2023 Microsoft study compared the two for teaching models knowledge: RAG consistently won, both for facts the model had seen before and for brand-new ones. Models struggled to learn new facts from unsupervised fine-tuning at all.
  • Raw domain text can make a model worse at following instructions. A 2023 study found that continued pretraining on raw domain corpora added knowledge but “drastically” hurt prompting ability, and fixed it by turning the raw text into reading-comprehension exercises.

So the first two rungs are where you start: a system prompt that states the domain's rules and risk posture, a glossary (as data you version, not prose you forget), and RAG over the sources experts actually trust. That leaves one gap that's easy to miss: if your users search in jargon and your documents are written differently, retrieval fails before the LLM ever sees the answer. That's a vocabulary problem in the embedding model, and it has its own cheap fix.

Domain embeddings and rerankers: the cheapest training you'll ever do

General embedding models are trained on web text. They know “heart attack”. They may not know that in your hospital “MI” means the same thing, or that “pressure” in “pressure area care” is about skin, not blood. Fixing this doesn't need a GPU cluster:

  • Collect (query, passage) pairs: a real question and the passage that answers it. Sources: search logs with clicks, support tickets linked to articles, or an LLM writing questions for each passage (then an expert spot-checks a sample).
  • Train contrastively: pull each query's vector toward its passage and push it away from the other passages in the batch. In sentence-transformers this is MultipleNegativesRankingLoss: every other passage in the batch acts as a free “wrong answer”.
  • Add hard negatives: passages that look similar but are wrong (“pressure ulcer” for a blood-pressure query). Random negatives are too easy; hard negatives teach the fine distinctions your experts care about.
  • Do the same for the reranker (the cross-encoder that rescores the top results, Lesson 3.4) if you use one: it reads query and passage together, so it learns domain distinctions even better.

How cheap? In one public walkthrough, fine-tuning the open BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 model on 6,300 synthetic question–passage pairs from a company's SEC filing took about 3.5 minutes on one 24 GB GPU (about $0.07 of cloud time) and improved retrieval NDCG@10 (a ranking-quality score from 0 to 1) from 0.768 to 0.825, about 7.4%.

Rung 3: fine-tune for format and behaviour, not facts

Lesson 7.1 covers the mechanics. For domain work the rule is simple: fine-tune when the failing dimension is format, conventions or style and you have (or can get experts to write) a few hundred to a few thousand gold examples. Keep the facts in RAG and train the model to use retrieved context the way an expert would: quote the clause, cite the guideline, flag what's missing. Fine-tuning a model on this month's policy table means it's wrong next month and you can't tell which answers came from stale weights.

Rungs 4–5: continued pretraining and domain models

Continued pretraining (also called domain-adaptive pretraining) means taking an open base model and continuing its original training, predicting the next token, on a large pile of raw domain text. The classic evidence is Don't Stop Pretraining (2020): a second phase of pretraining on 2–8 billion tokens of in-domain text (biomedical papers, computer-science papers, news, reviews) improved results in all four domains, and the gains were biggest where the domain was most different from the model's original training data.

✏️ Worked example: what does continued pretraining cost in compute?

Rule of thumb: training costs about 6 × parameters × tokens floating-point operations (FLOPs): roughly 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass, per parameter, per token.

  • A 7B model, 10B tokens of domain text: 6 × 7×10⁹ × 10×10⁹ = 4.2 × 10²⁰ FLOPs.
  • One H100 GPU peaks near 1,000 TFLOP/s (10¹⁵ per second) in bf16; real training typically achieves about 40% of peak, so about 4 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s.
  • 4.2 × 10²⁰ ÷ 4 × 10¹⁴ ≈ 1.06 × 10⁶ seconds ≈ 295 GPU-hours: about $900 at an illustrative $3 per GPU-hour, or under two days on 8 GPUs.
  • BloombergGPT for comparison: 6 × 50.6×10⁹ × 569×10⁹ tokens ≈ 1.7 × 10²³ FLOPs, about 400× more; 53 days × 512 GPUs ≈ 651,000 GPU-hours.

The lesson: at small scale, compute is not the expensive part. Collecting and cleaning billions of tokens, mixing in general text so the model doesn't forget how to follow instructions, re-doing SFT afterwards, evaluating, and repeating all of it when a better base model ships next quarter are what cost you.

When does rung 4 earn its keep? When the domain's language itself is foreign to general models (a proprietary codebase or query language, a low-resource language, dense chemistry or patent text), you have billions of tokens of it, and you must self-host an open model anyway (Lesson 7.3) for privacy, cost or latency. Rung 5, a model from scratch, is for organisations whose data is the moat, whose scale justifies a research team, and who accept that general models may overtake them, as the hook showed.

Tokenizers and domain jargon

A model's tokenizer is fixed when it's trained. General tokenizers chop rare domain words into many small pieces, which costs tokens and can make the words harder for the model to use. When AllenAI built SciBERT, they trained a new vocabulary on scientific papers and found it shared only 42% of its tokens with the original general vocabulary. Domain evals: built with experts, not by you

Every rung decision above depends on an eval set, and in a domain you are not qualified to write it alone. What works:

  • Experts write the cases: 50–200 real tasks (de-identified), each with what a good answer must contain, what it must never say, and how bad each mistake is. Harvey's legal benchmark, for example, uses lawyer-written rubrics that give points for each requirement and negative points for hallucinations, plus a separate “source score”: the share of correct statements backed by an accurate source.
  • Score along the axes the domain cares about. Google's Med-PaLM work had clinicians rate answers on factuality, precision, possible harm and bias, not just “correct or not”.
  • Calibrate an LLM judge against experts (Lesson 5.3): experts grade 50 answers, the judge grades the same 50, and you only automate once they agree well enough. Re-check agreement each quarter.
  • Weight by severity. One harmful dosage error matters more than ten clumsy sentences; report severe-error rate separately from average score.

Keeping domain knowledge fresh

Domains change: new regulations, new drugs, new products, new internal acronyms. Design for it from day one. Keep facts in the index with source dates and versions, re-ingested on a schedule (Lesson 4.7). Keep the glossary as versioned data that experts can edit. Keep trained artifacts about behaviour, so they age slowly. Re-run the domain eval on every base-model upgrade: often the new general model plus your context beats your old fine-tune, and you can delete a rung.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

sentence-transformers (v6 as of late 2026) for embedding and reranker fine-tuning, Hugging Face TRL + PEFT for SFT/LoRA (Lesson 7.1), and for continued pretraining at scale, training frameworks such as the Hugging Face Trainer or NVIDIA NeMo on open base models. Below: a domain embedding fine-tune with mined hard negatives (imports follow the v6 docs), then a plain-Python domain eval harness.

from datasets import Dataset
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, SentenceTransformerTrainer, SentenceTransformerTrainingArguments
from sentence_transformers.sentence_transformer.losses import MultipleNegativesRankingLoss
from sentence_transformers.sentence_transformer.training_args import BatchSamplers
from sentence_transformers.sentence_transformer.evaluation import InformationRetrievalEvaluator
from sentence_transformers.util.hard_negatives import mine_hard_negatives

model = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5")
# (query, passage) pairs from search logs / tickets / LLM-written questions checked by experts
pairs = Dataset.from_list([{"anchor": q, "positive": p} for q, p in load_pairs()])

# hard negatives: similar-looking passages that are NOT the answer (skip the top ranks: they may be true positives)
train = mine_hard_negatives(pairs, model, num_negatives=1, range_min=10, range_max=50,
                            sampling_strategy="top", output_format="triplet")

# held-out eval: {qid: query}, {doc_id: passage}, {qid: {relevant doc_ids}}
ir_eval = InformationRetrievalEvaluator(queries=test_queries, corpus=corpus, relevant_docs=relevant, name="clinical")
print("before:", ir_eval(model))                       # recall@k, MRR@10, NDCG@10 ...

args = SentenceTransformerTrainingArguments(
    output_dir="models/bge-clinical", num_train_epochs=3, per_device_train_batch_size=32,
    learning_rate=2e-5, warmup_steps=0.1, bf16=True,
    batch_sampler=BatchSamplers.NO_DUPLICATES,                 # in-batch negatives must not be duplicates
)
trainer = SentenceTransformerTrainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=train,
                                     loss=MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model), evaluator=ir_eval)
trainer.train()
print("after:", ir_eval(model))
model.save_pretrained("models/bge-clinical/final")
# Domain eval harness: expert-written cases, severity-weighted, run on every change
import json, re
from collections import defaultdict

# cases.jsonl: {"id", "category", "input", "must_include": [...], "must_not": [...], "severity": 1-3}
cases = [json.loads(l) for l in open("cases.jsonl")]

def grade(case, answer):
    missing = [m for m in case["must_include"] if m.lower() not in answer.lower()]
    banned  = [m for m in case["must_not"] if re.search(m, answer, re.I)]
    return {"pass": not missing and not banned, "severe": bool(banned) and case["severity"] == 3,
            "missing": missing, "banned": banned}

def run(system, label):                                  # system: input -> answer (prompt, RAG, fine-tune...)
    by_cat, severe = defaultdict(list), 0
    for c in cases:
        g = grade(c, system(c["input"]))
        by_cat[c["category"]].append(g["pass"]); severe += g["severe"]
    print(label, {k: f"{sum(v)}/{len(v)}" for k, v in by_cat.items()}, "severe errors:", severe)

# Compare rungs on the SAME cases; string checks catch the basics, an expert-calibrated judge grades the rest (5.3)

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Live on rungs 1–2 plus an embedding fine-tune, and spend the saved money on experts writing evals. Your edge is the workflow and the data pipeline, not weights. Re-run evals on every new frontier model: you get its gains for free. Fine-tune (often by distilling, Lesson 7.2) when cost or latency forces a small model.

Big tech / large enterprise

May own billions of tokens of proprietary text and need self-hosting for regulation, so continued pretraining of open models becomes rational, run as a platform that other teams build on. Even then, most product teams ship rungs 1–3 on top of it, and the build-vs-wait decision is revisited with every major base-model release.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our model doesn't understand our legal domain. Should we fine-tune or train our own?” A senior answer:

  • Asks what fails, with examples: vocabulary, facts, format, reasoning or risk posture? Proposes building a 50–100 case expert eval first.
  • Walks the ladder bottom-up: prompt + glossary, RAG with citations, embedding fine-tune if retrieval misses jargon, SFT only for format/behaviour.
  • Keeps facts out of weights because they change, and names freshness, citations and permissions as reasons.
  • Knows when rung 4 is justified (foreign language of the domain, billions of tokens, must self-host) and can estimate it (6 × N × D).
  • Cites evidence: general models + prompting/RAG matching domain models (Medprompt, BloombergGPT vs GPT-4).
🏭 In the real world
A finance model trained from scratch 🧪 Research · 2023 BloombergGPT (arXiv 2303.17564)

How: a 50.6B model trained on a mix of 363B tokens of Bloomberg's financial data and 345B general tokens, about 53 days on 512 A100s, with a public “training chronicles” appendix.

Why this method: Bloomberg owns one of the largest financial text archives, and the mix kept general ability while lifting financial tasks. A follow-up study (arXiv 2305.05862) then found general GPT-4 ahead on most financial benchmarks it tested.

Domain-adaptive pretraining 🧪 Research · 2020 Don't Stop Pretraining (arXiv 2004.10964)

How: continued pretraining of RoBERTa on 2–8B tokens per domain, then on the task's own unlabelled text, across four domains and eight tasks.

Why this method: gains in every domain, largest where the domain was furthest from the original training data, and adapting to an unrelated domain did clearly worse: the text has to be relevant.

Prompting beats a medical fine-tune 🏢 Microsoft · 2023 Medprompt (arXiv 2311.16452)

How: nearest-neighbour few-shot examples, self-generated chain-of-thought and choice-shuffle ensembling on general GPT-4, with no medical training.

Why this method: topped all nine MultiMedQA benchmarks and passed 90% on MedQA, beating Med-PaLM 2, which had medical fine-tuning; the same recipe helped on law, accounting and nursing exams.

RAG vs fine-tuning for knowledge 🏢 Microsoft · 2023 Fine-Tuning or Retrieval? (arXiv 2312.05934)

How: compared unsupervised fine-tuning with RAG on knowledge-intensive tasks, for facts the model had and hadn't seen.

Why this method: RAG consistently outperformed fine-tuning; models struggled to absorb new facts through fine-tuning unless shown many variations of each fact.

A $0.07 domain embedding fine-tune 🧰 Open source · 2024 Phil Schmid: Fine-tune Embedding models for RAG

How: 6.3k synthetic question–passage pairs from an SEC filing, MultipleNegativesRankingLoss with Matryoshka dimensions, bge-base, 4 epochs, one 24 GB GPU.

Why this method: about 3.5 minutes of training lifted NDCG@10 by ~7.4% (0.768 → 0.825); retrieval quality is often the cheapest domain win.

🤯 Fun fact · 58% new words

When AllenAI trained a tokenizer vocabulary on scientific papers for SciBERT, only 42% of its tokens overlapped with the general BERT vocabulary of the same size. Science and the web are, at the token level, more than half different languages.

Check yourself

1. You plan continued pretraining of an 8B open model on 5 billion tokens of domain text. Your GPUs achieve about 400 TFLOP/s (4 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s) each. Roughly how many GPU-hours?
6 × 8×10⁹ × 5×10⁹ = 2.4 × 10²⁰ FLOPs. ÷ 4 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s = 6 × 10⁵ seconds ≈ 167 GPU-hours. Compute is affordable; curating 5B clean tokens, avoiding forgetting and redoing SFT afterwards are the real costs.
2. An insurance assistant gives wrong coverage limits. The policy rules change every month. A colleague proposes fine-tuning on the policy documents. What do you do?
This is a facts + freshness failure. Facts in weights go stale and can't be cited or traced; retrieval updates the same day the document does, and the effective date lets the model pick the rule that applied. Fine-tuning is for behaviour and format.
3. Your bank's internal search fails on queries like “RWA impact of the CRR3 change”: the right documents exist, but they rarely use those acronyms. Retrieval recall is the problem. What's the best first fix?
The generator never sees the answer if retrieval misses it. A glossary fixes known acronyms today; contrastive fine-tuning on real pairs teaches the embedding model your vocabulary in minutes of GPU time. Measure recall@k before and after on held-out queries.
📝 TL;DR
  • Domain expertise = vocabulary, facts, formats, reasoning conventions, risk tolerance. Diagnose which fails with an expert-built eval.
  • Climb the ladder bottom-up: prompt + glossary → RAG → embedding/reranker fine-tune → SFT for format → continued pretraining → own model. Each rung costs ~10× more.
  • Facts belong in retrieval, behaviour in weights. General models + context often match domain models (Medprompt, BloombergGPT vs GPT-4).
  • Continued pretraining compute ≈ 6 × params × tokens; the real cost is data, forgetting and redoing it on every new base model.
