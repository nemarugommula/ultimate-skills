Customizing & serving models: the cheat sheet

When to change the weights, how to do it cheaply, where open models come from, and how to serve them to thousands of people at once, on one page.

🧾 Cheat sheet

7.1 Fine-tuning, SFT & LoRA

Behaviour → fine-tune · knowledge → RAG

Fine-tune for format, style, narrow high-volume tasks, or to swap a big model for a small one. Prompted baseline + eval first. Full fine-tuning ≈ 16 bytes/param of training state; LoRA trains r·(d + k) per matrix (~0.1–1% of params); QLoRA = 4-bit NF4 base, so 65–70B fits on one 48 GB GPU. Watch train vs eval loss (overfitting) and a general-skills eval (forgetting).

TRL SFTTrainer + PEFT LoraConfig, Unsloth, Vertex AI / Bedrock / Together

7.2 Preference tuning & distillation

DPO loss = −log σ(β · margin)

RLHF: reward model + PPO + KL leash to a reference. DPO: one loss on (chosen, rejected) pairs, no reward model. Models learn your raters' biases (length!). Distillation = SFT a student on a teacher's filtered answers: cheap data, big serving savings. Synthetic-data risks: collapse, contamination, homogeneity, licence terms.

TRL DPOTrainer / KTOTrainer / GKDTrainer, LLM judges, Bedrock distillation

7.3 Open models & local inference

GB = params × bits ÷ 8 · tok/s ≲ bandwidth ÷ size

Hub repos: weights, config, tokenizer, chat template, model card, licence (Apache/MIT vs custom terms like Llama's 700M-MAU clause). Q8 ≈ lossless, Q4_K_M ≈ the sweet spot, <3 bits hurts; a smaller model at 4–5 bits beats a bigger one at 2. Self-host for privacy, control and steady high volume; utilization decides the cost.

Hugging Face Hub, Transformers, Ollama, llama.cpp/GGUF, LM Studio, Transformers.js, WebLLM

7.4 Serving at scale

KV/token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × bytes

Prefill is compute-bound (TTFT); decode is memory-bound (TPOT), so batch. Continuous batching + PagedAttention are the baseline; add prefix caching, chunked prefill, speculative decoding ((1 − αγ+1)/(1 − α) tokens per pass), FP8. TP for big models, replicas for traffic; autoscale on queue depth and KV usage.

vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, NVIDIA Dynamo, Prometheus metrics

7.5 Domain adaptation

RAG → domain fine-tune → continued pretraining

Making a model an expert in your field: retrieval for facts, domain fine-tuning for the field's tasks and style, continued pretraining on large amounts of domain text when the language itself is foreign. Fine-tune the embedding model too if search misses domain jargon, mind domain vocabulary, and build evals with real experts.

Embedding fine-tuning, domain corpora, expert-labelled eval sets

🏗️ Build it yourself

🧩 The problem

A bank's internal assistant classifies and drafts replies for 2 million customer emails a month (~67,000 a day) into 40 categories, using a frontier API with a 4,000-token few-shot prompt. Compliance now says customer emails must not leave the bank's own cloud account, and the API bill is too high. You have 18 months of emails with the category and the reply an agent actually sent.

Before you open the answer, write down: fine-tune or not (and which kind), which model and licence, what hardware and quantization, how you'd serve it, the eval plan, rough costs, and what could go wrong.

Reveal One good design + the classic mistakes

Requirements: data stays in the bank's cloud account (so self-host an open model there); classification accuracy at least the current system's; draft replies in the bank's tone; p95 latency of a few seconds is fine (not chat); auditability.

Approach: this is behaviour (a 40-way label and a house style) on a narrow task, with lots of labelled history: a textbook fine-tuning case. SFT a LoRA adapter on an open 7–14B instruct model with an Apache 2.0 licence. Training data: a few thousand de-duplicated, recent emails per major category (rarer ones over-sampled), PII handled per policy, the agent's actual reply as the target. Split by date (train on older months, test on the last two) so it can't leak. Optionally a later DPO pass using replies agents edited (edited = chosen).

Knowledge stays out of the weights: product rates and policies change, so retrieve them at reply time (RAG) instead of training them in.

Hardware: a 14B model in FP8 is ~15 GB; with ~1,000-token emails plus a 300-token reply, KV cache per request is ~0.2 GB (40 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2 bytes ≈ 160 KiB/token), so one 80 GB GPU holds hundreds of concurrent requests. 67,000 requests a day is under one request per second on average, so two GPUs (one per zone, for redundancy) is plenty; most of the cost is the floor, not the load.

Serving: vLLM with continuous batching, prefix caching for the shared system prompt, the LoRA adapter loaded with --enable-lora, an OpenAI-compatible endpoint inside the bank's network, autoscaling on queue depth with a minimum of two replicas.

Eval plan: accuracy and per-class recall on the held-out months vs the current API system; LLM-judge plus agent ratings for reply quality and tone; a regression set of tricky emails (complaints, fraud, legal threats) that must route to humans; shadow mode for two weeks, then an A/B rollout.

Costs (illustrative): before: 67,000 × 4,300 input tokens × $3/M ≈ $860/day for input alone. After: two GPUs at ~$2.50/hour ≈ $3,650/month, plus training runs of tens of dollars and the team's time. Re-tune whenever the base model is upgraded.

Classic mistakes: fine-tuning the product rates into the model; a random train/test split (the same email thread on both sides); skipping the licence check; evaluating only overall accuracy so rare, high-risk categories are silently worse; sizing GPUs from weights alone and forgetting the KV cache; training on replies that agents later corrected.

Boss quiz 👾

1. You want to fine-tune a 70B model on one 48 GB GPU. Which approach makes that realistic?
16-bit weights alone are ~141 GB, so plain LoRA can't fit and full fine-tuning needs ~16 bytes/param. QLoRA stores the base at ~4.1 bits (~36 GB) and trains only small adapters: the 65B-on-48 GB result from the QLoRA paper.
2. Policy log-ratios: chosen +0.5, rejected −1.5, β = 0.1. What's the DPO margin and roughly the loss?
Margin = β × (0.5 − (−1.5)) = 0.1 × 2.0 = 0.2. Loss = −log σ(0.2) ≈ −log 0.55 ≈ 0.60, down from log 2 ≈ 0.69 at the start. 2.0 forgets to multiply by β.
3. A laptop has 16 GB of unified memory (about 11 GB usable for the model). Which is the best choice for a coding assistant?
70B at ~3 bits is still ~28 GB and badly degraded; 8B in 16-bit is 16 GB. A mid-size model at 4–5 bits fits with room for the KV cache and keeps most of its quality, and code suffers early from aggressive quantization.
4. Llama 3.1 8B (128 KiB of KV cache per token, 16-bit) on one 80 GB H100 with ~56 GB free after weights. About how many 32k-token conversations fit?
32,768 × 128 KiB = 4 GiB ≈ 4.3 GB per conversation; 56 ÷ 4.3 ≈ 13. At 8k tokens it's ~52; an FP8 KV cache doubles either number.
5. Your self-hosted model has great median latency at night but p95 TTFT explodes at 9 am while GPU compute sits at 40%. What do you change first?
Requests are waiting for KV-cache space, not compute. Queue depth and KV usage are the right signals, and because new replicas take minutes to load weights, you scale ahead of the known peak.
