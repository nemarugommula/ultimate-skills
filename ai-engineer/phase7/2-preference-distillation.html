Phase 7 · Lesson 2

Preference tuning, distillation & synthetic data

SFT teaches a model to copy good answers. Preference tuning teaches it which of two answers is better. Distillation lets a big model write the training data for a small one. All three are powerful, cheap, and full of traps.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • SFT (supervised fine-tuning) on (prompt, ideal answer) pairs, and LoRA adapters (Lesson 7.1).
  • LLM-as-judge: using a model to grade outputs, and its biases (position, length) (Lesson 5.3).
  • Log-probability: how likely the model thinks a piece of text is; a whole answer's log-prob is the sum over its tokens (Lesson 0.2).
🎯 Hook

In March 2023, Stanford researchers asked OpenAI's text-davinci-003 to write 52,000 instruction-and-answer examples, for less than $500 of API calls. They fine-tuned Meta's LLaMA 7B on them. The result, Alpaca, behaved qualitatively like text-davinci-003 in their tests, and the whole thing cost under $600.

Then came the twist: Alpaca was released for research only, with commercial use prohibited. LLaMA's licence was non-commercial, and OpenAI's terms forbade using its outputs to develop competing models. Distillation is cheap; permission isn't.

Numbers first: a teacher's homework is cheap

You want a small model to handle 100,000 requests a day, as well as your big model does. Have the big model (the teacher) answer 20,000 real prompts, then SFT a small model (the student) on its answers.

What decides whether it works is not the money, it's quality (how close the student gets), coverage (do the 20,000 prompts look like real traffic?), and permission (are you allowed to train on that teacher's outputs?).

Prices are illustrative; GPU costs depend on utilization (Lesson 7.3). Fine-tuning compute for 20,000 examples on rented GPUs is usually tens of dollars.

💡 Big idea It's often easier to judge than to write. Preference tuning turns “A is better than B” into training signal, so the model learns what people prefer, not just what one example said. Distillation turns a big model's answers into training data for a small one. Both move behaviour into weights cheaply; both copy the flaws of whoever did the judging or writing.
🧠 Analogy: the apprentice chef

SFT is the apprentice copying the head chef's recipes exactly. Preference tuning is the head chef tasting two of the apprentice's dishes and saying “this one”: no recipe needed, and the apprentice learns taste. Distillation is the apprentice learning from a famous chef's cookbook instead of from the chef: cheap, fast, and only as good as the cookbook, which you may not be licensed to copy.

RLHF in one picture

RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) is the recipe behind InstructGPT and early ChatGPT. The model being trained is called the policy. It writes two answers; a person picks the better one; a reward model learns to predict those picks; then an RL algorithm (usually PPO, proximal policy optimization) nudges the policy toward answers the reward model scores highly, with a KL penalty (a measure of how far it drifts) that keeps it close to a frozen copy of the starting model, the reference. Without that leash, the policy finds answers that fool the reward model.

DPO: preference tuning without the RL

DPO (direct preference optimization; Rafailov et al., 2023) showed you can skip the reward model and the RL loop. Its paper title says why: “your language model is secretly a reward model”. For each preference pair (prompt x, chosen answer yw, rejected answer yl), DPO compares how much the policy has raised each answer's log-probability relative to the reference model:

loss = −log σ( β · [ (log πθ(yw|x) − log πref(yw|x)) − (log πθ(yl|x) − log πref(yl|x)) ] ) The bracket is the margin: how much more the model now favours the chosen answer than the rejected one, compared with where it started. σ is the sigmoid; β (often 0.1) sets how strongly preferences push the model away from the reference.

It's just a classification loss on pairs: no sampling during training, no reward model to host, and it runs in the same trainer library as SFT. The usual recipe is SFT first, then DPO. Variants you'll see: KTO (works with single thumbs-up/down instead of pairs), IPO, ORPO and SimPO (simplified losses), and online DPO (fresh pairs from the current model). The full derivation is in the RL from Zero course: reward models, RLHF with PPO and DPO and its family.

✏️ Worked example: one DPO pair by hand
  • Start: the policy equals the reference, so both log-ratios are 0. Margin = 0, loss = −log σ(0) = −log 0.5 = 0.693.
  • After some training: log-ratio of the chosen answer is +1.0, of the rejected −1.0. With β = 0.1, margin = 0.1 × (1.0 − (−1.0)) = 0.2. σ(0.2) ≈ 0.55, so loss ≈ 0.598.
  • Same model, β = 0.5: margin = 1.0, σ(1.0) ≈ 0.73, loss ≈ 0.313. A bigger β makes each nat of change count for more, so the loss saturates after smaller moves away from the reference.

The gradient on a pair is proportional to σ(−margin): pairs the model already gets right barely move it; pairs it gets wrong move it most.

Collecting preference data

  • Pairwise beats scores. People disagree on whether an answer is a 6 or a 7 out of 10, but agree far more on “A or B”.
  • Your product already produces it: thumbs up/down (KTO-style), regenerate clicks, “which response do you prefer?” prompts, and user edits (the edited version is chosen, the original rejected).
  • AI feedback (RLAIF): an LLM judge labels the pairs, guided by written principles (Anthropic's Constitutional AI). Swap A/B order and average to cancel position bias (Lesson 5.3).
  • Write labelling guidelines and measure agreement between labellers. Raters (human or AI) tend to prefer longer, more confident answers; if you don't control for that, your model learns verbosity, not quality.

Distillation: teacher outputs → student SFT

Distillation trains a small student to imitate a big teacher. For LLMs the simplest form is just SFT on the teacher's answers to your prompts (“sequence-level” distillation). If you can see the teacher's token probabilities (open models only), the student can also match its whole probability distribution at each token, which carries more information per example; on-policy distillation lets the teacher grade the student's own attempts so it learns from its own mistakes.

  1. Prompts: sample real traffic (with consent and PII handling, Lesson 8.3), or generate varied ones.
  2. Teacher answers: the best prompt you can write, maybe several samples per prompt.
  3. Filter: keep answers that pass tests, a schema check or an LLM judge. Filtering usually matters more than volume.
  4. Train the student (SFT, often LoRA), optionally followed by DPO on teacher-ranked pairs.
  5. Evaluate against the teacher on a held-out set the teacher never saw in its prompts.

Synthetic data and its risks

Synthetic data is training data written by a model. It's now a normal part of training: Self-Instruct (2022) bootstrapped instructions from GPT-3 itself, and Microsoft's phi-1 (2023) reached 50.6% on the HumanEval coding benchmark with just 1.3B parameters, trained partly on 1B tokens of GPT-3.5-written “textbooks” and exercises. The risks:

RiskWhat happensMitigation
Model collapseTraining repeatedly on model outputs loses the rare cases first: a 2024 Nature paper found the tails of the original distribution disappear.Keep real data in every round; never train only on your own outputs.
ContaminationThe teacher reproduces benchmark or test items, so your eval looks great and means nothing.Deduplicate synthetic data against eval sets (n-gram or embedding overlap, Lesson 2.5).
HomogeneityThousands of answers in one voice with the same structure; the student becomes bland and brittle.Vary seeds, personas, prompts and temperature; measure diversity.
Inherited errors & biasThe student copies the teacher's mistakes, confidently.Filter with tests and judges; spot-check by hand.
Licence / termsThe provider's terms may forbid training competing models on its outputs.Read the terms; prefer permissively licensed open teachers.
⚠️ Licences and terms (as of 2026; not legal advice)
  • OpenAI's terms of use forbid using output “to develop models that compete with OpenAI”.
  • Anthropic's commercial terms forbid using the services to build a competing product, “including to train competing AI models”.
  • Llama 3.1's community licence allows using its outputs to improve other models, but a distributed model must start its name with “Llama” and you must display “Built with Llama”.
  • Open models under Apache 2.0 or MIT (for example Qwen3, gpt-oss, DeepSeek-R1) are far less restrictive. Terms change between model versions, so check each one (Lesson 7.3), and ask your legal team before you ship.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

TRL's DPOTrainer (and KTOTrainer for thumbs-up/down data, GKDTrainer for on-policy distillation); any OpenAI-compatible client to call the teacher; Amazon Bedrock has a managed distillation feature; Argilla or Label Studio for human preference labelling.

# 1) Distil: teacher answers → filtered SFT data
import json
from openai import OpenAI
teacher = OpenAI(base_url=TEACHER_URL, api_key=KEY)   # an open model you're allowed to distil
with open("distil.jsonl", "w") as f:
    for p in prompts:
        a = teacher.chat.completions.create(model=TEACHER, messages=[{"role": "user", "content": p}])
        ans = a.choices[0].message.content
        if passes_checks(p, ans):                      # schema, tests, LLM judge…
            f.write(json.dumps({"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": p},
                                             {"role": "assistant", "content": ans}]}) + "\n")
# → train the student with SFTTrainer exactly as in Lesson 7.1

# 2) Preference-tune: rows {"prompt": [...], "chosen": [...], "rejected": [...]}
from datasets import load_dataset
from peft import LoraConfig
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer
pairs = load_dataset("json", data_files="pairs.jsonl", split="train")
trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model="./student-sft",                          # start from the SFT model
    args=DPOConfig(output_dir="student-dpo", beta=0.1, learning_rate=5e-6),
    train_dataset=pairs,
    peft_config=LoraConfig(r=16, target_modules="all-linear", task_type="CAUSAL_LM"),
)
trainer.train()

If you don't pass a ref_model, TRL uses the model as it was before DPO started as the reference. In TRL's docs, β defaults to 0.1, and a higher β means less deviation from the reference.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Distil a permissively licensed open teacher on your real prompts into a small model, filter hard, and evaluate against the teacher. Collect thumbs and edits from day one; when you have a few thousand pairs, one DPO run on top of the SFT model is usually the next big win.

Big tech

Paid labelling teams with guidelines and agreement checks, AI judges for scale, reward models and on-policy RL for frontier models, synthetic-data pipelines with decontamination against every benchmark, and legal review of every data source.

🎤 Interview angle

“We want a cheaper model that's as good as our GPT-class one on our task. How?” A senior answer:

  • Distillation: teacher answers on real, representative prompts, filtered by checks, then SFT a small model; optionally DPO on teacher-ranked pairs.
  • Names the risks: licence/terms, coverage of rare cases, contamination of the eval set, inherited errors.
  • Evaluates student vs teacher on a held-out set, plus a canary set of rare cases, and keeps a fallback route to the teacher for low-confidence requests (Lesson 8.2).
  • Knows DPO vs RLHF: DPO is the default for product teams; PPO-style RL needs a reward model and is harder to run.
🏭 In the real world
Reasoning distilled into small models 🏢 DeepSeek · 2025 DeepSeek-R1 (GitHub)

How: fine-tuned open Qwen2.5 and Llama 3 models (1.5B to 70B) on 800k samples curated with DeepSeek-R1, and released the weights (each keeps its base model's licence: Apache 2.0 for Qwen, Llama's for Llama).

Why this method: distilling the big model's reasoning beat running RL directly on small models; their 32B distilled model outperformed OpenAI's o1-mini across various benchmarks.

AI feedback + DPO, no human labels 🧪 Research · 2023 Zephyr: Direct Distillation of LM Alignment

How: Hugging Face distilled SFT from a teacher, then ran DPO on pairs ranked by a teacher model (“dDPO”), in a few hours of training.

Why this method: Zephyr-7B beat Llama2-Chat-70B, the best open RLHF model at the time, on MT-Bench, with no human annotation.

A fully open post-training recipe 🧪 Research · 2024 Tülu 3 (Allen Institute for AI)

How: SFT, then DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards (answers that can be checked), on Llama 3.1 base models, with all data and code released.

Why this method: each stage fixes something the previous one can't; the result beat the official Llama 3.1 instruct models and closed models like GPT-4o-mini on their benchmarks.

The warning shot on synthetic data 🧪 Research · 2024 AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data (Nature)

How: trained models generation after generation on the previous generation's outputs.

Why this method: indiscriminate use of model-generated data caused irreversible defects, with the tails of the original distribution disappearing first.

🤯 Fun fact · the 175B model lost to the 1.3B one

In OpenAI's InstructGPT paper, people preferred the answers of a 1.3B model trained with SFT and RLHF to those of the 175B GPT-3 base model, despite 100× fewer parameters. Preference tuning doesn't add knowledge, but it makes the knowledge usable.

Check yourself

1. You need 50,000 teacher answers of ~600 output tokens each (ignore input). At an illustrative $15 per million output tokens, roughly what does generation cost?
50,000 × 600 = 30 M tokens; 30 × $15 = $450. Data generation is rarely the expensive part. Filtering, evaluation and permission are.
2. After DPO on your users' thumbs-up/down and A/B picks, answers got 40% longer and support agents complain they're padded. Most likely cause?
Preference tuning learns whatever separates chosen from rejected. If longer answers win more often, length is the easiest signal. Fix the data: guidelines, length-controlled comparisons, or pairs where the concise answer wins.
3. To save money, a team plans to retrain its model every month only on conversations its own model generated. What's the main long-term risk?
Each round trains on a slightly narrower, slightly wronger copy of the last. Mixing in fresh human data and filtering synthetic data is the standard defence.
📝 TL;DR
  • Preference tuning learns from “A beats B”; RLHF uses a reward model and RL with a KL leash, DPO does it with one classification-style loss.
  • Your model learns your raters, including their length and confidence biases: write guidelines and check them.
  • Distillation = SFT a small student on a big teacher's filtered answers; cheap to generate, big serving savings, capped near the teacher.
  • Synthetic data risks: collapse, contamination, homogeneity, inherited errors, and licence terms. Keep real data, filter, decontaminate, read the terms.
