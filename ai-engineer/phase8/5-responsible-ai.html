Responsible AI: bias, privacy, governance & regulation

Your model's mistakes are your company's mistakes, legally and morally. This lesson is the engineering side of responsibility: how to measure bias, handle personal data, document what you built, and know which laws apply before a lawyer has to tell you.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Evals turn “is it good?” into numbers on a labelled set (Lesson 5.1); you'll use the same tools to measure fairness.
  • PII (personally identifiable information) and redaction were covered in Lesson 8.3; traces and logs in 8.1.
  • A rough sense of sampling noise: a rate measured on 30 examples can easily be off by 15 points.
🎯 Hook

In 2022, after a death in his family, Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. It told him he could book a full-price flight and apply for the cheaper bereavement fare afterwards. That wasn't the airline's policy, and his refund request was refused.

At the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, Air Canada argued, in effect, that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The tribunal disagreed: the company is responsible for all the information on its website, chatbot included. It ordered Air Canada to pay C$650.88 in damages plus interest and fees (about C$812 in total). A small sum, and a big precedent: “the AI said it” is not a defence.

Models learn from human text, so they learn human patterns, including the unfair ones. The engineering response is the same as for any quality problem: measure it with a controlled test, set a threshold, fix, re-measure.

💡 Big idea Responsible AI is not a separate ethics phase at the end. It's ordinary engineering discipline applied to new risks: test for unfair outcomes like you test for wrong answers, minimise and protect personal data like any sensitive asset, document what the system does and doesn't do, keep a human accountable for high-stakes decisions, and know the rules of the markets you ship to.
🧠 Analogy: the building inspector

Nobody thinks fire exits and load calculations are “anti-architecture”. They're what lets you put people in the building. Bias audits, model cards and risk reviews are the fire exits of AI products: boring, mostly invisible, and the reason you can ship to hospitals, banks and governments instead of only to hobbyists.

Bias and fairness testing

The workhorse method for LLMs is the counterfactual audit: send the same input many times, changing only a demographic signal (a name, a pronoun, a dialect), and compare outcomes. Two standard numbers:

  • Selection rate per group = share of that group given the good outcome (shortlisted, approved).
  • Impact ratio = a group's selection rate ÷ the highest group's rate. US employment guidelines (the “four-fifths rule”) generally treat a ratio below 0.8 as evidence of adverse impact; New York City's Local Law 144 requires yearly bias audits reporting impact ratios for automated hiring tools.
✏️ Worked example: the four-fifths rule

An LLM screener shortlists 58 of 200 résumés with group-A names (29%) and 40 of 200 identical résumés with group-B names (20%).

  • Impact ratio = 20% ÷ 29% = 0.69. Below 0.8: evidence of adverse impact.
  • Is it noise? A 95% interval for each rate at n = 200 is roughly ± 6 points (1.96 × √(0.25 × 0.75 ÷ 200) ≈ 0.06), so 29% vs 20% is borderline on its own. Because the résumés are paired (same CV, different name), compare each pair's score directly: that test is much more sensitive.
🎮 Playground · Bias audit simulator

A scripted résumé screener scores each CV 1–10 and shortlists scores ≥ 7. You set the hidden truth (how much it penalises group-B names) and the audit size; the audit only sees outcomes. What you should see: small audits are noisy; the four-fifths rule can pass by luck and fail by luck. The paired score gap, which compares each CV with its own twin, catches a 0.5-point penalty clearly at 1,000 pairs, and even a 0.2-point penalty that passes the four-fifths rule. Blinding names helps, but if the CV still contains proxies for group membership, part of the bias survives. Passing a threshold is not the same as being fair.

Privacy, data retention and IP

ConcernWhat to do
Data minimisationSend the model only what the task needs; redact PII before prompts and logs (Lesson 8.3).
RetentionSet how long prompts, outputs and traces are kept; delete on schedule and on user request. Logs are the most common leak.
Provider termsRead the data-usage terms: whether inputs are used for training, how long they're stored, where they're processed, and whether zero-retention options exist for your account.
Tenant isolationOne customer's documents must never be retrieved for another (permission filters in RAG, Lesson 4.7).
Training-data IPKnow the licence of anything you fine-tune on; the EU AI Act requires general-purpose model providers to have a copyright policy.
Output IPThe US Copyright Office concluded in 2025 that prompts alone don't give enough human control to make you the author of the output; human creative contributions can still be protected.

Transparency, oversight and governance

  • Disclosure: tell people when they're talking to an AI, and label AI-generated media. The EU AI Act makes both legal duties (from August 2026).
  • Human oversight: for high-stakes decisions (hiring, credit, medical, legal) the model recommends and a person decides, with the information and authority to overrule it. Watch for automation bias: people rubber-stamping the model.
  • Documentation: a model card (Mitchell et al., 2019) states intended use, out-of-scope uses, evaluation results broken down by group, and known limitations. A datasheet (Gebru et al., 2018) does the same for a dataset.
  • Risk review: before launch, list who could be harmed and how, the controls in place, and who signs off. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) organises this into four functions: Govern (culture, roles, policies), Map (context and risks), Measure (test and track them) and Manage (prioritise and act). Its 2024 Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) adds risks specific to generative models. NIST notes the framework is being revised.

Regulation: the EU AI Act in one table

The EU AI Act sorts AI systems by risk to health, safety and fundamental rights. It applies to anyone placing AI systems on the EU market or whose system's output is used in the EU. As amended by the 2026 “AI Omnibus” (in force since 27 July 2026), the dates are:

Providers of general-purpose AI models (the foundation-model makers) have had their own transparency and copyright duties since 2 August 2025. Fines for prohibited practices reach €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Elsewhere: in the US there's no single AI law; rules are sector- and state-specific (e.g. NYC's hiring-tool audits).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Fairness metrics: Fairlearn (MetricFrame, selection rates and ratios by group), IBM's AI Fairness 360. Documentation: model cards (Hugging Face model card templates), datasheets. Frameworks: NIST AI RMF and its Generative AI Profile; ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems. Audits: a counterfactual test harness in your eval suite (Lesson 5.6).

# A counterfactual name-swap audit: same CV, different names, measure the gap
import itertools, statistics
from fairlearn.metrics import MetricFrame, selection_rate

NAMES = {"A": names_group_a, "B": names_group_b}   # from a published audit name list
rows = []
for cv, (group, names) in itertools.product(cvs, NAMES.items()):
    for name in names[:5]:                         # several names per group, not one
        score = screen(cv.replace("{NAME}", name))   # your LLM call, fixed prompt + model version
        rows.append({"cv": cv.id, "group": group, "score": score, "shortlist": int(score >= 7)})

mf = MetricFrame(metrics=selection_rate,
                 y_true=[r["shortlist"] for r in rows],      # required argument; unused by selection_rate
                 y_pred=[r["shortlist"] for r in rows],
                 sensitive_features=[r["group"] for r in rows])
print(mf.by_group)                                  # selection rate per group
print("impact ratio:", mf.ratio())                # min / max; flag below 0.8
# Then a paired test: mean score gap per CV across groups, with a confidence interval

Use several names per group (a single name tests that name, not the group), fix the prompt and model version, and rerun the audit on every model or prompt change, like any regression test.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Write a one-page model card (purpose, limits, who it's not for), disclose the AI in the UI, redact PII and set log retention, and add a counterfactual name-swap test to the eval suite if your product touches people's opportunities. Ask early whether anything you do is in a high-risk category in your markets.

At scale

A responsible-AI review board and risk process (often mapped to NIST AI RMF or ISO/IEC 42001), standard model and system cards, fairness dashboards per release, red-teaming, incident reporting, data-protection impact assessments, regional compliance teams for the EU AI Act, and contractual terms with every model provider.

🎤 Interview angle

“We want to use an LLM to pre-screen job applicants. What do you need to think about?” A senior answer:

  • Flags it as high-risk: EU AI Act Annex III (from 2 Dec 2027), NYC Local Law 144 bias audits, and anti-discrimination law everywhere.
  • Designs a counterfactual audit with selection rates, impact ratios and a paired test, run before launch and on every model change.
  • Keeps a human decision-maker, explains to candidates that AI is used, and logs inputs and outputs for audit, with retention limits.
  • Minimises data: no protected attributes or obvious proxies in the prompt; strips photos and names where possible.
  • Writes a model card with intended use, limits and per-group results.
🏭 In the real world
A company is liable for its chatbot 📜 Moffatt v. Air Canada · 2024 The Guardian

How: a tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions and held the airline to what its bot said.

Why this method: the reason grounded answers, abstaining (Lesson 3.5) and human escalation matter commercially, not just technically.

Name-swap audit of GPT résumé ranking 🏢 Bloomberg · 2024 Methodology, code and data

How: asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to rank equally qualified résumés that differed only in demographically distinctive names, many times, for four jobs.

Why this method: the classic field-experiment design, adapted to LLMs; reporters published the code so others can rerun it on newer models.

Auditing LLM advice by name 🧪 Research · 2024 What's in a Name? (Salinas, Haim, Nyarko)

How: 42 templates across scenarios (car-purchase negotiations, election predictions and more) with names associated with different races and genders, comparing the numbers models suggested.

Why this method: numeric outputs make disparities measurable; advice systematically disadvantaged names associated with racial minorities and women, with Black women faring worst; giving numerical anchors in the prompt counteracted the bias.

Mandatory bias audits for hiring tools 🏢 New York City · 2023 Local Law 144 (AEDT)

How: employers using automated employment decision tools need an independent bias audit within the past year, published impact ratios, and notice to candidates; enforced since 5 July 2023.

Why this method: turns “is it fair?” into a recurring, reported measurement, the same impact-ratio maths as the playground.

Documenting models 🧪 Research · 2019 Model Cards for Model Reporting (Mitchell et al.)

How: a short, standard document per model: intended use, factors, metrics, evaluation data, results by group, caveats.

Why this method: makes limits visible to the people deploying the model; now standard on Hugging Face and in provider system cards.

🤯 Fun fact · the 80% rule is older than the PC

The four-fifths rule used to audit 2020s AI hiring tools comes from the US Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, adopted in 1978, written for paper tests and interviews. The maths of fairness auditing hasn't changed; only the thing being audited has.

Check yourself

1. An AI tool approves 45% of applicants from group X and 33% from group Y. What's the impact ratio, and does it pass the four-fifths rule?
Lower rate ÷ highest rate = 33 ÷ 45 ≈ 0.73, below 0.8, so it's evidence of adverse impact. (1.36 is the ratio upside down; 0.88 subtracts instead of dividing.) Then check it isn't noise with enough samples and a paired test.
2. Your team ships a support chatbot to EU customers. Under the EU AI Act, what's the main obligation?
A general support chatbot is in the transparency tier: disclose it's an AI (and label generated content). It becomes high-risk only if it's used for things like deciding access to essential services or employment.
3. A 40-résumé name-swap audit shows an impact ratio of 0.95. A manager says “we're fair, ship it.” What's the best reply?
With 40 résumés, selection rates swing by about ±13 points from noise alone; a real penalty can hide. A paired test on more CVs gives a confidence interval. Blinding helps but doesn't remove proxies, and you still need to measure.
📝 TL;DR
  • You're responsible for what your AI says and decides (Moffatt v. Air Canada).
  • Measure bias with counterfactual audits: selection rates, impact ratio (four-fifths rule), and a paired score test with enough samples. Passing a threshold ≠ fair.
  • Minimise, redact and expire personal data; read provider data terms; mind training-data and output IP.
  • Disclose AI, keep humans accountable for high-stakes calls, write model cards, run risk reviews (NIST AI RMF).
  • EU AI Act: banned / high-risk (from Dec 2027 and Aug 2028) / transparency (Aug 2026) / minimal; fines up to €35M or 7%.
