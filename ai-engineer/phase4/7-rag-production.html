Phase 4 · Lesson 7

RAG in production: freshness, permissions, caching & cost

A demo answers questions. A production RAG system answers them for the right person, from current data, within a latency and cost budget, and forgets what it's told to forget.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Metadata filters on vector search (Lesson 3.4) and vector databases (Lesson 2.3).
  • Embedding a corpus costs tokens; changing the embedding model means re-embedding (Lessons 2.1 and 4.2).
  • Prompt injection: instructions hidden in retrieved content (preview of Lesson 8.3).
🎯 Hook

In August 2024 the security firm PromptArmor disclosed an attack on Slack AI. Slack AI's retrieval searched public channels even when the user hadn't joined them (Slack called that intended behaviour). So an attacker could post a message with hidden instructions in a public channel. When a victim later asked Slack AI about something in their own private channel, retrieval pulled in the attacker's message too, and the instructions could make the answer leak the private data through a link.

No database was breached. The leak happened in the retrieval scope: what the system was allowed to put into the prompt, for whom, alongside what.

Numbers first: the serving budget

A typical RAG request, end to end (illustrative, measure yours):

StageTypical latencyTypical cost driver
Cache lookup (embed question + search cache)20–50 msTiny
Query rewrite (small LLM, Lesson 4.1)300–800 ms≈ 300 tokens
Embed query + vector search with filters20–100 msDatabase capacity
Rerank top 50 (cross-encoder)100–300 msReranker calls
Generation: TTFT (time to first token) + streaming0.5–1 s + ≈ 3–5 s≈ 3,000 input + 300 output tokens: usually most of the bill

Generation dominates both latency and cost, so the two biggest levers are sending fewer, better tokens (Lessons 3.5 and 4.2) and not calling the model at all when you've answered the same question before (caching). Every other production concern in this lesson is about doing all of that correctly: for the right user, from fresh data.

💡 Big idea In production, the retrieval scope is a security boundary and a correctness boundary. Whatever retrieval can return, the model can repeat. So permissions are enforced inside the search (not after it, not in the prompt), caches are keyed by who is allowed to see the answer, deletes reach every copy, and freshness is a number you monitor, not a hope.
🧠 Analogy: the library with a locked room

A library has a public floor and a locked archive. A good librarian never fetches from the archive for someone without a key, even if the book is the best match. They don't fetch it and then hide it behind their back either: they shouldn't have walked in. And if a book is withdrawn, it comes off the shelf, out of the photocopy tray and out of the “popular answers” binder at the front desk.

Permissions: filter inside the search

ApproachWhat happensVerdict
No filter, “the prompt says don't reveal HR data”Restricted chunks enter the context; one injection or misread and they're in the answerA leak waiting to happen
Post-filter: retrieve top-k, then drop what the user can't seeSafe, but if 4 of your top 5 were restricted, you answer from 1 chunk (or none)Safe, poor recall
Pre-filter: the ACL (access-control list) is a metadata filter inside the vector querySearch only among chunks the user may read; you still get k good resultsThe standard
Separate index per tenant (namespace)Hard isolation between customers; per-tenant deletes are trivialFor multi-tenant SaaS

Store each chunk's allowed groups (copied from the source system: SharePoint, Drive, Confluence, your database) as metadata at ingestion, and pass the user's groups from your auth system at query time, never from the model. Microsoft states the rule for 365 Copilot plainly: it only surfaces organizational data the individual user has at least view permission for.

What you should see: with no filter, Alice (an engineer) asking about salary bands gets the HR-only salary table and offer rules in her context. Post-filtering stops the leak but leaves her with fewer chunks than k. Pre-filtering gives her a full set of chunks she's allowed to read. Freshness: how stale is the answer?

Your index is a copy of the source systems, and copies go stale. The useful number is the staleness window: time from a change in the source (a policy edited, a ticket closed, a doc deleted) until retrieval reflects it.

StrategyStaleness windowCost
Nightly full rebuildup to 24 hRe-embeds everything every night: wasteful at scale
Scheduled incremental sync (changed since last run, by timestamp or content hash)minutes to an hourOnly changed chunks are re-embedded
Event-driven: CDC (change data capture) or webhooks per changeseconds to minutesA pipeline to operate; the right choice for tickets, chats, inventory
Don't index it: query the live system as a tool (Lessons 4.4 and 4.6)zeroA call per question; only for structured, fast sources (prices, stock, order status)

Two more freshness traps live at serving time: caches (a cached answer is as stale as its TTL, time-to-live) and derived data (summaries, graph communities and hypothetical questions from Lessons 4.2–4.3 must be regenerated when their sources change). How to run the ingestion jobs, queues and backfills that keep an index fresh at scale is Lesson 4.8.

✏️ Worked example: freshness, deletes and a model change

A knowledge base of 2 million documents ≈ 20 million chunks of 300 tokens = 6 billion tokens.

  • Nightly full rebuild with an embedding model at $0.02 per million tokens: 6,000 × $0.02 = $120 a night ≈ $3,600 a month, and up to 24 h stale.
  • Incremental: if 0.5% of chunks change per day, 100,000 chunks × 300 = 30M tokens = $0.60 a day, and staleness drops to however often you sync.
  • Changing the embedding model (vectors from different models can't be compared, so it's all or nothing): 6B tokens once, $120 at $0.02/M or $780 at $0.13/M, plus running old and new indexes side by side until the new one passes your eval. The money is small; the time, rate limits and cutover are the work (Lesson 4.8).
  • A deletion request must reach: the source copy in your doc store, every chunk's vector, caches holding answers built from it, derived summaries and graph nodes, and logs or eval sets that copied it. GDPR's Article 17 requires erasure “without undue delay”.

Embedding prices: OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small and -large list prices as of October 2026. Many vector indexes mark deleted vectors with tombstones and reclaim space later, so check that deleted items are also excluded from search immediately.

Monitoring retrieval in production

Offline evals (Lesson 5.4) tell you the system was good on launch day. In production, watch signals that move when retrieval breaks:

  • Empty or low-score retrievals: share of queries whose best chunk scores below a threshold; spikes mean a new topic or a broken index.
  • Abstention rate (“I don't know”) and citation coverage (answers with at least one citation).
  • Freshness lag: newest indexed timestamp vs source; failed ingestion jobs.
  • User signals: thumbs, citation clicks, reformulated questions right after an answer.
  • Sampled LLM-judge scores on live traces for groundedness and relevance (Lesson 5.6), plus per-stage latency and cost (Lesson 8.1).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Vector databases with filtered search and multi-tenancy: Qdrant (payload index with is_tenant), Pinecone (one namespace per tenant), Weaviate (native multi-tenancy), pgvector (Postgres filters, row-level security, iterative index scans). Semantic caches: Redis (LangCache), GPTCache. Tracing and monitoring: Langfuse, LangSmith, Arize Phoenix, OpenTelemetry (Lesson 8.1).

# Qdrant: tenant isolation + document ACLs enforced inside the vector query
from qdrant_client import QdrantClient, models
client = QdrantClient(url="http://localhost:6333")
client.create_payload_index("chunks", field_name="tenant_id",
    field_schema=models.KeywordIndexParams(type=models.KeywordIndexType.KEYWORD, is_tenant=True))

hits = client.query_points("chunks", query=query_vector, limit=5,
    query_filter=models.Filter(must=[
        models.FieldCondition(key="tenant_id", match=models.MatchValue(value=user.tenant_id)),
        models.FieldCondition(key="allowed_groups", match=models.MatchAny(any=user.groups)),  # from your auth system
    ])).points

# Semantic cache key: never share answers across permission sets or index versions
cache_key = (embed(question), frozenset(user.groups), INDEX_VERSION)
-- pgvector: the same filter in SQL; iterative scans keep k results under selective filters
SET hnsw.iterative_scan = strict_order;
SELECT id, content FROM chunks
WHERE tenant_id = $1 AND allowed_groups && $2      -- array overlap with the user's groups
ORDER BY embedding <=> $3                          -- cosine distance
LIMIT 5;

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

One database (Postgres + pgvector or a managed vector DB), tenant_id and allowed_groups on every chunk from day one (retrofitting ACLs is painful), incremental sync by content hash, an exact-match cache keyed by user groups, and traces with per-stage timings.

At scale

Permission sync services per source system, event-driven indexing, per-tenant indexes for big customers, deletion pipelines with audit trails, semantic caches with per-topic thresholds and TTLs, latency budgets per route, and dashboards for staleness, empty retrievals and groundedness.

🎤 Interview angle

“Make our internal RAG assistant safe to roll out to the whole company.” A senior answer:

  • Permissions: ACLs synced from source systems as chunk metadata, enforced as pre-filters with the user's groups from auth; never “the prompt says don't”.
  • Caches and derived data keyed by permission set and index version; deletes propagate everywhere, with an audit trail.
  • Freshness as an SLO (service-level objective): incremental or event-driven sync, staleness monitored, live tools for volatile data.
  • Budget: per-stage latency and cost, caching strategy, and production monitoring of retrieval quality; mentions prompt injection from retrieved content.
🏭 In the real world
Retrieval scope as an attack surface 🏢 Slack / PromptArmor · 2024 Data exfiltration from Slack AI via indirect prompt injection

How: Slack AI retrieved from public channels the user wasn't in; an attacker's instructions in a public channel rode along with the victim's private data into the prompt.

Why it matters: permission filtering alone isn't enough when retrieved text can carry instructions; what you retrieve together is part of the security model.

Permission-trimmed enterprise retrieval 🏢 Microsoft Data, privacy and security for Microsoft 365 Copilot

How: Copilot only surfaces organizational data that the individual user has at least view permission for, relying on the existing permission models of services such as SharePoint.

Why this method: the source systems already know who can see what; RAG must inherit those permissions rather than invent its own, which also means oversharing in the source becomes oversharing in answers.

Multi-tenancy in one collection 🧰 Open source Qdrant: multitenancy

How: a tenant field on every point, indexed as a keyword payload with is_tenant: true, and a filter on it in every query.

Why this method: thousands of small tenants are cheaper in one collection than in thousands; the tenant index lets the engine organise storage per tenant for fast filtered search.

A namespace per customer 🏢 Pinecone Implement multitenancy

How: one namespace per tenant on a serverless index; every upsert and query targets exactly one namespace.

Why this method: isolation by construction, and offboarding a tenant is deleting a namespace; plans allow from 100 to 1,000,000 namespaces per index.

The right to be forgotten 📜 History · 2016 GDPR Article 17

How: data subjects can require erasure of their personal data, and the controller must erase it without undue delay when grounds apply.

Why it matters for RAG: personal data copied into chunks, vectors, caches, summaries and logs is still personal data; deletion has to reach every copy.

🤯 Fun fact · the filter that ate your results

pgvector's README spells out a classic surprise: approximate search finds candidates first and filters after, so if a condition matches 10% of rows, an HNSW index with the default hnsw.ef_search of 40 returns only about 4 matching rows on average, even if you asked for 10. The fix is iterative index scans (or partial indexes), which keep searching until enough rows pass the filter.

Check yourself

1. 200,000 queries a day, $0.012 per uncached answer. A semantic cache reaches a 30% hit rate, with 2% of hits wrong. Daily saving, and wrong answers served from cache?
Hits: 200,000 × 0.3 = 60,000 × $0.012 = $720 saved (minus small lookup costs). Wrong: 60,000 × 0.02 = 1,200 a day. Whether that's worth it depends on what a wrong answer costs you; often it means a stricter threshold.
2. A contractor asks the internal assistant about “Q3 layoffs” and receives a summary of an exec-only planning doc. Retrieval has a metadata filter on allowed_groups. Where should you look first?
If the index filter works, the leak comes through something keyed without permissions (caches) or derived from restricted content (summaries, communities), or the chunk's ACL metadata is wrong because sync failed. Derived data must inherit the strictest ACL of its sources.
3. Support agents complain the assistant cites a refund policy that changed this morning. Your index rebuilds nightly. Best fix?
Hourly full rebuilds are wasteful and still stale. Re-embed only what changed as it changes, invalidate cached answers that depended on it, and monitor the lag so you know when it breaks.
📝 TL;DR
  • Whatever retrieval returns, the model can repeat: enforce ACLs inside the search (pre-filter), with groups from auth, and namespaces per tenant when isolation matters.
  • Caches and derived data are copies: key them by permissions and index version, and make deletes reach every copy.
  • Freshness is a staleness window you measure: incremental or event-driven updates, live tools for volatile data.
  • Generation dominates latency and cost: send fewer tokens, cache carefully (false hits are confident wrong answers), and monitor retrieval in production.
