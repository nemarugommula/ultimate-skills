Phase 4 · Lesson 2

Advanced indexing: parent-child, hierarchical, multi-vector, ColBERT

Naive RAG embeds a chunk and hands that same chunk to the model. Advanced indexes break that rule: what you search with and what you read don't have to be the same text.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Chunking: small chunks match precisely but may miss context; big chunks are complete but blurry (Lesson 3.3).
  • Embedding: one vector per piece of text; similar meaning → nearby vectors (Lesson 2.1). A vector index such as HNSW (a graph-based approximate nearest-neighbour index) finds the nearest ones fast (Lesson 2.2).
  • Reranking: a slower model re-scores the top candidates (Lesson 3.4).
🎯 Hook

QuALITY is a benchmark of multiple-choice questions about long stories and articles, the kind where you must understand the whole text, not find one sentence. In 2024 a Stanford team stopped feeding the model flat chunks. They clustered the chunks, had an LLM summarise each cluster, clustered the summaries, summarised again, and searched the whole tree at once.

That method, RAPTOR, paired with GPT-4, improved the best published QuALITY result by 20 points of absolute accuracy. Same documents, same model, just a smarter index.

Numbers first: the precision–completeness squeeze

Take one handbook question, “How long do Plus members have to ask for a refund?”. The answer needs two adjacent sentences: “Refunds are available within 30 days…” and “This window extends to 60 days for members of the Plus program.”

On real corpora with thousands of sections, the blur of big chunks also costs you retrieval accuracy: the Dense X Retrieval study found that indexing fine-grained propositions instead of passages raised average Recall@5 by 12.0 and 9.3 points for two unsupervised retrievers.

💡 Big idea Decouple the retrieval unit from the reading unit. Index small, sharp, self-contained things to find (sentences, propositions, summaries, hypothetical questions, even single tokens), and keep a pointer from each to the bigger thing the model should read (the parent section, a window of neighbours, the whole page). Almost every technique here is a different choice of “what to search” and “what to return”.
🧠 Analogy: the index at the back of a book

A book's index lists tiny, precise terms (“refund window, 60 days … p. 42”). You never read the index entry as the answer: you follow it to page 42 and read the page. The table of contents is a different index for a different job: chapter summaries for broad questions (“what's this book about?”). Small-to-big retrieval is the back-of-book index; hierarchical summaries are the table of contents.

Multi-vector and propositions: index what people will ask

A chunk of a pricing table embeds badly: numbers and pipes, little meaning. Index an LLM-written summary (“Prices of the Team and Business plans per seat per month”) or a few hypothetical questions (“How much is the Team plan?”, “Is SSO included in Business?”) and point each back to the chunk. Questions match questions far better than they match tables. Propositions push the same idea to atomic facts: “Plus members can get refunds within 60 days of delivery.” Each is standalone, so pronouns and missing subjects disappear.

For the chunk below, write 3 questions a customer might ask that this chunk answers. One per line. Refunds are available within 30 days of delivery. This window extends to 60 days for members of the Plus program. → How long do I have to return an item? → Do Plus members get longer to ask for a refund? → Is the refund window 30 or 60 days? (embed these 3; each points to the chunk)

Hierarchical summaries: RAPTOR

“What are the main themes in this quarter's feedback?” has no single chunk that answers it. RAPTOR (Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval) builds a tree bottom-up: embed the chunks, cluster them (with Gaussian mixture models), have an LLM summarise each cluster, then repeat on the summaries until one root remains. At query time the paper's best variant, the collapsed tree, puts every node from every level into one pool and retrieves the best matches up to a token budget, so specific questions hit leaves and broad ones hit summaries. (The alternative, walking the tree top-down layer by layer, did consistently worse.)

🎮 Playground · A RAPTOR-style tree

Nine pieces of customer feedback (leaves, green), three cluster summaries and one root summary (purple, simulated LLM-written summaries). Ask a specific question and a broad one, with normal leaf-only retrieval and with the collapsed tree. Retrieved nodes get a teal outline.

With leaves only, a broad question retrieves a couple of individual complaints and the model “summarises” from a keyhole: at best two of the three themes. The collapsed tree retrieves the root and a cluster summary, covering all three, while the specific question about the Toronto parcel still lands on its leaf. The price is paid once, at indexing: one LLM call per cluster per level, and a rebuild of the affected branches when documents change.

Late interaction: ColBERT

A single vector squeezes a whole chunk into one point. ColBERT (Khattab and Zaharia, 2020) keeps one small vector (128 numbers) per token. To score a document, each query token finds its most similar document token and the maxima are added up: MaxSim. It's called late interaction because query and document are encoded separately (so documents can be pre-computed) and only meet at the end, in this cheap step.

query token ↓ · doc token →jacketwarrantydefectszippersmax
warranty0.200.950.500.100.95
zip0.300.100.400.900.90

Illustrative similarities. Score = 0.95 + 0.90 = 1.85. Every query word must find its own match, so rare terms (product codes, names, “zip”) aren't averaged away.

The paper reported quality competitive with BERT cross-encoder rerankers while running two orders of magnitude faster, with four orders of magnitude fewer FLOPs (floating-point operations) per query. The catch is storage, which ColBERTv2's compression attacks.

✏️ Worked example: what each index costs to store

1 million chunks of 200 tokens.

  • Single vector, 1,024 dimensions × 4 bytes (float32): 1,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 = 4.1 GB.
  • ColBERT, 200 token vectors × 128 dimensions × 2 bytes (float16): 1,000,000 × 200 × 128 × 2 = 51.2 GB, about 12× more.
  • ColBERTv2 residual compression shrinks late-interaction indexes by 6–10×: 51.2 ÷ 10 to 51.2 ÷ 6 ≈ 5.1–8.5 GB.
  • Multi-vector (chunk + 1 summary + 3 questions = 5 single vectors): 5 × 4.1 = 20.5 GB, plus an LLM call per chunk at indexing. At an illustrative $1 per million input and $5 per million output tokens, 300 tokens in and 80 out per chunk: 1M × (300 × $1 + 80 × $5) ÷ 1M = $700, once.

None of these is outrageous, but each is a line in the budget, and each must be rebuilt when you change the embedding model (Lesson 4.7).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Sentence window or parent-document retrieval: a parser setting, no extra LLM cost, often a clear win. Add hypothetical questions only for content that embeds badly (tables, FAQs with odd wording). Keep everything in one vector database with a parent ID in metadata.

At scale

Several indexes per corpus (chunks, propositions or questions, summaries), a late-interaction or cross-encoder reranker, and index builds run as versioned batch pipelines with their own evals. Teams measure index size and rebuild time as carefully as recall.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our retrieved chunks match well but the answers miss context. And sometimes nothing matches broad questions.” A senior answer:

  • Names the cause: retrieval unit = reading unit. Proposes small-to-big or sentence windows for the context problem.
  • For broad, corpus-level questions, proposes hierarchical summaries (RAPTOR) or GraphRAG's community summaries (Lesson 4.3), with their indexing cost.
  • Mentions multi-vector indexing (questions/summaries) for tables and jargon, and ColBERT when exact terms matter, with the storage math.
  • Says how to measure: recall@k and context precision on a labelled set, tokens per answer, and index size/rebuild time.
🏭 In the real world
A tree of summaries 🧪 Research · 2024 RAPTOR (Stanford)

How: recursively embed, cluster (Gaussian mixtures) and summarise chunks into a tree; retrieve from all levels at once (“collapsed tree”).

Why this method: questions about whole documents need integrated context. With GPT-4 it improved the best QuALITY result by 20% absolute accuracy; summaries averaged a 72% compression of their children.

Index facts, not passages 🧪 Research · 2023 Dense X Retrieval

How: an LLM rewrites passages into propositions (atomic, self-contained factoids), and those are indexed.

Why this method: proposition-level retrieval beat sentence and passage level for every dense retriever tested; for unsupervised retrievers average Recall@5 rose by 12.0 and 9.3 points (35% and 22.5% relative).

Token-level vectors, compressed 🧪 Research · 2021 ColBERTv2 (Stanford)

How: late interaction with residual compression of token vectors and denoised training.

Why this method: kept late interaction's quality in and out of domain while cutting its storage footprint 6–10×, the main obstacle to production use.

Parent-document retrieval as a built-in 🧰 Open source LangChain ParentDocumentRetriever

How: splits documents into parents and children, embeds children, stores parents in a document store, and returns parents by ID at query time.

Why this method: its docstring states the tension directly: small documents for accurate embeddings, long enough documents to retain context. One retriever gives you both.

🤯 Fun fact · ColBERT's padding trick

ColBERT pads every query with special mask tokens up to a fixed length (32 in the original setup). Those extra tokens get their own vectors too, and act as a learned, soft query expansion: the model uses them to add related terms you never typed. ColPali (Lesson 4.5) uses the same trick for page images.

Check yourself

1. You switch 2 million chunks (250 tokens each) from one 768-dimension float32 vector to ColBERT token vectors (128 dims, float16, no compression). Roughly how big is the new index?
Single: 2M × 768 × 4 B ≈ 6.1 GB. ColBERT: 2M × 250 × 128 × 2 B = 128 GB, ≈ 21×. That's why ColBERTv2's 6–10× compression (or using ColBERT only as a reranker on top candidates) matters.
2. Retrieved sentences match questions perfectly, but answers often say “the policy doesn't specify”. Logs show the retrieved text is “This applies to members only.” What's the cheapest fix?
The match is fine; the reading unit is too small (“this” has lost its subject). Returning the surrounding context fixes it without any extra LLM calls at indexing time.
3. Users of your contracts assistant ask both “What's the termination notice period in the Acme MSA?” and “What are the riskiest themes across all our vendor contracts?”. Which index design fits both?
Specific questions need precise leaves; corpus-wide questions need pre-computed summaries that no single chunk contains. A collapsed tree (or GraphRAG, Lesson 4.3) serves both from one pool.
📝 TL;DR
  • Search with small, sharp units; read bigger ones: small-to-big, sentence windows, auto-merging.
  • Index what people will ask: summaries, hypothetical questions, propositions, each pointing back to the source chunk.
  • Broad questions need summaries no chunk contains: RAPTOR's collapsed tree retrieves from every level.
  • ColBERT keeps a vector per token and scores with MaxSim: precise, but plan for ~10× storage before compression.
