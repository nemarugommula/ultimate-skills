Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Query transformation: rewriting, multi-query, HyDE, decomposition

Users type questions the way people talk. Documents are written the way experts write. Before you search, it often pays to turn the question into a better search.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): retrieve the most relevant chunks of your documents, put them in the prompt, then generate (Lesson 3.1).
  • Dense search compares embedding vectors; BM25 scores keyword overlap; hybrid search merges both rankings (Lessons 2.1 and 2.4).
  • Top-k: how many chunks you hand to the model; recall@k: how often the right chunk is among them (Lesson 3.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2022, researchers at Carnegie Mellon and Waterloo tried something that sounds wrong. Instead of searching with the user's question, they asked a language model to write a fake answer first (details made up and all) and searched with that. They called it HyDE.

On the TREC DL19 web-search benchmark, a search model with no task-specific training scored 44.5 nDCG@10 (a ranking-quality score out of 100). Searching with the fake answer instead: 61.3. A model fine-tuned on hundreds of thousands of labelled examples scored 62.1. No training at all, just a better query.

Numbers first: what a transformation costs

Every technique in this lesson adds work before retrieval, usually one call to a small, fast LLM. Here's the typical bill on top of a plain RAG query (illustrative; measure yours).

Latency mostly comes from output tokens: a 20-token rewrite is fast; a 150-token hypothetical document is slower. Searches can run in parallel.

The win can be large because retrieval is the bottleneck: if the right chunk isn't retrieved, nothing downstream can save the answer.

💡 Big idea The user's message is a first draft of a search query, not the query itself. Query transformation uses a cheap LLM call to turn it into one or more queries that look like the documents you're searching: standalone, specific, in the documents' own vocabulary, and one question at a time.
🧠 Analogy: the reference librarian

You walk up to a librarian and say “and how long do I have to send it back?” A good librarian doesn't search the catalogue for those words. They remember you mentioned your Plus membership (rewrite), think of a few ways the policy might be titled (multi-query), picture what the answer page would look like (HyDE), check the general returns policy first (step-back), and if you asked two things at once, look them up separately (decomposition).

The five transformations

1. Rewriting: make the question standalone

In a chat, the latest message leans on earlier ones: “and what about Plus?”, “how long for that?”. Searching for those words alone finds nothing useful. A rewrite prompt sees the conversation and outputs one self-contained query. This is the single most useful transformation in chat products, and it's cheap.

Conversation so far: user: I'm a Plus member and bought a jacket six weeks ago. assistant: Thanks! How can I help with your jacket? user: and how long do I have to send it back? Rewrite the last user message as one standalone search query. Keep names, numbers and constraints. Output only the query. → How many days do Plus members have to return a jacket for a refund?

The research version, Rewrite-Retrieve-Read (Ma et al., 2023), even trained a small rewriter with reinforcement learning from the reader's feedback. Most products just prompt a small model.

2. Multi-query + fusion (RAG-Fusion)

One phrasing can miss; several phrasings rarely all miss. Ask the LLM for 3–5 different versions of the query, search with each, then merge the ranked lists with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): every list gives each document a score of 1 ÷ (k + rank), and you add them up. Documents that rank well in many lists float to the top. (k is usually 60; it softens the gap between rank 1 and rank 2.)

RRF(d) = Σlists 1 / (k + ranklist(d))

This pattern was popularised as RAG-Fusion in 2023. An evaluation at Infineon found its answers more comprehensive, but also that some drifted off topic when the generated queries strayed from the original. Keep the original query in the mix.

3. HyDE: search with a hypothetical answer

Questions and answers look different. “my zip came apart, am I covered?” shares almost no words with “Every jacket has a two-year warranty against defects.” HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al., 2022) asks the LLM to write a short passage that would answer the question, embeds that passage, and searches with its vector. The fake passage may contain wrong details, but it is written in answer vocabulary, so its vector lands near the real answer. Query2doc (Microsoft, 2023) does the same for keyword search by appending the generated passage to the query, improving BM25 by 3–15% on MS MARCO and TREC DL.

Write a short passage from a store help page that answers: my zip came apart after a month of hiking, am I covered? → A broken zipper is a defect covered by the jacket warranty. To make a claim, send a photo of the defect to support. (embed this, not the question)

The danger: if the question is ambiguous or needs facts the model can't know (like your conversation), the hypothetical answer invents a specific number and drags retrieval toward the wrong chunk. You'll cause exactly that below.

4. Step-back prompting: ask the general question first

Over-specific questions (“I clicked the fastest option at 1:30 on Tuesday, when do I get my parcel?”) are full of details no document mentions. Step-back prompting (Google DeepMind, 2023) first asks a more general question (“What are the delivery speeds and order cutoff times?”), retrieves or reasons about the principle, then answers the specific case. In the paper it improved PaLM-2L by 7% and 11% on MMLU Physics and Chemistry, 27% on TimeQA and 7% on the multi-hop MuSiQue benchmark.

5. Decomposition: one question at a time

“Compare the refund window and the warranty length for Plus members” needs two unrelated chunks. One embedding of the whole sentence lands between them and often finds only one. Decomposition splits it into sub-questions, retrieves for each, and answers from the union. When later sub-questions depend on earlier answers (“who manages the team that owns X?”), you answer them in order; that's least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al., 2022), and it becomes iterative, agentic retrieval in Lesson 4.4.

And one decision before all of them: routing

Some questions shouldn't hit your vector store at all: “thanks!” needs no retrieval, “how much revenue did EMEA make in March?” belongs in SQL (Lesson 4.6), “what happened in the news today?” needs web search. A router (a classifier or a cheap LLM call) picks the source and strategy first. Lesson 4.4 builds one.

Search is the course's offline toy hybrid search (embedding + BM25, fused with RRF). Real embedding models are much better at synonyms, but they fail the same way on follow-ups, compound questions and over-specific phrasing.

What you should see: rewrite fixes four of five on its own, which is why it's the default in chat. HyDE is brilliant for the zipper question but, without the conversation, it invents “30 days” for the follow-up and retrieves the wrong policy. Only decomposition gets both halves of the compare question, at the price of sending more chunks. No single transformation wins everywhere, so production systems pick per question (that's routing) or chain two: rewrite, then decompose.

✏️ Worked example: RRF by hand

Three query variants return these top-3 lists (k = 60):

  • Variant A: D7, D8, D2   ·   Variant B: D7, D8, D0   ·   Variant C: D0, D1, D7
  • D7 = 1/61 + 1/61 + 1/63 = 0.01639 + 0.01639 + 0.01587 = 0.04866
  • D8 = 1/62 + 1/62 = 0.03226;   D0 = 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.03227;   D1 = 1/62 = 0.01613;   D2 = 1/63 = 0.01587

D7 wins: it was near the top everywhere. D8 and D0 tie almost exactly: being #2 twice is worth the same as #1 once plus #3 once. Notice RRF never looks at raw similarity scores, only ranks, which is why it can fuse dense, keyword and multi-query lists whose scores aren't comparable.

Cost: 4 variants (original + 3) means 1 LLM call (≈ 60 output tokens) + 4 embeddings + 4 searches. At $0.02 per million embedding tokens, embedding four 15-token queries costs about $0.0000012: the LLM call and the latency matter, the extra searches barely do.

🎮 Playground · Multi-query fusion, by the numbers

The original question alone misses the warranty chunks. Turn generated variants on and off (simulated LLM output), and slide RRF's k. Watch the fused list: the gold chunks (✓) need several lists to agree. Variant 4 has drifted off topic: see what it does to the fused ranking.

With k = 1, being ranked first is worth twice as much as being second, so one confident list can dominate. With k = 60, ranks 1 and 2 are worth almost the same and agreement across lists decides. That's why 60 is the usual default.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Most teams write these as a few lines of prompt + code, often with a small fast model. Frameworks have them built in: LangChain's MultiQueryRetriever (in langchain_classic since LangChain 1.0), LlamaIndex's QueryFusionRetriever (RRF via mode="reciprocal_rerank") and HyDEQueryTransform. Managed search has it too: Azure AI Search's generative query rewriting (preview) creates up to 10 rewrites with a fine-tuned small model.

from anthropic import Anthropic
client = Anthropic()
FAST = "claude-haiku-5-5"   # a small, fast model (as of 2026)

def llm(prompt, max_tokens=200):
    r = client.messages.create(model=FAST, max_tokens=max_tokens,
                               messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
    return r.content[0].text.strip()

def rewrite(history, message):
    return llm(f"Conversation:\n{history}\nUser: {message}\n\n"
               "Rewrite the last user message as ONE standalone search query. Output only the query.")

def variants(q, n=3):
    out = llm(f"Write {n} different search queries for: {q}\nOne per line, no numbering.")
    return [q] + [line for line in out.splitlines() if line.strip()][:n]  # keep the original

def rrf(ranked_lists, k=60):
    scores = {}
    for ranking in ranked_lists:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

q = rewrite(history, user_message)
fused = rrf([search(v, k=20) for v in variants(q)])[:5]   # run searches in parallel in production
# The same with LlamaIndex: several queries, fused with RRF
from llama_index.core.retrievers import QueryFusionRetriever
retriever = QueryFusionRetriever([vector_retriever, bm25_retriever], similarity_top_k=5,
                                 num_queries=4, mode="reciprocal_rerank")
nodes = retriever.retrieve("my zip came apart, am I covered?")
⚖️ Trade-offs
Technique✅ Gains❌ Costs and risks
RewriteFixes follow-ups; cheap; easy to log and debugCan drop a constraint (“for Plus members”) or add one the user never said
Multi-query + RRFRobust recall; fuses any retrieversN× searches; drifted variants add noise; more chunks to rerank
HyDEBridges question vs answer vocabulary; zero trainingSlowest; hallucinated specifics pull toward wrong chunks; weak on private jargon the model has never seen
Step-backFinds the general rule behind a specific caseThe model must still apply the rule correctly; extra retrieval
DecompositionThe only fix for compound questionsMore searches and chunks; dependent sub-questions need sequential, slower loops
🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Add a rewrite step for anything conversational, keep the original query alongside it, and log both. Use hybrid search so keywords still work. Add multi-query only after your eval shows recall misses that phrasing explains.

At scale

A router picks the transformation per query class, a small fine-tuned model does rewrites in tens of milliseconds, variants run in parallel, results are cached, and each transformation is an A/B-tested stage with its own retrieval eval and latency budget.

🎤 Interview angle

“Users complain the chatbot ignores context in follow-up questions. What do you do?” A senior answer:

  • Diagnoses first: log the actual search query and retrieved chunks for failing turns; confirm it's a retrieval miss, not generation.
  • Adds query rewriting with conversation history (small model, short output), keeping the original query too.
  • Knows the menu: multi-query + RRF for recall, HyDE for vocabulary gaps, decomposition for compound questions, and their latency/cost.
  • Measures recall@k on a set of multi-turn conversations before and after, plus p95 latency (Lessons 5.4 and 8.2).
🏭 In the real world
Search with a fake answer 🧪 Research · 2022 HyDE (Gao et al.)

How: an instruction-following model writes a hypothetical document; an unsupervised encoder (Contriever) embeds it; the vector retrieves real documents.

Why this method: no relevance labels needed. On TREC DL19, nDCG@10 rose from 44.5 (Contriever) to 61.3, close to a fine-tuned retriever's 62.1.

Rewrites inside a managed search engine 🏢 Microsoft · 2024 Azure AI Search query rewriting

How: a fine-tuned small language model generates up to 10 query rewrites before first-stage retrieval, then a cross-encoder reranks the top 50.

Why this method: Microsoft reports +4 points NDCG@3, especially for keyword indexes and low-recall queries, with a small model chosen for low latency.

RAG-Fusion for product questions 🏢 Infineon · 2024 RAG-Fusion: a New Take on RAG

How: generate several queries from the user's question, retrieve for each, fuse with reciprocal rank fusion, answer from the fused set.

Why this method: manual evaluation found more accurate and comprehensive answers, with the caveat that answers strayed when generated queries drifted from the original.

Abstraction before reasoning 🧪 Research · 2023 Step-Back Prompting (Google DeepMind)

How: ask a higher-level question about concepts or principles, retrieve or reason about it, then answer the original.

Why this method: gains of 7–27% across STEM, knowledge QA and multi-hop tasks with PaLM-2L, with the same idea working for GPT-4 and Llama 2.

🤯 Fun fact · the oldest trick in search

Reciprocal rank fusion predates LLMs by more than a decade: Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher published it at SIGIR 2009, where the simple formula beat more elaborate fusion methods. The k = 60 you'll see in almost every library comes from that paper.

Check yourself

1. Multi-query with 4 variants, each searched for its top 20. Using RRF with k = 60, a chunk ranked #1 in one list and absent from the others scores 1/61 ≈ 0.0164. A chunk ranked #5 in all four lists scores about…
4 × 1/(60 + 5) = 4/65 ≈ 0.0615. Consistent agreement across lists beats a single top rank; that's the whole point of fusion (and why a single drifted variant can't dominate).
2. Your support bot gets “and for international orders?” as the third message of a chat about shipping times. Retrieval returns random chunks. What's the best first fix?
The query lacks its subject, which lives in the conversation. A rewrite with history restores it cheaply. HyDE without history would guess; a bigger k just adds noise and tokens.
3. “Which is cheaper per seat, the Team plan or the Business plan, and does either include SSO?” What transformation fits best?
It's a compound question needing several separate facts that probably live in different chunks. One vector for the whole sentence tends to land between them and retrieve only one.
📝 TL;DR
  • The user's message is a draft; a cheap LLM call can turn it into queries that look like your documents.
  • Rewrite for follow-ups (the default in chat), multi-query + RRF for recall, HyDE for vocabulary gaps, step-back for over-specific questions, decomposition for compound ones.
  • Each costs an LLM call and some latency; keep the original query in the mix and measure recall@k before and after.
  • No transformation wins everywhere: route per question type (Lesson 4.4).
