Phase 4 · Lesson 3

GraphRAG & knowledge graphs

Some answers aren't inside any chunk. They live in the connections between chunks: who owns what, who reports to whom, what depends on what. Graphs make those connections searchable.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Top-k vector retrieval finds the k chunks most similar to the question (Lessons 2.2 and 3.4).
  • Structured output: an LLM can return JSON in a schema you define, such as a list of entities and relations (Lesson 1.3).
  • Hierarchical summaries answer broad questions no single chunk answers (Lesson 4.2).
🎯 Hook

LinkedIn's customer-service team had years of support tickets. Plain RAG chunked them like any text, losing which section of which ticket a sentence came from, and how tickets linked to each other. In 2024 they rebuilt the index as a knowledge graph: each ticket a small tree of sections, tickets connected by explicit links (“clone of”) and by similarity. At question time an LLM finds the relevant ticket and translates the question into a graph query (Cypher, for the Neo4j database).

Retrieval quality (MRR, mean reciprocal rank) rose 77.6% over the baseline. Deployed for about six months, it cut the team's median per-issue resolution time by 28.6%.

Numbers first: the question top-k can't answer

Twelve short internal docs. One says “The Payments API is owned by the Ledger team.” Another says “Priya Nair manages the Ledger team.” Ask: “Who manages the team that owns the Payments API?”

RetrieverWhat it returns (top-3)Can it answer?
Vector searchThree chunks that mention the Payments API: owner, an outage, what it doesNo: the chunk naming Priya never mentions the Payments API, so it never scores high
Graph traversalPayments API → OWNED_BY → Ledger team ← MANAGES ← Priya NairYes, with both source chunks as citations

Similarity search ranks chunks by how much they look like the question. A multi-hop question needs a chunk that looks nothing like the question, reachable only through an intermediate fact. The cost of fixing it with a graph is paid at indexing: an LLM call per chunk to extract the facts, which is typically hundreds of times the cost of just embedding the corpus (worked out below).

💡 Big idea A knowledge graph stores facts as entities (nodes: people, teams, services, products) and relations (typed edges: OWNS, MANAGES, CALLS). An LLM can read your documents and extract them. Then two new kinds of question become answerable: multi-hop (follow edges: owner → manager → their boss) and global (summarise clusters of the graph: “what are the main themes across all of this?”).
🧠 Analogy: the detective's corkboard

A detective doesn't solve the case by rereading the one witness statement that sounds most like the question. They pin every person and place on a board and connect them with string. The answer is a path: the car belongs to the cousin, the cousin works at the warehouse, the warehouse is next to the bank. Vector search reads statements; a graph follows the string.

How GraphRAG builds its index

  1. Chunk the documents (Microsoft's GraphRAG paper used 600-token chunks with 100 tokens of overlap).
  2. Extract: an LLM reads each chunk and outputs entities (name, type, description) and relations (source, target, description). Optional extra “gleaning” passes ask “did you miss any?” to catch more.
  3. Merge duplicates (“Priya”, “P. Nair”) and summarise each entity's descriptions.
  4. Detect communities: an algorithm (Leiden) groups densely connected entities into nested clusters: teams within orgs within the company.
  5. Summarise communities: an LLM writes a report for every cluster at every level. These are pre-computed answers to “what's going on in this part of the corpus?”.

Then there are two main ways to query:

Query modeHow it worksFor questions like
Local searchFind the entities named in the question, pull their neighbours, relations, and source chunks“Who manages the team that owns the Payments API?”
Global searchMap-reduce over community reports: each report produces a partial answer; an LLM merges them“What are the main risks across all incident reports?”

In the paper, on two corpora of about 1 million tokens each, global search beat plain vector RAG on comprehensiveness in 72–83% of head-to-head comparisons for podcast transcripts and 72–80% for news articles, judged by an LLM. Using only the top-level community summaries needed over 97% fewer tokens than summarising the source text directly.

The cost: extraction isn't free

✏️ Worked example: indexing 10,000 documents

10,000 docs × 1,000 tokens. Chunks of 600 with 100 overlap → ⌈(1,000 − 600) ÷ 500⌉ + 1 = 2 chunks per doc → 20,000 chunks.

  • Vector RAG: embed 20,000 × 600 = 12M tokens × $0.02 per million = $0.24.
  • GraphRAG extraction, with 1 gleaning pass: 40,000 LLM calls. Each sends the chunk plus a long instruction-and-examples prompt (assume 1,500 tokens) and returns ≈ 400 tokens of entities and relations: 40,000 × 2,100 = 84M input and 40,000 × 400 = 16M output tokens.
  • With a small model at an illustrative $0.50 / $2 per million in/out: 84 × 0.5 + 16 × 2 = $74; add ≈ 25% for entity and community summaries → ≈ $93, about 385× the embedding cost. With a frontier model at $5 / $25: ≈ $1,025.

Not ruinous for 10,000 documents. But it scales with every re-index, and with 10 million documents it's a project with a budget. That's why Microsoft built cheaper variants: FastGraphRAG (noun-phrase extraction with classic NLP instead of an LLM; only community reports use the LLM) and LazyGraphRAG, which defers summarisation to query time and reports indexing costs identical to vector RAG and 0.1% of full GraphRAG.

The family of graph RAG methods

MethodIdeaTrade-off
Microsoft GraphRAG (2024)LLM-extracted entity graph + Leiden communities + community reports; local, global and DRIFT (a hybrid) searchBest for global “sensemaking” questions; most expensive indexing
LightRAG (HKU, 2024)Graph + vectors with dual-level retrieval: low-level (specific entities) and high-level (broad themes); incremental updatesCheaper and easier to update; modes local / global / hybrid / naive / mix
HippoRAG (Ohio State, 2024)Knowledge graph + Personalized PageRank from the question's entities, inspired by how the hippocampus indexes memoriesMulti-hop in one retrieval step; reported 10–30× cheaper than iterative retrieval
Hand-built knowledge graph (Neo4j etc.)Your schema (teams, services, owners) from systems of record, queried with Cypher; LLM translates questions (text-to-Cypher)Most precise when the data is already structured; no extraction errors, but someone owns the schema

Notice the last row: if “who owns which service” already lives in a service catalogue or HR system, load it, don't extract it from prose. LLM extraction is for knowledge that only exists as text.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Microsoft GraphRAG (pip install graphrag, CLI-driven), LightRAG (lightrag-hku), Neo4j with the neo4j-graphrag package (KG-building pipeline, vector and text-to-Cypher retrievers), and graph databases such as Neo4j, Amazon Neptune or Memgraph.

# Microsoft GraphRAG, from the official getting-started guide
pip install graphrag
graphrag init                      # writes settings.yaml + .env; pick chat and embedding models
graphrag index                     # or: graphrag index --method fast  (FastGraphRAG, cheaper)
graphrag query "What are the top themes in this story?"                  # global (default)
graphrag query "Who is Scrooge and what are his main relationships?" --method local
# A multi-hop question as one Cypher query (Neo4j Python driver)
from neo4j import GraphDatabase
driver = GraphDatabase.driver("neo4j://localhost:7687", auth=("neo4j", password))
records, _, _ = driver.execute_query("""
    MATCH (s:Service {name: $service})-[:OWNED_BY]->(t:Team)<-[:MANAGES]-(m:Person)
    OPTIONAL MATCH (m)-[:REPORTS_TO]->(boss:Person)
    RETURN t.name AS team, m.name AS manager, boss.name AS boss""",
    service="Payments API", database_="neo4j")
# Text-to-Cypher: an LLM writes the MATCH from the question + the graph schema.
# Run it with a read-only user, exactly like text-to-SQL (Lesson 4.6).

GraphRAG's own docs warn that indexing “can consume a lot of LLM resources” and suggest starting with the tutorial dataset and a fast, inexpensive model.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Don't start with GraphRAG. Check your eval: are failures really multi-hop or global? Try metadata (owner, team fields on chunks) and an iterative two-step search first. If relationships already live in a database, give the model a SQL or Cypher tool instead.

At scale

A knowledge graph built from systems of record, enriched by LLM extraction from documents, with entity resolution as a dedicated pipeline. Graph and vector indexes are queried together, community summaries are refreshed on a schedule, and extraction quality has its own eval.

🎤 Interview angle

“When would you use GraphRAG instead of vector RAG?” A senior answer:

  • Names the two question types vectors fail: multi-hop (answer is a path through facts) and global (answer is a summary of the whole corpus).
  • Compares the options: iterative retrieval (query-time cost), LLM-extracted graph (index-time cost, ~hundreds of × embedding), curated graph from systems of record.
  • Mentions local vs global search, community summaries, and cheaper variants (LazyGraphRAG, LightRAG).
  • Wants evidence first: a labelled set showing which question types fail, and the cost of re-indexing as documents change.
🏭 In the real world
Support tickets as a knowledge graph 🏢 LinkedIn · 2024 RAG with Knowledge Graphs for Customer Service QA

How: each ticket is parsed into a tree of sections; tickets link by explicit relations and by embedding similarity; an LLM translates questions into Cypher queries over Neo4j.

Why this method: chunking threw away ticket structure and cross-ticket links. MRR rose 77.6%, and median per-issue resolution time fell 28.6% after about six months in production.

From local to global 🏢 Microsoft Research · 2024 GraphRAG: A Graph RAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization

How: LLM-extracted entity graph, Leiden communities, pre-generated community summaries, map-reduce answers.

Why this method: “what are the main themes?” is summarisation, not retrieval. Global search won 72–83% of comprehensiveness comparisons against vector RAG on a 1M-token podcast corpus.

Graph RAG without the indexing bill 🏢 Microsoft Research · 2024 LazyGraphRAG

How: no up-front LLM summarisation; lightweight indexing, with graph-style exploration and summarisation done at query time.

Why this method: full GraphRAG's indexing cost was prohibitive for some uses; LazyGraphRAG's indexing costs are reported identical to vector RAG and 0.1% of full GraphRAG.

Graph + vectors, dual-level 🧪 Research · 2024 LightRAG (HKU)

How: extracts entities and relations into a graph, indexes them with vectors, and retrieves at a specific-entity level and a broad-theme level, with incremental updates for new documents.

Why this method: keeps graph benefits while making updates cheap; its docs recommend a fast, non-reasoning model for extraction because it runs on every chunk.

🤯 Fun fact · Google said it in 2012

When Google launched its Knowledge Graph in 2012, the slogan was “things, not strings”: search should understand entities and their relationships, not just match words. It started with more than 500 million objects and 3.5 billion facts, drawn from sources such as Freebase and Wikipedia. GraphRAG is the same idea, except the graph is extracted from your own documents by an LLM.

Check yourself

1. 50,000 chunks, extraction with no gleaning, 2,000 input and 500 output tokens per call, at $1 / $4 per million tokens. Roughly what does extraction cost?
Input: 50,000 × 2,000 = 100M tokens × $1/M = $100. Output: 50,000 × 500 = 25M × $4/M = $100. Total ≈ $200, before summaries, and each gleaning pass roughly adds another round.
2. Your engineering assistant answers “who owns service X?” well, but not “who should I page if the service behind the checkout page is down?” What's the best first step?
It's a multi-hop question (checkout → depends on → service → owned by → team → on-call), and the relationships already exist as structured data. Querying the system of record is cheaper and exact; LLM extraction is for knowledge that only exists as text.
3. Which question benefits most from GraphRAG's global search?
Global search answers corpus-wide sensemaking questions from pre-computed community summaries. The first two are single-chunk lookups, which plain vector retrieval handles well and cheaply.
📝 TL;DR
  • Vector search fails when an answer is a path through facts (multi-hop) or a summary of everything (global).
  • GraphRAG: LLM extracts entities and relations, Leiden finds communities, LLM summarises them; local search follows edges, global search maps over summaries.
  • The cost is at indexing: typically hundreds of times the embedding cost. Cheaper variants: FastGraphRAG, LazyGraphRAG, LightRAG.
  • If the relationships already live in a system of record, load them into a graph or SQL and query them; extract only what exists only as text.
