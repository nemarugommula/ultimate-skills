Phase 0 · Lesson 1

What an AI engineer does (and how it differs from ML engineering)

Calling a language model takes five lines of code. Making it right, cheap, fast and safe for a million users is the job.

🎯 Hook

In 2022 a passenger asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares after a grandparent died. The chatbot gave wrong advice about claiming the discount later, so they bought full-price tickets. In February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered the airline to pay $812 in compensation (CBC).

The airline had argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The tribunal called that “a remarkable submission”: the chatbot is part of the company's website, and the company owns what it says. Someone has to own the answers an AI system gives. In most companies, that someone is the AI engineer.

Numbers first: the API call is the easy part

Picture a support assistant answering 10,000 questions a day. Each question sends about 3,000 tokens (pieces of text, roughly ¾ of a word each; Lesson 0.3) to a mid-priced model and gets 300 tokens back.

That table is the whole lesson in miniature. The model call is cheap and easy. The engineering is everything around it: getting the right information into the prompt, measuring quality, controlling cost and latency, handling failures, and keeping it safe.

💡 Big idea An AI engineer builds products on top of foundation models: large models someone else already trained (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Qwen…). The model is a powerful but probabilistic component: the same question can get different answers, and it can be confidently wrong. The job is to turn that component into a reliable system, and to prove it's reliable with measurements.
🧠 Analogy: farmers and chefs

ML engineers and researchers are the farmers: they grow the ingredients, training models from data. AI engineers are the chefs: they rarely grow anything, but they combine ingredients (a model, your company's documents, tools, prompts) into a dish real people eat, every day, at a predictable cost. A good chef tastes constantly. In AI engineering, tasting is called evaluation (evals): running the system on test questions and scoring the answers.

The lines blur. At a startup one person might do all four. AI engineers increasingly fine-tune small models too (Phase 7), but they start from a trained model and an API, not from a dataset and a GPU cluster. That's why a strong software engineer can become a strong AI engineer: the skills are mostly product engineering, systems thinking and measurement.

✏️ Worked example: where the time goes in one request

One question to the support assistant, with typical numbers:

  • Retrieval (search your documents for the 5 best passages): ~80 ms.
  • Gateway (auth, logging, routing to a provider): ~5 ms.
  • Model: time to first token (TTFT) ~600 ms, then 300 output tokens at ~80 tokens/second = 3.75 s.
  • Total ≈ 0.08 + 0.005 + 0.6 + 3.75 ≈ 4.4 s. Output generation is ~85% of it.

So if users complain the bot is slow, the first fix is rarely a faster database. It's fewer output tokens, streaming the answer so the first words appear at 0.6 s (Lesson 0.5), or a faster model (Lesson 0.4). Knowing where the milliseconds and dollars go is a core AI-engineering skill (Lesson 0.3).

What “senior” means here

A junior AI engineer gets a demo working. A senior one owns the outcome in production. Concretely:

AreaDemo mindsetSenior mindset
Quality“I tried 5 questions and it looked great.”A versioned eval set of 100–500 real questions, run on every change; known failure categories (Phase 5).
Cost“It's under a cent per call.”Cost per task and per user, budgets, caching and routing to cheaper models (Lesson 8.2).
Latency“It answers in a few seconds.”p50/p95 latency targets, streaming, TTFT budgets (Lesson 0.3).
Reliability“The API works.”Retries, timeouts, fallbacks to another provider, rate-limit handling (Lessons 0.5, 8.1).
Safety“The model is aligned, it'll be fine.”Prompt-injection defenses, permission checks, PII handling, human review where stakes are high (Lesson 8.3).
Change“Let's switch to the new model, it's better.”“Better on what? Let's run our evals on it first.” (Lesson 0.4)

The pattern: if the problem lives in what the model sees, how it's called, what it does with tools, or how its answers are measured, it's AI engineering. If it lives in training a model's weights from data, it's ML engineering. If it lives in moving and storing data, it's data engineering, and AI engineers are among its most demanding customers.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The core kit: a provider SDK (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Gen AI) or a provider-agnostic layer like LiteLLM (Lesson 0.5); a vector database such as pgvector or Qdrant (Phase 2); an eval tool (Phase 5); tracing (Lesson 8.1). And the habit that matters most, from day one: a tiny eval loop.

from openai import OpenAI

client = OpenAI()   # reads OPENAI_API_KEY from the environment

POLICY = open("refund_policy.md").read()
golden = [   # real questions + a fact the answer must contain
    ("How long do refunds take to arrive?", "5 business days"),
    ("Can Plus members return items after 45 days?", "60 days"),
]

passed = 0
for question, must_contain in golden:
    r = client.responses.create(
        model="gpt-6.1-terra",
        input=f"Answer using only this policy:\n{POLICY}\n\nQuestion: {question}",
    )
    passed += must_contain.lower() in r.output_text.lower()
print(f"{passed}/{len(golden)} passed")   # run this on every prompt or model change

Model IDs change every few months; check the provider's models page. Phase 5 replaces “contains this string” with better metrics and LLM judges.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

One AI engineer owns the whole stack: prompt, retrieval, a hosted API, a 50-question eval in a spreadsheet, and the on-call pager. Speed matters most; pick the best hosted model, measure, then optimize cost once usage is real.

Big tech

Platform teams run the LLM gateway, eval infrastructure, safety reviews and GPU fleet; product AI engineers build features on top. More process (privacy review, red-teaming, launch gates), much more traffic, so a 10% cost or latency win is worth weeks of work.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between an AI engineer and an ML engineer?” and “How would you ship an LLM feature?” A senior answer:

  • Frames the role around products on foundation models: the model is a component; the system around it is the work.
  • Walks the stack: context (prompt, retrieval), model choice, tools, evals, serving (gateway, caching, fallbacks), guardrails.
  • Puts numbers on it: cost per request, p95 latency, an eval set and a quality bar before launch.
  • Talks about ownership after launch: monitoring, regressions when the provider updates a model, incidents.
🏭 In the real world
Naming the role 📜 History · 2023 swyx: The Rise of the AI Engineer

How: argued that foundation-model APIs created a new engineering role between ML research and product engineering, focused on shipping with models rather than training them.

Why this method: the essay predicts there will be many more AI engineers than ML engineers, because so much value comes from building with models rather than building them.

The platform, step by step 🧪 Chip Huyen · 2024 Building a Generative AI Platform

How: builds a generative-AI platform up one component at a time: enhance context (RAG), add guardrails, add a model router and gateway, then caching, agent patterns, and observability.

Why this method: start with the simplest pipeline (query → model) and add each layer only when a measured problem demands it; it's the same stack as the playground above.

An assistant at scale 🏢 Klarna · 2024 Klarna AI assistant handles two-thirds of customer service chats

How: a customer-service assistant built on OpenAI models, live globally in the Klarna app, handling multilingual customer service, refunds and returns.

Why this method: Klarna reported 2.3 million conversations in the first month, two-thirds of its service chats, the work of about 700 full-time agents and a 25% drop in repeat inquiries. Numbers like those are what AI engineers are asked to deliver and then defend.

Simple beats clever 🏢 Anthropic · 2024 Building effective agents

How: after working with many teams building LLM agents, Anthropic found the most successful ones used simple, composable patterns rather than complex frameworks.

Why this method: fewer moving parts are easier to evaluate, debug and pay for. Add complexity (agents, multi-step workflows) only when it measurably helps (Phase 6).

🤯 Fun fact · you may never train a model

The essay that named the role quotes Andrej Karpathy, a founding member of OpenAI: “One can be quite successful in this role without ever training anything.” That's why this course spends most of its time on context, retrieval, evals and systems, and only reaches training in Phase 7.

Check yourself

1. A feature handles 20,000 requests a day, each 2,000 input and 200 output tokens, on a model priced $1 / $5 per million input / output tokens. Roughly what's the monthly (30-day) model bill?
Per request: 2,000 × $1/M + 200 × $5/M = $0.002 + $0.001 = $0.003. × 20,000 = $60 a day × 30 = $1,800. Always split input and output; output tokens are priced higher.
2. After a model upgrade, your support bot starts inventing a “price-match guarantee” that doesn't exist. Who should own the fix, and what's the first step?
You don't own the model's weights, you own the system. Turn the bug into eval cases, check what the model was given (retrieval, prompt), fix it, and make sure the eval would have caught it before the upgrade shipped.
3. Which behaviour best signals a senior AI engineer?
Seniors own outcomes: they make quality measurable and trade it off deliberately against cost and latency. A bigger model or a fancier framework is a means, chosen when the measurements say so.
📝 TL;DR
  • AI engineers build products on top of pre-trained foundation models; ML engineers train models; data engineers move data.
  • The stack: app → prompt & context (+ retrieval) → gateway → model → tools, wrapped by guardrails and evals.
  • The model bill is often small next to the cost of wrong answers: quality is the job, and evals are how you see it.
  • Senior = owns quality, cost, latency, reliability and safety in production, with numbers.
