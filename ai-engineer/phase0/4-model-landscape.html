Phase 0 · Lesson 4

The model landscape: closed vs open, choosing a model

There are hundreds of models and a new “best” one every month. The skill isn't knowing today's leaderboard; it's choosing well for your task and proving it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Reasoning models “think” in extra tokens before answering, and those are billed as output (Lessons 0.2, 0.3).
  • Cost per request = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

In April 2025, Meta's new Llama 4 Maverick launched at #2 on LM Arena, a popular leaderboard where people vote between two models' answers. Researchers soon noticed Meta's own announcement said the Arena version was an “experimental chat version”, optimized for conversationality: not the model developers could download (TechCrunch).

A study weeks later found Meta had tested 27 private variants on the Arena before the Llama 4 release (The Leaderboard Illusion). A leaderboard tells you how a model did on someone else's test. Your users are not that test.

If the small model is good enough for your task, choosing the frontier one costs 100× more for nothing. If it isn't, the cheap choice costs you in wrong answers (Lesson 0.1). Only measurement on your task tells you which.

💡 Big idea Choose models like you'd choose a database: from requirements (quality bar, cost per task, latency, context size, privacy, licence), shortlist two or three candidates, then decide with your own eval, not a public leaderboard. And design so switching models is cheap, because you'll switch.
🧠 Analogy: hiring

A public benchmark is a candidate's university grades: useful for a shortlist, weak at predicting how they'll do your job. Your eval set is the work-sample test. You wouldn't hire the top graduate for a data-entry role at 10× salary, and you wouldn't hire anyone without seeing them do the actual work.

Open-weight ≠ open source. Open-weight means you can download and run the trained parameters. Open source, strictly, would also include the training data and code, which most releases don't provide. Read the licence before you ship.

Reasoning vs fast, big vs small

  • Reasoning models spend thinking tokens before answering. Most current frontier APIs expose an effort knob instead of an on/off switch: OpenAI's reasoning.effort (from none or low up to max, depending on the model), Anthropic's effort setting with adaptive thinking. Higher effort = better on multi-step problems, slower, more output tokens.
  • Small models are excellent at narrow jobs: classification, extraction, routing, short rewrites. They're the default for high volume and real-time (voice, autocomplete), and can run on a laptop or phone.
  • Routing combines them: a small model (or rule) sends easy requests to the cheap tier and hard ones to the big one (Lesson 8.2).

Three ways a public number misleads you: the model may have seen the test (contamination), the test may measure something else than your task (domain, language, format), and small differences are noise. The playground lets you cause all three.

✏️ Worked example: how big is the error bar on your eval?

You test a model on 50 examples and it gets 40 right: 80%. How sure are you?

  • Standard error ≈ √(p(1 − p) ÷ n) = √(0.8 × 0.2 ÷ 50) = √0.0032 ≈ 0.057.
  • 95% interval ≈ 80% ± 1.96 × 5.7% ≈ 80% ± 11 points (69% to 91%).
  • With 200 examples: √(0.16 ÷ 200) ≈ 0.028 → ± 5.5 points. Four times the data halves the error bar.

So “Model B scored 83% and Model C 80% on our 50 examples” means you can't tell them apart yet. Leaderboard gaps of a point or two are often the same story.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Model pages from each provider (context, price, cut-off, effort levels), Hugging Face for open models and their licences, and a small bake-off script over your own labelled examples. LiteLLM calls many providers with one interface and can estimate each call's cost.

import json, time, litellm

CANDIDATES = ["openai/gpt-6-luna", "anthropic/claude-haiku-5-5", "gemini/gemini-3.5-flash-lite"]
golden = [json.loads(l) for l in open("tickets.jsonl")]   # 100+ real labelled examples
PROMPT = "Classify the ticket as billing, bug, account or other. Reply with the label only."

for model in CANDIDATES:
    correct, cost, t0 = 0, 0.0, time.time()
    for ex in golden:
        r = litellm.completion(model=model, max_tokens=10, messages=[
            {"role": "system", "content": PROMPT},
            {"role": "user", "content": ex["text"]}])
        correct += r.choices[0].message.content.strip().lower() == ex["label"]
        cost += litellm.completion_cost(completion_response=r)
    print(f"{model:30} acc={correct/len(golden):.0%}  cost=${cost:.4f}  {time.time()-t0:.0f}s")

Keep the eval set and script in the repo, and rerun it whenever a provider ships a new model. Phase 5 turns this into a proper eval harness.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Start with a strong balanced closed model to find product-market fit fast; keep a 100-example eval; once traffic is real, test the small tier and route easy requests to it. Self-host only when privacy or unit economics force it.

Big tech

Approved-model catalogs, procurement and data-processing agreements, a gateway that routes across several providers and in-house open models, per-feature model choices backed by eval dashboards, and migration plans for every model retirement date.

🎤 Interview angle

“Which model would you use for this, and why?” A senior answer:

  • Asks for requirements first: quality bar, volume, latency target, data constraints, budget, context size.
  • Shortlists by category (small/balanced/frontier, closed/open, reasoning effort), with a rough cost per request for each.
  • Decides with an eval on real examples and says how big it must be to separate candidates.
  • Designs for change: a gateway or abstraction layer, pinned model versions, evals rerun on every upgrade, a fallback model.
🏭 In the real world
Ranking models by human votes 🧪 Research · 2024 Chatbot Arena: An Open Platform for Evaluating LLMs by Human Preference

How: crowdsourced users chat with two anonymous models side by side and vote; ratings come from pairwise comparisons (the paper analyzes over 240K votes).

Why this method: open-ended chat quality is hard to score automatically, so preferences fill the gap. It measures general chat preference, which is why it can't replace your task eval.

How leaderboards get distorted 🧪 Research · 2025 The Leaderboard Illusion

How: analyzed Arena data and found undisclosed private testing lets some providers try many variants and publish only the best score; 27 private variants were tested by Meta before Llama 4.

Why this method: picking the best of many noisy scores inflates the published one. Same lesson as the playground: one number, selected, is not a measurement.

Catching contamination 🧪 Scale AI · 2024 A Careful Examination of LLM Performance on Grade School Arithmetic (GSM1k)

How: wrote a fresh set of problems (GSM1k) matching the style and difficulty of the popular GSM8K benchmark, then compared models on both.

Why this method: some model families dropped up to 8% on the fresh set, evidence of overfitting to the public one. Fresh, private test questions are the antidote, and your own eval set is exactly that.

Fixing a benchmark's bad questions 🏢 OpenAI · 2024 Introducing SWE-bench Verified

How: human annotators screened SWE-bench's real GitHub-issue tasks and kept 500 verified to be well-specified with fair tests.

Why this method: some original tasks were hard or impossible to solve, so the benchmark systematically underestimated models. Benchmarks need evals too.

🤯 Fun fact · the answer key was wrong

When researchers re-checked MMLU, one of the most cited LLM benchmarks, they found errors in 57% of the questions they analyzed in its virology section, and estimated that about 6.5% of all MMLU questions contain errors (“Are We Done with MMLU?”, 2024). Near the top of a leaderboard, models were partly being ranked on mistakes in the answer key.

Check yourself

1. Your eval: Model X 86%, Model Y 82%, both on the same 40 examples. What can you conclude?
√(0.84 × 0.16 ÷ 40) ≈ 0.058, × 1.96 ≈ ±11 points. To separate a 4-point gap you need hundreds of examples (or a paired comparison on the same items, Lesson 5.2).
2. A hospital wants an assistant that summarizes patient notes. Policy: patient data must never leave its own servers. What's the shortlist?
The constraint rules out vendor APIs regardless of quality. Open-weight models can run on-premises; pick the size the hardware allows and let an eval on realistic notes decide.
3. You classify 2 million support tickets a day into 8 categories. Frontier and small models both score 96% on your 500-example eval. What do you do?
Equal measured quality on a decent-sized eval means the price difference is pure waste. Keep a monitoring loop so you notice if the ticket mix drifts and the small model starts slipping.
📝 TL;DR
  • Every major provider offers small, balanced and frontier tiers, roughly 10× apart in price; open-weight families let you self-host.
  • Choose from requirements: quality bar, cost, latency, context, privacy, licence; then decide with your own eval.
  • Public benchmarks suffer from contamination, mismatch with your task, label errors and noise; arenas reward style and can be gamed.
  • Small evals have big error bars (±11 points at n = 50); design for switching models, because you will.
