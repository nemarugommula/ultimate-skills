Phase 0 · Lesson 6

When not to use an LLM: rules, classic ML & small models

An LLM can do almost any language task. That doesn't make it the right tool for every one. Senior engineers pick the simplest thing that meets the bar, and keep the LLM for the part only it can do.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Token pricing: you pay per input and output token on every call; latency is roughly time to first token plus output length ÷ speed (Lesson 0.3).
  • Model tiers: small, workhorse and frontier models are roughly 10× apart in price (Lesson 0.4).
  • A classifier is any program that maps an input to one of a fixed set of labels (“refund”, “shipping”, “spam”…). That's the shape of most tasks in this lesson.
🎯 Hook

In February 2024, a Hugging Face engineer needed a classifier for investor sentiment in financial news. Instead of calling GPT-4 on every sentence, he asked an open LLM (Mixtral) to label 1,811 example sentences, then fine-tuned RoBERTa-base, a small model of about 0.13 billion parameters, on those labels.

The small model scored 94% accuracy, the same as GPT-4 on the expert-labelled test set. Analysing a million sentences would cost about $2.70 on a small rented GPU versus about $3,061 with GPT-4, with 0.13 seconds of latency instead of often several seconds. Same quality, a thousand times cheaper. The LLM's best job here was teacher, not worker.

Three orders of magnitude separate the top and bottom of that table. For a fixed-label task at high volume, the LLM is often the most expensive, slowest and least predictable option. But it's also the only one that works on day one with zero labelled data, and the only one that can write a summary or answer an open question.

💡 Big idea Match the tool to the shape of the task, not to what's exciting. A fixed set of labels, lots of traffic and labelled history points down the ladder (rules → classic ML → small model). Open-ended language, no data yet, or low volume points up (LLM). The best production systems are usually hybrids: cheap tools handle the easy majority, the LLM handles the hard remainder, and the LLM's answers become training data for the cheap tools.
🧠 Analogy: staffing a hospital

You don't send every patient to the chief surgeon. A sign on the door handles “pharmacy is on floor 2” (rules). A triage nurse with a checklist sorts most arrivals in seconds (a classifier). The surgeon sees the cases nobody else can handle (the LLM). And a hard decision with legal weight goes to a person who signs their name (human review). Sending everyone to the surgeon is slow, expensive, and wastes the surgeon.

Two clarifications. “Deterministic” means the same input always gives the same output. Rules and trained models are deterministic until you change them. An LLM may vary between calls (sampling, Lesson 0.5), and a hosted model can change behaviour when the provider updates it (Lesson 0.4). “Explainable” means you can show why a decision was made: a rule's text, a model's weights. When a decision must be justified to a customer or an auditor, that matters more than a point of accuracy.

Use the LLM as a teacher: label, then distil

The biggest objection to classic ML and small models is “we don't have labelled data”. LLMs remove most of that objection. The pattern from the hook, which you'll build properly in Lesson 7.2:

  1. Launch with the LLM. It works on day one. Log every input and output.
  2. Check its labels. Have a person label a few hundred examples to measure the LLM's accuracy (Lesson 5.1). Improve the prompt until it's good enough to teach.
  3. Label in bulk. Run the LLM over a few thousand to tens of thousands of real inputs, ideally with a batch API at a discount (Lesson 8.2).
  4. Train the student. Fine-tune a small model (or fit logistic regression) on those labels. This is distillation: a big model's behaviour copied into a small one.
  5. Route. The student handles what it's confident about; uncertain cases go back to the LLM, whose answers become more training data.

Research backs this up: Google's Distilling step-by-step trained a 770M-parameter T5 model that beat few-shot prompted 540B PaLM on a benchmark, and a 2024 study found fine-tuned BERT-style models beat zero-shot GPT-4 and Claude Opus on every text-classification task it tested.

Hybrid systems: the cascade

You rarely choose one rung. A cascade sends each request down the ladder until something is confident enough to answer:

ticket ─▶ rules ──match?──▶ answer (password resets, “where is order AB-123456”) │ no ▼ classifier ──confidence ≥ threshold?──▶ answer (most routine tickets) │ no ▼ LLM ──▶ answer, and log it as a future training label (the hard, rare, ambiguous ones)

The confidence threshold is the dial. A classifier outputs a probability for each label; if the top one is, say, ≥ 0.90 you trust it, otherwise you escalate. Raise the threshold and fewer requests stay cheap but those that do are more accurate. Lower it and you save money by accepting more mistakes.

✏️ Worked example: the cascade at 10M tickets a day

Suppose rules catch 30%, the classifier is confident on 45%, and the LLM (workhorse tier) gets the remaining 25%.

  • LLM for everything: 10M × $0.00085 (= 400 × $2/1M + 5 × $10/1M) = $8,500 a day.
  • Cascade: rules ≈ $0; classifier runs on the 7M tickets the rules didn't catch: 7M × $2.70/1M ≈ $19; LLM on 2.5M: 2.5M × $0.00085 = $2,125. Total ≈ $2,144 a day.
  • That's 75% cheaper: about $6,350 a day, or roughly $2.3M a year, saved.
  • Latency: 75% of tickets are answered in ~20 ms instead of ~1 s. Average ≈ 0.30 × 0.1 ms + 0.45 × 20 ms + 0.25 × (20 + 1,000) ms ≈ 264 ms, versus ~1,000 ms.

The catch: every rung has its own error rate, so you must measure end-to-end accuracy on a labelled set (Phase 5) before claiming the cascade is “as good”. The playground below lets you see where it stops being as good.

Switch to the small LLM tier: everything gets cheaper, and the cascade now improves accuracy over the small LLM alone.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Python's re for rules; scikit-learn for logistic regression and pipelines; XGBoost / LightGBM for tabular data; Hugging Face Transformers (and SetFit for few-shot) for small fine-tuned models; any LLM SDK (Lesson 0.5) for the fallback and for labelling.

import re
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

ORDER_ID = re.compile(r"\b[A-Z]{2}-\d{6}\b")

# texts, labels: past tickets labelled by agents, or by an LLM you checked
clf = make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2),
                    LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
clf.fit(texts, labels)

def route(ticket: str) -> tuple[str, str]:
    if ORDER_ID.search(ticket) and "where" in ticket.lower():
        return "order_status", "rule"
    probs = clf.predict_proba([ticket])[0]
    if probs.max() >= 0.90:                      # the confidence threshold
        return clf.classes_[probs.argmax()], "classifier"
    label = llm_classify(ticket)                  # your LLM call (Lesson 0.5)
    log_for_training(ticket, label)               # tomorrow's training data
    return label, "llm"

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Ship with an LLM: you have no labels and low volume, and the engineer-weeks a classifier would take cost more than the API bill. But log everything from day one, add the two or three obvious rules, and put a monthly reminder to check the bill. When one task's LLM spend passes the cost of a few engineer-weeks a year, distil it.

Big tech

At billions of calls, every rung exists. Spam, fraud, ranking and moderation run on classic ML and small models trained on huge labelled sets, with strict latency budgets. LLMs label data, handle the long tail, explain decisions to reviewers, and generate features for the small models. Teams track cost per decision and which stage made it.

🎤 Interview angle

“We classify 10 million support tickets a day with GPT-class calls. The bill is huge. What would you do?” A senior answer:

  • Does the math first: per-call cost × volume, and the latency budget.
  • Looks at the traffic: which share is templated (rules), which is routine (classifier), which is genuinely hard.
  • Proposes distillation: use the existing LLM outputs (checked against human labels) to train a small model, then a cascade with a confidence threshold and LLM fallback.
  • Insists on an eval set to compare end-to-end accuracy before and after, and on monitoring stage shares and drift after launch.
  • Knows when not to: low volume, fast-changing labels, or open-ended outputs keep the LLM.
🏭 In the real world
LLM labels, small model works 🏢 Hugging Face · 2024 Synthetic data: save money, time and carbon with open source

How: Mixtral labelled 1,811 financial-news sentences (with chain-of-thought and several attempts per label); a RoBERTa-base model was fine-tuned on them in minutes for under $1.

Why this method: the student matched GPT-4 and its teacher at 94% accuracy, at about $2.70 per million sentences versus about $3,061 with GPT-4, and 0.13 s latency.

Small models that beat big ones 🧪 Research · 2023 Distilling Step-by-Step (Google)

How: the LLM provides labels and its reasoning; the small model is trained to predict both.

Why this method: a 770M T5 model outperformed few-shot prompted 540B PaLM on a benchmark using only 80% of the available data, a model over 700× smaller.

Cascades across models 🧪 Research · 2023 FrugalGPT (Stanford)

How: send each query to a cheap model first and escalate to pricier ones only when a learned scorer says the answer isn't reliable.

Why this method: matched the best single model (GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction on their benchmarks. The same idea works with a classifier as the first rung.

Fine-tuned beats zero-shot for classification 🧪 Research · 2024 Bucher & Martini

How: compared zero-shot GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Claude Opus with fine-tuned BERT-style models on sentiment, stance, emotion and political-position tasks.

Why this method: fine-tuning on application-specific data won in every case. If you have labels and a fixed task, a small trained model is the strong baseline to beat.

Rules + models for PII detection 🧰 Open source Presidio analyzer

How: each detector is a regex-based pattern recognizer (with context words and validation logic), an ML model, or a combination of both.

Why this method: credit-card and phone formats are exact patterns that regex nails for free; names and addresses need a model. A hybrid uses each where it's strongest (Lesson 8.3).

🤯 Fun fact · the trees keep winning

In the 2016 XGBoost paper, the authors counted 29 winning solutions published on Kaggle's blog in 2015: 17 used XGBoost, and eight used nothing else. Six years later, a 45-dataset benchmark still found tree-based models state of the art on medium-sized tabular data. And Google's Rules of ML guide opens with rule #1: “Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning.”

Check yourself

1. 10 million requests a day, each 500 input + 10 output tokens, on a $2 / $10 per million tokens model. Roughly what's the daily bill?
Per request: 500 × $2/1M + 10 × $10/1M = $0.001 + $0.0001 = $0.0011. Times 10M = $11,000 a day, about $4M a year. For a fixed-label task, that's the budget a distilled small model is competing against.
2. You must flag fraudulent card payments in under 50 ms. You have three years of transactions (amount, merchant, country, device, history) labelled with chargebacks. First choice?
Tabular data, plenty of labels, a tight latency budget and a need to explain decisions: that's the home turf of gradient-boosted trees. Rules cover the known patterns (blocked countries, velocity limits). An LLM would be slower, pricier and no better on numeric features.
3. You're launching comment moderation for a new product: about 20,000 comments a day, 15 languages, no labelled data, and the policy is still changing weekly. What's the senior plan?
No labels, a moving policy and modest volume favour the LLM now. Logging plus human review turns its outputs into a checked dataset. Once the labels stop changing and the bill grows, a distilled small model (with the LLM as fallback) becomes the cheaper, faster option.
📝 TL;DR
  • Pick the simplest tool that meets the bar: rules → classic ML → small fine-tuned model → LLM → human.
  • Fixed labels + high volume + labelled data → go down the ladder; open-ended output, no data or low volume → LLM.
  • LLMs make great teachers: label with the LLM, check it, distil into a small model, keep the LLM as fallback.
  • Cascades (rules → classifier → LLM) cut cost and latency several-fold, but measure end-to-end accuracy and tune the threshold.
