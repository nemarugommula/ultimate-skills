Phase R · Recap

Recap: AI red teaming & security

How attackers see an AI system, so you can find and fix the weaknesses in your own: threat models, injection and jailbreaks, agents and tools, data and model attacks, and the engagement that ties them together. Here's the phase on one page, a matching game, and a system to red-team yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🗺️

R.1 Threat modelling

Risk = likelihood × impact (1–5 each). Lethal trifecta: private data + untrusted content + a way out. Remove a leg by design.

Four questions: what are we building, what can go wrong, what will we do, did we do enough. Threat-model the data flows, not the prompt: every place untrusted text enters, and every place output leaves, is a trust boundary.

🧰 STRIDE, OWASP LLM & Agentic Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, Threat Dragon, a threat register in CI

💉

R.2 Injection & jailbreaks

At p per attempt, n attempts succeed with 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. One refusal proves nothing.

Instructions and data share one token stream. Indirect injection arrives in content the app reads. Spotlighting, instruction hierarchy and classifiers lower the rate; architecture (what a fooled model may do) bounds the damage.

🧰 garak, PyRIT, promptfoo; spotlighting, tool-result roles, dual-LLM patterns

🤖

R.3 Agents & tools

An injection borrows the agent's whole blast radius. Least privilege per task, allow-listed destinations, confirm side effects with full arguments.

Exits are tool arguments, public destinations, rendered URLs and executed code. MCP tool descriptions are prompt text: watch for poisoning, shadowing and rug pulls; review, pin and scan servers. Sandbox code, budget loops (denial of wallet), track provenance.

🧰 a tool gateway with policy code, Snyk Agent Scan (formerly mcp-scan), sandboxes, AgentDojo

🧪

R.4 Data & model attacks

Poison works by count, not percentage: 5 docs per question beat a million-doc corpus; 250 docs backdoored 600M–13B models.

Data is code: provenance and review for corpora and training sets. What a model saw can leak (memorized text, membership, hidden prompts, the model itself). Model files can execute code.

🧰 safetensors, pinned revisions, dedup, canaries, rate limits, DP (Opacus), garak leakreplay

📋

R.5 The engagement

0 hits in n attempts → true rate under ≈ 3 ÷ n. Rank by severity × likelihood; money and other users' data go first.

Written scope and RoE, an attack library mapped to OWASP and ATLAS with a code oracle per test, automation for breadth plus humans for chains, fixes verified with variants, every finding a CI regression test, private disclosure.

🧰 promptfoo redteam, garak, PyRIT, Inspect, pytest regression suites

🧭

The frameworks

OWASP LLM Top 10 2026: LLM01 Prompt Injection · LLM02 Sensitive Info · LLM03 Excessive Agency · LLM04 Supply Chain · LLM05 Poisoning…

NIST AI 100-2 names the attacker's goal (availability, integrity, privacy, misuse, supply chain). MITRE ATLAS describes techniques and real case studies (16 tactics as of its October 2026 release). Cite all three in reports.

🧰 genai.owasp.org, atlas.mitre.org, NIST AI 100-2 E2025

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “Walk me through how you'd red-team an agent that reads a user's email and can send messages on their behalf. Where do you start, what do you test, how do you know you're done, and what do you hand back to the team?”

A strong answer covers: written authorization and rules of engagement (staging, synthetic mailboxes, a mail sink instead of real sending); a threat model that spots the lethal trifecta (private inbox + attacker-controlled incoming email + the send tool as the exit); an attack library mapped to OWASP LLM01/LLM02/LLM03 and ATLAS: indirect injection in emails, exfiltration via sent mail, links and rendered images, tool misuse (wrong recipients, mass sends), memory poisoning if it remembers, plus jailbreaks and system-prompt extraction; canaries planted in the inbox so leaks are provable; automated variants with repeated trials and a validated judge, plus manual chaining sessions; results as ASR with attempts and severity × likelihood; fixes by design (recipients limited to the thread, approval for new recipients, no external images, untrusted text in the tool-result role) verified with variants; and a regression suite in CI with a release gate.

🏗️ Red-team it yourself: the telecom support agent

✏️ The problem

Brightline Mobile (fictional) is launching a customer-support agent in six weeks. It answers questions with RAG over 4,000 help-centre articles and a community forum, can look up the logged-in customer's account (plan, bills, address), can issue bill credits up to $25 per conversation, and accepts uploaded PDFs (“here's my bill, why is it so high?”). It runs on a hosted model behind the company's API, serves about 30,000 conversations a day once live, and remembers a short summary of past conversations per customer.

You own the system and have two weeks and one teammate. Write the threat model, the test plan (categories, tools, attempts, rules of engagement), the findings you expect, and the fixes. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Threat model (R.1)

  • Assets: other customers' personal data (address, bills), money (credits), the company's trust and brand, the API budget, the system prompt's business rules.
  • Trust boundaries: customer messages; uploaded PDFs (attacker-controlled); the community forum (anyone can post) inside the RAG index; long-term memory (written from past chats); tool calls out (account lookup, credit); rendered replies in the web widget.
  • Lethal trifecta check: private data (account lookup) + untrusted content (PDFs, forum, chat) + an exit? The credit tool moves money, and the widget can render links and images. So: yes, unless the exits are closed.
  • Attackers: a customer after free credits; someone after another customer's data; a forum poster spreading a phishing link; a bot farm running up costs.

Rules of engagement (R.5)

  • Written sign-off from the product owner; staging with the production prompts, model and index snapshot; synthetic customers (two tenants' worth, with canary records); the credit tool wired to a sandbox ledger; a capped model budget; same-day escalation for anything touching real data or money.

Done means

  • Zero open criticals and highs; credit-abuse and cross-customer categories at 0 in 300 attempts (under about 1%) after the fixes; every finding has a regression test in CI; a monthly scheduled scan and a re-test on every model or prompt upgrade.
  • A report with scope, method, coverage against OWASP and ATLAS, findings with ASR and attempts, the chains, fixes and the residual risk the product owner signs off.

Classic mistakes

  • Putting the $25 cap and “only the logged-in customer” in the system prompt and calling it done.
  • Testing each attack once, by hand, and reporting “not vulnerable”.
  • Indexing the forum because it “improves coverage”, with no review or trust tier.
  • Running tests against production with real customer accounts, or real money in the credit tool.
  • Fixing findings without regression tests, so the next prompt change quietly reopens them.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A jailbreak works on 2% of attempts. An attacker scripts 50 tries. Roughly what's the chance at least one works?
1 − 0.98⁵⁰ = 1 − 0.364 ≈ 63.6%. Per-attempt rates compound, which is why one refusal proves nothing and why you bound the damage by design (Lesson R.2).
2. An assistant reads the user's inbox, searches internal docs, and can send email to anyone. Which single change best breaks the lethal trifecta?
Private data and untrusted content are the product; the open exit is what turns an injection into a breach. Restricting the exit removes a leg by design, and the prompt becomes a second layer rather than the only one (Lessons R.1 and R.3).
3. After a fix, your suite runs a cross-customer data attack 150 times with zero successes. What can you report?
Rule of three: 0 in n gives a 95% upper bound of about 3 ÷ n = 2%. That's real evidence, not proof, and a regression test keeps it true after future changes (Lesson R.5).
4. A new publisher's fine-tuned model comes as a signed model.safetensors. What does that guarantee?
Safetensors removes code execution on load; a signature proves origin and integrity. A backdoor can live in the weights, so pin the revision, sandbox it, and run behavioural evals on your own task data (Lesson R.4).
5. Two findings were each rated high: an injected email makes the agent include data in its reply, and the chat widget loads external images. How should the report treat them?
Chained, they're a zero-click exfiltration path. Reports list chains because the combined severity is higher than either part, and the cheapest strong fix is usually closing the exit (Lessons R.3 and R.5).
