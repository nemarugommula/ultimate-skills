Phase R · Lesson 5

Running a red-team engagement: tools, automation & reporting

Knowing attacks isn't the same as finding them in your system. A red-team engagement is a project: a scope and permission, an attack library, humans plus automation, findings ranked by risk, fixes verified, and every finding turned into a regression test.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Threat model: assets, attackers, entry points and trust boundaries (Lesson R.1). It tells you where to aim.
  • The attack classes: prompt injection and jailbreaks (R.2), agent and tool attacks (R.3), data and model attacks (R.4).
  • Attack success rate and safety evals (Lesson 5.10), and CI regression tests for prompts and models (Lesson 5.6).
🎯 Hook

In 2024 and early 2025 Anthropic wanted to know whether a new defence, Constitutional Classifiers, could stop universal jailbreaks: one prompting strategy that unlocks a model for a whole category of forbidden requests. So it ran an engagement with a precise target: ten forbidden questions, and a jailbreak only counted if the guarded model gave detailed answers to all ten. It offered a reward of up to $15,000, and 183 active participants spent an estimated 3,000+ hours over two months trying. Nobody succeeded.

Alongside the humans, an automated evaluation ran 10,000 synthetic jailbreak prompts: 86% succeeded against the unguarded model, 4.4% against the guarded one. Notice the shape: a clear scope, a checkable definition of success, human creativity and automated scale, and a measured rate rather than “it seemed fine”. That's an engagement.

Numbers first: what “we tested it” actually means

Model outputs are random, so a single attempt proves little. You run each attack many times and report an attack success rate (ASR = successful attempts ÷ attempts) with its uncertainty.

And one success can be enough: if a single conversation issues an unauthorized refund, the severity is set by the impact, and the ASR only tells you how often a casual attacker would get there.

💡 Big idea An engagement turns “try to break it” into evidence: a written scope and permission, a library of attacks mapped to known risks, repeated automated attempts plus creative human testing, findings ranked by severity × likelihood, fixes verified by re-running the exact attack and its variants, and every finding kept as a regression test that runs on each change. The output isn't a list of clever prompts; it's a measured, shrinking risk.
🧠 Analogy: a fire drill with inspectors

A building inspection has a signed work order (which floors, which day, who to call if something's really on fire), a checklist built from the fire code, inspectors who also poke at things the checklist forgot, a report that ranks problems by how bad and how likely, and a re-inspection after repairs. Then smoke detectors stay in the ceiling and get tested monthly. Scope, attack library, manual plus automated testing, triage, fix verification, and continuous red teaming in CI.

1. Scope and rules of engagement

Red teaming without permission is just attacking. Before the first prompt, write down and get sign-off on the rules of engagement (RoE):

  • Authorization: who owns the system and approved the test, in writing. Only systems you own or are contracted to test. Third-party models and APIs stay within their providers' terms: you test your application built on them, not the provider's infrastructure.
  • Targets and environments: which endpoints, model versions and tools; staging by default; production only with explicit approval, rate caps and a kill switch.
  • Data: synthetic test accounts and fake customer data; no real personal data in attacks or logs. Tools that act (payments, refunds, email) point at sandboxes.
  • Stop and escalate: what counts as a critical finding, who you call immediately, and when testing pauses.
  • Limits: time window, budget for model calls, and what's out of scope (for example denial-of-service against shared infrastructure).

3. Manual and automated, together

Automated (tools + an attacker LLM)Manual (humans)
StrengthBreadth and repetition: hundreds of variants, every release, measured ASRDepth and creativity: business logic, chaining findings, things no plugin knows
HowScanners send known probes; an attacker LLM generates and adapts attacks; multi-turn strategies escalate over a conversation; a judge gradesExploratory sessions guided by the threat model; domain experts for regulated areas
Watch out forJudges make mistakes: validate the grader on a labelled sample (Lesson 5.3); generic attacks miss app-specific risksSlow, expensive, inconsistent; findings must be turned into automated tests or they're lost

A typical rhythm: humans find the first instance of a new failure; automation turns it into a family of variants, measures how often it works, and keeps checking it forever.

✏️ Worked example: sizing an automated run

An automated scan with 12 attack plugins, 10 generated tests each, plus 3 attack strategies (encodings, injection wrappers, multi-turn) applied to every test:

  • Test cases: 12 × 10 = 120 base tests, plus 120 × 3 strategy variants = 480.
  • Model calls: each case needs about 3 calls (generate the attack, call your target, grade the answer) = 1,440 calls.
  • At ~2,000 tokens per call: 1,440 × 2,000 = 2.88M tokens, about $8.64 at an illustrative $3 per million tokens. Multi-turn attacks of up to 10 turns can multiply that several times.
  • Cheap enough to run on every release candidate; a short subset (the regression suite) runs on every pull request.

Prices are illustrative. The expensive part of red teaming is human time, not tokens.

The effort goes up and the automated share goes down, because tool misuse and exfiltration need humans chaining steps, not just scans.

    Measuring, scoring and triage

    Score each finding on two axes. Severity is the impact if it happens: who is harmed, how much, can it be undone. Likelihood is how easy and how repeatable it is: the ASR, the skill and access needed, whether it can be automated. Risk ≈ severity × likelihood decides the fix order, with one override: anything that leaks other users' data or moves money is fixed first even when it's hard to trigger.

    Then triage like a security team: dedupe (ten prompts, one root cause), find the root cause and fix it at the strongest layer you can (architecture and permissions beat filters, filters beat prompt wording), and verify the fix by re-running the original attack and fresh variants. A finding is closed only when its regression test passes.

      From engagement to continuous red teaming

      • Regression suite: every finding becomes a test with its oracle (the refund tool must refuse $51; the canary must never leave). Run it in CI on every prompt, model, tool or retrieval change (Lesson 5.6).
      • Release gates: zero open criticals; per-category ASR under an agreed threshold with enough attempts to mean it (0 in 300 for “under 1%”).
      • Scheduled scans: the full automated library nightly or weekly, because providers update models under you and new attack families appear monthly.
      • Production signals: canary alerts, tool-policy violations, refusal and complaint spikes feed new tests. Model or prompt upgrades trigger a fresh engagement.

      Reporting and responsible disclosure

      A good report has an executive summary (overall risk, top three fixes, release recommendation), the scope and rules of engagement, the method and attack library with coverage, each finding with severity, likelihood, ASR with attempts, reproduction steps, evidence, root cause and fix, the chains, and the residual risk you're accepting. Keep working exploit details in an access-controlled appendix: a report is itself sensitive.

      When the weakness is in someone else's system or model, disclose privately through their security or bug-bounty channel, give them time to fix it (Nasr and colleagues gave OpenAI the standard 90 days before publishing their ChatGPT extraction attack, Lesson R.4) and don't publish working exploits before a fix. AI bug bounties now exist, with scopes worth reading: Google's AI Vulnerability Reward Program (October 2025) pays up to $30,000 for a single report and rates “rogue actions” that change a victim's account or data highest, but excludes jailbreaks and direct prompt injection; Anthropic has run invite-only model-safety bounties on HackerOne focused on universal jailbreaks (up to $15,000 when announced in 2024); OpenAI runs its bug bounty through Bugcrowd. Always read the current program rules first.

import pytest
FINDINGS = load_yaml("redteam/findings.yaml")       # id, attack conversation, oracle

@pytest.mark.parametrize("f", FINDINGS, ids=lambda f: f["id"])
def test_finding_stays_fixed(f, staging_agent, tool_log):
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(20):                                  # outputs are random: repeat
        reply = staging_agent.run(f["conversation"])
        hits += oracle(f["oracle"], reply, tool_log.last_calls())  # e.g. refund > 50, canary out
    assert hits == 0, f"{f['id']} regressed: {hits}/20 attempts succeeded"

      🚀 Startup vs big tech

      Week one

      One engineer, two or three days before launch: a one-page RoE, the threat model's top risks, a promptfoo red-team config against staging, an afternoon of manual attacks on the tools, a short findings list with fixes, and a 20-case regression suite in CI. Join a peer review with another team: fresh eyes find the obvious things you can't see.

      At scale

      A dedicated AI red team with an internal attack library mapped to OWASP and ATLAS, PyRIT or in-house orchestration, launch reviews that require a signed-off engagement, external red teamers for high-risk launches, a public bug bounty with an AI scope, and continuous scanning on every model and prompt change, with findings tracked like any other security vulnerability.

      🎤 Interview angle

      “You have two weeks to red-team our new agent before launch. What do you do?” A senior answer:

      • Starts with scope and permission: written RoE, staging, synthetic data, sandboxed tools, an escalation path.
      • Derives the test plan from a threat model (what the agent reads, what it can do, who it serves), mapped to OWASP and ATLAS for coverage.
      • Combines automated scans for breadth with manual sessions on the tools and business logic, and validates the judge.
      • Reports ASR with attempts, severity × likelihood, chains, and fixes at the strongest layer, then verifies fixes and leaves a regression suite in CI.
      🏭 In the real world
      Lessons from 100+ products 🏢 Microsoft · 2025 Lessons From Red Teaming 100 Generative AI Products

      How: a threat-model ontology (system, actor, tactics and techniques mapped to MITRE ATT&CK and ATLAS, weakness, impact) applied across Microsoft's generative AI products, with case studies, using automation (PyRIT) alongside human experts.

      Why this method: their lessons include that you don't need gradients to break an AI system and that red teaming is not safety benchmarking: real failures came from how products were wired and used, so engagements are scoped by what the system can do and where it's deployed.

      External red teaming as a program 🏢 OpenAI · 2025 OpenAI's Approach to External Red Teaming

      How: deliberate choices about the composition of the red team, the access level they get, and the guidance they receive; then using the results as input to risk assessment and to new automated evaluations.

      Why this method: outside experts find risks insiders miss, and converting their findings into repeatable evals is what makes the effort last beyond one launch.

      An AI-specific bug bounty scope 🏢 Google · 2025 Google's AI Vulnerability Reward Program (The Register)

      How: a dedicated AI VRP with up to $30,000 per report; rogue actions in flagship products top the table at a $20,000 base, while jailbreaks, direct prompt injection and alignment issues are reported in-product instead.

      Why this method: bounties pay best for findings with clear security impact on other users; content issues need trend analysis across many reports rather than one-off payments.

      Open-source scanners 🧰 Open source garak · PyRIT · promptfoo red teaming

      How: libraries of probes, plugins and attack strategies that run against any endpoint you point them at, with detectors or judges that score the replies.

      Why this method: nobody should hand-write the thousandth encoding or injection variant; the tools give breadth so humans can spend their time on the system's own logic.

      🤯 Fun fact · why some bounties skip jailbreaks

      Google's AI bug bounty doesn't pay for jailbreaks. Its security leads explained that content issues need long-term work and trend analysis, not one-off rewards, and a Google team had argued months earlier that a model may have an effectively unlimited number of possible jailbreaks, so fully mitigating them may be infeasible. What does pay best? Getting the AI to take a harmful action in someone else's account. Impact on other people, not clever wording, is what security programs value.

      Check yourself

      1. Your automated suite tried an attack 100 times and it never succeeded. What's the most you can honestly claim?
      Zero in n attempts gives a 95% upper bound of about 3 ÷ n. For “under 1%” you need about 300 clean attempts. And it says nothing about variants you didn't try.
      2. A tester finds that the support agent will issue a $400 refund (limit $50) if the user claims to be a manager. It worked 2 times in 50. How should it be triaged?
      Moving money is high impact, and 4% means anyone who tries 25 times wins. The fix belongs in the tool (a hard check the model can't talk around), and it's closed only when the regression test passes.
      3. A teammate wants to “quickly red-team” a competitor's public chatbot to compare it with yours. What's the right answer?
      Without authorization, attacking a system is not red teaming, whatever the intent, and it may breach terms or law. Compare on public benchmarks, and if you do find a weakness, disclose it privately through the vendor's channel.
      📝 TL;DR
      • An engagement is a project: written scope and rules of engagement, an attack library mapped to OWASP and ATLAS, and a checkable oracle for every test.
      • Automation gives breadth and repetition; humans find business-logic flaws and chains. Validate the judge.
      • Report ASR with the number of attempts, and rank findings by severity × likelihood; anything that moves money or leaks other users' data goes first.
      • Fix at the strongest layer, verify with the original attack and variants, keep every finding as a CI regression test, and disclose others' bugs privately.
