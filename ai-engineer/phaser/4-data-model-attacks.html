Phase R · Lesson 4

Data & model attacks: poisoning, extraction, membership inference

Prompt injection attacks the conversation. These attacks go after what the model learned, what it reads, and the files it's made of: poison in, secrets out. You'll poison a knowledge base, watch a confidence score give away training data, and fix both.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): search your documents, paste the best matches into the prompt, then generate (Lessons 3.1 and 3.4).
  • Fine-tuning = continuing a model's training on your own examples; open-weight models are downloaded as files you run yourself (Lessons 7.1 and 7.3).
  • Threat model: what you protect, from whom, through which entry points (Lesson R.1). This lesson fills in the entry points that sit in your data and model supply chain.
🎯 Hook

In October 2025, Anthropic, the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute published the largest pretraining-poisoning experiments to date. They trained models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, and found that 250 poisoned documents planted a backdoor in every one of them, even though the biggest model saw more than 20 times as much clean data.

For the 13B model those 250 documents were about 420,000 tokens: 0.00016% of its training data. The backdoor they tested was deliberately harmless (a trigger word that makes the model output gibberish), but the lesson for defenders is not: an attacker doesn't need to control a percentage of your data, just a handful of documents in it.

💡 Big idea Data is code. Anything that shapes your model's behaviour (pretraining data, fine-tuning sets, the RAG corpus, model files and adapters) is an input an attacker can write to, so it needs provenance and review like code does. And anything the model has seen can leak back out through its outputs (training data, membership, the system prompt, the model itself), so every output channel needs limits and monitoring. Two directions: poison in (integrity) and secrets out (confidentiality).
🧠 Analogy: the restaurant kitchen

A supplier slips one bad ingredient into a delivery: that's poisoning, and the dish still looks fine. A rival orders every dish on the menu a thousand times and reverse-engineers the recipes: model extraction. Someone asks the chef enough leading questions that he recites a regular's private order: training-data extraction. Someone notices the chef cooks one dish with suspicious confidence and concludes a particular person ate here: membership inference. And a pre-made sauce jar that runs code when you open the lid: a malicious model file. The fixes are kitchen basics: trusted suppliers, inspected deliveries, sealed jars, limits on orders and a log of who ordered what.

Poisoning: retrieval is the easiest door

Training a model on poisoned data takes a training run. Poisoning a RAG system takes one document upload, and the effect is live on the next query. PoisonedRAG showed what the attacker's document needs: (1) it must be retrieved for the target question, which is easy if it simply contains the question's wording, and (2) once retrieved, it must convince the model of the attacker's answer. Put several such documents in, and they outnumber the genuine ones in the top results.

✏️ Worked example: why 5 documents beat a million
  • Your knowledge base has 1,000,000 chunks. An attacker adds 5 per target question: 5 ÷ 1,000,005 ≈ 0.0005% of the corpus.
  • But retrieval doesn't sample the corpus; it ranks it. Your system sends the top 5 to the model. If the 5 poisoned chunks echo the question more closely than the 2 genuine chunks do, the top 5 is 100% poison.
  • Corpus size didn't dilute anything: the attacker competes with the few documents relevant to that question, not with the million. That's why “we have so much data, a few bad docs won't matter” is wrong for RAG, and (per the 2025 result) not reliably true for pretraining either.

What you should see: two poisoned documents per question flip all four answers, and adding 400 unrelated documents changes nothing, because the attacker only competes with the two relevant genuine documents. The allow-list stops an outside attacker but costs you the forum tip (one clean question goes unanswered) and does nothing once the attacker has an editor account. The provenance filter (reviewed documents only) stops both, but a brand-new genuine page also waits for review: freshness is the price. Reranking with dedup collapses the attacker's near-identical copies, which helps a lot but not completely; a real attacker paraphrases each copy with an LLM, which defeats simple dedup. The monitor notices that retrieved sources disagree, falls back to reviewed pages and alerts a human, but slide the poison to 5 and it goes quiet: the genuine documents are pushed out of the top 5, so there's nothing left to disagree with. Every layer has a gap; you need several.

Backdoors and sleeper agents

A backdoor is behaviour that only appears when a trigger is present: a rare word, a date, a code comment. The model passes every normal eval, because normal inputs don't contain the trigger. Anthropic's Sleeper Agents paper (January 2024) deliberately trained models that write secure code when the prompt says the year is 2023 but insert exploitable code when it says 2024, and then tried to remove the behaviour with standard safety training. Supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training all failed to remove it; the backdoor was most persistent in the largest models, and adversarial training could teach the model to recognize its trigger better, hiding the behaviour rather than removing it.

Two practical consequences for AI engineers. First, if you fine-tune on data you didn't fully vet (scraped forums, user feedback, a vendor's dataset), you inherit whatever is in it, so review and filter fine-tuning data like code. Second, if you download an open-weight model or adapter from an unknown publisher, “it passed our evals” is weak evidence: you can't eval for a trigger you don't know. Prefer publishers with a track record, pinned versions, and behavioural tests on your own task data.

Secrets out: what the model saw can come back

AttackThe question the attacker answersKey result
Training-data extraction“What exact text did you train on?”From GPT-2: hundreds of verbatim sequences, including names, phone numbers and emails, some present in only one training document (2020). From ChatGPT: a “divergence” prompt made it emit training data 150× more often than normal (2023).
Membership inference“Was this record in your training data?”Shown against commercial ML-as-a-service models, including on a hospital discharge dataset (2016). On large pretrained LLMs it often barely beats guessing (2024), but models fine-tuned for several epochs on small private datasets can be a different story.
Model extraction / distillation“Can I copy your model's behaviour or weights?”Full embedding projection layers of two OpenAI models for under $20 (2024). In February 2026 Anthropic reported distillation campaigns using about 24,000 fraudulent accounts and over 16 million exchanges with Claude.
System-prompt extraction“What are your hidden instructions?”OWASP's 2026 guidance is blunt: assume hidden context is discoverable, never put credentials in it, and never rely on its secrecy for authorization.

Why does memorization happen? Mostly duplication: a sequence that appears 10 times in the training data is generated about 1,000 times more often than one that appears once (Kandpal et al., 2022). Deduplicating training data is the single cheapest privacy defence, and it also makes models better. Bigger models memorize more, and longer prompts that match the start of a training sequence extract more.

Membership inference, by intuition

A model is usually a little more confident on examples it trained on than on fresh ones. An attacker who can see a confidence score (or a log-probability) sets a threshold: above it, guess “member”. The gap between the two distributions is the leak, and it grows with overfitting and with duplicates. Modern work measures attacks by their true-positive rate at a very low false-positive rate (say 1%), because an attack that confidently identifies even a few people is a privacy breach, even if its average accuracy looks like a coin flip.

What you should see: with a small gap, the best average accuracy is near 60%, which sounds harmless. Turn on duplicates and the TPR at 1% FPR roughly doubles, and most of the duplicated members sit above a high threshold: those are exactly the records an attacker can name with confidence. Deduplication removes that tail; regularization or differential privacy (DP, training with clipped, noised updates so no single example matters much) shrinks the whole gap but leaves the duplicates exposed: DP protects each copy, so a record that appears 10 times gets far weaker protection. Dedup first. Hiding scores raises the attacker's cost but isn't a fix: label-only attacks (Choquette-Choo et al., 2020) infer membership from the outputs alone and break defences that only mask confidence scores, while differential privacy held up.

The supply chain: model files are executable

Python's pickle format, long the default for PyTorch checkpoints, can run arbitrary code while a file is being loaded. That's how JFrog's 2024 scan found around 100 genuinely malicious models on Hugging Face, one opening a reverse shell to an outside server when loaded. The fixes are boring and effective:

  • safetensors: a weights-only format with no code in it. Prefer it for every model you download or publish.
  • torch.load(..., weights_only=True): the default since PyTorch 2.6. Keep PyTorch patched; this flag itself had a bypass CVE in 2025 (CVE-2025-32434).
  • Pin revisions (a commit hash, not main), verify hashes and signatures (OpenSSF Model Signing / Sigstore), keep an AI bill of materials, and load untrusted artifacts in a sandbox.
  • Remember OWASP's warning: safe formats reduce risk but don't eliminate it. A backdoor can live in the weights or graph of a perfectly “safe” file, and parsers have had memory-corruption bugs. Signing proves who published it, not that it's benign.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

safetensors and huggingface_hub for safe, pinned downloads; Hugging Face's pickle scanning and third-party model scanners as one layer; garak (NVIDIA's open-source LLM vulnerability scanner) has probes for training-data replay (leakreplay) and system-prompt extraction (sysprompt_extraction); promptfoo red teaming has rag-poisoning, rag-document-exfiltration and prompt-extraction plugins (Lesson R.5); Opacus for differentially private PyTorch training.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

You probably don't pretrain, so your exposure is the RAG corpus, your fine-tuning data and the model files you download. Index only sources you control or review; store source, author and review status as metadata; safetensors and pinned revisions only; no secrets in prompts; a canary in the system prompt; rate limits on the API. A day of work.

At scale

Data provenance pipelines with signed datasets and an AI bill of materials, dedup and PII scrubbing before every training run, poisoning and backdoor evals on each checkpoint, membership-inference audits before releasing fine-tuned models trained on customer data, distillation detection (account clustering, behavioural fingerprints) on the public API, and a model registry that refuses unsigned artifacts.

🎤 Interview angle

“Users can upload documents that our assistant later searches. What could go wrong?” A senior answer:

  • Names RAG poisoning (false answers that win retrieval) and indirect prompt injection (instructions hidden in documents, Lesson R.2), and notes that a few documents can dominate the top-k for a target question regardless of corpus size.
  • Separates tenants: one user's uploads must never be retrieved for another user (permissions at retrieval, Lesson 4.7).
  • Layers defences: provenance metadata and trust tiers, review or quarantine for shared content, dedup, trust-weighted ranking, citations so users can see sources, and a conflict monitor.
  • Says how they'd test it: a poisoning suite with planted documents and a measured attack success rate, run in CI (Lesson R.5).
🏭 In the real world
A near-constant number of poison documents 🧪 Research · 2025 Anthropic, UK AISI & Alan Turing Institute: A small number of samples can poison LLMs of any size

How: pretrained models from 600M to 13B parameters on clean data plus 100, 250 or 500 poisoned documents that pair a trigger phrase with gibberish, and measured whether the trigger worked.

Why this method: it tested the assumption that bigger datasets dilute poison. They don't: 250 documents worked at every size, so defenders should plan around absolute counts of untrusted documents, not percentages.

Knowledge-corruption attacks on RAG 🧪 Research · 2024 PoisonedRAG (Zou et al.)

How: crafted texts that satisfy a retrieval condition (they rank for the target question) and a generation condition (they make the model give the chosen answer), injected into knowledge bases of millions of texts.

Why this method: 90% attack success with five texts per question, and the defences they evaluated were insufficient, which is why provenance and review matter more than detection tricks.

Extracting training data from a production chatbot 🧪 Research · 2023 Nasr, Carlini et al.: Extracting training data from ChatGPT

How: a prompt that made the aligned model “diverge” from chat behaviour and emit memorized text, verified against a large index of public web data.

Why this method: it showed alignment hides memorization rather than removing it, so base models and production systems both need testing. They disclosed to OpenAI and waited a standard 90 days before publishing (responsible disclosure, Lesson R.5).

Detecting distillation at API scale 🏢 Anthropic · 2026 Detecting and preventing distillation attacks

How: classifiers and behavioural fingerprinting on API traffic, including detection of prompts that elicit step-by-step reasoning for training data, and detection of coordinated activity across many accounts.

Why this method: per-account rate limits fail when an attacker spreads 16 million exchanges over about 24,000 accounts; you have to detect the pattern, not the volume.

Malicious models on a public hub 🏢 JFrog · 2024 Malicious Hugging Face ML models with silent backdoor

How: scanned uploaded models for code that runs on load, mostly in PyTorch pickle files, and found about 100 with real payloads, one opening a reverse shell.

Why this method: the attack needs no ML at all, just a file format that executes code, which is why safetensors and weights-only loading are the fix.

🤯 Fun fact · the sixty-dollar dataset

Big image datasets like LAION-400M are distributed as lists of URLs, not images. Carlini and colleagues noticed that some of those domains had expired, so anyone could buy them and serve different images to everyone who downloaded the dataset afterwards. For about $60 they could have controlled 0.01% of the dataset. They told the maintainers instead, and recommended a cheap fix: publish a hash of every image so downloaders can check they got the same one.

Check yourself

1. Your RAG index has 2 million chunks and you send the top 5 to the model. Only 2 genuine chunks answer “What's the refund window?”. An attacker plants 4 chunks that copy the question's wording. Roughly what share of the model's context for that question can the attacker control?
Retrieval ranks; it doesn't sample. The attacker competes only with the 2 relevant genuine chunks, so 4 well-crafted chunks can take 4 of the 5 slots (80%), and the model tends to follow the majority. Corpus size doesn't dilute targeted poison.
2. A teammate wants to download a promising fine-tuned model from an unknown publisher; the repo has a pytorch_model.bin and a model.safetensors. What's the best plan?
Safetensors removes code execution on load, and pinning stops the file changing under you. But a backdoor can live in the weights themselves, so a sandbox and your own evals are still needed, and an unknown publisher stays a risk you note in the threat model.
3. A membership-inference audit of your model fine-tuned on support tickets reports 56% average accuracy, close to guessing. Are you done?
Average accuracy hides the tail. A few duplicated or unusual records can be identified with high confidence even when the average is near 50%, which is why attacks are judged by TPR at, say, 1% FPR. Dedup and DP shrink the tail; hiding scores only raises the cost.
📝 TL;DR
  • Data is code: training data, fine-tuning sets, RAG corpora and model files are all attacker-writable inputs that need provenance and review.
  • Poison works by count, not percentage: a handful of documents can dominate retrieval for a target question or plant a backdoor in pretraining.
  • What a model saw can leak: verbatim training data, membership, hidden prompts, the model's behaviour itself. Dedup, scrub, filter outputs, rate-limit and monitor.
  • Model files can execute code: safetensors, pinned revisions, signatures, sandboxed loading, and behavioural evals for what formats can't catch.
