Phase 1 · Lesson 2

Reasoning: chain-of-thought, self-consistency & reasoning models

Letting a model “think out loud” before answering can triple its accuracy on multi-step problems, or burn 20× the tokens on a question a child could answer. Learn when to buy thinking, and how much.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The model generates one token at a time, and each token it writes becomes input for the next (Lesson 0.2).
  • Output tokens usually cost several times more than input tokens and take most of the response time (Lesson 0.3).
  • Temperature controls randomness: at 0 the model nearly always picks its top token; higher values give varied answers (Lesson 0.5).
  • Prompt elements and few-shot examples (Lesson 1.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2022, a team from the University of Tokyo and Google added five words to the end of math word problems: “Let's think step by step.” Nothing else changed: no examples, no new model.

On the MultiArith benchmark, OpenAI's text-davinci-002 went from 17.7% to 78.7% correct. On GSM8K (grade-school math), from 10.4% to 40.7%. The model already “knew” how to do the arithmetic. It just needed room to write the intermediate steps before committing to an answer.

Numbers first: buying accuracy with tokens

One model (Google's PaLM, 540 billion parameters), one benchmark (GSM8K, grade-school math word problems), three ways of asking:

MethodAccuracyOutput tokens per question (typical)
Answer directly17.9%~5
Chain-of-thought: 8 worked examples that show the steps56.9%~100
Chain-of-thought + self-consistency: sample 40 reasoning paths, take the majority answer74.4%~4,000

Accuracies from Wei et al. 2022 and Wang et al. 2022. Token counts are rough illustrations for this kind of problem.

Each step up roughly adds 20–40 points of accuracy and multiplies output tokens by 20–40×. On a hard problem that's a bargain. On “what's our refund window?” it's pure waste. That trade-off is the whole lesson.

💡 Big idea A model spends the same small amount of computation on every token it writes. Asking it to write out intermediate steps gives it more tokens, so more computation, before the answer, and each step can build on the last. Reasoning is a dial you pay for in output tokens and latency: turn it up for multi-step problems, down for lookups.
🧠 Analogy: scratch paper

Ask someone for 17 × 24 in their head and to answer instantly: lots of mistakes. Give them scratch paper and they write 17 × 20 = 340, 17 × 4 = 68, 340 + 68 = 408. Same person, same knowledge, far more reliable. Self-consistency is asking five people to do it on their own paper and going with the most common answer. A reasoning model is someone who has been trained to reach for scratch paper on their own, and decides how many pages to use.

Two caveats. Wei et al. found CoT only helped models of roughly 100 billion parameters or more; smaller models wrote fluent but illogical chains and did worse. And for reasoning models, OpenAI's guidance is to skip “think step by step”: they already reason internally, and the extra instruction can even hurt. Ask for a clear task and let effort control the thinking.

Self-consistency: why voting works, and when it backfires

If each sampled answer is right with probability p, independently, the majority of n votes is right with probability

P(majority correct) = Σk > n/2 C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

With p = 0.7 that's 0.78 for 3 votes, 0.84 for 5, 0.95 for 15. Voting amplifies whatever the model does more than half the time. Real errors are often scattered (38, 44, 40…) while correct paths agree (42, 42, 42), which helps even more. But if most wrong paths fall into the same trap and p < 0.5, voting makes you confidently wrong.

✏️ Worked example: 5 votes at p = 0.7
  • Exactly 3 right: C(5,3) · 0.7³ · 0.3² = 10 × 0.343 × 0.09 = 0.309
  • Exactly 4 right: C(5,4) · 0.7⁴ · 0.3 = 5 × 0.2401 × 0.3 = 0.360
  • All 5 right: 0.7⁵ = 0.168
  • Majority right: 0.309 + 0.360 + 0.168 = 0.837, up from 0.70, for 5× the tokens.
  • Same maths at p = 0.4 (a trap the model usually falls into): 0.317. Five votes made it worse than one.

Cost: 400 reasoning tokens per path × 5 paths = 2,000 output tokens. At an illustrative $15 per million output tokens that's $0.03 per question instead of $0.006. Run the 5 in parallel and latency barely changes; run them one after another and it's 5× slower.

What you should see: with scattered errors, even p = 0.4 improves with more votes, because the right answer is still the most common one. Switch to a shared trap and drop p to 0.3: the curve falls, and 25 votes are right only about 8% of the time (one sample: 30%). That is why self-consistency shines on math (many ways to be wrong) and helps less on yes/no questions (only one way to be wrong). A bonus: the share of votes the winner got is a free confidence signal; Wang et al. found low agreement flagged likely errors.

Reasoning models and effort settings

Since 2024, the frontier providers ship models that think before answering. You don't write CoT prompts; you set how hard they should think. The knob names, as of late 2026 (check your provider's docs, these change):

The industry moved from fixed thinking budgets (“think for up to 8,000 tokens”) to adaptive thinking: the model decides per request whether and how much to think, and effort sets its default posture. Either way, you pay for the thinking even when you don't see it.

For hard problems, only the top level reaches an 85% target at all. Low effort looks cheap per request, but it's wrong most of the time, and a wrong answer that a human has to redo is the most expensive answer of all.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Effort settings in the OpenAI, Anthropic and Gemini SDKs; for self-consistency, plain parallel calls (asyncio.gather) and collections.Counter; DSPy has ChainOfThought modules if you build pipelines.

import asyncio, re
from collections import Counter
import anthropic

client = anthropic.AsyncAnthropic()

# 1) A reasoning model: set the effort, not a "think step by step" prompt
async def solve(question, effort="medium"):
    r = await client.messages.create(
        model="claude-opus-5-5",              # check current model IDs
        max_tokens=16000,
        thinking={"type": "adaptive"},          # the model decides how much to think
        output_config={"effort": effort},      # low | medium | high | xhigh | max
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
                   question + "\nEnd with: ANSWER: <number>"}],
    )
    text = "".join(b.text for b in r.content if b.type == "text")
    m = re.search(r"ANSWER:\s*(-?[\d.]+)", text)
    return m.group(1) if m else None

# 2) Self-consistency: n independent samples in parallel, majority vote
async def vote(question, n=5):
    answers = [a for a in await asyncio.gather(*[solve(question, "low") for _ in range(n)]) if a]
    best, count = Counter(answers).most_common(1)[0]
    return best, count / n          # answer + agreement (a cheap confidence signal)

# OpenAI: client.responses.create(model=..., reasoning={"effort": "low"}, input=...)

Thinking tokens show up in the usage you're billed for (as output tokens) even when the thinking text is hidden or summarized. Log them per request so you can see what reasoning costs you.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Use a reasoning model at its default effort for anything hard, and a low effort (or a small fast model) for simple, high-volume routes. Log thinking tokens and latency per route from day one. Skip self-consistency unless a single wrong answer is expensive.

At scale

Per-route effort chosen from eval curves like the one above, a router that sends easy requests to cheap settings (Lesson 8.2), self-consistency or verifier checks on high-stakes decisions, and latency budgets enforced in the gateway. Effort settings are re-tuned on every model upgrade, since a new model's “medium” isn't the old one's.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our agent's answers are good but p95 latency (the 95th-percentile response time) is 40 seconds and costs tripled after we switched to a reasoning model. What do you do?” A senior answer:

  • Measures first: thinking tokens per route, accuracy per effort level on an eval set, and which requests actually need reasoning.
  • Routes: low effort or a smaller model for easy traffic, high effort only where the eval shows a gain.
  • Knows the maths of self-consistency (amplifies p > 0.5, hurts when errors are correlated) and uses it only for checkable, high-stakes answers.
  • Watches user-facing latency: streaming, summarized thinking or progress messages, parallel sampling, and a cap on output tokens.
🏭 In the real world
Chain-of-thought prompting 🧪 Research · 2022 Wei et al. (Google)

How: eight few-shot examples that show the intermediate reasoning, then the answer.

Why this method: PaLM 540B went from 17.9% to 56.9% on GSM8K, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier, with no training. The gains only appeared in models of about 100B parameters and up.

Self-consistency 🧪 Research · 2022 Wang et al. (Google)

How: sample 40 diverse reasoning paths instead of one greedy path, then take the majority final answer.

Why this method: +17.9 points on GSM8K, +11.0 on SVAMP, +12.2 on AQuA over CoT, and the agreement rate doubles as a signal of when the model doesn't know.

Tree of Thoughts 🧪 Research · 2023 Yao et al. (Princeton, Google DeepMind)

How: the model proposes and evaluates partial solutions, exploring a search tree with lookahead and backtracking.

Why this method: on the Game of 24 puzzle, GPT-4 with CoT solved 4% of tasks; with ToT, 74%. Search beats a single left-to-right pass when early choices matter.

Effort instead of CoT prompts 🏢 OpenAI · 2026 Reasoning best practices

How: keep prompts simple and direct, avoid “think step by step” on reasoning models, and choose a reasoning.effort per task.

Why this method: these models already reason internally; explicit CoT instructions are unnecessary and can hurt, while effort trades quality against latency and cost directly.

🤯 Fun fact · thirteen ways to add 2 + 3

A 2024 Tencent AI Lab paper, “Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=?”, found that o1-style reasoning models used on average 1,953% more tokens than conventional models to answer “what is 2 plus 3?”, and one wrote out 13 separate solutions before answering. Overthinking is real, which is why effort settings and routing exist.

Check yourself

1. Each sample is right with p = 0.8 and errors are independent. Roughly what's the majority-vote accuracy with 3 samples?
Need 2 or 3 right: 3 × 0.8² × 0.2 + 0.8³ = 0.384 + 0.512 = 0.896. Three times the cost for about 10 points; whether that's worth it depends on what a wrong answer costs you.
2. You moved your FAQ bot (500,000 questions a day) to a reasoning model at high effort. Accuracy went from 94% to 95%, and cost and latency went up 6×. What should you do?
Lookups barely benefit from thinking. Pick effort per route from measured curves. Adding CoT instructions to a reasoning model is unnecessary and can hurt.
3. For which task is self-consistency (sample several, majority vote) the best fit?
Voting needs comparable final answers: a number or a label. Free-form text can't be majority-voted without another model to judge it (Lesson 5.3).
📝 TL;DR
  • Writing intermediate steps gives the model more computation before it answers: big gains on multi-step problems, paid in output tokens and latency.
  • CoT (few-shot or “Let's think step by step”) for non-reasoning models; reasoning models just need a clear task and an effort level.
  • Self-consistency: majority of n samples. It amplifies p > 0.5 and hurts when errors share a trap; agreement is a free confidence score.
  • Pick effort per route from an eval curve: cheap for lookups, high for hard problems. Thinking tokens are billed even when hidden.
