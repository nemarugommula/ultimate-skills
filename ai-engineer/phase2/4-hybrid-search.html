Phase 2 · Lesson 4

Hybrid search: BM25 + dense, fusion & sparse vectors

Embeddings understand meaning but blur exact strings. Keyword search nails exact strings but misses paraphrases. Run both, fuse the rankings, and you get the strengths of each for a few extra milliseconds.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Dense retrieval: embed the query and documents, return the nearest vectors (Lessons 2.1 and 2.2).
  • Embeddings blur exact numbers and codes, and can't see words they never learned (2.1).
  • Vector databases can store several kinds of vectors and run filters (2.3).
🎯 Hook

Anthropic's retrieval team gives the example of a support user searching for “Error code TS-999”. An embedding model may return general content about error codes and miss the one document that mentions TS-999 exactly. Old-fashioned keyword search finds it instantly.

In their tests, adding keyword search (BM25) to their improved embeddings cut top-20 retrieval failures from 3.7% to 2.9%, on top of the gains from better chunks. The 30-year-old algorithm still pulls its weight next to the neural one.

This isn't just folklore. The BEIR benchmark (Thakur et al., 2021) tested retrievers on 18 datasets they weren't trained for and found BM25 a robust baseline that many neural retrievers failed to beat out of their home domain. When Microsoft released E5 in 2022, it was the first embedding model to outperform BM25 on BEIR zero-shot without labelled data. Plain sentence: dense and keyword search fail on different queries, so combining them covers both.

💡 Big idea Run two retrievers in parallel, one that matches words exactly (BM25 or a sparse vector) and one that matches meaning (dense embeddings), then fuse their ranked lists. Fusion by rank (RRF) needs almost no tuning and no score calibration, which is why it's the usual starting point.
🧠 Analogy: two detectives

One detective reads every file for the exact licence plate “KX-4013”; she never misses an exact clue but doesn't understand that “the getaway car” means the same thing. The other understands the story and connects “getaway car” with “fled in a vehicle”, but shrugs at plate numbers. You don't pick one. You ask both for their top suspects and trust the names that appear high on both lists most.

BM25 in one formula

BM25 (“Best Matching 25”, from the Okapi system at City University London in the 1990s, built on the probabilistic retrieval work of Stephen Robertson, Karen Spärck Jones and others) scores a document for a query by adding up, for each query word:

score = Σ IDF(word) × tf × (k₁ + 1) ÷ ( tf + k₁ × (1 − b + b × doclen ÷ avglen) )
  • IDF, inverse document frequency: rare words count much more than common ones. IDF = ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5) ÷ (n + 0.5)) for a word in n of N documents.
  • tf, term frequency: more mentions help, but with diminishing returns (k₁, usually 1.2 to 2.0).
  • b (usually 0.75): long documents get a penalty, so a word in a short title counts more than the same word buried in a manual.

It needs no training, runs on an inverted index (word → list of documents) and is microseconds fast. Its weakness is the flip side: no words in common, no match.

✏️ Worked example: why “4013” beats “error”

1,000 support articles. “error” appears in 300 of them; “4013” appears in 1. With k₁ = 1.5:

  • IDF(“error”) = ln(1 + 700.5 ÷ 300.5) = ln(3.33) = 1.20.
  • IDF(“4013”) = ln(1 + 999.5 ÷ 1.5) = ln(667.3) = 6.50: 5.4× more weight than “error”.
  • Term frequency saturates: for an average-length document, 1 mention scores 1.0, 2 mentions 1.43, 5 mentions 1.92, 10 mentions only 2.17. Stuffing a word ten times barely helps.

So for the query “error 4013”, the one article containing 4013 wins clearly. A dense model, which turns “4013” into a few generic number-ish sub-word tokens (Lesson 0.2), has no such sharp signal.

Fusing the two lists

BM25 scores (0 to maybe 20) and cosine similarities (0.2 to 0.9) live on different scales, so you can't just add them. Two common fixes:

  • RRF, reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, 2009): ignore scores, use ranks. Each document gets Σ 1 ÷ (k + rank) over the lists it appears in, with k = 60. In the paper, k = 60 was fixed during a pilot study, and RRF beat the best single system and established fusion methods (Condorcet, CombMNZ) by 4 to 5% on average.
  • Weighted score fusion (also called convex combination): normalize each list's scores to 0–1, then combine as α × dense + (1 − α) × keyword. Weaviate's alpha works this way (1 = pure vector, 0 = pure keyword). It can beat RRF when tuned on your data, but the weight is one more thing to tune and to drift.
✏️ Worked example: RRF by hand (k = 60)
  • Doc A: rank 1 in dense, absent from BM25: 1/61 = 0.0164.
  • Doc B: rank 3 in dense, rank 1 in BM25: 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.0323.
  • Doc C: rank 2 in dense, rank 4 in BM25: 1/62 + 1/64 = 0.0318.

Fused order: B, C, A. Being decent on both lists beats being first on one. The large k flattens the curve, so one list can't dominate with a single top hit.

Sparse learned vectors: BM25 that knows synonyms

A sparse vector has one slot per vocabulary word (tens of thousands), almost all zero: BM25 is in effect one. SPLADE (Formal et al., 2021) has a language model produce those word weights instead, and expand the text with related words it doesn't contain: an article about “refunds” also gets weight on “money”, “return” and “reimburse”. The result keeps exact-term matching and the speed of an inverted index while picking up some synonyms. SPLADE v2 reported more than 9% better NDCG@10 (a ranking-quality score) on TREC DL 2019 than the first version, and state-of-the-art results on BEIR at the time.

Many engines store sparse vectors natively: Qdrant and Pinecone (sparse + dense in one query), pgvector's sparsevec, Elasticsearch's sparse fields. The trade-off: you run another model at index and query time.

When hybrid helps (and when it doesn't)

Helps a lotHelps a little or not at all
Codes, IDs, SKUs, error numbers, version strings, names, jargon, acronyms; short keyword-style queries; multilingual mixes with product names; out-of-domain dataPurely conversational questions over clean prose; a strong domain-tuned embedding model; corpora where words and meaning line up anyway

Costs: a second index (an inverted index is small), a second query (parallel, a few ms), and the same text-processing choices as any search engine (tokenizing, stemming, languages). Measure it: run your eval set with dense only, BM25 only and hybrid, and keep hybrid only if it earns its place. Usually it does, and a reranker on the fused top 50 adds more (Lesson 3.4).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Native hybrid: Elasticsearch / OpenSearch (BM25 + kNN + the rrf retriever), Weaviate (hybrid with alpha), Qdrant (prefetch dense + sparse, fuse with RRF or DBSF), Pinecone (sparse-dense), pgvector with Postgres full-text search. DIY: rank_bm25 or bm25s in Python plus your embeddings and a 5-line RRF.

# DIY hybrid: BM25 + embeddings + reciprocal rank fusion
from rank_bm25 import BM25Okapi
import numpy as np

bm25 = BM25Okapi([d.lower().split() for d in docs])        # use a real tokenizer in production
def hybrid(query, q_vec, doc_vecs, k=60, top=10):
    kw = np.argsort(-bm25.get_scores(query.lower().split()))[:50]
    dense = np.argsort(-(doc_vecs @ q_vec))[:50]             # normalized vectors: dot = cosine
    score = {}
    for ranking in (kw, dense):
        for rank, i in enumerate(ranking):
            score[i] = score.get(i, 0) + 1 / (k + rank + 1)
    return sorted(score, key=score.get, reverse=True)[:top]

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Turn on your database's built-in hybrid (or add Postgres full-text search next to pgvector) with RRF defaults. Add five ID-style and five paraphrase queries to your eval set so you can see each retriever's blind spot.

At scale

Several retrievers (BM25 with field boosts, dense, learned sparse, sometimes per language), fused and then reranked by a cross-encoder, with weights learned from click logs and A/B tested. Query routing sends obvious ID lookups straight to exact match.

🎤 Interview angle

“Users say search can't find tickets by their ticket number, but understands questions fine. What do you do?” A senior answer:

  • Diagnoses it: embeddings blur exact tokens like IDs; this is a retrieval-method gap, not a model-size problem.
  • Adds BM25 (or sparse vectors) in parallel and fuses with RRF; maybe routes regex-shaped queries (“TCK-\d+”) straight to an exact lookup.
  • Explains the fusion choice (RRF vs weighted α) and why scores can't be added raw.
  • Proves it with an eval slice per query type (IDs, paraphrases, names) and watches that paraphrase quality doesn't drop.
🏭 In the real world
Contextual embeddings + contextual BM25 🏢 Anthropic · 2024 Introducing Contextual Retrieval

How: each chunk, prefixed with a short generated context, is indexed both as an embedding and in a BM25 index; results are combined, then reranked.

Why this method: embeddings miss exact matches like “TS-999”; adding BM25 took failures from 3.7% to 2.9%, and reranking to 1.9% (from a 5.7% baseline).

Rank fusion that needs no tuning 🧪 Research · 2009 Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack et al., SIGIR)

How: score each document by Σ 1/(60 + rank) across rankings.

Why this method: it consistently beat the individual systems and the standard fusion methods, using only ranks, so it works for retrievers whose scores aren't comparable. It's now built into Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Qdrant, Weaviate and others.

BM25 is hard to beat out of domain 🧪 Research · 2021 BEIR benchmark

How: 10 retrieval systems (lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction, rerankers) evaluated zero-shot on 18 datasets.

Why this method: testing outside the training domain exposed that dense retrievers often underperform BM25 there, which is the evidence behind keeping BM25 in the loop.

Learned sparse retrieval 🧪 Research · 2021 SPLADE (Formal et al.)

How: a model outputs sparse, expanded term weights with a sparsity penalty, served from an ordinary inverted index.

Why this method: keeps the exact matching and efficiency of bag-of-words search while learning which related terms to add.

🤯 Fun fact · the magic 60

The k = 60 you'll find in so many hybrid search systems comes from one line in a short 2009 paper: the authors fixed it during a pilot investigation and found it near-optimal. Elasticsearch's rank_constant still defaults to 60 today.

Check yourself

1. With RRF (k = 60), doc X is ranked 2nd by dense and 2nd by BM25; doc Y is ranked 1st by dense and absent from BM25. Which ranks higher?
X: 1/62 + 1/62 = 0.0323. Y: 1/61 = 0.0164. Appearing high on both lists roughly doubles your score, which is exactly the behaviour you want from fusion.
2. You fuse by adding raw BM25 scores (0 to 25) to cosine similarities (0.3 to 0.9). What goes wrong?
Scores on different scales can't be added directly. Normalize (min-max or distribution-based, like Qdrant's DBSF) and weight them, or use rank-based RRF, which ignores the scales entirely.
3. A legal search tool must find clauses by exact citation (“§ 4.2(b)”) and by meaning (“can the landlord end the lease early?”). Best design?
Citations are exact tokens that embeddings blur; “end the lease early” vs “early termination” needs meaning. Hybrid covers both; a reranker then orders the fused shortlist with full attention to the query.
📝 TL;DR
  • Dense search wins on paraphrases; BM25 wins on exact codes, IDs, names and rare words. They fail differently.
  • BM25 = rare words weigh more (IDF), repeated words saturate (k₁), long docs are penalized (b).
  • Fuse with RRF (Σ 1/(60 + rank)) by default; weighted score fusion needs normalization and tuning.
  • Learned sparse vectors (SPLADE) add expansion to exact matching.
  • Measure hybrid on query slices (IDs vs paraphrases), then add a reranker on top.
