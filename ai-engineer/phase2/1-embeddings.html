Embeddings: turning meaning into vectors

An embedding model turns any text into a list of numbers. Texts that mean the same thing get nearby numbers, even with no words in common. That one trick powers search, RAG, recommendations, clustering and dedup.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector is just a list of numbers, like [0.12, -0.40, 0.88]. You can picture a 2-number vector as a point on a map.
  • Tokens: about 4 characters of English each; embedding APIs charge per token, like chat APIs (Lesson 0.3).
  • Calling a model API from Python with an SDK (Lesson 0.5).
🎯 Hook

In January 2024 OpenAI released two new embedding models. The bigger one, text-embedding-3-large, makes vectors of up to 3,072 numbers. The surprise: you can cut that vector down to its first 256 numbers and it still beats the previous model, text-embedding-ada-002, which used 1,536 numbers, on the standard MTEB benchmark.

A vector 6× smaller that works better. That is only possible because of how these models are trained (Matryoshka training, below), and it changes the bill for storing and searching millions of documents. The small sibling also dropped the price 5×, to $0.02 per million tokens.

In plain words: you send text, you get back a fixed-length list of numbers that describes its meaning. Two texts are related if their vectors point in nearly the same direction. “Find documents about X” becomes “find the vectors nearest to X's vector”, which is just arithmetic.

💡 Big idea An embedding model is trained so that texts with similar meaning land close together in a space with hundreds of dimensions. Once meaning is geometry, “related”, “duplicate”, “same topic” and “answers this question” all become “nearby”, and computers are very fast at nearby.
🧠 Analogy: GPS coordinates for meaning

Imagine a huge city where every sentence ever written has an address. All the sentences about refunds live in one neighbourhood, password problems in another, dogs and puppies in a park across town. “I want my money back” and “What's your refund policy?” share no words, but they live on the same street. An embedding model is the city's address service: give it a sentence, it gives you coordinates. Real models use hundreds of coordinates instead of two, so there is room for many kinds of “nearby” at once (topic, tone, language, intent).

How a model learns the map (just enough)

Modern text embedding models are transformers (Lesson 0.2) trained with contrastive learning: show the model millions of pairs that belong together (a question and its answer, a title and its article, two paraphrases) and pull their vectors together, while pushing random pairs apart. Microsoft's E5 models, for example, were trained this way on CCPairs, a large curated set of text pairs from sources such as Reddit, Stack Exchange and Wikipedia. After training, the model has never seen your documents, but it has learned which kinds of text tend to go together.

This also explains the limits. The model only knows the relationships in its training pairs. Your internal product codes, legal clauses or medical abbreviations may be in a part of the map it barely learned.

Measuring “close”: cosine, dot product, distance

✏️ Worked example: three measures, three different answers

Query a = (3, 4). Two documents: b = (4, 3) and c = (6, 8), which points exactly the same way as a but is twice as long.

  • Cosine: a·b = 12 + 12 = 24, |a| = |b| = 5, so cos(a, b) = 24 ÷ 25 = 0.96. cos(a, c) = 50 ÷ (5 × 10) = 1.00. Winner: c.
  • Dot product: a·b = 24, a·c = 50. Winner: c, by a lot, partly because c is longer.
  • Euclidean: |a − b| = √(1 + 1) = 1.41, |a − c| = √(9 + 16) = 5. Winner: b.

Now normalize every vector to length 1 (divide by its length). Then the dot product equals the cosine, and distance² = 2 − 2·cosine, so all three rank documents identically. OpenAI's docs say their embeddings are already normalized to length 1, so a plain dot product gives the cosine. The rule: use the measure the model card recommends, and if vectors are normalized, use the fastest one.

For the geometry behind this, see the Math for AI lessons on the dot product and cosine and length and distance.

This uses the course's tiny offline toy embedding: it knows a few dozen synonym groups (refund ≈ money back, puppy ≈ dog) and treats other words as weak hashed features. A real model knows far more words and nuance. The 2D map is a squashed view (like a flat map of the round Earth), so distances on screen are approximate; the neighbour list uses the full vectors.

What you should see: “I was billed by mistake” lands with the money sentences although it shares no word with “Why was I charged twice?”. “The weather is lovely today” has no strong neighbour, because our toy model knows none of those words. A real model would know them, but it would hit the same wall with your company's private jargon.

Where embeddings get it wrong

Embedding similarity is “these texts are about the same kind of thing”, not “these texts say the same thing”. Three classic traps, which you can cause yourself below:

  • Different words, same meaning: embeddings shine (this is why they beat keyword search for paraphrases).
  • Same words, different meaning: “get my money back” vs “get my password back” share most words but different intent. Good models separate them; keyword overlap can't.
  • Negation and small details: “I love this phone” vs “I do not love this phone”. Researchers who built the NevIR benchmark found most retrieval models, including state-of-the-art ones, rank such negation pairs no better than random. Embeddings also blur exact numbers, dates and codes (Lesson 2.4 fixes that with keyword search).

Dimensions, Matryoshka and the storage bill

More dimensions can hold more nuance, but every number costs memory, disk and search time, for every document, forever.

DimensionsBytes per vector (float32)10 million chunks
3,07212,288123 GB
1,5366,14461 GB
7683,07231 GB
2561,02410 GB

Matryoshka Representation Learning (named after Russian nesting dolls; Kusupati et al., 2022) trains the model so that the first numbers of the vector carry the coarse meaning and later numbers add detail. You can keep just the first 256 or 512 numbers and still search well. The paper reports up to 14× smaller embeddings at the same accuracy on an image task. In practice: OpenAI's v3 models take a dimensions parameter; Google's gemini-embedding-2 accepts 128 to 3,072 dimensions (768, 1,536 and 3,072 recommended); open models like nomic-embed-text-v1.5 support it through sentence-transformers' truncate_dim. If you cut a vector yourself, re-normalize it before using a dot product. Shrinking each number (8-bit or even 1-bit instead of 32-bit) is the other lever, covered in Lesson 2.2.

✏️ Worked example: the bill for 10 million chunks

10 million chunks × 500 tokens = 5 billion tokens to embed once.

  • text-embedding-3-small at $0.02 per million: 5,000 × $0.02 = $100.
  • text-embedding-3-large at $0.13 per million: $650. gemini-embedding-001 at $0.15: $750 (half that with batch).
  • Queries are tiny: 1 million searches a day × 20 tokens = 20 million tokens = $0.40 a day with the small model.
  • Storage at 1,536 floats: 61 GB; at 256 dims: 10 GB. That gap decides whether your index fits in one machine's RAM.

Embedding is usually the cheap part. Storage, the search index and re-embedding everything when you change models are the real costs. Prices are as of 2026; check the providers' pricing pages.

The usual scoreboard is MTEB, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (Muennighoff et al., 2022): originally 8 task types, 58 datasets and 112 languages, with a public leaderboard on Hugging Face and a multilingual extension (MMTEB) in 2025. Read it with care:

  • The original paper's headline finding was that no single model dominates every task. The overall average hides that; look at the retrieval columns if you're building search.
  • Benchmark text is not your text. Support tickets, code, contracts and non-English data can reorder the ranking.
  • Differences of a point or two rarely survive contact with your data. Build 50 to 200 real queries with known answers and measure recall on your corpus (Lesson 5.4). That beats any leaderboard.

Two gotchas that silently cost accuracy

  • Asymmetric prefixes. Many models expect queries and documents to be marked differently, because a short question and a long passage are different kinds of text. E5's model card says every input must start with query: or passage: ; gemini-embedding-001 takes task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY" or "RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT"; sentence-transformers has encode_query() and encode_document(), which add the model's prefixes for you. Forget them and nothing crashes; recall just quietly drops.
  • Vectors from different models don't mix. Each model has its own map. A query embedded with model B searched against documents embedded with model A returns noise. Changing models means re-embedding the whole corpus, so store the model name and version with every vector.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Hosted APIs: OpenAI, Google Gemini, Voyage AI, Cohere (all similar: send a list of texts, get a list of vectors). Open models: sentence-transformers (v6 as of late 2026) with models from the Hugging Face Hub; Ollama for local embedding endpoints. Benchmarks: the MTEB leaderboard and the mteb Python package to run it on your own models.

from openai import OpenAI
import numpy as np

client = OpenAI()
resp = client.embeddings.create(
    model="text-embedding-3-small",
    input=["How do I get my money back?", "Refunds are available within 30 days."],
    dimensions=512,                       # Matryoshka: ask for a shorter vector
)
q, d = (np.array(e.embedding) for e in resp.data)
print(q @ d / (np.linalg.norm(q) * np.linalg.norm(d)))   # cosine similarity

# Open model, local: separate query and document encoders handle the prefixes
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
model = SentenceTransformer("Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B")   # add truncate_dim=256 for Matryoshka models
qv = model.encode_query(["How do I get my money back?"])
dv = model.encode_document(["Refunds are available within 30 days.", "Shipping takes 3 to 5 days."])
print(model.similarity(qv, dv))         # cosine by default: higher = more related

Store, next to every vector: the model name and version, the dimensions, and the text that was embedded. You will need all three the day you upgrade models.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Pick a cheap, well-known hosted model (for example text-embedding-3-small or voyage-4-lite), keep 512 to 1,536 dims, store the model name with each vector, and write 50 test queries. Spend your time on chunking (Lesson 3.3) and hybrid search (2.4), not on leaderboard hunting.

At scale

Benchmark several models on internal eval sets per language and domain, often fine-tune an open model on their own click or ticket data, shrink vectors with Matryoshka and quantization to cut a cluster's RAM, and treat a model change as a migration: dual-write new vectors, backfill, compare, then switch.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you pick an embedding model for our product?” A senior answer:

  • Starts from constraints: languages, domain, data privacy, volume (cost to embed and store), latency.
  • Uses MTEB's retrieval scores to shortlist 2 or 3 models, then decides on an eval on our own queries (recall@10).
  • Mentions dimensions and storage (N × d × 4 bytes), Matryoshka truncation and quantization.
  • Flags the operational traps: query/document prefixes, normalization and metric, and that switching models means re-embedding everything.
🏭 In the real world
Shortenable embeddings in a mainstream API 🏢 OpenAI · 2024 New embedding models and API updates

How: text-embedding-3 models were trained so a dimensions parameter can shorten the vector; average MTEB score rose from 61.0% (ada-002) to 64.6% (3-large).

Why this method: customers pay for vector storage and search on every document; letting them trade a little accuracy for a much smaller vector, without retraining, matches how vector databases are actually billed.

One benchmark, many tasks 🧪 Research · 2022 MTEB: Massive Text Embedding Benchmark

How: evaluated 33 models on 8 task types (retrieval, classification, clustering, reranking, semantic similarity and more) across 58 datasets.

Why this method: models tuned for one task were being reused for others without evidence; the benchmark showed no single model wins everywhere, which is why you read the column for your task.

Query and passage prefixes 🧰 Open source E5 model card (Microsoft)

How: E5 models are trained with query: and passage: prefixes, and the card tells you to add them at inference time too.

Why this method: questions and answers are different kinds of text; marking which is which lets one model embed both sides of an asymmetric search well.

Embeddings ignore “not” 🧪 Research · 2023 NevIR: Negation in Neural Information Retrieval

How: asked retrieval models to rank two documents that differ only by a negation.

Why this method: most models, including state-of-the-art ones, did no better than random; cross-encoder rerankers (Lesson 3.4) did best. It's the evidence behind “embeddings find the topic; something else must check the details”.

🤯 Fun fact · king − man + woman

The 2013 word2vec paper from Google (Mikolov et al.) reported that with its word vectors, vector(“King”) − vector(“Man”) + vector(“Woman”) lands closest to the vector for “Queen”. Directions in the space had picked up relationships like gender and royalty with nobody programming them. Today's sentence embeddings are the same idea, scaled from words to whole passages.

Check yourself

1. You store 2 million chunks as 1,024-dimension float32 vectors. Roughly how much memory do the raw vectors take?
2,000,000 × 1,024 numbers × 4 bytes = 8,192,000,000 bytes ≈ 8.2 GB, before any index overhead. Truncating to 256 dims would make it about 2 GB.
2. To save money, a teammate switches the query embedding to a cheaper model but leaves the stored document vectors as they were. Search results turn to nonsense. Why?
Even two models with the same number of dimensions put meanings in completely different places. Vectors are only comparable within one model (and one prefix convention). Store the model version with each vector and plan model changes as a full re-embed.
3. The top model on the MTEB leaderboard does worse than your old model on your legal-contract search. What's the best next step?
MTEB averages many public tasks; it says little about your domain. A small in-domain eval decides it. Domain models (Voyage sells law, finance and code models, for example) or fine-tuning an open model often win on specialist text.
📝 TL;DR
  • An embedding is a fixed-length vector for a text; similar meanings get nearby vectors, even with no shared words.
  • Cosine, dot product and distance agree when vectors are normalized; use what the model card says.
  • Cost is mostly storage and re-embedding: N × d × 4 bytes. Matryoshka models let you keep fewer dimensions.
  • Pick models with your own eval, not the leaderboard average; mind query/document prefixes; never mix models in one index.
  • Embeddings find the topic but blur negation, numbers and exact codes: pair them with keyword search and rerankers.
