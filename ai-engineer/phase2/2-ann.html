Phase 2 · Lesson 2

Similarity search & ANN indexes: HNSW, IVF, PQ

Comparing a query with every vector works for thousands of documents and collapses at millions. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes look at a tiny fraction of the vectors and still find almost all the right ones.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An embedding is a vector for a text; similar meanings are close (Lesson 2.1).
  • Closeness is measured with cosine similarity, dot product or Euclidean distance; for normalized vectors they rank the same (2.1).
  • A database index trades extra memory and build time for fast lookups (like a B-tree for WHERE id = 42).
🎯 Hook

Spotify built and open-sourced its nearest-neighbour library Annoy in 2013, and it powered features like Discover Weekly for a decade. By 2023 Spotify's engineers wrote that newer methods could give results twice as accurate in the same time, or similar quality in one-tenth the time. So they built Voyager, based on the HNSW algorithm you'll meet here: more than 10× faster than Annoy at the same recall, with up to 4× less memory, and queried hundreds of millions of times a day.

Same vectors, same question (“which songs are near this one?”). The only thing that changed was the index.

Numbers first: why exact search breaks

Exact (“brute-force”, or flat) search compares the query with every vector: N × d multiply-adds per query.

Vectors (d = 1,536)Multiply-adds per queryRaw vector memory (float32)
10,000 (a product's docs)15 million: trivial, milliseconds61 MB
1 million1.5 billion6.1 GB
10 million15 billion per query, times every query per second61 GB
1 billion (web scale)1.5 trillion6.1 TB

Up to about 100,000 vectors, exact search is fine and gives perfect answers: don't add an index you don't need. Beyond that, we accept answers that are almost always the true nearest neighbours in exchange for doing 100× to 1,000× less work. That is ANN, approximate nearest neighbour search.

We measure “almost” with recall@k: of the true k nearest neighbours, what share did the index return? Recall@10 = 0.95 means 9.5 of the real top 10, on average. Every ANN index is a dial between recall, latency and memory.

💡 Big idea You don't need to look at every vector to find the closest ones. Organize the vectors once (a graph of neighbours, or clusters), then at query time only visit the promising part. Compress them (quantization) and they fit in RAM. Then tune one knob per index to buy recall with latency.
🧠 Analogy: finding a house without a full map

HNSW is asking for directions: take the motorway to the right region (top layer, few long hops), then main roads, then walk door to door asking “is anyone closer to the address?” (bottom layer). IVF is a postcode system: first find the few postcodes nearest the address, then check every house in just those postcodes. PQ is describing each house with a short code (“red roof, two floors, corner plot”) instead of a full photo, so the whole city fits in one notebook.

HNSW: a graph you walk downhill

HNSW, Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs (Malkov and Yashunin, 2016), is the most widely used ANN index today. Every vector is a node linked to a handful of near neighbours. A few nodes are also copied into sparser upper layers that have longer links, like express lanes. The paper notes the structure resembles a skip list.

  • Search: start at the top layer's entry point, greedily hop to whichever neighbour is closer to the query, drop a layer when stuck, and repeat. On the bottom layer, keep a candidate list of size efSearch instead of just one best node, so the walk can step around dead ends.
  • M: links per node (typical 16; pgvector's default is 16). More links = better recall and robustness, more memory, slower builds.
  • efConstruction: candidate list size while building (pgvector default 64, hnswlib 200). Higher = better graph, slower build.
  • efSearch: candidate list size at query time (pgvector default 40). The knob you tune live: higher = better recall, more distance computations.

Raise efSearch and recall climbs toward 100% while the work grows, still far below 500. IVF: search only the nearest clusters

IVF, the inverted file index, runs k-means clustering (Lesson 2.5) to split the vectors into nlist clusters, each with a centre (centroid). At query time it compares the query with the centroids, then scans every vector in the nprobe nearest clusters. The trap: a true neighbour sitting just across a cluster border is missed when nprobe = 1. pgvector's IVFFlat guidance is a good start: nlist ≈ rows ÷ 1,000 up to 1 million rows (√rows above that), and nprobe ≈ √nlist.

PQ and quantization: shrink the vectors

Quantization stores each vector with fewer bits.

  • Scalar quantization (int8): each float32 becomes one byte: 4× smaller, small recall loss.
  • Binary quantization: keep only the sign of each number, 1 bit: 32× smaller. Hugging Face measured about 92.5% of retrieval quality kept, and about 96% when the top candidates are rescored with the full-precision query.
  • PQ, product quantization (Jégou, Douze and Schmid, 2011): cut the vector into m sub-vectors, run k-means on each part with 256 centroids, and store just the centroid number (1 byte) per part. A 1,536-dim vector with m = 96 becomes 96 bytes instead of 6,144: 64× smaller. Distances are approximate, so systems fetch extra candidates and rescore them.

The classic billion-scale recipe combines them: IVF + PQ (Faiss's IndexIVFPQ), or HNSW over quantized vectors. Disk-based graph indexes such as Microsoft's DiskANN keep compressed vectors in RAM and the full vectors and graph on SSD: its 2019 paper served a billion vectors from one machine with 64 GB of RAM at under 3 ms mean latency and 95%+ recall@1.

✏️ Worked example: will 10 million vectors fit on one machine?

10 million chunks, 1,536 dimensions.

  • Raw float32: 10,000,000 × 1,536 × 4 bytes = 61.4 GB.
  • HNSW links with M = 16: about 2 × 16 links × 4 bytes = 128 bytes per vector on the bottom layer → 1.3 GB more (a rough estimate; implementations differ).
  • int8: 15.4 GB. Binary: 1.9 GB. PQ with 96 bytes: 0.96 GB.
  • Or truncate to 512 Matryoshka dims first (Lesson 2.1): float32 20.5 GB, int8 5.1 GB.

A 64 GB server can't hold the raw HNSW index; it holds the int8 or PQ version easily. That's usually the real reason teams quantize: RAM, not speed.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Faiss (Meta's library: flat, IVF, PQ, HNSW, GPU), hnswlib, Spotify's Voyager, Google's ScaNN. Most of the time you'll set these knobs inside a vector database instead (Lesson 2.3): pgvector's m, ef_construction and hnsw.ef_search, or Qdrant's hnsw_ef.

import faiss, numpy as np

d = 1536
xb = np.random.rand(100_000, d).astype("float32")   # your document vectors
xq = np.random.rand(5, d).astype("float32")         # queries
faiss.normalize_L2(xb); faiss.normalize_L2(xq)            # unit vectors: inner product = cosine

flat = faiss.IndexFlatIP(d); flat.add(xb)                  # exact baseline, for measuring recall
D_true, I_true = flat.search(xq, 10)

hnsw = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(d, 16, faiss.METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT)   # M = 16
hnsw.hnsw.efConstruction = 200
hnsw.add(xb)
hnsw.hnsw.efSearch = 64                                   # tune live: recall vs latency
D, I = hnsw.search(xq, 10)

recall = np.mean([len(set(I[i]) & set(I_true[i])) / 10 for i in range(len(xq))])
print(f"recall@10 = {recall:.2f}")

# Memory-bound? IVF + PQ: 1,024 clusters, 96 bytes per vector
ivfpq = faiss.IndexIVFPQ(faiss.IndexFlatIP(d), d, 1024, 96, 8, faiss.METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT)
ivfpq.train(xb); ivfpq.add(xb); ivfpq.nprobe = 16

Always keep an exact (flat) baseline on a sample to measure recall. An index with untested recall is a guess. The ANN-Benchmarks site compares libraries on public datasets.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Under a few hundred thousand chunks, use exact search or the database's default HNSW and move on. Measure recall@10 against flat search on 100 queries once, so you know the index isn't the problem when answers go wrong.

At scale

Billions of vectors, memory budgets per shard, quantization with rescoring, GPU index builds, recall SLOs (for example recall@10 ≥ 0.95 at p99 under 50 ms) monitored like latency, and index rebuilds scheduled because deletes and drift slowly degrade the graph or the clusters.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design similarity search over 500 million embeddings.” A senior answer:

  • Does the memory math first (500M × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 1.5 TB raw) and concludes: shard, quantize, or both.
  • Picks an index for the access pattern: HNSW for low latency and steady inserts, IVF-PQ or a disk graph when memory-bound, rescoring with full vectors on the top few hundred.
  • Defines success as recall@k at a latency target, measured against exact search on a sample, and says which knob (efSearch, nprobe) trades one for the other.
  • Mentions filters, deletes and rebuilds as the operational pain (Lesson 2.3).
🏭 In the real world
The graph index everyone uses 🧪 Research · 2016 HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin)

How: a multi-layer graph where each element's top layer is chosen at random with exponentially decaying probability; search starts at the top and descends.

Why this method: separating long and short links gives logarithmic-style scaling, and a neighbour-selection heuristic keeps recall high on clustered data. It strongly beat previous open-source methods.

A billion vectors on four GPUs 🏢 Meta (Facebook AI) · 2017 Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs

How: GPU brute-force, IVF and product-quantized search with a fast k-selection kernel; the code became Faiss.

Why this method: compressed-domain search (PQ) on GPUs built a k-NN graph over 1 billion vectors in under 12 hours on 4 GPUs, 8.5× faster than the previous GPU state of the art.

From Annoy to HNSW for music 🏢 Spotify · 2023 Introducing Voyager

How: an in-memory HNSW library (built on hnswlib) with 8-bit floating-point storage, Python and Java bindings.

Why this method: in-memory indexes keep services stateless on Kubernetes; HNSW gave 10× speed at equal recall, and 8-bit storage cut memory up to 4×.

Quantize for what matters 🏢 Google · 2020 Announcing ScaNN

How: anisotropic quantization penalizes compression error that distorts large inner products (the top results) more than other error.

Why this method: search only cares about the best matches being scored right; on the glove-100-angular benchmark ScaNN handled roughly twice the queries per second of the next-fastest library at the same accuracy.

🤯 Fun fact · in high dimensions, everything is far

Pick two random directions in 2D and their cosine is all over the place. In 1,536 dimensions, random vectors are almost exactly perpendicular: their cosine has a spread of only about 1/√1,536 ≈ 0.026. That's why a cosine of 0.3 between real embeddings can be meaningful, why scores from different models can't be compared, and why ANN is hard: in high dimensions, “nearest” and “second nearest” are often nearly the same distance away.

Check yourself

1. 50 million vectors of 768 dimensions, compressed with PQ to 64 bytes each. About how much RAM do the codes need, compared with float32?
float32: 50M × 768 × 4 = 153.6 GB. PQ: 50M × 64 bytes = 3.2 GB, 48× smaller. You'd typically keep full vectors on disk to rescore the top candidates.
2. Your HNSW index has recall@10 of 0.82 against exact search, and latency has headroom. What's the first knob to turn?
efSearch is the query-time knob: a larger candidate list explores more of the graph, raising recall at some latency cost, with no rebuild. If recall plateaus, then rebuild with larger M or efConstruction.
3. An IVF index with nprobe = 1 often misses a document that's clearly relevant. What's the most likely cause?
IVF only scans the clusters whose centroids are nearest the query. Vectors near cluster borders fall into the next cell. Raising nprobe (a few to tens of clusters) fixes it at the cost of scanning more vectors.
📝 TL;DR
  • Exact search costs N × d per query; fine under ~100k vectors, too slow at millions.
  • ANN indexes trade a little recall for 100× to 1,000× less work: HNSW (graph), IVF (clusters), PQ and quantization (compression).
  • Knobs: efSearch and nprobe buy recall with latency; M and quantization trade memory for recall.
  • Do the memory math (N × d × 4 bytes) first: RAM usually decides the design.
  • Measure recall@k against exact search on a sample; never assume it.
