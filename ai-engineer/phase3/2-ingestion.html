Phase 3 · Lesson 2

Ingestion: turning messy documents into clean, labelled text

Every later stage of RAG only ever sees the text your parser produced. Parse a page badly and the answer is gone before search even starts.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The RAG pipeline: at index time you parse, chunk, embed and store documents; at query time you retrieve chunks and generate (Lesson 3.1).
  • Tokens: about 4 characters of English each; every token you index or send costs something (Lesson 0.3).
  • Vector databases store each vector with a small record of fields you can filter on (Lesson 2.3).
🎯 Hook

LinkedIn's customer-service team built RAG over years of past support tickets. The usual approach treats every ticket as plain text and cuts it into chunks, which throws away the ticket's structure: which part is the problem, which is the fix, which tickets are related.

Their 2024 version kept that structure during ingestion (as a knowledge graph, Lesson 4.3) instead of flattening it. Retrieval quality (MRR, a ranking score from Lesson 5.4) rose by 77.6% over the baseline, and after about six months in use by the support team, median time to resolve an issue had fallen by 28.6%. Same model family, same questions. What changed was what went into the index.

Numbers first: where your knowledge actually lives

Adobe estimates there are about 3 trillion PDFs in the world. Inside a company, knowledge sits in PDFs, web pages and wikis, Word files, slide decks, spreadsheets, scanned forms and email threads. None of these is “text”. Each must be parsed (turned into text with its structure), cleaned, labelled with metadata, and deduplicated before it's worth chunking.

PDF is the hardest, because it was designed for printing, not reading. A PDF page is a list of instructions like “draw these glyphs at these coordinates”. There are no paragraphs, no reading order and no table cells in the file, only characters at positions. A parser has to reconstruct all of that.

💡 Big idea Garbage in, garbage retrieved. The embedding model, the reranker and the LLM never see your document, only the text your ingestion produced. Look at the parsed output of a sample of every source type before you tune anything else.
🧠 Analogy: packing for a house move

Chunks are moving boxes. Ingestion is the packing: you sort the contents of each room (parse), throw away the junk mail instead of boxing it (clean), write “kitchen · glasses · fragile” on every box (metadata), and don't pack the same set of plates three times (dedup). Skip the labels and you'll open twenty boxes to find one glass. Pack the junk and every box is a little heavier and a little harder to search.

✏️ Worked example: what ingestion costs

Boilerplate. A 200-page manual, about 500 tokens of real text per page, a 12-token header and a 10-token footer on every page:

  • Noise: (12 + 10) × 200 = 4,400 tokens, about 4.4% of the 100,000 tokens of content.
  • Cut into 400-token chunks, that's 250 chunks, and nearly every one contains “Confidential · Page N of 200”. Search for “confidential” and every chunk matches. Strip it once, at ingestion.

Time. IBM's Docling report measured roughly 1 to 2.5 pages per second on a 16-core laptop CPU with OCR off, and OCR on CPU (with EasyOCR) at upwards of 30 seconds per page.

  • 100,000 digital pages at 2 pages/s: 100,000 ÷ 2 = 50,000 s ≈ 14 hours on one machine.
  • If 10,000 of them are scans: 10,000 × 30 s = 300,000 s ≈ 83 hours. OCR dominates, so detect which pages need it, run them on GPUs or in parallel, and never re-parse unchanged files.

Compare embedding the result: 50 million tokens at an illustrative $0.02 per million (OpenAI's listed price for text-embedding-3-small) is about $1. Parsing, not embedding, is usually the slow and fiddly part of indexing.

Cleaning: small fixes, big effects

  • Normalise text: Unicode normalisation (so “ﬁ” becomes “fi”), join words hyphenated at line ends, collapse stray whitespace.
  • Remove page furniture: lines that repeat on most pages (headers, footers, page numbers) are easy to detect by counting.
  • Strip boilerplate: navigation, cookie banners, legal footers, quoted email replies, signatures.
  • Keep structure as Markdown: headings (#, ##), lists and tables survive as plain text and give the chunker natural cut points (Lesson 3.3).
  • Redact what shouldn't be searchable: personal data, secrets, keys (Lesson 8.3). Anything in the index can end up in an answer.

Deduplication

The same paragraph often exists many times: the FAQ page, the help widget, the PDF version, last year's edition, an email template. Duplicates cost storage, but the real damage is at query time: if five copies of one paragraph fill your top 5, the model sees one fact five times and nothing else (Lesson 3.4 fixes this at retrieval with MMR). Remove exact duplicates with a hash of the normalised text, and near-duplicates with MinHash (a fast way to estimate how many word sequences two documents share) or embedding similarity above a high threshold, such as 0.95 (Lesson 2.5). Keep the newest or most authoritative copy, and record where the others lived.

Metadata: the labels that make filters possible

Store each chunk with a small record: where it came from and what it's about. Some fields help the model cite (source, title, section, URL, page). Others let you filter before or during search, which similarity alone can never do: date or version, product, region, language, and access control (who may see it, often called an ACL: access-control list). Metadata is cheap to store at ingestion and painful to add later, because adding a field means re-processing everything.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Docling (IBM, MIT licence: layout analysis, reading order, table structure, optional OCR; PDF, Office, HTML, images), Unstructured (splits files into typed elements: Title, NarrativeText, ListItem, Table…), PyMuPDF (fast, with Markdown output, table finding and OCR hooks), Apache Tika (a Java server that extracts text and metadata from over a thousand file types), and hosted parsers such as LlamaParse or cloud document-AI services when you'd rather pay per page than run models. For HTML, main-content extractors such as trafilatura.

def fingerprint(text):

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

One parser for everything (Docling or Unstructured), output as Markdown, metadata of source, title, section, URL and date. Eyeball 20 parsed pages from each source type and fix the worst problems. Hash for exact duplicates. Re-parse only files whose hash changed.

At scale

A connector per source system (wiki, drive, ticketing, CRM) that streams changes and deletions, permissions synced from the identity system, per-format parsers routed by file type, OCR only on pages without a text layer, near-duplicate detection, and parse-quality checks that alert when a source's output suddenly shrinks. Lesson 4.8 builds this pipeline.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our RAG over 50,000 internal PDFs gives poor answers. Where do you start?” A senior answer:

  • Looks at the parsed text for a sample of failing questions before touching the model: is the answer even in the index, and is it readable?
  • Splits the corpus by type: digital vs scanned PDFs, tables, slides, and picks a parser per type (layout-aware, OCR where there's no text layer).
  • Designs metadata for the filters the product needs: dates and versions, product, region, and permissions enforced at retrieval.
  • Adds dedup and incremental re-indexing, and measures with a labelled question set (Lesson 5.4).
🏭 In the real world
Keeping structure at ingestion 🏢 LinkedIn · 2024 RAG with Knowledge Graphs for Customer Service Question Answering

How: parsed historical support tickets into a graph that keeps each ticket's sections and the links between related tickets, instead of flat text chunks.

Why this method: flat chunking cut tickets apart; the structured version beat the baseline by 77.6% in MRR and cut median resolution time by 28.6% in production.

An open-source layout-aware parser 🧰 Open source Docling Technical Report (IBM, 2024)

How: page images go through a layout model (trained on DocLayNet) and a table-structure model (TableFormer), then reading order and structure are reassembled into one document.

Why this method: runs on ordinary hardware, keeps data in-house and outputs Markdown or JSON ready for chunking.

Benchmarking parsers fairly 🧪 Research · 2024 OmniDocBench

How: a benchmark of annotated pages from nine kinds of source (papers, textbooks, newspapers, handwritten notes…), scoring pipeline tools and end-to-end vision-language models.

Why this method: parsers differ a lot by document type, so test on documents like yours rather than trusting one leaderboard number.

Typed elements, not one string 🧰 Open source Unstructured: partitioning

How: partitions files into elements such as Title, NarrativeText and ListItem, with strategies from “fast” to “hi_res” (the one that extracts PDF tables).

Why this method: typed elements let you drop headers and footers, keep tables whole and use titles as chunk boundaries.

🤯 Fun fact · the parser that parses itself

Docling's quick-start example converts the Docling technical report, straight from its arXiv URL, and prints the Markdown. The first line of output is the paper's own title. It's a neat test: a parsing paper is full of the things parsers find hard: two columns, tables, figures and footnotes.

Check yourself

1. You must parse 40,000 scanned pages with OCR at about 30 seconds per page on CPU. Roughly how long on one machine?
40,000 × 30 s = 1,200,000 s ÷ 3,600 ≈ 333 hours ≈ 14 days. That's why you run OCR only where there's no text layer, in parallel or on GPUs, and cache results so unchanged files are never re-parsed.
2. Your HR assistant serves all employees. The index contains salary bands that only managers may see. What's the right protection?
Anything that reaches the prompt can leak, through a clever question or a prompt injection. Permission filters at retrieval mean restricted chunks are never retrieved for the wrong user in the first place.
3. Questions about a price table in your PDFs keep getting wrong answers, though the numbers appear in the retrieved chunks. Most likely cause?
“Boots 1 year 60 days” is ambiguous without the header row. Parse tables with a table-structure model into Markdown or HTML, or write one self-describing sentence per row (“Boots: warranty 1 year, return window 60 days”).
📝 TL;DR
  • PDFs are glyphs at coordinates: reading order, columns and tables must be reconstructed. Scans need OCR, or the text simply isn't there.
  • Use a layout-aware parser, output Markdown, strip page furniture and boilerplate, and look at the output before tuning anything downstream.
  • Deduplicate (hash for exact, MinHash or embeddings for near) so copies don't crowd out other facts.
  • Store metadata at ingestion: source, title, section, date, product, region and permissions. Filters can't use a field you never stored.
