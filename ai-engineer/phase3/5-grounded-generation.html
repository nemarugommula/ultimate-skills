Grounded generation: citations, abstaining & context packing

Retrieval put the right chunks in front of the model. Now the prompt has to make it answer from them, say where each claim came from, and admit when they don't contain the answer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Prompting basics: clear instructions, XML-style tags to separate parts of a prompt (Lesson 1.1).
  • Context engineering: what goes in the context window, in what order, and why the middle gets less attention (Lesson 1.4).
  • Retrieval and reranking produce a short list of chunks with scores (Lesson 3.4).
🎯 Hook

Legal research vendors built RAG products and marketed them as “hallucination-free” or as “avoiding” hallucinations. In 2024 a Stanford team ran the first pre-registered evaluation of them: hundreds of real legal questions, every answer checked by hand.

The tools did hallucinate less than a general chatbot. But Lexis+ AI and Thomson Reuters' Westlaw AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI each still produced hallucinated answers between 17% and 33% of the time, often with citations that looked authoritative. Retrieval is necessary. It isn't sufficient: generation has to be grounded too, and checked.

Numbers first: three ways good context still fails

FailureEvidence
The model answers even when the context doesn't contain the answerGoogle researchers (2024) found that strong models (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) answered well when the context was sufficient, but often gave incorrect answers instead of abstaining when it wasn't.
The answer is in the context, but in the middle“Lost in the Middle” (2023): with the relevant passage in the middle of 20 documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo did worse than with no documents at all (56.1% closed-book).
The prompt is ordered badlyAnthropic reports that putting long documents first and the question at the end can improve response quality by up to 30% in their tests, especially with many documents.

So grounded generation is a set of habits, each of which fixes one of these: give every chunk an ID, put the documents first and the question last, instruct the model to answer only from the documents, cite IDs, say “I don't know” when they don't cover it, and resolve conflicts by date or authority. Then check the answer against the chunks.

💡 Big idea A model will always produce an answer. Your prompt decides whether “the documents don't say” is an allowed answer, and your citations decide whether a human (or a checker) can verify the rest. Grounded = every claim traceable to a chunk, and silence where there is none.
🧠 Analogy: a careful journalist

A good reporter attributes every claim to a source (“according to the 2026 handbook…”), says “we couldn't confirm this” rather than inventing a quote, and when two sources disagree, reports both and says which is more recent or more official. An editor then checks each sentence against the notes. Citations, abstaining, conflict handling and faithfulness checks are exactly that newsroom discipline, applied to an LLM.

The RAG prompt template

Almost every production RAG prompt has the same four parts. Here they are in order, with the reason for each line:

SYSTEM: You answer questions for Acme Gear customers using ONLY the documents provided. - Cite the id of every document you use, like [c1]. Each sentence needs a citation. ← traceability - If the documents don't contain the answer, say you don't know and suggest contacting support. ← abstaining - If documents disagree, prefer the most recent one and mention the conflict. ← conflicts - Answer in at most 3 sentences. ← format <documents> ← documents first <document id="c1" title="Customer handbook" updated="2026-01-15"> Refunds are available within 30 days of delivery. Plus members get 60 days. </document> <document id="c2" title="Plus FAQ (archived)" updated="2023-05-02"> Plus members can request a refund within 45 days of delivery. </document> </documents> Question: How long do Plus members have to ask for a refund? ← question last

The IDs and dates come from the metadata you stored at ingestion (Lesson 3.2). Without them, the model can neither cite nor resolve the conflict between c1 and c2.

Citations

  • ID citations in the prompt (above): cheap and universal. Validate after generation: every cited ID must exist in the context, and every sentence should cite one.
  • Quote first, then answer: ask the model to copy the relevant sentences into <quotes> tags, then answer from them. Anthropic recommends this for long documents; it focuses the model, and quotes are easy to check by string matching.
  • API-native citations: Anthropic's Messages API accepts documents with "citations": {"enabled": true} and returns each claim with the exact cited_text and its location, guaranteed to point into the documents you sent. OpenAI's and Google's file-search and grounding tools return source annotations in a similar spirit.

Abstaining, conflicts, order and compression

  • Abstain on two signals. In the prompt (“if the documents don't contain the answer, say so”), and before the prompt: if the reranker's top score is below a threshold you tuned, don't call the model at all (Lesson 3.4). Show the user what to do next (contact support, rephrase).
  • Conflicts. Give the model what it needs to decide: dates, document type (policy vs blog vs ticket), version. Tell it the rule (newest wins, official beats informal) and to mention the conflict. Better still, stop superseded documents reaching the index or filter them out (Lesson 3.2).
  • Order. Documents first, question last. Put the best chunk first (or last, nearest the question), not in the middle. With few chunks, order matters less; with 20 it matters a lot.
  • Compression. Fewer, better tokens. Keep only the relevant sentences of each chunk (a cheap model or the reranker's relevant spans can pick them), drop near-duplicates, and cap the context at a token budget. Less noise often means better answers, not just cheaper ones.
✏️ Worked example: the token budget of one grounded answer
  • System instructions: 150 tokens. Five chunks × (400 tokens of text + 20 of ID, title and date) = 2,100. Question: 30. Total input: 150 + 2,100 + 30 = 2,280 tokens.
  • Output: a 3-sentence cited answer ≈ 200 tokens.
  • At illustrative prices of $3 per million input and $15 per million output tokens: 2,280 × 3 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.00684, plus 200 × 15 ÷ 10⁶ = $0.003, ≈ $0.0098 per answer; at 100,000 questions a day, ≈ $984 a day.
  • Compress each chunk to its 150 most relevant tokens and input falls to 150 + 5 × 170 + 30 = 1,030 tokens: the input bill drops by more than half.

Faithfulness, by hand: break an answer into claims and count those supported by the cited chunks. 5 claims, 2 supported → faithfulness = 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4. That's the metric RAG evaluation tools such as RAGAS automate with an LLM judge (Lesson 5.4).

A rule-based stand-in for a real checker: a sentence passes when its numbers appear in the cited chunk, nearly all its content words do, and it doesn't drop or add a “not”. Real systems use an NLI model (natural language inference: does passage A entail sentence B?) or an LLM judge, which also handles paraphrases this toy misses.

Each has a citation, so a reader would trust it. A checker like this, run on every answer, turns those into flags, retries or removed sentences.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

The four-part template above, chunk IDs with titles and dates, an abstain instruction, and a regex that rejects answers citing IDs that weren't in the context. Show citations as links in the UI. Read 50 answers yourself.

At scale

Native citations or quote extraction, a faithfulness judge on a sample of live traffic, abstention tuned on reranker scores, authority and freshness rules from metadata, per-domain templates versioned like code (Lesson 1.5), and dashboards for abstain rate and unsupported-claim rate.

🎤 Interview angle

“The answers cite sources, but users found citations that don't support the claim. What do you do?” A senior answer:

  • Separates the cases: wrong chunk retrieved (retrieval), right chunk but claim not in it (generation), conflicting chunks (data).
  • Tightens the prompt: answer only from documents, quote-then-answer, abstain, conflict rule, documents first and question last.
  • Adds verification: citation IDs must exist; an NLI or LLM-judge check per sentence; regenerate or drop unsupported sentences.
  • Measures faithfulness and citation precision on a labelled set and on sampled production traffic (Lesson 5.4).

RAG isn't hallucination-free 🧪 Research · 2024 Magesh et al., Hallucination-Free? Assessing AI Legal Research Tools

How: a pre-registered evaluation of commercial RAG-based legal research tools, with every answer graded for correctness and grounding.

Why it matters: despite retrieval, each tool hallucinated 17% to 33% of the time; grounding and verification are separate engineering problems.

Native citations 🏢 Anthropic · 2025 Introducing Citations on the Anthropic API

How: documents are split into sentences, and the model's answer comes back with pointers to the exact passages it used.

Why this method: customer Endex reported source hallucinations and formatting issues falling from 10% to 0%, with 20% more references per response.

Sufficient context 🧪 Research · 2024 Joren et al. (Google), Sufficient Context: A New Lens on RAG

How: classified each retrieved context as sufficient or not to answer the question, then studied model behaviour in each case.

Why it matters: large models rarely abstain when context is insufficient; the authors use a sufficiency signal to decide when to answer at all.

Position matters 🧪 Research · 2023 Liu et al., Lost in the Middle

How: moved the passage that answers a question through 10, 20 and 30-document contexts.

Why it matters: a U-shaped curve: best at the start or end, worst in the middle. Put your best chunks at the edges and keep k modest.

🤯 Fun fact · citations that cost nothing

With Anthropic's Citations, the quoted cited_text in the response doesn't count toward output tokens, and it isn't counted as input when you send it back in later turns. Internally the model emits compact pointers, and the API expands them into the quoted text for you.

Check yourself

1. A checker splits an answer into 8 claims; 6 are supported by the cited chunks. What's the faithfulness score?
Supported ÷ total = 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. One in four claims has no support in the context: high enough to show users a warning, retry, or drop those sentences.
2. The 2026 policy says 30 days; a 2022 blog post in the index says 14 days. Users sometimes get “14 days”. Best combination of fixes?
The model can't resolve a conflict it can't see the basis for. Metadata in the prompt plus a stated rule fixes generation; filtering superseded documents fixes the source of the conflict.
3. Users ask things your documents don't cover, and the assistant answers anyway with plausible details. What should you add first?
Without permission to abstain, models fill gaps with plausible text, and larger models are often more confident, not more cautious. Abstain in the prompt and before the prompt.
📝 TL;DR
  • Template: instructions → documents with IDs, titles and dates → question last. Answer only from the documents, cite IDs, abstain, resolve conflicts by date or authority.
  • Abstain twice: in the prompt, and before it when the reranker's best score is low.
  • Put the best chunks at the edges, keep k modest, compress to the relevant sentences.
  • Verify: cited IDs must exist and each claim must be supported (faithfulness). Citations can be hallucinated too.
