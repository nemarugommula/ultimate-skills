Chunking strategies: the decision that makes or breaks RAG

Before a document can be searched, it's cut into pieces. Cut badly and the right answer is never found, however good your model is.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Embeddings turn a piece of text into a vector, and similar meanings land close together (Lesson 2.1).
  • RAG = retrieve the most relevant pieces of your documents, put them in the prompt, then generate (Lesson 3.1).
  • Tokens: about 4 characters of English each; you pay for every token you send (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

Anthropic's retrieval team describes a chunk from a financial filing that says: “The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter.” Someone asks about ACME Corp's revenue growth in Q2 2023. That chunk holds the answer, but on its own it never says which company or which quarter, so search struggles to find it and the model can't trust it.

Their fix was to change how chunks are made: put a short line of document context in front of each one before indexing it. Together with keyword search and reranking, that cut failed retrievals from 5.7% to 1.9%. No new model needed, just better pieces.

Prices are illustrative; check your provider's pricing page. Small, stable knowledge bases are the exception: Anthropic's own advice is that under about 200,000 tokens you can often skip retrieval and put everything in the prompt, especially with prompt caching (Lesson 1.4).

So we split documents into chunks, embed each chunk, and at question time retrieve only the few that match. The catch: the answer has to be inside a chunk that gets retrieved, and that chunk has to make sense on its own.

💡 Big idea A chunk is the unit your system can find and the unit the model can read. Good chunks are small enough to match a question precisely, but big and self-contained enough to answer it. Every chunking strategy is a different way to balance those two.
🧠 Analogy: cutting a cookbook into index cards

You're turning a cookbook into index cards so you can grab the right one fast. Cut every 100 words, and one card ends mid-recipe: “…then bake for 40 minutes.” Bake what? Cut one card per recipe, and each card is complete, though a long recipe makes a bulky card. Write the recipe's name at the top of every card, and even a small card makes sense on its own. Those three choices are fixed-size chunking, structure-aware chunking, and contextual chunking.

The strategies

StrategyHow it cutsGood forWatch out for
Fixed-size (+ overlap)Every N tokens or characters, optionally repeating the last M at the start of the next chunk.A quick baseline; uniform text.Cuts mid-sentence; the pronoun problem (“this window…”).
Sentence-basedPacks whole sentences until the size limit.Prose, FAQs, support articles.Facts that span two sentences can still be split.
RecursiveTries the biggest natural break first (blank line), then line, then sentence, then word, until pieces fit.The usual default (LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter).Needs a sensible size; defaults may be poor.
Structure-awareSplits on the document's own structure: Markdown headers, HTML sections, PDF pages, code functions.Docs with headings, manuals, code, slides.Sections can be huge; combine with a size cap.
SemanticEmbeds each sentence and cuts where the meaning shifts (similarity between neighbours drops).Long unstructured text that changes topic.Embedding cost at indexing time; threshold tuning.
ContextualAny of the above, then adds a short description (doc title, section, or an LLM-written summary line) to each chunk before embedding.Chunks that depend on context: filings, contracts, handbooks.An LLM call per chunk at indexing time (cheap with caching).
Late chunkingEmbeds the whole document with a long-context embedding model first, then pools the token vectors per chunk, so each chunk “remembers” its surroundings.When chunks rely on earlier context.Needs a long-context embedding model that exposes token vectors.

Two more you'll meet in Lesson 4.2: small-to-big (search small chunks, but hand the model the bigger parent section) and proposition chunking (rewrite text into standalone facts).

✏️ Worked example: what overlap costs

A 10,000-token document, chunk size 500 tokens.

  • No overlap: 10,000 ÷ 500 = 20 chunks.
  • Overlap 100 tokens: each new chunk starts 400 tokens after the last. Chunks = ⌈(10,000 − 500) ÷ 400⌉ + 1 = ⌈23.75⌉ + 1 = 25 chunks.
  • That's 25% more chunks to embed and store, and some search results will be near-duplicates of each other.

Overlap buys you a safety margin when a fact straddles a cut. It isn't free, and it isn't magic: if the chunks are tiny, overlap just makes more tiny chunks.

Chunk size is a trade-off you can measure

Small chunks match a question precisely but may not hold the whole answer. Big chunks hold the answer but dilute the match, and every retrieved chunk costs tokens. The only honest way to pick is to measure on your own questions.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

LangChain text splitters (RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter, MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter), LlamaIndex node parsers (SentenceSplitter, SemanticSplitterNodeParser), document parsers like Unstructured or Docling that keep headings and tables (Lesson 3.2), and Chonkie for fast standalone chunking.

from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=1200,        # measured in characters by default (~300 tokens)
    chunk_overlap=150,
    separators=["\n\n", "\n", ". ", " ", ""],  # biggest break first
)
chunks = splitter.split_text(handbook_text)

# Contextual chunking: prefix each chunk with where it came from before embedding
records = [{
    "text": chunk,                                       # what the model will read
    "embed_text": f"{doc_title} › {section_of(chunk)}\n{chunk}",  # what gets embedded
    "metadata": {"doc_id": doc_id, "section": section_of(chunk)},
} for chunk in chunks]

Anthropic's version asks an LLM for a one- or two-sentence context per chunk (usually 50–100 tokens) and uses prompt caching so the whole document is only processed once; they estimate about $1.02 per million document tokens, paid once at indexing time.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Recursive splitting at 300–800 tokens with ~10–15% overlap, split on headings if you have them, and keep the title and section in metadata. Build a 30-question eval set the same day, so every later change is measured instead of guessed.

At scale

Per-source pipelines (contracts by clause, code by function, slides by page, tickets by thread), contextual chunks, small-to-big retrieval, and chunk configs versioned and A/B-tested against a retrieval eval. Changing the chunker means re-embedding everything, so it's planned like a database migration.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you chunk these documents?” A senior answer:

  • Starts from the documents and the questions: structure, length, tables, how self-contained the facts are.
  • Names a baseline (recursive, a size range, a little overlap) and why, plus structure-aware splitting where structure exists.
  • Mentions the context problem (pronouns, missing entity names) and a fix (headers in chunks, contextual retrieval, small-to-big).
  • Says how they'd measure it: recall@k on a labelled question set, then end-to-end answer quality (Lessons 5.1 and 5.4).
🏭 In the real world
Context added to every chunk 🏢 Anthropic · 2024 Introducing Contextual Retrieval

How: before embedding, an LLM writes a short context for each chunk (which document, which section, what it's about), and the context is added to both the embedding and the BM25 keyword index.

Why this method: top-20 retrieval failures fell 35% with contextual embeddings (5.7% → 3.7%), 49% adding contextual BM25 (→ 2.9%) and 67% with reranking (→ 1.9%), without changing the embedding model.

Measuring chunkers instead of guessing 🏢 Chroma · 2024 Evaluating Chunking Strategies for Retrieval

How: compared popular chunkers and settings on the same retrieval benchmark, scoring recall and precision of the retrieved tokens.

Why this method: the choice of strategy changed recall by up to 9%; some popular defaults performed relatively poorly, while a well-tuned recursive splitter often did well.

Page-level chunks for PDFs 🏢 NVIDIA · 2025 Finding the Best Chunking Strategy for Accurate AI Responses

How: tested token-based, section-based and page-level chunking across several datasets, measuring end-to-end answer accuracy.

Why this method: page-level chunking had the highest average accuracy (0.648) and the most consistent results, and the best token size differed by dataset, which is why you measure on your own data.

Embed first, chunk later 🧪 Research · 2024 Late Chunking (Jina AI)

How: run the whole document through a long-context embedding model, then average the token vectors inside each chunk's span to get that chunk's embedding.

Why this method: each chunk's vector is computed with the rest of the document in view, so “it” and “this window” still carry their meaning, without an extra LLM call per chunk.

🤯 Fun fact · the default nobody chose

Many RAG prototypes run on whatever chunk size the tutorial used. Chroma's study found exactly that kind of untuned default among the weaker configurations it tested. One of the cheapest wins in AI engineering is simply measuring two or three chunk sizes on 50 real questions.

Check yourself

1. A 6,000-token document, chunk size 400, overlap 100. Roughly how many chunks?
Each chunk starts 300 tokens after the previous one: ⌈(6,000 − 400) ÷ 300⌉ + 1 = ⌈18.7⌉ + 1 = 20, versus 15 with no overlap.
2. Retrieval keeps returning “This window extends to 60 days for members” for refund questions, but your model ignores it or misreads it. What's the most direct fix?
The chunk depends on the sentence before it (“this window” = the refund window). Structure-aware or recursive splitting keeps them together; contextual chunking adds “Refunds:” to the chunk so it stands alone.
3. You're indexing a codebase and a Markdown docs site. Which chunking is the best starting point?
Both sources have structure that matches how people ask questions (“what does parse_config do?”, “how do I install?”). Splitting along it keeps units whole; a size cap stops giant functions or sections.
📝 TL;DR
  • The answer must live inside a chunk that gets retrieved, and that chunk must make sense on its own.
  • Start with recursive or structure-aware splitting, a few hundred tokens, a little overlap; keep titles and sections as metadata.
  • Fix context loss with section headers, contextual retrieval or late chunking; fix size with small-to-big (4.2).
  • Chunk size is a trade-off between precision, completeness and tokens: measure it on your own questions.
