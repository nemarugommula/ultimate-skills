Phase 3 · Lesson 1

Why RAG: hallucination, cut-offs and private data

A language model is a brilliant reader and writer with a fixed, fuzzy memory. Retrieval-augmented generation hands it the right pages at the moment it needs them.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Tokens and context windows: models read and bill in tokens (about 4 characters of English each), and can only read so many at once (Lesson 0.3).
  • Embeddings turn text into vectors so that similar meanings land close together (Lesson 2.1).
  • A vector database stores those vectors and finds the nearest ones to a query fast (Lessons 2.2 and 2.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2022, Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares after a death in his family. The chatbot told him he could buy a full-price ticket and apply for the discount within 90 days of the ticket being issued. That wasn't the airline's policy, and his refund request was refused.

In February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ruled against the airline, which had argued that the chatbot was a “separate legal entity” responsible for its own actions. The tribunal disagreed: the chatbot is part of Air Canada's website. Air Canada had to pay C$650.88 in damages plus interest and fees. The correct policy was on another page of the same website. The bot just wasn't grounded in it.

Numbers first: what a model doesn't know

A model learns from a huge snapshot of public text, then stops learning. Ask it about your business and three gaps show up.

GapWhat it looks likeHow much of your data the model saw
Private dataYour handbook, tickets, contracts, code, wiki0%: it was never public
Knowledge cut-offThe model's training data ends at a date; your prices changed yesterday0% of anything after the cut-off
Long-tail factsA rare fact seen a few times in training is stored vaguely, if at allSome, blurred

When a model lacks a fact, it doesn't go quiet. It writes the most plausible continuation, which reads exactly like a true answer. That fluent, confident, wrong output is a hallucination. You can't fix a missing fact by asking more nicely; you have to give it the fact.

There are only three places knowledge can come from at answer time:

  1. The weights (what it learned in training, changed by fine-tuning: more training on your data, Lesson 7.1).
  2. The context window (whatever text you put in the prompt).
  3. A tool it calls, such as an API or database query (Lesson 6.2).

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is option 2 done automatically and selectively: for each question, retrieve the few most relevant passages from your documents, augment the prompt with them, then let the model generate an answer from them.

💡 Big idea Stop asking the model to remember your facts; ask it to read them. Models are far more reliable at reading a passage you hand them than at recalling something from training. RAG turns a memory problem into a reading problem, and a search problem.
🧠 Analogy: the open-book exam

A closed-book exam tests memory: a student who doesn't remember the answer guesses, confidently, to earn partial credit. An open-book exam tests reading: the student finds the right page and quotes it. RAG makes every question an open-book exam, with a librarian who fetches the five most relevant pages before the student starts writing. Fine-tuning is weeks of studying: great for learning a style of answering, unreliable for memorising every fact. Long context is wheeling the whole library onto the desk: it works for a small library, and it's slow and expensive for a big one.

The RAG pipeline: index time and query time

RAG has two halves that run at different times. Index time (offline, whenever documents change): load documents, parse them to clean text (Lesson 3.2), cut them into chunks (3.3), embed each chunk and store the vectors. Query time (online, per question, in a second or two): embed the question, retrieve the nearest chunks (3.4), put them into a prompt with instructions (3.5), and generate the answer. Lesson 3.6 builds the whole thing.

After the policy update, the old index still says 49 dollars: RAG is only as fresh as its index. Re-index (seconds, no retraining) and the new price appears.

Where the idea came from

The name comes from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research, University College London and New York University. They called what a model stores in its weights parametric memory, and an external index of text non-parametric memory, and combined the two:

  • Retriever: DPR (Dense Passage Retrieval), a pair of BERT encoders that embed questions and passages, over Wikipedia (December 2018) split into 100-word chunks: 21 million of them, searched with FAISS (Meta's vector-search library) using an HNSW graph index (Lesson 2.2).
  • Generator: BART-large, a 400-million-parameter sequence-to-sequence model, reading the question concatenated with the retrieved passages.
  • Trained end to end, it set the state of the art on three open-domain question-answering benchmarks.

Their neatest result was index hot-swapping. They asked about 82 world leaders who had changed between 2016 and 2018. With a 2016 Wikipedia index the model answered 70% of 2016 leaders correctly; with a 2018 index, 68% of 2018 leaders. With mismatched indexes it dropped to 12% and 4%. Swapping the index updated the model's knowledge without retraining a single weight.

Today's RAG is simpler: the generator is a large model behind an API that you don't train at all, the retriever is an off-the-shelf embedding model, and the two meet in the prompt.

They combine. A support assistant might be fine-tuned (or just prompted) for tone, use RAG for policies, and call an order API for “where's my parcel?”. And for knowledge, research favours retrieval: a 2023 Microsoft study comparing the two found that RAG consistently beat unsupervised fine-tuning, both for facts the model had seen in training and for entirely new ones.

✏️ Worked example: long context vs RAG, in dollars

A 150,000-token knowledge base (about 375 pages), 10,000 questions a day, input priced at an illustrative $3 per million tokens.

  • Everything in the prompt: 150,000 × $3 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.45 per question → $4,500 a day.
  • With prompt caching (Lesson 1.4; cached reads often bill at about a tenth of the normal input price): ≈ $0.045 per question → $450 a day, plus cache writes.
  • RAG: 5 chunks × 500 tokens + 500 tokens of instructions and question = 3,000 tokens → $0.009 per question → $90 a day.

Long context also means slower answers, because the model must read 150,000 tokens before writing the first word. But it has no retrieval step to get wrong, so for small, stable collections it can be the better choice. Anthropic's own rule of thumb: under about 200,000 tokens (around 500 pages), consider skipping RAG and putting everything in a cached prompt. Prices are illustrative; check your provider's pricing page.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Each stage has its own tools, and you'll meet them all in this phase: parsers (Docling, Unstructured, PyMuPDF; 3.2), splitters (LangChain, LlamaIndex, Chonkie; 3.3), embedding models and vector databases (Phase 2), rerankers (Cohere Rerank, bge-reranker; 3.4), an LLM API (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini; 3.5), and frameworks that wire them together (LangChain, LlamaIndex; 3.6). Strip away the libraries and RAG is this:

# The whole idea. search() is any retriever: a vector DB, BM25, or both (Lesson 2.4).
import anthropic
client = anthropic.Anthropic()

def answer(question):
    chunks = search(question, k=5)                        # 1. retrieve
    context = "\n".join(f"[{c.id}] {c.text}" for c in chunks)
    prompt = (f"<docs>\n{context}\n</docs>\n"                # 2. augment
              "Answer only from the docs and cite [ids]. "
              "If they don't contain the answer, say you don't know.\n"
              f"Question: {question}")
    msg = client.messages.create(                            # 3. generate
        model="claude-haiku-5-5", max_tokens=400,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
    return msg.content[0].text

Model names change often; pick a current one from your provider's model list (as of late 2026, Anthropic's include claude-haiku-5-5 and claude-sonnet-5-5).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

If the docs fit in a cached prompt, start there and ship. When they don't, build a plain RAG pipeline (Lesson 3.6) with a managed vector store, and write 30 real questions with known answers before tuning anything. Don't fine-tune for facts.

At scale

RAG becomes a platform: connectors for dozens of sources, incremental re-indexing, permission filters synced from the identity system, hybrid search and reranking, evals in CI, and per-team indexes. Fine-tuning appears later, for tone or to make a cheaper model good enough, not to teach facts.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our chatbot makes things up about our products. Should we fine-tune it?” A senior answer:

  • Names the cause: the model lacks the facts (private, fresh), so it guesses. Fine-tuning is a poor way to inject facts and can't cite or update quickly.
  • Proposes grounding: RAG over the product docs (or long context if they're small), with citations and an instruction to say “I don't know”.
  • Routes live data (prices, stock, orders) to tools, not to an index.
  • Says how they'd prove it: a labelled question set measuring retrieval hits and answer correctness before and after (Lessons 3.6 and 5.4).
🏭 In the real world
The paper that named it 🧪 Research · 2020 Lewis et al., Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks

How: a dense retriever over 21M Wikipedia chunks feeds passages to a BART generator; the two are fine-tuned together.

Why this method: knowledge in an index can be inspected, cited and swapped; the hot-swap experiment updated world knowledge without retraining.

Retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts 🧪 Research · 2023 Ovadia et al., Fine-Tuning or Retrieval?

How: compared unsupervised fine-tuning with RAG for injecting knowledge on several knowledge-intensive tasks.

Why this method: RAG consistently won; models struggled to learn new facts through fine-tuning unless shown many variations of each fact.

“Just use a longer prompt” threshold 🏢 Anthropic · 2024 Introducing Contextual Retrieval

How: for knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens (around 500 pages), include everything in the prompt and use prompt caching.

Why this method: the simplest system that works; caching makes the repeated long prompt much cheaper and faster.

An ungrounded bot, in court 🏢 Air Canada · 2024 The Guardian: Air Canada ordered to pay customer misled by chatbot

How: the chatbot answered a policy question with a rule that didn't exist; the real policy was elsewhere on the site.

Why it matters: the company is liable for what its bot says. Grounding answers in the official policy, with a citation, is the engineering fix.

🤯 Fun fact · nobody loves the name

Patrick Lewis, the paper's lead author, has apologised for the acronym. He told NVIDIA's blog that the team “definitely would have put more thought into the name” had they known how widespread it would become. The unflattering three letters now name an entire industry.

Check yourself

1. A 60,000-token manual, 20,000 questions a day, input at $3 per million tokens. Sending the whole manual every time costs about how much per day (no caching)?
60,000 × $3 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.18 per question; × 20,000 = $3,600 a day. RAG at 3,000 tokens per question would be $180; caching the manual would also cut it sharply.
2. Customers ask “Is the blue tent in stock at my nearest store?”. Stock changes every minute and lives in an inventory database. What should answer it?
Live, structured values belong to tools. A nightly index is stale within minutes, and fine-tuned weights are stale immediately. RAG is for documents; tools are for systems of record.
3. Why is fine-tuning usually the wrong first move to make a model know your 3,000 policy documents?
Fine-tuning shines at style, format and narrow behaviour. For knowledge, retrieval wins on accuracy, freshness, citations and permissions (Ovadia et al., 2023). Many providers do offer fine-tuning APIs; it's just the wrong tool for this job.
📝 TL;DR
  • Models hallucinate when they lack a fact: private data, anything after the cut-off, rare details. Give them the fact instead of hoping they remember it.
  • RAG = retrieve relevant chunks, put them in the prompt, generate from them. Index time (parse, chunk, embed, store) is separate from query time (embed, retrieve, prompt, answer).
  • Small, stable docs: long context with caching. Large or changing docs: RAG. Style and format: fine-tuning. Live values: tools.
  • RAG is only as fresh as its index and only as good as its retrieval; the rest of this phase is about making both good.
