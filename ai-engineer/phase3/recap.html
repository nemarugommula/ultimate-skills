Phase 3 · Recap

Recap: RAG fundamentals

Why retrieval beats memory, and every stage of a RAG pipeline: ingestion, chunking, retrieval and reranking, grounded generation, and the end-to-end system with its first eval. Here's the phase on one page, a diagnosis game, and a system to design yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

📚

3.1 Why RAG

Small and stable (under ~200k tokens) → long context + caching. Large or changing → RAG. Style → fine-tune. Live values → tools.

Models guess when they lack a fact (private, after the cut-off, rare). RAG makes it a reading problem: retrieve, augment, generate. Fresh only as the index.

🧰 any LLM API, a vector store, prompt caching

📄

3.2 Ingestion

PDF = glyphs at coordinates. Parse layout-aware to Markdown; OCR only pages with no text layer; dedup; store every field you'll filter on.

Two columns read across, flattened tables and silently missing scans ruin answers before search starts. Permissions are metadata enforced at retrieval.

🧰 Docling, Unstructured, PyMuPDF, Apache Tika

✂️

3.3 Chunking

Chunks ≈ ⌈(N − size) ÷ (size − overlap)⌉ + 1. Start recursive or by heading, 300–800 tokens, 10–15% overlap.

The answer must sit inside one retrievable chunk that makes sense alone. Add section or document context to each chunk; measure sizes on your own questions.

🧰 LangChain splitters, LlamaIndex node parsers, Chonkie

🎯

3.4 Retrieval & reranking

Retrieve 20–100 (bi-encoder, often hybrid) → rerank to 3–10 (cross-encoder). MMR λ ≈ 0.5–0.7. Filter during search.

Recall first: a reranker can't recover what the first stage missed. Raw cosine thresholds are unreliable; reranker scores decide abstaining.

🧰 Cohere Rerank, bge-reranker, sentence-transformers CrossEncoder

🔖

3.5 Grounded generation

Instructions → documents with IDs and dates → question last. Faithfulness = supported claims ÷ claims.

Answer only from the documents, cite IDs, say “I don't know”, newest official source wins. Best chunks at the edges. Verify citations: they can be invented too.

🧰 Anthropic Citations, RAGAS, NLI checkers

🏗️

3.6 End to end

The LLM dominates latency and cost; time to first token is what users feel. Log every stage; 20–50 golden questions before tuning.

Score retrieval hits and answers separately, and fix the biggest failure bucket first. Frameworks save boilerplate; hand-written code shows every step.

🧰 Chroma / pgvector, LangChain, LlamaIndex, Langfuse

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “A user types a question into our help assistant and gets a cited answer two seconds later. Walk me through every step that happened, including the ones that ran last week when the documents were indexed. Then: an answer is wrong. How do you find out which step broke?”

A strong answer covers: parsing to clean Markdown with metadata, chunking with context, embedding and storing; at query time embedding the question, a filtered (often hybrid) first stage of 20 to 100 candidates, a cross-encoder reranker down to about 5, a grounded prompt with IDs and dates, a streamed answer with citations. Then debugging from the logs: was the source chunk retrieved at all (ingestion, chunking, first stage), ranked into the top k (reranking), or present but misused (prompt, conflicts, model)? Plus the eval set that measures each.

🏗️ Build it yourself: the HR policy assistant

✏️ The problem

A company with 8,000 employees in 6 countries wants an assistant that answers HR policy questions (leave, expenses, benefits, travel) with citations. There are about 3,000 policy documents, averaging 8 pages; about 20% are scanned PDFs; many policies differ by country; some sections are for managers only; policies change weekly. Expect 2,000 questions a day, a target of under 1.5 s to the first token, and an LLM budget of about $1,000 a month.

Using this phase's ideas, sketch the index-time and query-time pipelines, with numbers, and your eval plan. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Requirements

  • Answer from the current policy for the employee's country, citing policy and section; say so and link to HR when the policies don't cover it.
  • Never show manager-only content to non-managers. Updated policies searchable within hours.

Numbers

  • Corpus: 3,000 × 8 pages × ~500 tokens = 12 million tokens. Far beyond any prompt, so RAG, not long context.
  • Chunks at 400 tokens: 12,000,000 ÷ 400 = 30,000 (about 34,000 with 50-token overlap). Embedding them all at $0.02 per million tokens: about $0.24.
  • OCR: 600 scanned documents × 8 pages = 4,800 pages × 30 s = 144,000 s = 40 hours on one CPU; 8 parallel workers make it about 5 hours, once. Afterwards only changed files are re-parsed.
  • Per question: ~2,300 input + ~250 output tokens plus a rerank ≈ $0.0126 at the illustrative prices of Lesson 3.6; × 2,000 × 30 days ≈ $756 a month. Inside budget, with room for a judge on a sample.
  • Latency: ~0.7 s to the first token with 5 chunks and a reranker (Lesson 3.6's budget), so streaming keeps it well under 1.5 s.

Index time

  1. Connectors to the policy sources; hash each file and re-parse only changed ones (Lesson 4.8 scales this).
  2. Layout-aware parsing to Markdown; OCR only for pages without a text layer; tables kept as Markdown.
  3. Metadata per document: policy_id, country (or global), audience (all / managers), effective_date, version, status (current / superseded).
  4. Chunk by heading at ~400 tokens, with a context prefix such as “Travel policy › Per diems › Germany”. Dedup copies across intranet pages.

Query time

  1. From the logged-in user: where = {country ∈ [user.country, global], audience ∈ user.groups, status = current}, applied inside the search.
  2. Hybrid first stage (policy names and codes matter), top 40 → cross-encoder → top 5.
  3. Top reranker score below a tuned threshold → “I couldn't find this; here's how to contact HR”.
  4. Grounded prompt: documents with IDs, titles, country and effective date first; question last; cite; prefer the current country-specific policy over global; mention conflicts.
  5. Stream the answer; show citations as links to the policy section; log the full trace.

Eval plan

  • 50 golden questions from real HR tickets, spread across countries; 10 unanswerable; 10 permission tests (non-managers asking about manager-only content).
  • Metrics: retrieval hit rate, answer correctness, correct abstains, faithfulness on a sample, and a leak rate that must be zero. Run on every change; add real failing questions from the logs each week.

Classic mistakes

  • The UK policy answering a German employee, because country wasn't stored or filtered.
  • Permissions in the prompt (“don't reveal manager content”) instead of in the retrieval filter.
  • Old policy versions left in the index, with no dates for the model to compare.
  • Scanned PDFs indexed as empty text, so questions about them silently get “I don't know”.
  • Logging questions that contain personal or medical details without retention and redaction rules.

What this design doesn't cover yet: rewriting vague questions (“how much can I claim?”), multi-hop questions across policies, and keeping permissions in sync in real time. Those are Phase 4 (4.1, 4.4, 4.7).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A 12-million-token corpus is cut into 400-token chunks with no overlap. How many chunks?
12,000,000 ÷ 400 = 30,000. With 50 tokens of overlap each chunk advances 350 tokens, so about 34,000 (Lesson 3.3).
2. A stable 120,000-token product FAQ, 500 questions a day. Simplest approach that works well?
Under about 200,000 tokens and stable, long context with prompt caching avoids every retrieval failure mode, and 500 questions a day keeps the bill modest (Lesson 3.1).
3. Vector search usually ranks the answer chunk between 15th and 30th. You retrieve 10 and rerank to 5. What should you change?
The reranker only sees what the first stage returns, and the answer is outside the 10. Widen the pool (and improve the first stage with hybrid search) so the reranker can lift it to the top (Lesson 3.4).
4. The model's answer cites [c7], but the prompt only contained chunks c1 to c5. What should the system do?
Citations are claims too, and models can invent them. A one-line check that cited IDs exist in the context catches this; native citation APIs guarantee valid pointers (Lesson 3.5).
5. On 30 golden questions, the source chunk was retrieved for 24, and 18 answers were correct, all of them among the retrieved ones. What share of the failures happened at generation?
12 failures: 6 not retrieved (retrieval) and 24 − 18 = 6 retrieved but wrong (generation) → 6 ÷ 12 = 50%. Both stages need work; per-stage logging is what lets you see it (Lesson 3.6).
