Phase 3 · Lesson 4

Retrieval & reranking: finding the five chunks that matter

Fast search finds fifty candidates; a slower, smarter model picks the five the LLM will read. Then make sure those five aren't the same fact five times.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Embeddings and cosine similarity: query and chunk become vectors; similar meanings score close to 1 (Lesson 2.1).
  • ANN indexes (approximate nearest neighbour, such as HNSW) find the closest vectors among millions in milliseconds (Lesson 2.2).
  • Hybrid search combines keyword search (BM25) with vectors, often by RRF, reciprocal rank fusion (Lesson 2.4).
  • Chunks are the pieces you retrieve (Lesson 3.3).
🎯 Hook

In January 2019, two researchers at NYU took BERT, a model that had come out a few months earlier, and used it for one narrow job: given a question and one candidate passage, read them together and score how relevant the passage is. They didn't replace the search engine. They just re-sorted its top results.

That simple reranker went straight to the top of the MS MARCO passage-ranking leaderboard, 27% better (relative, in MRR@10: mean reciprocal rank, which rewards putting the right passage near the top of the first 10) than the previous state of the art. Five years later Anthropic's retrieval tests told the same story: adding a reranker on top of their best retrieval setup cut failed retrievals from 2.9% to 1.9%.

Numbers first: why two stages?

There are two ways to score a question against a chunk:

  • A bi-encoder (the embedding model from Phase 2) encodes the question and every chunk separately into vectors. Chunk vectors are computed once, at index time; a query costs one encoding plus a fast vector search.
  • A cross-encoder reads the question and a chunk together, in one pass, so every word of the question can attend to every word of the chunk. It sees “Plus members” and “60 days” side by side and judges whether this passage answers the question. Much more accurate, but nothing can be precomputed: it must run once per (question, chunk) pair, at query time.

The Sentence-BERT paper put numbers on the gap. Finding the most similar pair in 10,000 sentences with a BERT cross-encoder means scoring 10,000 × 9,999 ÷ 2 ≈ 50 million pairs: about 65 hours. With a bi-encoder: about 5 seconds. So nobody cross-encodes a whole corpus. You do both:

1,000,000 chunks ──(bi-encoder + ANN, ~10–50 ms)──▶ top 50 candidates ──(cross-encoder reranker, ~50–300 ms)──▶ top 5 ──▶ LLM prompt

Latencies are typical orders of magnitude; they depend on hardware, model size and chunk length.

Two words you'll use constantly: top-k, how many results a stage keeps, and recall@k, the share of questions whose answer chunk is somewhere in the top k. The LLM can only use what's in its top k, so recall@k of the final stage is the number that matters most.

💡 Big idea Recall first, precision second. The first stage casts a wide, cheap net so the answer chunk is somewhere in the top 50. The reranker spends real compute putting it at the top. A reranker can only reorder what it's given: if the first stage missed the chunk, no reranker can bring it back.
🧠 Analogy: hiring

A thousand people apply. You can't interview them all, so a quick résumé screen picks 20 (fast, crude, generous). Then you interview those 20 properly (slow, accurate) and make 5 offers. Screen too strictly, say down to 5, and the best candidate may never get an interview. Interview everyone, and hiring takes a year. Retrieval works the same way: the bi-encoder is the résumé screen, the cross-encoder is the interview.

The first stage uses this course's toy embedding. The “cross-encoder” is a labelled, rule-based stand-in: it reads question and chunk together and rewards what a real cross-encoder learns to notice: the chunk contains the kind of answer asked for (a number of days, a price), names the same product or group, and isn't just a pile of keywords. Real rerankers learn these signals from millions of labelled pairs.

What you should see: the overview pages (“Refund policy overview: refunds, returns, reimbursement…”) win on similarity because they're stuffed with the same words as the question. The answer chunk sits at rank 6, 8 or 9: retrieved, but not in the top 5 the LLM reads. Turn the reranker on and every answer chunk jumps to rank 1. Now drop the first stage to 5 candidates: some answers are no longer in the pool, and the reranker can't help.

✏️ Worked example: recall and reranker cost by hand
  • Recall@5 without reranking: of the 8 questions, 5 have their answer chunk in the first-stage top 5 → 5 ÷ 8 = 62.5%. With reranking over the top 20: 8 ÷ 8 = 100%.
  • Reranker work: 50 candidates × (300-token chunk + 20-token question) = 16,000 tokens per query through the cross-encoder. At 100 queries per second, that's 1.6 million tokens a second: why rerankers are small models, and why the candidate count stays around 20 to 100.
  • Hosted rerankers bill per search or per document, and usually cap request size (Cohere recommends at most 1,000 documents per request). Self-hosted ones cost GPU time.

Choosing k, and when to say “nothing found”

  • Final k trades recall against tokens and noise. Typical starting points: 3 to 10 chunks to the LLM, 20 to 100 candidates to the reranker. Measure recall@k on your own questions (Lesson 5.4) and pick the smallest k that holds the answers.
  • Similarity thresholds (“drop anything below 0.75”) are tempting but treacherous: raw cosine scores aren't calibrated and their scale differs between embedding models. In the playground, the reset-link answer chunk has a similarity of just 0.14 and still ranks 5th. A fixed threshold would have thrown away the answer.
  • Reranker scores are better for that decision: they're trained as relevance judgements, and some (Cohere's current models, for example) are documented as calibrated. A low top score is a good signal to abstain (Lesson 3.5).

Diversity: MMR

Reranking fixes order. It doesn't fix redundancy. If the same refund paragraph lives on the FAQ page, in the handbook, in an email template and in last year's PDF, all four copies score almost identically, and your top 5 becomes one fact repeated. MMR (maximal marginal relevance, Carbonell and Goldstein, 1998) picks results one at a time and penalises each candidate for being similar to what's already picked:

next pick = argmax over remaining d of λ · sim(question, d) − (1 − λ) · max over picked s of sim(d, s)

λ = 1 is plain relevance ranking. λ = 0 is pure novelty (relevance ignored). Values around 0.5 to 0.7 are common.

Metadata filters: before or after?

Filters (Lesson 3.2) restrict search to chunks whose metadata matches: product, date, region, permissions. Where you apply them matters. Post-filtering runs the vector search first, then drops non-matching results. Ask for the top 20 when only 5% of the corpus matches the filter, and on average just 20 × 0.05 = 1 result survives. Filtering during the search keeps looking until it has k matches: Qdrant, Weaviate and Pinecone filter inside the index, and pgvector (0.8 and later) offers iterative index scans for exactly this reason. For permissions, always filter in the query, never after generation.

res = collection.query(query_embeddings=[embed(q)], n_results=50,
                       where={"$and": [{"audience": "public"}, {"region": {"$in": ["CA", "all"]}}]})
candidates = res["documents"][0]

# 2a. Rerank with a hosted model (Cohere v2 API)
import cohere
co = cohere.ClientV2()
rr = co.rerank(model="rerank-v5.0-fast", query=q, documents=candidates, top_n=5)
top = [(candidates[r.index], r.relevance_score) for r in rr.results]

# 2b. ...or with an open cross-encoder you host
from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
ce = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3")
scores = ce.predict([(q, d) for d in candidates])
top = sorted(zip(candidates, scores), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:5]

if top[0][1] < MIN_SCORE:                     # tune MIN_SCORE on labelled data
    return "I couldn't find this in our documents."

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Hybrid first stage (Lesson 2.4) with top 30 to 50, a hosted reranker down to 5, metadata filters for permissions and dates. Log the scores. One afternoon of work, and often the single biggest quality jump after fixing ingestion.

At scale

Self-hosted rerankers fine-tuned on click and feedback data, multi-stage cascades (a cheap reranker on 500, an expensive one on 50), per-tenant filters inside the ANN index, latency budgets per stage, and offline evals of recall@k and nDCG (a ranking-quality score, Lesson 5.4) on every change.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our RAG answers are often incomplete, though the right document exists. How would you debug retrieval?” A senior answer:

  • Measures first: for a labelled set, where does the answer chunk rank? Not retrieved at all (a recall problem: chunking, hybrid search, query rewriting in Lesson 4.1) vs retrieved but ranked below k (an ordering problem: reranking).
  • Proposes retrieve-many-then-rerank-few, explaining bi-encoders vs cross-encoders and the latency budget.
  • Checks for duplicates filling the top k (dedup, MMR) and for filters applied after search.
  • Avoids raw cosine thresholds; uses reranker scores to decide when to abstain.
🏭 In the real world
The reranker that topped MS MARCO 🧪 Research · 2019 Nogueira & Cho, Passage Re-ranking with BERT

How: BERT reads the query and one passage together and outputs a relevance score; it re-sorts the top results of a keyword search.

Why this method: full attention between query and passage beats separate encodings; +27% relative MRR@10 over the previous best.

Why bi-encoders exist 🧪 Research · 2019 Reimers & Gurevych, Sentence-BERT

How: trained BERT to produce standalone sentence embeddings compared by cosine similarity.

Why this method: the most-similar-pair search over 10,000 sentences dropped from about 65 hours (cross-encoding) to about 5 seconds.

Retrieve 150, rerank to 20 🏢 Anthropic · 2024 Introducing Contextual Retrieval

How: initial retrieval of the top 150 chunks, a reranking model scores each against the query, the top 20 go to the model.

Why this method: on top of contextual embeddings and BM25, reranking took the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 2.9% to 1.9%.

LLMs as rerankers 🧪 Research · 2023 Sun et al., Is ChatGPT Good at Search? (RankGPT)

How: prompted GPT-class models to output a ranking of candidate passages, then distilled that skill into small models.

Why this method: properly instructed LLMs matched or beat supervised rerankers, and a distilled 440M model beat a 3B supervised one on the BEIR benchmark.

Relevance plus novelty 📜 History · 1998 Carbonell & Goldstein, The Use of MMR

How: score each candidate as a weighted mix of relevance to the query and dissimilarity to results already chosen.

Why this method: users want “relevant novelty”, not ten copies of the best hit; the same one-line formula is still the default diversity option today.

Check yourself

1. You retrieve the top 40 by vector search and then apply a filter that only 10% of your corpus matches. About how many results survive, on average?
40 × 0.10 = 4, and sometimes zero. That's post-filtering. pgvector's README uses this very example: with HNSW's default candidate list of 40 and a filter matching 10% of rows, about 4 rows come back. Filter inside the search (or enable iterative scans) so the database keeps looking until it finds k matches.
2. Your logs show the answer chunk usually isn't anywhere in the top 50 candidates. What helps most?
A reranker can only reorder what it receives, and sending 10 of the same 50 doesn't help either. This is a recall problem, so fix recall: keyword + vector search (2.4), chunking (3.3), query transformations (4.1).
3. Why don't we simply run the cross-encoder over every chunk in the corpus?
Embeddings of chunks are computed once and searched in milliseconds. A cross-encoder needs the question, so nothing can be precomputed. That's why it only sees the top 20 to 100 candidates.
📝 TL;DR
  • Two stages: a bi-encoder (embeddings + ANN, often hybrid with BM25) retrieves 20 to 100 candidates cheaply; a cross-encoder reranks them and keeps 3 to 10.
  • Recall first: the reranker can't recover a chunk the first stage missed. Measure recall@k and where the answer ranks.
  • Don't trust raw cosine thresholds; use reranker scores to decide when nothing relevant was found.
  • Use MMR (λ ≈ 0.5 to 0.7) or dedup so the top k isn't one fact repeated; apply metadata filters during the search, never after.
