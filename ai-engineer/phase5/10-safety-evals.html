Phase 5 · Lesson 10

Safety, red-team & robustness evals

A model that refuses everything is perfectly safe and perfectly useless. Safety evals measure two failure rates at once, against attackers who adapt, and robustness evals check that your score survives a typo.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An eval = a set of test inputs, a way to grade each output, and a score you track over time (Lesson 5.1).
  • LLM-as-judge: a model grades outputs against a rubric, and you check it against humans (Lesson 5.3).
  • Confidence intervals: a pass rate measured on n examples is only known to within about ±1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n) (Lesson 5.1).
  • Prompt injection: instructions hidden in text the model reads (a user message, a web page, an email) that hijack it. How to defend against it is Lesson 8.3; this lesson is about measuring it.
🎯 Hook

In February 2024 Google paused Gemini's ability to generate images of people, three weeks after launching it. Asked for people in specific historical contexts, it produced images that were inaccurate and sometimes offensive. Google's explanation named two opposite failures: tuning meant to show a range of people “failed to account for cases that should clearly not show a range”, and over time the model became “way more cautious than we intended” and refused some harmless prompts entirely.

One safety tuning, two failure modes: too permissive in one place, too restrictive in another. A safety eval that only counted harmful outputs would have scored it well. That's the core lesson: you always measure both sides.

Numbers first: 99% safe is not a number you can ship on

Your support assistant handles 2,000,000 conversations a day. Suppose 0.5% are adversarial (people trying to make it misbehave) and the rest are ordinary users.

Rate on your evalPer day in production
Attack attempts0.5% of traffic10,000
Attacks that succeed (attack success rate 1%)“99% safe”100 harmful or policy-violating replies
Benign requests wrongly refused (over-refusal 4%)“only 4%”79,600 annoyed users
Your red-team set: 0 successes out of 300 attacks“0%”The true rate could still be up to 1% (95% upper bound ≈ 3 ÷ 300, the “rule of three”)

Traffic and rates are illustrative. Real attack rates vary hugely by product: a public chatbot sees far more probing than an internal tool.

Three things follow. Rates that look tiny are large counts at scale. Over-refusal hurts more people than attacks do. And “zero failures in our test” is a statement about your test size, not about safety.

💡 Big idea A safety eval reports a pair of numbers: how often attacks get harmful output (attack success rate) and how often harmless requests get refused (over-refusal rate). Every defence moves both. And because attackers adapt, the attack set is never finished: red-teaming keeps finding new failures, and each one becomes a permanent regression test.
🧠 Analogy: airport security

An airport measures two things: weapons that got through, and innocent passengers pulled aside. Make the scanner more sensitive and fewer weapons pass, but the queue explodes. Regulators also send undercover testers with fake weapons hidden in new ways, because the threats in last year's test are not the ones coming next year. Red teams are those testers; over-refusal is the queue; your regression suite is the list of every trick that ever worked.

Who decides an attack “worked”?

The grader is half the eval. Early jailbreak papers often counted any reply that didn't start with “I'm sorry” as a success. The StrongREJECT authors showed such graders significantly overstate jailbreak effectiveness compared with human judges: many “successful” jailbreaks produce vague, useless answers, because the tricks that bypass safety training also degrade the model's capabilities. Grade whether the output gives real, specific help toward the harm, and calibrate that judge against human labels (5.3, 5.8).

✏️ Worked example: an attack success rate with honest error bars

You run 200 role-play jailbreaks; the judge marks 12 as successful. ASR = 12 ÷ 200 = 6%.

  • Wilson 95% interval (better than ±1.96·SE when the rate is near 0): 3.5% to 10.2%. Report “6% (3.5–10.2%)”.
  • After a fix, a 50-attack spot check finds 1 success: 2%, but the interval is 0.4% to 10.5%. It overlaps the old one: you haven't shown an improvement yet. Rerun the same 200.
  • Zero successes out of n? The 95% upper bound is about 3 ÷ n: 0 of 300 → “at most ~1%”. Exact: 1 − 0.051/300 = 0.99%.
  • Over-refusal on 250 benign prompts: 10 refusals = 4% (2.2–7.2%). Always print both pairs side by side.

Red-teaming: manual, automated, and the regression suite

Red-teaming means deliberately attacking your own system to find failures before users do. It's how the attack set in your eval gets filled.

Triage: severity × likelihood

A red-team campaign returns hundreds of findings. Rank them like security bugs, not by count:

Rare (needs an expert attacker)Likely (works for a curious user)
Severe: another customer's data leaks, money moves, dangerous instructionsFix before launch; add monitoringLaunch blocker
Mild: rude tone, off-brand jokes, mentions a competitorBacklogFix soon; cheap prompt or filter change

Then every confirmed finding goes into a regression suite: the exact prompt (and the conversation or poisoned document around it), the expected safe behaviour, and its severity. Past incidents never come back unnoticed, and the suite runs in CI like any other eval (Lesson 5.6). Keep it partly private: if your attack set leaks into training data or prompt tweaks are tuned against it, it stops measuring anything.

Robustness and consistency: does the score survive a typo?

Lesson 1.5 met Sclar et al.: changing only the formatting of a few-shot prompt (separators, casing, spacing) moved LLaMA-2-13B's accuracy on one task by up to 76 points, and the best format for one model was often not best for another. Their recommendation applies to every eval: report a range across plausible prompts, not one number from one prompt.

  • Perturbations to test: typos and casing (real users type badly), paraphrased instructions, reordered few-shot examples or options, different separators and templates, extra whitespace, translated inputs.
  • Counterfactual fairness is the same machinery: swap only a name or demographic detail and count how often the answer changes. Anthropic's discrim-eval does this across 70 decision scenarios; OpenAI's 2024 study did it with user names in ChatGPT.
  • Consistency: run the same input k times at your production temperature. Report the agreement rate; users notice when the same question gets different answers.

Reporting: system cards and launch reviews

Model providers publish model cards or system cards: documents describing a model's intended use, the safety evals run and their results, red-team findings and known limits. OpenAI's o1 system card, for example, reports every disallowed-content eval with two metrics side by side, not_unsafe and not_overrefuse, plus jailbreak results on StrongREJECT. Do the same internally for each launch: a one-page table of each risk, the eval set and its size, the rate with its interval, the threshold you agreed, and the owner. It turns “is it safe?” from an argument into a checklist.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

promptfoo red-teaming (generates attacks from plugins = what to test and strategies = how to deliver it, then grades them), garak (NVIDIA's open-source LLM vulnerability scanner with probe libraries), PyRIT (Microsoft's open-source framework for automated, multi-turn red-teaming), AgentDojo (prompt-injection benchmark for tool-using agents), StrongREJECT (jailbreak benchmark and grader), XSTest / OR-Bench (over-refusal), and safety classifiers as graders such as Llama Guard (Lesson 8.3).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Write your policy in one page (what the product must never do). Run promptfoo or garak against the real app with a handful of plugins, read every failure, and turn the real ones into a 50–100 case safety regression set, plus 50 borderline-but-benign prompts for over-refusal. Gate releases on both numbers.

At scale

Dedicated red teams and external experts before each launch, automated attack generation in CI, separate suites per risk (injection, PII, harmful content, bias) with severity levels and owners, held-out private attack sets, counterfactual fairness audits, bug bounties, and system cards that publish both sides of the trade-off.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you evaluate the safety of our new agent before launch?” A senior answer:

  • Starts from a threat model: who attacks, through which inputs (user turn, documents, tool results), and what the worst outcome is (data leak, money moved, harmful text).
  • Measures pairs: attack success per category with intervals, and over-refusal on borderline benign prompts.
  • Combines automated attack generation (promptfoo, garak, PyRIT) with manual red-teaming, and grades with a calibrated judge, not keyword matching.
  • Triages by severity × likelihood, turns findings into a regression suite in CI, and adds robustness (perturbed prompts) and fairness (name swaps) checks.
  • Says what's not covered: zero failures in 300 tests still allows ~1% in production, so monitoring and incident response stay on (8.3).
🏭 In the real world
Reporting both sides of the trade-off 🏢 OpenAI · 2024 OpenAI o1 System Card

How: each disallowed-content eval is scored for not_unsafe and not_overrefuse, including XSTest's benign prompts; jailbreaks are measured on production jailbreaks, human red-team attacks and StrongREJECT.

Why this method: one number hides the trade-off. On StrongREJECT the reported score rose from 0.22 for GPT-4o to 0.72 for o1, shown next to over-refusal so nobody can buy safety with refusals unnoticed.

Measuring exaggerated safety 🧪 Research · 2023 XSTest

How: 250 safe prompts in ten types (homonyms like “kill a Python process”, safe contexts, privacy about public figures) plus 200 unsafe contrast prompts made by minimal edits.

Why this method: pairing safe and unsafe twins separates real caution from keyword panic. Llama 2 chat with its original system prompt fully refused 38% of the safe prompts; adding a guardrail prompt to Mistral cut unsafe answers but created exaggerated safety.

Grading jailbreaks properly 🧪 Research · 2024 A StrongREJECT for Empty Jailbreaks

How: forbidden prompts that need specific harmful information, and an automated grader that scores how useful the response actually is to the attacker.

Why this method: existing graders significantly overstated jailbreak success versus human judges, because jailbreaks that bypass safety tuning also tend to reduce the model's capabilities.

Counterfactual name swaps at scale 🏢 OpenAI · 2024 Evaluating fairness in ChatGPT

How: answered the same requests with user names that carry different gender, race or ethnicity associations, and had a language model (whose ratings were checked against human raters) flag differences reflecting harmful stereotypes.

Why this method: isolates the name as the only change. It found harmful-stereotype differences in around 0.1% of cases overall, up to around 1% in some domains on older models: rare, but measurable only with a paired design.

Prompt-injection benchmark for agents 🧰 Open source AgentDojo (ETH Zurich)

How: 97 realistic tool-using tasks (email, e-banking, travel booking) and 629 security test cases where tool results carry injected instructions; scores both task success and whether the attacker's goal was achieved.

Why this method: injection is about actions, so it's graded in an environment where actions happen; it measures utility and security together, the same pair as above.

🤯 Fun fact · the chatbot that gave out real phone numbers

In DeepMind's 2022 “Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models”, an attacker LM generating test questions found tens of thousands of offensive replies from a 280B-parameter chatbot. It also found the chatbot offering personal and hospital phone numbers as its own contact details. Generating test cases automatically surfaced failures at a scale hand-written tests couldn't reach.

Check yourself

1. Your automated red team ran 400 prompt-injection attacks and none succeeded. What's the most you can honestly claim?
Rule of three: with 0 failures in n trials, the 95% upper bound is about 3 ÷ n = 0.75%. And it only covers attacks resembling your test set; new attack styles aren't covered at all.
2. After adding a strict input classifier, jailbreak success drops from 9% to 2%. A week later, support tickets complain the bot won't explain medication dosages or how to kill a stuck process. What did your eval miss?
Every defence moves both numbers. Without a borderline-benign set (XSTest-style homonyms and safe contexts) you only saw the side that improved.
3. A loan-assistant prompt is tested on 500 applications. You want to know whether the applicant's name affects its recommendation. Which eval design fits?
A counterfactual swap holds everything else fixed, so any change is caused by the name. Comparing different applications mixes the name with income, history and everything else.
📝 TL;DR
  • Measure safety as a pair: attack success rate (per attack type, with intervals) and over-refusal on borderline benign prompts.
  • Cover harmful content, jailbreaks, direct and indirect injection (graded on actions), PII leakage, bias via counterfactual swaps, robustness, consistency and hallucination.
  • Red-team manually and automatically (promptfoo, garak, PyRIT); grade with a calibrated judge, triage by severity × likelihood, and keep every finding as a regression test.
  • Report a range across prompt variants, not one prompt's score; 0 failures in n tests still allows up to ~3/n.
