Phase 5 · Lesson 11

Comparing models & prompts rigorously: benchmarks, arenas, statistics

“B scored 2 points higher” is where most model decisions stop, and where most wrong model decisions start. Here's how to know whether a difference is real, what public leaderboards can and can't tell you, and how to pick on quality, cost and latency at once.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Error bars: a pass rate p measured on n items has a standard error √(p(1−p)/n); the 95% interval is about ±1.96 of those (Lesson 5.1).
  • Paired comparison: run both versions on the same items and look at the items where they differ (Lesson 5.6's CI gate).
  • Pairwise judging: show two answers, ask which is better (Lesson 5.3).
  • Benchmarks: public test sets used to rank models, and why they don't predict your task (Lesson 0.4).
🎯 Hook

Since 2024, SWE-bench Verified had been the coding number in model launches. On 23 February 2026 OpenAI said it had stopped reporting it and asked other developers to stop too. State-of-the-art had crept from only 74.9% to 80.9% in six months, so they audited 138 problems their o3 model didn't reliably solve: 59.4% had material problems in their tests or descriptions, for example tests that rejected correct solutions.

Worse, every frontier model they probed could reproduce the reference fix or verbatim task details for some problems: they'd seen the test during training. Near the top, the benchmark was measuring memory and broken tests as much as skill. OpenAI pointed people to SWE-bench Pro and privately written tasks instead. A score is only as good as the test, the statistics and the honesty behind it.

Numbers first: is 82% better than 80%?

Your current prompt A scores 80% on 500 eval items. A new prompt B scores 82%. Two ways to put an error bar on the 2-point gain, using the same results:

Unpaired (treat as two separate samples)Paired (same items; they disagree on 8% of them)
Standard error of the difference√(0.8·0.2/500 + 0.82·0.18/500) = 2.5 points√((0.08 − 0.02²)/500) = 1.26 points
95% interval of the gain2 ± 4.9 → [−2.9, +6.9]2 ± 2.5 → [−0.5, +4.5]
Items needed to reliably detect a 3-point gain (80% → 83%, 80% power)≈ 2,630 per version≈ 700 (8% disagree) · ≈ 520 (6% disagree)

All computed with the standard normal-approximation formulas; verify with the simulator below. “80% power” means a real 3-point gain shows up as significant in 80% of eval runs.

Same data, half the noise. Most items are answered the same way by both prompts; those items say nothing about which is better, and pairing stops them adding noise. Even so, 500 items can't confirm a 2-point gain. Small differences need big, paired evals, or they're not decisions, they're coin flips.

💡 Big idea Compare candidates on the same items, report the difference with its interval, count every variant you tried, and decide on quality, cost and latency together. Public benchmarks and arenas are for building a shortlist; your own eval, analysed this way, makes the decision.
🧠 Analogy: timing two runners

Time one runner on Monday in the rain and the other on Friday with a tailwind, and the weather swamps the difference between them. Race them side by side on the same track, same day, and the weather cancels out: that's pairing. If you hold 20 races and only report the one your runner won, you've proved nothing: that's multiple comparisons. And the fastest runner isn't the right hire if the job is a marathon on a budget: that's the Pareto front.

Each benchmark has a life cycle: introduced hard, climbed quickly, then saturated (top models all near the ceiling, so differences are noise or label errors) and often contaminated (test items leak into training data through the web). When a benchmark saturates, a new one replaces it.

Contamination and gaming

  • Contamination: the model has seen test items or answers in training. Signs: it reproduces items verbatim, or scores much worse on freshly written look-alikes (GSM1k vs GSM8K, Lesson 0.4). Defences: held-out or private splits, refreshed questions, canary strings that let training pipelines filter benchmark data.
  • Gaming: picking the prompt, sampling settings or number of attempts that flatter one model; testing many private variants and publishing the best (The Leaderboard Illusion, 0.4); reporting best-of-N runs as if they were one; comparing numbers from different evaluation harnesses. Always ask: same harness, same prompt, same number of attempts, how many runs?

Arenas: ranking by pairwise human preference

An arena shows a person two anonymous answers to their own prompt and asks which is better. From many such battles it fits one rating per model with the Bradley–Terry model: the chance A beats B depends only on the gap between their ratings. On the familiar Elo scale:

P(A beats B) = 1 ÷ (1 + 10(RB − RA) / 400)

✏️ Worked example: what a rating gap means
  • Gap 100 points: 1 ÷ (1 + 10−0.25) = 64% win rate. Gap 30: 54%. Gap 400: 91%.
  • So two models 30 points apart differ by four votes in a hundred, and the rating is about average preference across everyone's prompts, not yours.
  • LMArena fits these ratings by logistic regression over all battles (its open-source Arena-Rank package also gives confidence intervals) rather than updating chess-style Elo one game at a time, so the order of battles doesn't matter.
  • Style control: adding answer length and Markdown use (headers, bold, lists) as extra variables in the same regression. Arena reported that style strongly affects rankings: some small models dropped, and models such as Claude 3.5 Sonnet rose substantially once style was controlled.

The same Bradley–Terry likelihood trains reward models in RLHF (RL course 7.3) and is derived in Math for AI 5.3. You can run a private mini-arena too: pairwise LLM-judge or human comparisons on your own prompts (5.3, 5.8), fitted the same way.

Why your own eval set beats a leaderboard

A leaderboard averages over someone else's prompts, languages, grading and settings. Your eval uses your inputs, your rubric, your prompt and your production temperature, and you can pair it, rerun it and read the failures. Use leaderboards to pick 3–5 candidates; use your eval (Lessons 5.1–5.9) to choose. Lesson 0.4's playground showed a small private eval overturning a leaderboard ranking.

✏️ Worked example: same results, two verdicts

500 items. A passes 402 (80.4%), B passes 416 (83.2%): +2.8 points. Look at disagreements: 7 items only A got right, 21 only B got right; the other 472 match.

  • Unpaired two-proportion test: z = 1.15, p = 0.25, 95% interval ≈ 2.8 ± 4.8 → [−2.0, +7.6]. Verdict: no evidence.
  • McNemar: if B were no better, the 28 disagreements would split 50/50. Getting 21 or more out of 28 (either direction) has probability p = 0.013.
  • Paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples of the 500 items): 95% interval for the gain [+0.8, +5.0]. Verdict: B is better, by somewhere between about 1 and 5 points.

The paired analysis isn't more optimistic; it's more precise. And it hands you the 7 items B broke, which you should read before shipping (5.1's error analysis).

What you should see: at n = 500, a 3-point true gap and 8% disagreement, the paired test calls B the winner in about 60% of runs (its power; about 700 items would give 80%) and almost never names A. The unpaired test on the very same results almost never calls anything: it throws the pairing away. “Higher score wins” looks great here because both ran on the same items, but it has no notion of confidence: drop n to 100 and raise disagreement to 30% (two very different models) and it crowns the wrong system in roughly a quarter of runs, with nothing to warn you. Then try 20 equal prompts: with no real improvement anywhere, “top scorer” always finds a winner, and even the paired test finds a “significant” one in roughly a quarter of runs. Correct for it, or confirm on fresh items.

Quality isn't the only axis: the Pareto front

A candidate is dominated if another one is at least as good on quality, cost and latency, and strictly better on one. The ones left are the Pareto front: every real choice is a trade-off between them. Then add your constraints (a p95 latency budget, a cost ceiling) and one more rule from the statistics: if two candidates are statistically tied on quality, take the cheaper or faster one.

What you should see: at the default 8-second budget the top scorer is the frontier model with few-shot examples, but at n = 300 several setups are statistically tied with it, and the recommendation is one that costs a fifth as much. Raise the latency budget to 20 s: the reasoning model tops quality, yet the pick barely changes. Tighten it to 3.5 s and the frontier setups drop out; the fine-tuned small model, tied with the best remaining option, wins at a fraction of the cost. Raise n to 3,000 and the intervals shrink until real differences separate, so fewer setups count as tied. The self-hosted open model is dominated: the fine-tuned small model beats it on all three axes. In practice the latency and cost numbers come from your tracing (Lesson 8.1), not a price sheet.

Reporting results honestly

  • Report the difference between candidates with its interval and the test used (“+2.8 points, 95% CI [+0.8, +5.0], paired bootstrap, n = 500”), not two bare percentages.
  • State n, the eval version, the prompt, temperature, number of runs per item, and the harness.
  • Say how many variants you tried, and whether the winner was confirmed on held-out items.
  • Show per-category results; an average can hide a category that got worse (5.7).
  • Put cost and latency next to quality, measured on the same traffic.
  • Decide the threshold before you look (“ship if the lower bound of the gain is above −1 point and cost doesn't rise”), so the data can't move the goalposts.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

NumPy + SciPy for paired bootstraps and exact McNemar tests (statsmodels also has mcnemar); eval frameworks that store per-item results so you can pair them (promptfoo, Braintrust, LangSmith, Inspect, Lesson 5.6); lm-evaluation-harness and Inspect to run public benchmarks under one consistent setup; Arena-Rank (uv pip install arena-rank), LMArena's open-source Bradley–Terry ranking with confidence intervals, for your own pairwise comparisons.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binomtest

def compare(a, b, n_boot=10_000, seed=0):
    """a, b: 0/1 scores of two systems on the SAME eval items, in the same order."""
    a, b = np.asarray(a, float), np.asarray(b, float)
    d = b - a                                    # per-item difference: -1, 0 or +1
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(d), size=(n_boot, len(d)))   # resample items, keep pairs together
    boot = d[idx].mean(axis=1)
    lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
    b_only = int(((b == 1) & (a == 0)).sum())    # items only B got right
    a_only = int(((a == 1) & (b == 0)).sum())    # items only A got right
    p = binomtest(b_only, b_only + a_only, 0.5).pvalue   # exact McNemar test
    return d.mean(), (lo, hi), a_only, b_only, p

diff, (lo, hi), a_only, b_only, p = compare(scores_prompt_a, scores_prompt_b)
print(f"gain {diff:+.1%}  95% CI [{lo:+.1%}, {hi:+.1%}]  A-only {a_only}  B-only {b_only}  p={p:.3f}")
# with judge scores (e.g. 1-5) the same bootstrap works; McNemar is only for pass/fail

With several samples per item (temperature > 0), average each item's samples first, then bootstrap over items. If items come in groups (many questions per document), resample whole groups instead of single items.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Shortlist 3 models from leaderboards, run them on your 200–300 item eval with the same prompt, compare pairwise with a bootstrap, and pick the cheapest one that's tied with the best. Keep per-item results in a spreadsheet so every later prompt change is a paired comparison. Chase big gains; don't argue about 1-point differences you can't measure.

At scale

Versioned eval suites with thousands of items, multiple runs per item, power analysis before experiments, held-out confirmation sets, internal pairwise arenas with human and judge raters, dashboards of quality vs cost vs latency per candidate, and a review step that asks how many variants were tried. Online A/B tests (5.6) confirm what the offline comparison predicted.

🎤 Interview angle

“A new model scores 3 points higher on our eval. Should we switch?” A senior answer:

  • Asks for n and whether the comparison was paired on the same items, and computes the interval of the difference (McNemar or bootstrap).
  • Checks run-to-run variance (temperature, repeated runs) and whether this was the best of many attempts.
  • Looks at per-category results and the items that got worse, not just the average.
  • Weighs cost and latency on real traffic, and prefers the cheaper option when quality is tied.
  • Treats public benchmarks and arena ratings as context, mentions contamination, and confirms with an online A/B test or canary before a full switch.
🏭 In the real world
Separating style from substance 🏢 LMArena · 2024 Does Style Matter? Style control in Arena

How: added answer length and counts of Markdown headers, bold text and lists as extra variables in the Bradley–Terry regression, so each model's score is its strength after accounting for style.

Why this method: length was the dominant style factor; with style controlled, some small models fell below most frontier models while models like Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama-3.1-405B rose substantially.

Open-sourcing the leaderboard maths 🧰 Open source Arena-Rank

How: a Python package implementing the Bradley–Terry model (and the style-control extension) behind LMArena's leaderboard, with closed-form confidence intervals instead of the bootstrap.

Why this method: lets anyone reproduce a ranking from vote data, or rank their own models from internal pairwise judgements with honest intervals.

A benchmark that keeps moving 🧪 Research · 2024 LiveBench

How: frequently updated questions drawn from recent sources (maths competitions, papers, news), each scored automatically against an objective answer.

Why this method: fresh questions limit contamination, and ground-truth scoring avoids the biases of LLM judges and crowd votes on hard questions.

Replacing a saturated exam 🧪 Research · 2025 Humanity's Last Exam

How: 2,500 questions written by subject-matter experts worldwide, each with an unambiguous, verifiable answer that can't be quickly found online.

Why this method: models were scoring over 90% on MMLU, so it no longer separated the best models; a benchmark has to be hard enough to have room left.

Measuring the noise in benchmarks 🧪 Research · 2024 Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks

How: measured how much benchmark scores move with nothing but the random seed of training, across many models, and tested ways to reduce it.

Why this method: differences smaller than seed variance aren't evidence. Simple changes, like scoring multiple-choice tasks as completions, often reduced variance for ~7B models.

🤯 Fun fact · the arena model is older than Elo

The model behind arena leaderboards is named after Bradley and Terry's 1952 paper, but Ernst Zermelo, the mathematician of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, worked it out in 1929 to rank chess players from tournament results, including a simple iteration to find the ratings. Today's LLM leaderboards are solving his chess problem with millions of chatbot votes.

Check yourself

1. Two prompts agree on all but 6% of items. You want to detect a 3-point gain with 80% power using a paired test. About how many items do you need?
n ≈ (1.96·√0.06 + 0.84·√(0.06 − 0.03²))² ÷ 0.03² = (0.480 + 0.205)² ÷ 0.0009 ≈ 520. An unpaired comparison of 80% vs 83% would need about 2,600 per prompt.
2. You tried 30 prompt variants on your 300-item eval. The best beats the current prompt by 2.5 points with p = 0.03. What's the right next step?
With 30 tries, one variant reaching p = 0.03 by luck is likely. Bonferroni would need p < 0.05 ÷ 30 ≈ 0.0017. A fresh confirmation set gives an unbiased estimate of the winner's gain.
3. On 300 items, model X scores 88% at $12 per 1,000 requests; model Y scores 87% at $2. Paired 95% interval for X − Y: [−2.4, +4.4] points. Both meet the latency budget. Which do you pick?
The interval includes 0, so the 1-point gap may be noise; Y is on the Pareto front and cheaper. If the last point really matters, enlarge the eval until it can detect it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Public benchmarks saturate and get contaminated; arenas measure average preference and reward style. Use both to shortlist, your own eval to decide.
  • Compare on the same items: McNemar or a paired bootstrap halves the noise; report the difference with its interval.
  • Plan n with a power analysis, average repeated runs, and correct for (or confirm after) trying many variants.
  • Choose on the Pareto front of quality, cost and latency; when quality is statistically tied, take the cheaper, faster one.
