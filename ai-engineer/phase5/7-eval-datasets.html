Phase 5 · Lesson 7

Building eval datasets: real logs, synthetic & adversarial cases, coverage

An eval can only find the failures its dataset contains. This lesson is about choosing the cases: where they come from, how to cover what matters, and how to stop the set from going stale or leaking.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A golden dataset is your curated set of eval cases, each with an expected answer or pass criteria; Lesson 5.1 listed the five sources (logs, failures, experts, synthetic, adversarial) and how error bars shrink with more cases.
  • Pass rate ± error bar: with n cases at pass rate p, the 95% range is about ±1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n) (Lesson 5.1).
  • Embeddings place similar texts close together, so you can measure how alike two questions are (Lesson 2.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2019 researchers at Stanford and elsewhere looked closely at a chest X-ray model that detects a collapsed lung (pneumothorax). Its overall score looked respectable: an AUC of 0.87 (AUC is a 0-to-1 score of how well a model ranks sick cases above healthy ones; 0.5 is a coin flip).

Then a radiologist labelled one extra detail: whether each scan already showed a chest drain, the tube doctors insert to treat the condition. 80% of the positive scans had one. On those, the model scored 0.94. On scans without a drain, the untreated patients who most need the alert, it scored only 0.77. Part of its apparent skill came from spotting the tube, not the disease. The authors called this hidden stratification: a subgroup that's failing, invisible because nobody sliced the test set that way.

💡 Big idea An eval dataset is a designed sample, not a pile of examples. Decide the slices that matter (intents, difficulty, languages, risk), make sure each slice has enough cases to be measured on its own, and report slice scores next to the overall one. A single number is a summary; the slices are the diagnosis.
🧠 Analogy: the crash-test programme

A car maker doesn't crash 500 cars in whatever way crashes happen most often (mostly slow rear bumps). It designs a matrix: front, side, rollover, small overlap, child seat, at several speeds, and makes sure every cell is tested. Real accident reports add new cells over time. Your eval set works the same way: logs tell you what's common, the matrix tells you what's covered, and every new production failure becomes a new test.

Design the coverage matrix first

Before collecting anything, write down the dimensions along which your system's behaviour should differ. Typical ones:

  • Intent or task type: what the user wants (refund, track order, change password, off-topic). Derive it from error analysis of 100 real traces (Lesson 5.1), not from guessing.
  • Difficulty: single fact, multi-part (needs two facts), needs reasoning, ambiguous, unanswerable.
  • Input form: language, typos, length, formality, attachments.
  • User or context: plan tier, region, new vs returning, persona (an angry customer writes differently).
  • Risk: a wrong answer about money or health matters more than one about shipping times.

Crossing two or three of these gives a grid. Then fill it with stratified sampling: split the pool into strata (cells), and sample from each one separately with a minimum per cell, instead of sampling the whole pool at random. To report a headline number that still reflects real traffic, weight each cell's score by its share of production: overall = Σ (share of cell × pass rate of cell).

✏️ Worked example: spending 300 labels

You can afford 300 labelled cases. Traffic shares: refunds 40%, shipping 30%, account 20%, billing 8%, non-English cancellations 2%.

  • Proportional (random from logs): 120, 90, 60, 24 and 6 cases. Six cases at a 40% pass rate gives an error bar of ±39 points: useless.
  • Floor + proportional: 30 cases per slice first (150), then the other 150 split by share: 90, 75, 60, 42, 33. The smallest slice now has an error bar near ±17 points, enough to see it's broken.
  • Headline score, weighted back to traffic: 0.40 × 97% + 0.30 × 96% + 0.20 × 92% + 0.08 × 65% + 0.02 × 40% = 92%, the same number the random sample estimates, now with every slice visible.

Rule of thumb: every slice you want to make decisions about needs its own 30 to 100 cases. A slice with fewer is a smoke test, not a measurement.

Real numbers vary, but the pattern is general: random sampling mirrors traffic and starves rare cells; a floor per cell makes them measurable; synthetic-only sets skip the messy edge column and look easier than reality.

What you should see: with random sampling the overall score lands near 92% and the “Cancellations × Edge” cell gets only a handful of cases (resample a few times: sometimes none, sometimes six, with wildly different pass rates). Stratified sampling gives every cell a floor, so the 40% cell shows up with a usable sample, and the traffic-weighted score still lands near the truth. Synthetic only scores higher than reality and leaves the whole edge column blind, because generated questions tend to be clean and well-formed.

Synthetic data: cheap coverage, with known traps

When you have no traffic yet, or a cell is too rare in logs, ask an LLM to write cases. Three patterns:

  • From documents (for RAG): pick a passage, ask the model for a question it answers, and keep the passage as the label. Tools like Ragas build a graph of your documents and generate single-hop questions (one passage) and multi-hop ones (needs two), as Lesson 5.4 introduced.
  • From dimensions: Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's recipe. Write about 20 tuples by hand, one value per dimension, like (Plus member, refund, frustrated, multi-part). Have an LLM generate more tuples, then turn each tuple into a natural message in a separate prompt. Splitting the steps avoids the same phrasing over and over.
  • Evolving difficulty: take a simple case and ask the model to rewrite it harder, step by step: add a constraint, require two facts, add a distractor, make it ambiguous. WizardLM's Evol-Instruct did this to build training data; it works for eval cases too.

The traps are predictable:

TrapWhat happensFix

Adversarial and edge cases

Take real cases and perturb them on purpose. This is the CheckList idea from software testing: for each capability, write tests that the output should not change under (invariance) or should change in a known direction.

Attacks (prompt injection, jailbreaks, data extraction) are their own must-pass suite: Lesson 5.10.

Labels: reference answers and acceptance criteria

Each case needs a way to decide pass or fail. Write the cheapest label that's still correct:

  • Exact reference for closed answers: a label, a number, a JSON object (Lesson 5.9 shows field-by-field scoring).
  • Key facts for open answers: must_include: ["60 days"], must_not_include: ["30 days"].
  • Acceptance criteria when many answers are fine: “asks which item; does not promise a refund; under 80 words”. A judge (Lesson 5.3) or a human (5.8) checks them.
  • Expected behaviour, not text, for edge cases: “clarifies”, “refuses”, “escalates to a human”.

Also store metadata: the cell (intent, difficulty, language), the source (log ID, ticket, synthetic), who labelled it, and when. Without tags you can't slice; without a source you can't re-check the label.

Splits, overfitting the eval, and contamination

If you tune your prompt for three weeks against the same 200 cases, the prompt gradually fits those cases, quirks included, and the score stops predicting production. Same fix as in machine learning:

  • Dev set: the cases you look at, debug and iterate on every day.
  • Held-out test set: same distribution, never inspected while tuning, run only at milestones (“is this release really better?”). If the dev score climbs and the test score doesn't, you've overfit the eval.
  • Contamination is the model having seen the test cases during training. For public benchmarks it's a real risk (Lesson 5.11); for your own data the risk is leakage you cause: eval cases pasted into few-shot examples, or into fine-tuning sets. Keep a list of case IDs and check training data against it.

Versioning and keeping it fresh

Treat the dataset like code. Store it as JSONL in git (or a dataset tool with versions), add cases through reviewed pull requests, and record the dataset version next to every score; “87% on v12” and “84% on v13” aren't comparable. Retire cases that have passed for months (they cost money and tell you nothing), and keep feeding it from production: every week, sample new traces, run error analysis, and turn new failure types into new cases and new cells. A dataset that never changes slowly stops describing your users.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

pandas for stratified sampling from exported traces; your tracing or eval platform (Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust, Arize Phoenix) to turn traces into dataset items with versions; Ragas for document-based test set generation (TestsetGenerator(llm=..., embedding_model=...).generate_with_langchain_docs(docs, testset_size=10)); sentence-transformers for dedup; any LLM SDK for dimension-based generation.

import json, pandas as pd, anthropic
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

# 1) Stratified sample from logs: up to 30 per (intent, difficulty) cell
logs = pd.read_json("traces_scrubbed.jsonl", lines=True)
evalset = (logs.sample(frac=1, random_state=0)            # shuffle, then take the first 30 per cell
               .groupby(["intent", "difficulty"]).head(30))
print(evalset.groupby(["intent", "difficulty"]).size())  # spot the cells that are still thin

# 2) Fill thin cells synthetically: tuples first, then messages (two separate prompts)
client = anthropic.Anthropic()
tuples = [("Plus member", "cancellation", "Spanish", "frustrated")]   # write ~20 by hand first
for who, intent, lang, mood in tuples:
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-haiku-5-5", max_tokens=200,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Write one message a {mood} {who} would send to an outdoor-gear shop's support chat "
            f"about a {intent}, in {lang}. Write like a real customer on a phone: short, "
            "informal, maybe a typo. Don't use the shop's policy wording. Output only the message."}])
    print(json.dumps({"input": msg.content[0].text, "tags": [intent, lang, "synthetic"]}))

# 3) Drop near-duplicates before a human reviews them
qs = ["How long do refunds take?", "How long does a refund take?", "Do you ship to Canada?"]
emb = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2").encode(qs, normalize_embeddings=True)
sim = emb @ emb.T                                   # cosine similarity (vectors are normalized)
keep = [i for i in range(len(qs)) if not any(sim[i, j] > 0.9 for j in range(i))]

The similarity threshold depends on the embedding model: look at pairs just above and below it before trusting it. Model names change; check your provider's current list.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Read 100 traces (or, pre-launch, 50 synthetic ones from dimension tuples) and name the intents. One JSONL file with tags for intent and difficulty, 5–10 cases per cell, every bug report added the day it arrives. A dev/test split from day one costs nothing.

At scale

Dataset owners per product area, a written taxonomy, stratified sampling jobs from production traces every week, PII scrubbing pipelines, synthetic generation for new launches and languages, versioned datasets with changelogs, slice dashboards that alert when one cell drops, and held-out sets guarded from training pipelines.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you build the eval set for this assistant?” A senior answer:

  • Starts from error analysis of real traces to get a taxonomy of intents and failure types, then a coverage matrix.
  • Combines sources: stratified log samples, tickets and failure reports as regressions, expert cases, synthetic for thin cells, perturbations for robustness.
  • Says how labels are written (references, key facts, acceptance criteria) and reviewed by people.
  • Reports per-slice scores with error bars, keeps a held-out test set, guards against leakage, versions the data, and refreshes it from production failures.
🏭 In the real world
A capability × test-type matrix for NLP 🧪 Research · 2020 Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP Models with CheckList

How: a grid of capabilities (vocabulary, negation, robustness to typos…) by test types (minimum functionality, invariance, directional expectation), with templates to generate many cases per cell.

Why this method: held-out accuracy overestimated real performance; in a user study, practitioners using the matrix wrote twice as many tests and found almost three times as many bugs, and a team behind a commercial model found new bugs in an extensively tested system.

Test sets generated from your documents 🧰 Open source Ragas: testset generation

How: builds a knowledge graph from your documents, then generates queries with a set mix of single-hop and multi-hop synthesizers (the default is half single-hop specific, a quarter each of two multi-hop kinds).

Why this method: question-per-chunk generation gives easy, single-passage questions; a planned mix of query types covers the harder cases RAG systems fail on.

Making questions harder, step by step 🧪 Research · 2023 WizardLM: Evol-Instruct

How: starting from simple instructions, an LLM rewrites each one into progressively more complex versions; the authors also built a complexity-balanced test bed for evaluation.

Why this method: people struggle to write many high-complexity cases by hand; evolving seeds gives a controllable difficulty ladder.

A fresh copy of a famous benchmark 🏢 Scale AI · 2024 A Careful Examination of LLM Performance on Grade School Arithmetic (GSM1k)

How: commissioned 1,250 new problems matched to GSM8k's style and difficulty, then compared each model's score on old vs new.

Why this method: some model families dropped up to 8 points on the unseen copy, and the drop correlated with how likely a model was to reproduce GSM8k examples: evidence of contamination. Frontier models showed little overfitting.

Refreshing questions every month 🧪 Research · 2024 LiveBench

How: questions drawn from recent math competitions, arXiv papers, news and datasets, scored against objective answers, with new questions added monthly.

Why this method: a static test set leaks into training data over time; regular refresh keeps the eval measuring skill instead of memory, the same reason your own set needs new production cases.

🤯 Fun fact · the canary string

Every task file in Google's BIG-bench benchmark contains a “canary” string, and contributors are told never to edit it. The point: if a model can reproduce that unique string, its training data probably included the benchmark. It's a tripwire for contamination, and you can plant the same kind of marker in your private eval files.

Check yourself

1. One slice of your eval set has 25 cases and passes 80% of them. Roughly what's the 95% error bar on that slice?
1.96 × √(0.8 × 0.2 ÷ 25) = 1.96 × 0.08 ≈ ±15.7 points. The slice is somewhere between about 64% and 96%: fine for spotting a disaster, not for comparing two prompts. For decisions about a slice, give it more cases.
2. Pre-launch, your fully synthetic eval set passes 97%. In the first week, users writing in Spanish and users with typos complain a lot. What's the best fix to the eval?
The synthetic set was too clean: it never covered messy or non-English inputs, so it couldn't fail on them. More of the same prompt gives more of the same. Add the missing dimensions, fill them with real and perturbed cases, and watch those slices separately.
3. After three weeks of prompt tuning against the same 200 cases, the score went from 78% to 94%. Before you announce it, what should you do?
Weeks of tuning against one set fits the prompt to that set. A held-out set from the same distribution tells you how much of the gain is real. (Do re-check failing labels, but deleting failures to raise the score is the opposite of evaluation.)
📝 TL;DR
  • Design the dataset: name the dimensions (intent, difficulty, language, risk), build a coverage matrix, and give every slice you care about its own 30–100 cases.
  • Mix sources: stratified logs, tickets and failures, expert cases, synthetic for thin cells, perturbations for robustness. Report slice scores, and weight back to traffic for the headline.
  • Synthetic data is cheap but too easy, duplicated and too clean: generate from dimension tuples, dedup, filter copied wording, review labels.
  • Keep a held-out test set, prevent leakage into prompts and training, version the data, and refresh it from production failures.
