Phase 5 · Lesson 1

Why evals: eval-driven development & golden datasets

A demo works on the five questions you tried. A product works on the next ten thousand. Evals are how you find out which one you've built, with numbers instead of vibes.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The same prompt can give different outputs each time because the model samples its next token (Lesson 0.5).
  • Prompts are code: they live in files, have versions and get changed in pull requests (Lesson 1.5).
  • A RAG pipeline has several parts that can each fail: retrieval, context packing, generation (Lessons 3.4–3.6).
🎯 Hook

Rechat built Lucy, an AI assistant for real-estate agents. Early on, prompt tweaks gave fast progress. Then, as Lucy did more, quality plateaued. Fixing one failure caused another, like whack-a-mole, and the team had no view of how well it worked across tasks “beyond vibe checks”, as Hamel Husain, who worked with them, put it.

The way out wasn't a cleverer prompt. It was an evaluation system: scoped unit-test-style checks, logged traces that humans and models graded, and A/B tests for big changes. Once every change was measured, the team could iterate fast again. This phase is about building that system.

Numbers first: is 85% better than 80%?

Your support bot passes 16 of 20 test questions: 80%. You rewrite the prompt and rerun: 17 of 20, 85%. Ship it?

Test questions (n)Pass rateStandard error √(p(1−p)/n)95% range ≈ ±1.96 × SE

With 20 questions, “80%” really means “somewhere between about 62% and 98%”. One lucky or unlucky question moves the score by 5 points. If the new prompt is truly better (85% vs 80%), a 20-question eval shows it scoring higher in only about 6 runs out of 10: barely better than a coin flip (we simulated it; you can too, below).

💡 Big idea An eval is a test suite for behaviour you can't check with assert output == expected: a set of inputs, a way to grade each output, and a score you track over time. It turns “it feels better” into “it passes 412 of 450 cases, up from 391, and here are the 9 that got worse”. Without that, every change is a guess, and you can't tell a real improvement from noise.
🧠 Analogy: the chef's spoon vs the kitchen's checklist

A chef tasting one spoonful is a vibe check: fast, useful, and blind to the dish that goes out wrong on Saturday night. A good restaurant also has recipes with exact measurements (deterministic checks), a head chef who tastes a sample of every service (graded samples, human or model), and reviews from real diners (online signals). Nobody drops the spoon. They just stop relying on it alone.

Eval-driven development

It's test-driven development for AI features. When you find a failure, you first add it to the eval set (it fails), then change the prompt, model or retrieval until it passes, and check that nothing else got worse.

🎮 The loop

Anthropic's agent team splits evals into two kinds. Capability evals start with a low pass rate and give you a hill to climb (“what can it do?”). Regression evals should pass close to 100% and catch backsliding (“can it still do what it did?”). When a capability eval gets saturated, its cases graduate into the regression suite.

Golden datasets: where the examples come from

A golden dataset is your curated set of eval cases with the expected answer or the criteria a good answer must meet. Its quality caps the quality of every decision you make with it.

SourceWhy it's valuableWatch out for
Real logs (sampled traffic)The questions users actually ask, in their messy words and proportions.Personal data: scrub it before it goes into a test file.
Failures (bug reports, thumbs-down, escalations)Every past bug becomes a regression test, so it can't come back silently.Over-weighting rare disasters vs everyday cases: tag them.
Expert-written edge casesCover policy corners and high-stakes questions logs haven't hit yet.Experts write tidier questions than users do.

Each case is one line of a file, versioned in git next to the prompt:

{"id": "refund-plus-017", "input": "im a plus member, bought boots 45 days ago, can i still return??", "expected": "Yes: Plus members have 60 days.", "must_include": ["60 days"], "must_not_include": ["30 days"], "tags": ["refunds", "plus", "from-logs"], "source": "ticket 88123", "added": "2026-10-02"}

This is the introduction; Lesson 5.7 goes deep on building datasets: coverage, stratification, synthetic generation and adversarial cases. Two habits that separate seniors from beginners. Keep a held-out test split you don't look at while tuning, or you'll slowly fit your prompt to your eval. And re-read the labels: a wrong “expected” answer silently punishes a correct model.

✏️ Worked example: how many examples do you need?

Pass rate p = 0.80. Standard error (SE) = √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), the typical distance between the score you measure and the true rate.

  • n = 20: SE = √(0.16 ÷ 20) = 0.089, so the 95% range is ±1.96 × 8.9 ≈ ±17.5 points.
  • For ±5 points you need 1.96² × 0.16 ÷ 0.05² ≈ 246 examples; for ±2.5 points, about 984. Halving the error bar costs 4× the examples.
  • Telling 80% from 85% (two separate samples, 80% chance of detecting it): n ≈ (1.96 + 0.84)² × (0.16 + 0.1275) ÷ 0.05² ≈ 900 per version.

Two ways out of buying 900 labels. Run both versions on the same questions and look at the per-question differences (a paired comparison: much less noise, Lesson 5.6). And early on, chase big effects: Anthropic notes that 20–50 tasks from real failures is a great start, because early changes move the score a lot. Small effects need big evals.

Error analysis: read the failures

A score tells you how much is broken. Error analysis tells you what. The method is plain and it works:

  1. Pull 50–100 failing or low-rated traces (the full record: input, retrieved context, tool calls, output).
  2. Read each one and write a short open note on what went wrong. Don't start from a fixed list like “hallucination”: let the categories come from the data (bottom-up).
  3. Group the notes into a handful of categories and count them. A spreadsheet pivot table is enough.
  4. Fix the biggest category first, and turn it into eval cases or a check so you can watch it shrink.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You can start with pytest and a JSONL file. Dedicated tools (promptfoo, Braintrust, LangSmith, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, DeepEval, Inspect) add dataset versioning, LLM judges, side-by-side diffs and CI integration; Lesson 5.6 compares them. The habit matters more than the tool.

# evals/test_support_bot.py: run with  pytest evals/ -q
import json, math, pytest
from app.bot import answer          # your real pipeline: retrieval + prompt + model

CASES = [json.loads(line) for line in open("evals/golden.jsonl")]

@pytest.mark.parametrize("case", CASES, ids=[c["id"] for c in CASES])
def test_golden(case):
    out = answer(case["input"]).lower()
    for s in case.get("must_include", []):
        assert s.lower() in out, f"missing {s!r}"
    for s in case.get("must_not_include", []):
        assert s.lower() not in out, f"should not say {s!r}"

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):                 # 95% interval for a pass rate, report it with the score
    p, d = k / n, 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

print(wilson(16, 20))   # (0.584, 0.919): "80%" on 20 cases is a wide claim

String checks like these only work for answers with a known key fact. For open-ended answers you'll grade with metrics (5.2) or an LLM judge (5.3), but the file, the loop and the error bars stay the same.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

One JSONL file of 30–50 cases pulled from real conversations and every bug you've seen, run by pytest on each prompt change. One afternoon a week reading 50 traces with a domain expert and counting failure categories. That alone beats most teams.

At scale

Thousands of cases per feature, tagged by category and risk, with owners. Separate capability, regression and safety suites; held-out test sets; statistical reporting with confidence intervals; dedicated labelling teams; GitHub reports running over 4,000 offline tests for Copilot, most in CI, plus internal canary rollouts.

🎤 Interview angle

“How do you know your LLM feature works, and how would you know if a change made it worse?” A senior answer:

  • Defines success for the task first (what a good answer must contain or do), not a generic “quality” score.
  • Builds a golden set from real traffic and past failures, versioned, tagged, with a held-out split.
  • Grades with the cheapest reliable method per criterion: code checks, then metrics, then an LLM judge calibrated on human labels.
  • Reports scores with error bars, compares versions on the same cases, and runs it in CI as a gate.
  • Does error analysis to decide what to fix, and feeds production failures back into the dataset.
🏭 In the real world
Evals broke a quality plateau 🏢 Rechat · 2024 Your AI Product Needs Evals (Hamel Husain)

How: three levels: scoped assertion tests run on every change, logged traces reviewed by humans and an LLM judge, and A/B tests for major changes.

Why this method: vibe checks had stopped working once the assistant's surface area grew; every fix broke something else. Measured iteration restored fast progress.

Error analysis found three problems behind most failures 🏢 NurtureBoss · 2025 A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products

How: a spreadsheet of conversations with open notes on what went wrong, an LLM to suggest a taxonomy, then each row labelled and counted.

Why this method: three issues (conversation flow, missed handoffs, date handling) caused over 60% of problems; targeted tests lifted date handling from 33% to 95%.

Start with 20–50 tasks from real failures 🏢 Anthropic · 2026 Demystifying evals for AI agents

How: build the first eval set early from real failures, separate capability evals (low pass rate, to climb) from regression evals (near 100%, to protect), and read the transcripts.

Why this method: early changes have large effects, so small sets are enough to see them; waiting for “hundreds of tasks” just delays measurement.

Error bars for evals 🧪 Research · 2024 Adding Error Bars to Evals (Evan Miller, Anthropic)

How: treats eval questions as a sample from a bigger population and gives formulas for standard errors, clustered questions, paired model comparisons and sample-size planning.

Why this method: many reported “improvements” are within noise; paired comparisons and power analysis tell you whether a difference is real and how many questions you need.

🤯 Fun fact · even famous benchmarks have wrong answers

When researchers re-checked MMLU, one of the most cited LLM benchmarks, they estimated that 6.49% of its questions contain errors, and 57% of the questions they analysed in the virology subset had problems (MMLU-Redux, 2024). If a public benchmark can be that wrong, so can your golden set: re-read your labels.

Check yourself

1. Your eval has 300 cases and the bot passes 90%. Roughly what's the 95% error bar on that score?
SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 300) = 0.0173, and 1.96 × 1.73 ≈ 3.4 points. A new prompt scoring 92% on a separate sample isn't clearly better; compare both on the same cases.
2. Thumbs-down rate doubled this week. What should you do first?
You don't know what's broken yet. Error analysis finds the category (maybe a new product the docs don't cover, maybe a retrieval bug) and tells you which fix and which eval to add. A bigger model or a generic metric is a guess.
3. You're starting the golden dataset for a new internal HR assistant. Best first source of cases?
Real questions match your users' wording and topics; failures become regression tests; an expert makes the labels trustworthy. Public benchmarks measure general ability, not your task. Synthetic cases help later for coverage, after a spot-check against real ones.
📝 TL;DR
  • An eval = inputs + a grading method + a tracked score. Vibe checks are a start, not a measurement.
  • Eval-driven development: add the failure as a case, fix it, check nothing else regressed, repeat.
  • Golden sets come from real logs and failures first, expert edge cases and synthetic data second; version them and keep a held-out split.
  • Report error bars: ±1.96·√(p(1−p)/n). 20 cases can't tell 80% from 85%; compare versions on the same cases.
  • Error analysis (read, note, categorize, count) tells you what to fix next.
