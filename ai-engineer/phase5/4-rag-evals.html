Phase 5 · Lesson 4

Evaluating RAG: retrieval & faithfulness metrics

A wrong RAG answer has two possible culprits: the retriever didn't find the right text, or the generator didn't use it properly. Measure them separately, or you'll keep fixing the wrong half.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): retrieve the top-k chunks for a question, put them in the prompt, generate (Lessons 3.1, 3.6).
  • Retrievers: dense (embeddings), BM25 (keywords) and hybrid; rerankers reorder the results (Lessons 2.4, 3.4).
  • LLM judges grade open-ended outputs and must be calibrated against humans (Lesson 5.3).
🎯 Hook

Legal research vendors marketed their RAG tools as “hallucination-free” or as avoiding hallucinations. In 2024, Stanford researchers ran the first preregistered evaluation of them, with a dataset designed to probe exactly where such systems break. Lexis+ AI and Thomson Reuters' Westlaw AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI each hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time.

Better than a general chatbot, but far from “free”. RAG reduces hallucination; it doesn't remove it. The only way to know your rate is to measure it, part by part.

Numbers first: two stages multiply

Suppose the right chunk is in the top 5 for 80% of questions, and when it is, the generator answers correctly and faithfully 90% of the time.

OutcomeShare of questionsWhat to fix
Right chunk retrieved, good answer0.80 × 0.90 = 72%Nothing
Right chunk retrieved, bad answer0.80 × 0.10 = 8%The generator: prompt, grounding, model (3.5)
Right chunk missing20%The retriever: chunking, hybrid search, reranking (3.3–3.4)

An end-to-end score of “72% correct” hides that retrieval causes more than twice as many failures as generation. Swapping in a bigger model would attack the smaller problem.

💡 Big idea Evaluate RAG as a pipeline. Retrieval metrics ask “did we fetch the text that contains the answer, near the top?”. Generation metrics ask “given that text, is the answer supported by it (faithful), and does it answer the question (relevant)?”. Then check end to end against reference answers. Each failure points to a different fix.
🧠 Analogy: the librarian and the writer

A report is wrong. Either the librarian brought the wrong books (retrieval), or the writer misread them or made things up (generation). You check the librarian by looking at which books came back for each request. You check the writer by asking, sentence by sentence, “which book says that?”. A good editor checks both, because firing the writer won't fix a bad librarian.

The RAG triad

TruLens (from TruEra) introduced the “RAG triad”: three reference-free checks, one per edge of the pipeline. RAGAS (Retrieval Augmented Generation Assessment) proposed a similar trio in 2023. None of the three needs a reference answer, so you can run them on production traffic.

MetricQuestion it answersNeeds a reference?How it's usually computed
Context relevance (context precision)Are the retrieved chunks relevant to the question, and are the relevant ones ranked first?No (or labelled chunks)Judge rates each chunk; RAGAS context precision averages precision@k at each relevant position.
Faithfulness (groundedness)Is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context?NoSplit the answer into claims; a judge or an NLI (natural language inference) model checks each against the context. Score = supported ÷ total.
Answer relevanceDoes the answer address the question that was asked?NoJudge rating, or RAGAS: generate questions from the answer and compare their embeddings to the real question.
Context recallDoes the retrieved context contain everything needed?YesSplit the reference answer into claims; check each can be found in the context.
Answer correctnessIs the answer right?YesCompare to a reference answer: exact checks, or a judge for factual agreement.

Retrieval metrics, precisely

For a question, you need the ranked list your retriever returned and a label for which chunks are relevant (the “qrels”, query relevance judgments). Then:

MetricDefinitionTells you
Recall@kRelevant chunks in the top k ÷ all relevant chunksDid the answer make it into the prompt at all? The most important retrieval number for RAG.
Hit rate@k1 if any relevant chunk is in the top k, else 0A simpler recall for single-answer questions.
Precision@kRelevant chunks in the top k ÷ kHow much noise you're paying for in tokens and distraction.
MRR (mean reciprocal rank)Average of 1 ÷ (rank of the first relevant chunk); 0 if noneHow high the first good chunk lands.
nDCG@k (normalized discounted cumulative gain)Σ gain ÷ log₂(rank + 1) over the top k, divided by the same sum for the ideal ordering. Gains can be graded (2 = answers it, 1 = partly relevant).Ranking quality with graded relevance; the standard IR (information retrieval) benchmark metric (BEIR reports nDCG@10).
✏️ Worked example: one ranked list

Top 5 results, relevant = ✓: ✗ ✓ ✗ ✓ ✗. There are 3 relevant chunks in the whole corpus.

  • Precision@5 = 2 ÷ 5 = 0.40. Recall@5 = 2 ÷ 3 = 0.67.
  • Reciprocal rank = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.50 (first relevant at rank 2).
  • DCG@5 (binary gains) = 1/log₂3 + 1/log₂5 = 0.631 + 0.431 = 1.062. Ideal: 3 relevant at ranks 1–3 = 1 + 0.631 + 0.5 = 2.131. nDCG@5 = 1.062 ÷ 2.131 = 0.50.

Moving the relevant chunk from rank 4 to rank 1 doesn't change precision or recall at 5, but lifts nDCG and MRR. That matters when you pack only the top few chunks, or when the model pays most attention to the first ones (Lesson 1.4).

Building a labelled retrieval set

  • Start from real questions (logs, tickets) and have someone who knows the docs mark which passages answer each. 50–100 questions is a useful first set.
  • Label documents or answer spans, not chunk IDs. Chunk IDs change every time you re-chunk (Lesson 3.3). Store “doc 17, the sentence containing ‘60 days’” and map it to whatever chunks contain it.
  • Synthetic questions help, with a known bias. Asking an LLM to write a question each chunk answers is cheap (RAGAS and LlamaIndex can generate test sets), but those questions copy the chunk's wording, which flatters keyword search. Mix in real, messy questions.
  • Include unanswerable questions, where the right behaviour is “I don't know” (Lesson 3.5).
  • Use an LLM to pre-label, humans to verify. ARES showed a few hundred human labels can correct a cheaper automated judge's estimates.

Faithfulness: “which chunk says that?”

Faithfulness is the RAG version of “no hallucinations”: every factual claim in the answer must be supported by the retrieved context. The standard recipe, used by RAGAS and DeepEval, is two LLM steps: (1) split the answer into atomic claims; (2) for each claim, decide if the context supports it. Score = supported claims ÷ total claims.

✏️ Worked example: four claims

Context: “Plus members can request a refund within 60 days of delivery. Refunds go back to the original payment method within 5 business days.”

Answer: “Plus members have 60 days to return items. The refund goes to your original card within 5 business days. You can also exchange boots in any store. Exchanges are free.”

  • 60 days for Plus: supported ✅. Original payment method in 5 days: supported ✅ (“card” is a fair reading of “payment method”).
  • Exchange in any store: not in the context ❌. Exchanges are free: not in the context ❌.
  • Faithfulness = 2 ÷ 4 = 0.50, even if stores really do take exchanges.

That last point is the key subtlety: faithful ≠ correct. A claim can be true but unsupported (the model used outside knowledge, which you can't trace or trust), or faithful but wrong (the retrieved doc is outdated). Track faithfulness and correctness.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

RAGAS (faithfulness, context precision/recall, factual correctness, test-set generation), DeepEval (FaithfulnessMetric, AnswerRelevancyMetric, contextual precision/recall in pytest), TruLens (the RAG triad), Arize Phoenix and Langfuse (run these evaluators on traces), promptfoo's context-faithfulness and context-recall assertions. For classic IR metrics, ranx or pytrec_eval, or ten lines of Python.

# RAGAS: score a batch of RAG runs (docs.ragas.io, "Evaluate a simple RAG system")
from ragas import EvaluationDataset, evaluate
from ragas.llms import LangchainLLMWrapper
from ragas.metrics import LLMContextRecall, Faithfulness, FactualCorrectness
from langchain_openai import ChatOpenAI

rows = [{"user_input": q, "retrieved_contexts": ctx, "response": ans, "reference": ref}
        for q, ctx, ans, ref in my_rag_runs]          # run your pipeline over the eval set first
result = evaluate(
    dataset=EvaluationDataset.from_list(rows),
    metrics=[LLMContextRecall(), Faithfulness(), FactualCorrectness()],
    llm=LangchainLLMWrapper(ChatOpenAI(model=JUDGE_MODEL)),   # pin the judge model
)
print(result)   # e.g. {'context_recall': ..., 'faithfulness': ..., 'factual_correctness': ...}

# Retrieval metrics need no LLM at all
def recall_at_k(ranked_ids, relevant_ids, k):
    return len(set(ranked_ids[:k]) & set(relevant_ids)) / len(relevant_ids)

def mrr(ranked_ids, relevant_ids):
    return next((1 / (i + 1) for i, d in enumerate(ranked_ids) if d in relevant_ids), 0.0)
# DeepEval: the same faithfulness idea as a pytest-style assertion
from deepeval import evaluate
from deepeval.metrics import FaithfulnessMetric
from deepeval.test_case import LLMTestCase

case = LLMTestCase(input=question, actual_output=answer, retrieval_context=retrieved_chunks)
evaluate(test_cases=[case], metrics=[FaithfulnessMetric(threshold=0.7, include_reason=True)])

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

50 real questions with labelled source passages: track recall@5 and MRR on every chunking or search change (free, seconds to run). Add a faithfulness judge on the same 50 answers. That pair tells you which half to fix.

At scale

Retrieval evals per corpus and language, graded labels and nDCG, labels tied to document versions, reference-free faithfulness and relevance scored on a sample of live traffic, and dashboards that break failures down by stage. Re-indexing (new embedding model, new chunker) must pass the retrieval eval before it ships.

🎤 Interview angle

“Users say your RAG assistant gives wrong answers. How do you find out why, and how do you prove the fix worked?” A senior answer:

  • Pulls failing examples and looks at the retrieved chunks for each: was the answer in the context or not?
  • Builds a labelled question set and measures retrieval (recall@k, MRR or nDCG) separately from generation (faithfulness, answer relevance), then end-to-end correctness.
  • Maps each failure type to its fix: missing context → chunking, hybrid search, reranking, query rewriting; unfaithful answers → grounding prompt, citations, abstaining, a stronger model.
  • Calibrates the faithfulness judge on human labels, and re-runs the same eval after the fix, with error bars.
🏭 In the real world
Reference-free RAG metrics 🧰 Open source Ragas: Automated Evaluation of RAG (2023)

How: LLM-based metrics for faithfulness (claims supported by context), answer relevance and context relevance, without human-annotated ground truth.

Why this method: faster evaluation cycles for RAG pipelines, especially when you don't have labelled answers yet.

Cheap judges, corrected by a few human labels 🧪 Stanford · 2023 ARES: An Automated RAG Evaluation System

How: fine-tunes small LM judges on synthetic data for context relevance, faithfulness and answer relevance, then uses prediction-powered inference with a few hundred human annotations to correct their errors.

Why this method: judges are cheap but imperfect; a small human-labelled set turns their scores into estimates with honest confidence intervals.

Measuring vendors' “hallucination-free” claims 🧪 Stanford · 2024 Hallucination-Free? Assessing AI Legal Research Tools

How: a preregistered dataset of legal queries, with each response classified as accurate, incomplete or hallucinated by a clear typology.

Why this method: closed RAG products can only be checked from the outside; a careful eval found 17–33% hallucination rates despite marketing claims.

nDCG@10 across 18 retrieval datasets 🧪 Research · 2021 BEIR: zero-shot IR benchmark

How: evaluates lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction and reranking retrievers on 18 datasets with the official TREC evaluation tool and nDCG@10.

Why this method: one in-domain test set hides generalization failures; testing across domains showed BM25 is a robust baseline that many dense models underperform out of domain.

🤯 Fun fact · the 1/log₂ in nDCG

The discount in nDCG, 1 ÷ log₂(rank + 1), means rank 1 counts fully, rank 3 counts half (1 ÷ log₂4), and rank 15 a quarter (1 ÷ log₂16). It's a model of a patient reader who keeps scanning but pays less attention as they go. For RAG, the “reader” is the LLM, and long-context studies (Lesson 1.4) suggest it, too, attends unevenly to what you pack into the prompt.

Check yourself

1. Three questions. The first relevant chunk is at rank 1 for Q1, rank 3 for Q2, and not retrieved for Q3. What's the MRR?
MRR = (1/1 + 1/3 + 0) ÷ 3 = 1.333 ÷ 3 = 0.44. Q3 contributes 0 because nothing relevant came back.
2. Faithfulness is 0.95, but answer correctness against references is only 0.60. Where's the problem most likely?
High faithfulness means the answers stick to the context. If they're still wrong, the context is the issue: the right passage wasn't retrieved (low context recall) or the source docs are stale.
3. You re-chunked the corpus and recall@5 on your labelled set dropped from 0.82 to 0.05 overnight. Most likely cause?
A collapse to near zero is an eval bug, not a retrieval bug. Labels keyed to chunk IDs break on every re-chunk; keying them to documents plus the answer text keeps the eval valid across chunking experiments.
📝 TL;DR
  • Evaluate retrieval and generation separately, then end to end: each failure has a different fix.
  • Retrieval: recall@k first, then MRR and nDCG for ranking quality; label documents or spans, not chunk IDs.
  • Generation: faithfulness = supported claims ÷ claims; answer relevance = does it address the question.
  • The RAG triad (context relevance, faithfulness, answer relevance) needs no references, so it works on live traffic.
  • Faithful ≠ correct: track correctness against references too.
