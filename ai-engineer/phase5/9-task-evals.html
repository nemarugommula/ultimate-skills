Phase 5 · Lesson 9

Evals by task: classification, extraction, summarization, code, chat

A practical cookbook. For each kind of task an AI engineer ships, what “correct” means, which metric measures it, which cheap checks catch most failures, and which tempting metric to avoid.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Precision = of what you flagged, how much was right; recall = of what you should have flagged, how much you caught; F1 is their harmonic mean. pass@k grades code by running it (all in Lesson 5.2).
  • LLM-as-judge grades open-ended outputs with a rubric, after calibration against humans (Lessons 5.3 and 5.8). Faithfulness = share of an answer's claims supported by the source (Lesson 5.4).
  • Structured output: asking the model for JSON matching a schema (Lesson 1.3). Latency and tokens drive cost (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2022, researchers compared news summaries from GPT-3 (prompted with just a task description) against BRIO, a model fine-tuned on the CNN news dataset. On ROUGE-L, the standard word-overlap metric, GPT-3 scored 24.7 against BRIO's 31.4, lower even than the trivial baseline of copying an article's first three sentences reported in earlier work.

Then they asked people. Annotators picked GPT-3's summary as the best for 58% of CNN articles, BRIO's for 36%. The metric was measuring similarity to the dataset's reference summaries, not quality. For every task in this lesson, the question is the same: what property do users actually care about, and what measures that?

Your system pulls 6 fields from each invoice (number, vendor, two dates, currency, total). On 200 invoices:

💡 Big idea There's no universal LLM metric. For each task, define correct in the user's terms, measure it with the cheapest grader that's still trustworthy (code first, then judges, then humans), report it per slice, and always put latency, cost and tokens next to quality.
🧠 Analogy: the decathlon

A decathlon has ten events, and each one is scored its own way: a stopwatch for the 100 m, a tape measure for the long jump, a bar height for the pole vault. Nobody times the shot put. Your AI product is a decathlon too: the router, the extractor, the summarizer and the chat each need their own measuring tool, and the total only makes sense if each event is measured properly.

Classification

Lesson 5.2 covered precision and recall for one class. Real routers have many, and the rare ones are often the costly ones.

✏️ Worked example: 91% accuracy, one class broken

A ticket router, 100 test tickets: 80 billing, 15 technical, 5 legal. Rows are the true class, columns the prediction:

→ billing→ tech→ legalrecall
billing (80)78200.975
tech (15)31200.80
legal (5)4010.20
precision0.9180.8571.00
  • Accuracy = (78 + 12 + 1) ÷ 100 = 91%. Micro-F1 (pooling every decision) is also 0.91 for single-label tasks.
  • Per-class F1: billing 0.945, tech 0.828, legal 2 × 1.0 × 0.2 ÷ 1.2 = 0.333. Macro-F1 (the plain average over classes) = 0.70.
  • Four of five legal tickets went to billing. If those are lawsuits, macro-F1 and legal recall are the numbers to gate on.

Two more things for LLM classifiers. Count the invalid-label rate (outputs that aren't one of your labels; constrain with structured output or enums). And if you use a confidence (the model's stated score, or the probability of the label token), check calibration: bucket predictions by confidence and compare with accuracy in each bucket. A router that says “95% sure” and is right 70% of the time will auto-route things it should escalate. The math is in Math for AI 5.6b.

Information extraction

Score per field, not per JSON string. For each field compare the prediction with the gold value and decide:

  • Correct: matches (after normalization). Counts as a true positive.
  • Wrong: both present but different. Counts as a false positive and a false negative (common convention).
  • Missing: gold has a value, prediction is null or absent. False negative.
  • Hallucinated: gold is null (the document has no PO number), prediction invents one. False positive, and often the most dangerous error.

Normalization decides whether a difference is real: trim and lowercase strings, strip punctuation, parse numbers (“$1,250.00” = 1250), convert dates to ISO 8601 (YYYY-MM-DD), map currency symbols to codes. Be careful: “03/04/2026” is March 4 in the US and 3 April in much of the world, so an honest normalizer refuses to guess. Also check the schema (required keys, types, formats) and business rules across fields (subtotal + tax = total; due date after invoice date). For lists like line items, align predicted and gold items first (by description, or with an assignment algorithm), then score fields inside each pair.

    Summarization

    A good summary is faithful (says nothing the source doesn't), covers the key points, and is short enough. Measure each:

    • Faithfulness: split the summary into atomic claims and check each against the source, the same recipe as RAG faithfulness (Lesson 5.4). FActScore applies it to long text with a knowledge source.
    • Coverage: list the 3–7 key points a summary must contain (written by an expert once per document, or extracted by an LLM and reviewed), then ask a judge which ones are present. Score = points covered ÷ points required.
    • Length and compression: words in the summary ÷ words in the source, plus a hard limit check. Judges and people both reward length, so control for it (Lesson 5.3).
    • Quality rubric for coherence and usefulness, ideally pairwise against the current version.

    Why not ROUGE? It counts overlapping words with one reference summary. Many good summaries use other words (the hook), and a summary can share most words with the reference while flipping one crucial fact. SummEval's re-evaluation of 14 metrics against expert and crowd ratings is the standard reference if you need to argue the point.

    Open question answering

    Grade correctness against a reference answer with a judge that accepts paraphrases (“semantically equivalent, every fact consistent”, Lesson 5.3), or with key-fact checks. Then measure abstention: on questions the system can't answer (not in the docs, out of scope), saying “I don't know” is the correct output.

    ✏️ Worked example: accuracy vs abstention

    100 answerable questions plus 20 unanswerable ones.

    • Version 1 answers all 120: 85 of the 100 right, and invents answers for all 20 unanswerable. Accuracy on answerable 85%; wrong answers given: 35 of 120 (29%).
    • Version 2 abstains on 18 unanswerable and on 8 hard answerable ones; answers 80 of the remaining 92 correctly. Accuracy on answerable 80%, but wrong answers given: 12 + 2 = 14 (12%), and abstention on unanswerable 90%.

    Which is better depends on the cost of a confident wrong answer. Report correct, wrong and abstained separately, never just “accuracy”.

    Code generation

    Run it. Hidden unit tests (that the model never saw) decide correctness; pass@k from Lesson 5.2 handles sampling. Engineering details that matter:

    • Sandbox every execution: a container with no network, CPU and memory limits, a timeout, and a throwaway filesystem. Generated code can delete files or loop forever.
    • For changes to an existing repo, the SWE-bench pattern: tests that failed before and must pass after (the fix works) plus tests that passed before and must still pass (nothing broke). SWE-bench stores them as FAIL_TO_PASS and PASS_TO_PASS and runs them in Docker.
    • Cheap extra checks: it compiles, lint and type checks pass, no forbidden imports or secrets, diff size reasonable. A judge can grade readability, but only after the tests pass. Agentic coding is Lesson 5.5.

    Translation and rewriting

    For translation, learned metrics trained on human quality ratings (COMET and its successors) track human judgment far better than word overlap; the 2022 WMT metrics task, which scores metrics against expert ratings, titled its results “Stop Using BLEU”. chrF (character-level overlap) is a cheap fallback. For rewriting (simplify, change tone, shorten), check constraints with code (length, reading level, required terms and names kept, no new numbers) and meaning preservation with a judge or entailment check: does the rewrite say the same thing as the original, no more, no less?

    Multi-turn chat

    Single-turn evals miss the failures of conversations. A 2025 study simulated over 200,000 conversations and found top models performed 39% worse on average when the same task arrived in pieces over several turns: they guessed early and didn't recover. Evaluate at two levels:

    • Per turn: is each reply correct given the history so far? Replay real conversations up to turn N and grade turn N.
    • Conversation: was the user's goal achieved, in how many turns, with or without escalation? Do rules from the system prompt still hold at turn 12? Does the persona stay consistent? Does it remember what the user said in turn 2?
    • Simulated users: an LLM plays a user with a goal, a persona and hidden facts, and talks to your bot until done; a grader checks the outcome (for agents, the end state of the database, Lesson 5.5). Run several trials per scenario, because conversations vary.

    Tool use and function calling

    Grade the call, not just the final message. Was a tool needed at all (calling a tool for “hi” is a failure)? Was it the right tool? Are the arguments right: required ones present, types correct, values acceptable? The Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard parses calls into a syntax tree and matches the function name and each parameter against possible answers, strictly on types; it also executes some calls and tests “relevance detection”, when no provided function fits. Multi-step tool use and trajectories are Lesson 5.5.

    Voice

    Speech recognition is graded with word error rate: WER = (substitutions + deletions + insertions) ÷ words in the reference. Reference “cancel my order for tuesday” (5 words), heard “cancel the order for tuesday please”: one substitution and one insertion, WER = 2 ÷ 5 = 40%. Normalize first (case, punctuation, numbers, contractions): the Whisper team found naive formatting differences inflated WER by up to 50% on some datasets. For voice agents, also measure latency to the first audio of the reply, interruption handling, and task success over the whole call.

    Latency, cost and tokens are eval metrics

    Every eval run should record, per case: input and output tokens, cost, time to first token, total latency, errors and timeouts. Then report p50 and p95 latency (the median, and the time 95% of requests finish under) and cost per successful task:

    ✏️ Worked example: the cheaper model isn't always cheaper
    • Model A: $0.002 per request, succeeds 80% of the time → $0.002 ÷ 0.80 = $0.0025 per success, plus a human fixing 20 tickets in 100.
    • Model B: $0.006 per request, 95% success → $0.006 ÷ 0.95 = $0.0063 per success, and 5 human fixes in 100.
    • If a human fix costs $2, A costs $0.002 + 0.20 × $2 = $0.40 per ticket; B costs $0.006 + 0.05 × $2 = $0.106. Prices illustrative; the method is what matters.

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Week one

    Pick one primary metric per feature and its cheapest grader: schema + field F1 for extraction, hidden tests for code, macro-F1 for the router, a calibrated binary judge for summaries. Log tokens and latency in the same run. One config file, run on every prompt change.

    At scale

    A metrics catalogue per task type with owners, shared graders with their own tests, per-slice dashboards, learned metrics and judges calibrated per domain and language, simulated-user suites for chat, sandboxed execution farms for code, and cost and latency budgets enforced as release gates.

    🎤 Interview angle

    “How would you evaluate this feature?” (pick any: an extractor, a summarizer, a voice agent). A senior answer:

    • Defines correct in the user's terms for that task, and names the metric that measures it and the one they would not use (accuracy on imbalanced classes, ROUGE, BLEU, raw WER).
    • Layers graders: deterministic checks first, then reference metrics, then a calibrated judge, then human spot checks for high stakes.
    • Handles the task's special cases: normalization and hallucinated fields, abstention, sandboxing, multi-turn simulation, tool-argument matching.
    • Reports latency, cost per successful task and tokens alongside quality, per slice, with error bars.
    🏭 In the real world
    Metrics judged against experts 🧪 Research · 2022 WMT22 Metrics Shared Task: Stop Using BLEU

    How: every submitted translation metric was scored on how well it correlates with expert human ratings (MQM error annotations) across news, social, e-commerce and chat.

    Why this method: the organisers concluded neural fine-tuned metrics are better than lexical overlap and robust across domains, hence the title.

    Function calls matched by syntax tree 🧪 UC Berkeley · 2024 Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard

    How: parses each generated call, checks the function name, that required parameters are present and no invented ones are used, and matches types and values strictly; some categories execute the call; relevance detection tests when no function fits.

    Why this method: string comparison would fail equivalent calls with reordered arguments, and a judge is too lenient on types; parsing makes tool-use grading exact and cheap.

    Conversations, not single prompts 🧪 Research · 2025 LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation

    How: split fully specified tasks into pieces revealed over several turns, simulated more than 200,000 conversations across six generation tasks, and compared with single-turn performance.

    Why this method: every top model tested did significantly worse in multi-turn, 39% on average, mostly from unreliability: early assumptions they didn't recover from. Single-turn evals can't see this.

    Normalize before you count errors 🏢 OpenAI · 2022 Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision (Whisper)

    How: built a text normalizer by inspecting where naive WER penalised innocuous differences (formatting, contractions, numbers) and released it for comparable evaluation.

    Why this method: transcripts humans would call correct still had high WER; on several datasets normalization cut WER by up to 50%. The authors flag the risk of overfitting the normalizer to their own model's style.

    Atomic facts for long-form factuality 🧪 Research · 2023 FActScore

    How: splits a generated biography into atomic facts and reports the share supported by a knowledge source; an automated estimator does the same with retrieval and a strong model.

    Why this method: long texts mix true and false statements, so a binary “factual?” label is too coarse; ChatGPT scored 58%, and the automated version came within 2% of human estimates at a fraction of the cost.

    🤯 Fun fact · WER can exceed 100%

    Because insertions count as errors, a recognizer that hears extra words can score worse than one that outputs nothing. Reference “yes” (1 word), heard “yes yes I do”: three insertions, WER = 3 ÷ 1 = 300%. jiwer's own docs note that an empty reference with a two-word hypothesis gives a WER of 2. It's a reminder that every metric has a definition, and the definition, not the name, is what you're optimizing.

    Check yourself

    1. Reference transcript: “book a table for four at seven” (7 words). The recognizer hears “book table for for at seven pm”. What's the WER?
    Align: book ✓, “a” deleted (1 D), table ✓, for ✓, “four” → “for” (1 S), at ✓, seven ✓, “pm” inserted (1 I). WER = (1 + 1 + 1) ÷ 7 = 0.43. Note that normalization wouldn't save “four” → “for”: that one changes the meaning.
    2. Your extraction prompt change raises schema-valid outputs from 92% to 100%. The PM wants to ship. What do you check first?
    A schema check says the shape is right, not the values. Forcing valid JSON can even push the model to fill fields it should leave null. Field-level scoring with normalization, and a look at false positives on null fields, tells you whether the data is right.
    3. A support chatbot scores 94% on your single-turn eval, but users say it “forgets” and contradicts itself in longer chats. Best next eval?
    Forgetting and self-contradiction only happen across turns. Simulated-user conversations (plus replayed real ones) test memory, consistency and recovery; repeated trials show how reliable it is, as in Lesson 5.5.
    📝 TL;DR
    • Per task, define correct in user terms: macro-F1 and per-class recall for routers; normalized field F1 plus schema and business rules for extraction; faithfulness and coverage for summaries; correctness and abstention for QA; hidden tests in a sandbox for code.
    • Learned metrics or judges for translation and rewriting; simulated multi-turn conversations for chat; name and argument matching for tool calls; normalized WER and latency for voice.
    • Avoid tempting proxies: accuracy on imbalanced data, whole-JSON exact match, ROUGE, BLEU, raw WER, single-turn-only scores.
    • Latency (p50/p95), tokens and cost per successful task are first-class eval metrics, recorded in the same run.
