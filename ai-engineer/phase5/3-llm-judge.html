Phase 5 · Lesson 3

LLM-as-judge: rubrics, pairwise & judge bias

When no formula can tell a good answer from a bad one, ask another model to grade it. That works surprisingly well, as long as you treat the judge as an instrument that must be calibrated, not an oracle.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Overlap and embedding metrics can't judge open-ended answers (Lesson 5.2).
  • Models reason better when asked to think step by step before answering: chain-of-thought, CoT (Lesson 1.2).
  • You can make a model return JSON that matches a schema (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2023, the team behind MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena compared GPT-4's verdicts on chatbot answers with expert humans'. On pairs where nobody called a tie, GPT-4 agreed with the humans 85% of the time, more than the humans agreed with each other (81%).

The same paper measured the catch. Shown two answers, Claude-v1 as a judge picked whichever came first in 75% of cases. Even GPT-4 gave the same verdict after the two answers were swapped only 65% of the time. A judge that good and that biased at once is exactly what you have to learn to use.

Numbers first: why teams use judges

You need to grade 1,000 support answers for helpfulness and correctness every time the prompt changes.

💡 Big idea An LLM judge is a measuring instrument. Like a new thermometer, it's only trustworthy after you've checked it against a reference, here a set of answers labelled by a domain expert. Measure agreement, find its biases, fix the prompt or the protocol, and re-check whenever the judge model or the rubric changes.
🧠 Analogy: the talent-show judge

A talent-show judge who sees acts in a fixed order tends to remember the first one more fondly (position bias), is impressed by acts that go on longer (verbosity bias), and quietly prefers performers from their own school (self-preference). Shows fight this by rotating the order, setting time limits, using a scoring sheet, and comparing judges' scores with the audience vote. You'll do the same for an LLM judge.

Kinds of judges

SetupWhat the judge seesUse it forWatch out for

Two choices that matter more than the setup. Ask for reasoning before the verdict (chain-of-thought judging, as in G-Eval): the critique improves the verdict and tells you why it failed. And prefer binary pass/fail per criterion over 1–10 scores when you start: experts agree on “acceptable or not” far more readily than on “6 or 7”.

You are grading a customer-support answer for Acme Gear. <question>{question}</question> <policy_context>{retrieved_policy}</policy_context> <answer>{answer}</answer> Criteria (judge each one separately): 1. CORRECT: every policy fact in the answer matches the policy context. 2. COMPLETE: it answers the actual question (including member-specific rules). 3. ACTIONABLE: the customer knows what to do next. Length is not a virtue: do not reward padding or repetition. First write a short critique for each criterion. Then output JSON: {"correct": "pass"|"fail", "complete": "pass"|"fail", "actionable": "pass"|"fail", "critique": "..."}

Known judge biases (and the fix for each)

BiasEvidenceFix
Position: prefers the first (or second) answerClaude-v1 favoured the first answer 75% of the time; GPT-4 was consistent after swapping in 65% (MT-Bench paper, 2023).Judge both orders; count a win only if both agree, or average the two margins.
Verbosity: prefers longer answersA “repetitive list” attack (same list, rephrased and prepended) fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 judges in 91.3% of cases, GPT-4 in 8.7%. AlpacaEval needed a length-controlled version.Rubric says length isn't a virtue; criteria for concision; length-controlled comparisons.
Self-preference: prefers its own model's outputsLLMs can recognize their own generations, and the stronger that recognition, the stronger the self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024).Use a judge from a different model family than the system under test, or a panel of judges.
Weak at maths and reasoningOn 20 maths judgments, GPT-4 wrongly accepted an incorrect answer 14 times by default, 6 with chain-of-thought, 3 when given a reference answer.Reference-guided judging; or check the number with code instead.
Criteria drift: the rubric changes as you read outputsIn the EvalGen study, users refined their criteria while grading (Shankar et al., 2024).Expect several rubric iterations; re-label the calibration set after big rubric changes.
✏️ Worked example: swap and average

The judge outputs a preference margin from −1 (second answer much better) to +1 (first answer much better). It has a position bias of +0.3 toward whatever comes first. The true margin is that B is slightly better: A − B = −0.2.

  • Order A, B: judge sees first − second = −0.2 + 0.3 = +0.1 → “A wins”. Wrong.
  • Order B, A: first − second = +0.2 + 0.3 = +0.5 → “B wins”.
  • Conservative rule: verdicts disagree → call it a tie. Average rule: A − B = (+0.1 − 0.5) ÷ 2 = −0.2 → B wins, by exactly the true margin: the +0.3 cancels.

Cost: two judge calls per pair. Swapping fixes position bias only; it does nothing about verbosity or self-preference.

Calibrating the judge against humans

The recipe (Hamel Husain calls a version of it “critique shadowing”):

  1. A domain expert labels 100–200 real outputs pass/fail (writing annotation guidelines and running a labelling effort well is Lesson 5.8), with a one-line critique each. Aim for a mix of passes and fails.
  2. Run the judge on the same outputs. Compare with a confusion matrix: how often judge and human agree on pass, on fail, and where they disagree.
  3. Read the disagreements. Fix the rubric, add the expert's critiques as few-shot examples, and rerun. Repeat until agreement stops improving.
  4. Keep part of the labelled set held out, and check the final judge on it.

Raw agreement can mislead. If 90% of answers pass, a judge that always says “pass” agrees 90% of the time and is useless. Cohen's kappa (κ) corrects for the agreement you'd get by chance:

κ = (po − pe) ÷ (1 − pe)    po = observed agreement, pe = agreement expected by chance from each rater's pass rate

κ = 1 is perfect, 0 is chance level. A common rough reading: above 0.6 is substantial, above 0.8 almost perfect. Also report the judge's hit rate on human passes and on human fails separately, so you know which way it errs.

✏️ Worked example: kappa by hand

100 answers. Human: 50 pass, 50 fail. Judge: 45 pass, 55 fail. They agree on 40 passes and 45 fails.

  • Observed agreement po = (40 + 45) ÷ 100 = 0.85.
  • Chance agreement pe = P(both pass) + P(both fail) = 0.50 × 0.45 + 0.50 × 0.55 = 0.50.
  • κ = (0.85 − 0.50) ÷ (1 − 0.50) = 0.70: substantial agreement, worth trusting with spot checks.

Now the “always pass” judge on a set where 90 of 100 truly pass: po = 0.90, pe = 0.90 × 1.0 + 0.10 × 0 = 0.90, so κ = 0. 90% agreement, zero skill.

Judge drift

Your eval score can change when nothing in your app changed: the provider updated the judge model, someone edited the rubric, or the judge prompt picked up a new few-shot example. Pin the judge's exact model version, version the judge prompt with the eval set, and rerun the calibration set whenever any of them changes. If the judge-human agreement moves, every historical comparison across that change is suspect.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

DeepEval (GEval for rubric criteria, pytest integration), promptfoo (llm-rubric, g-eval, select-best assertions), Braintrust with its open-source autoevals scorers, LangSmith and Langfuse evaluators, Inspect's model_graded_qa(). For calibration maths, scikit-learn's cohen_kappa_score.

from deepeval.test_case import LLMTestCase, SingleTurnParams
from deepeval.metrics import GEval

correct = GEval(
    name="Correctness",
    criteria="Is every policy fact in the actual output consistent with the expected output? Ignore style and length.",
    evaluation_params=[SingleTurnParams.ACTUAL_OUTPUT, SingleTurnParams.EXPECTED_OUTPUT],
    threshold=0.7,
)

def test_plus_refund():                     # run with: deepeval test run test_judge.py
    case = LLMTestCase(
        input="I'm a Plus member, can I return boots after 45 days?",
        actual_output=bot("I'm a Plus member, can I return boots after 45 days?"),
        expected_output="Yes. Plus members can return items within 60 days of delivery.",
    )
    assert_test(case, [correct])

A pairwise judge with the swap built in is a few lines: call it with (A, B) and (B, A), and keep the win only if both calls agree. Use a judge from a different model family than the one you're testing, and pin its version.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

One judge prompt per important criterion, binary verdicts with a critique, a strong model from a different family than your app's. The founder or a domain expert labels 100 outputs in a spreadsheet; iterate the judge until it agrees on 90%+ and check κ. Rerun that check monthly.

At scale

Judge prompts versioned and owned like code, calibration sets per product area refreshed from production, agreement dashboards, panels or fine-tuned judges for high-volume criteria, and human review queues for the cases where judges disagree. Judge changes go through the same review as model changes.

🎤 Interview angle

“You're using GPT-class models to grade your chatbot. Why should anyone trust those scores?” A senior answer:

  • The judge was validated against expert labels: confusion matrix, κ, error rates on passes and on fails, on a held-out set.
  • Known biases are controlled: swapped order for pairwise, a rubric that doesn't reward length, a judge from another model family.
  • Criteria are specific and binary with a critique, so failures are explainable.
  • The judge model and prompt are pinned and versioned, and calibration is rerun when either changes (drift).
  • Code checks still handle anything with a rule; humans review a sample of judge disagreements.
🏭 In the real world
LLM judges vs human preference 🧪 LMSYS · 2023 Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena

How: 80 multi-turn questions across 8 categories (MT-Bench) plus crowdsourced battles (Chatbot Arena); GPT-4 judges compared with about 3,000 expert and 3,000 crowd votes.

Why this method: human evaluation was too slow for fast model iteration; a validated judge with known, mitigated biases (swap order, CoT, references) reached human-level agreement.

Removing length bias from a leaderboard 🧪 Stanford · 2024 Length-Controlled AlpacaEval

How: a regression predicts the judge's preference from the length difference and other features, then asks what it would be at equal length.

Why this method: the auto-judge favoured longer outputs; controlling length raised its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98 and made it harder to game with verbosity.

Chain-of-thought, form-filling judges 🧪 Microsoft · 2023 G-Eval

How: the judge first generates evaluation steps from the criteria, then fills in a score form.

Why this method: reached a Spearman correlation of 0.514 with humans on summarization, beating earlier metrics by a large margin; the authors also flag a possible bias toward LLM-written text.

Align the judge with one domain expert 🏢 Hamel Husain · 2024 Using LLM-as-a-Judge For Evaluation (critique shadowing)

How: a domain expert makes binary pass/fail calls with written critiques; the critiques become few-shot examples in the judge prompt; agreement is tracked each iteration.

Why this method: it reached over 90% agreement in three iterations in his example; he warns to report true positive and true negative rates separately when classes are imbalanced.

Who validates the validators? 🧪 UC Berkeley · 2024 EvalGen (Shankar et al.)

How: an interface that proposes judge prompts and code assertions, then asks the user to grade a sample of outputs and keeps the graders that agree with them.

Why this method: LLM graders inherit LLM problems and need human validation; the study also found criteria drift, so plan to iterate the rubric.

🤯 Fun fact · when the human and the judge disagreed

In MT-Bench, when a human's choice differed from GPT-4's, the researchers showed that human GPT-4's reasoning and asked whether it was reasonable. Humans rated GPT-4's judgment reasonable in 75% of those cases, and changed their own choice in 34%. Judges are useful partners for human reviewers, not just replacements.

Check yourself

1. Judge and human agree on 80% of 100 labels. The judge passes 70% of answers; the human passes 60%. What's Cohen's κ, roughly?
pe = 0.7 × 0.6 + 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.54. κ = (0.80 − 0.54) ÷ (1 − 0.54) = 0.57: moderate. Also note the judge is lenient: it would report 70% pass when the truth is 60%.
2. In pairwise tests, your judge prefers prompt B's answers, which are 2× longer. Users say B's answers are padded. What's the best first fix?
That's verbosity bias. Swapping only cancels position bias. A rubric that names concision, criteria-level verdicts and a length-controlled comparison target the actual bias; human labels confirm the fix worked.
3. Your nightly eval score jumped 4 points overnight, but nobody merged anything. What's the likely cause, and the fix?
If the system under test didn't change, the measuring instrument did. Pin judge model versions and prompts, and rerun the labelled calibration set to see whether agreement with humans moved. (Do also check the error bars: on a small eval, 4 points can be noise.)
📝 TL;DR
  • LLM judges grade open-ended quality ~100× cheaper than humans and can match human agreement, once calibrated.
  • Prefer specific, binary criteria with a critique written before the verdict; give a reference when correctness matters.
  • Known biases: position (swap and combine), verbosity (rubric + length control), self-preference (different model family), maths (references or code).
  • Calibrate against expert labels with a confusion matrix and Cohen's κ = (po − pe) ÷ (1 − pe), not raw agreement.
  • Pin the judge model and prompt; rerun calibration when they change (judge drift).
