Phase 9 · Lesson 2

AI product & UX: trust, feedback loops & metrics

Your model will be wrong some of the time. Product design decides whether those errors are caught, cheap and rare where it matters, or land on a customer. And metrics decide whether you notice.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • LLM outputs are sampled: the same question can get different answers, and a fluent answer can be wrong (Lessons 0.2 and 0.5).
  • Grounded generation: answer from retrieved sources, cite them, abstain when unsure (Lesson 3.5).
  • Online evals: scoring live traffic, not just a test set (Lesson 5.6). Human-in-the-loop: a person approves risky steps (Lesson 6.6).
  • TTFT (time to first token): how long until the first word appears; streaming shows words as they're generated (Lessons 0.5, 8.2).
🎯 Hook

In 2022 a passenger asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares after a grandparent died. The chatbot said they could buy a full-price ticket and claim the reduced rate within 90 days. The airline's actual policy said otherwise, and it refused the refund.

In February 2024 a British Columbia tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay about $812. The airline had argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions; the tribunal member called that “a remarkable submission”. The bill was small. The lesson was not: your product owns everything your AI says, so design for the moment it's wrong.

No amount of prompt tuning makes that last column zero. So the product question isn't “is it accurate?” but what happens to the wrong ones: does a person catch them, can the user see they're wrong, can they be undone, and how much does each one cost?

💡 Big idea Match the AI's autonomy to three things: how much a wrong output costs, whether it can be undone, and whether the user can tell it's wrong. Then make errors visible (sources, uncertainty), cheap to fix (edit, undo), and turn every correction into data that makes the next version better.
🧠 Analogy: GPS, not autopilot

A satnav suggests a route, shows the alternatives and the arrival time, and reroutes when you ignore it. You stay the driver because the cost of a wrong turn is a few minutes and you can see the road. An autopilot that lands planes is held to a completely different standard, with certification, redundancy and a pilot ready to take over. Most AI features should be GPS. A few earn autopilot, and only with the evidence to match.

Designing for probabilistic outputs

PatternWhat it doesUse it when
Show sources (citations, links, highlighted passages)Lets users verify in seconds; exposes retrieval missesAny answer built on documents (Lesson 3.5)

Draft, don't sendThe user edits, then owns the resultEmails, documents, code: anything with the user's name on it

Those patterns line up into an autonomy ladder: suggest → draft → act after confirmation → act with undo → fully automatic. Each step up saves more human time and lets more errors through.

Customer emails want a person in the loop, drafting or confirming, because a sent email can't be unsent. Refunds want a confirmation plus routing of unsure cases. Clinic triage loses money at every level at a 5% error rate, and even at 0.2% the best choice still has a clinician confirming. Sometimes AI is the wrong tool, or only a helper.

Streaming and perceived latency

Jakob Nielsen's classic limits (Nielsen Norman Group) still frame it: about 0.1 s feels instant, about 1 s keeps the user's flow of thought, and about 10 s is the limit for keeping their attention. A 300-token answer at 50 tokens per second takes 6 s to generate, so showing the first words in under a second matters more than the total.

Human-in-the-loop that people actually use

  • Ask rarely, ask clearly. If an approval box appears on every step, people click “yes” without reading (automation bias). Reserve it for what your policy says matters, and show exactly what will happen: the refund amount, the email recipient, the diff.
  • Make the human faster than doing it themselves. Pre-filled fields, the evidence highlighted, one-click approve or edit. Otherwise the review step eats the value.
  • Audit the approvers. Sample approved items; if reviewers approve 99.9%, either the threshold is too conservative or nobody is really reviewing.
  • Every human decision is a label. Approvals, edits and rejections flow back into the eval set (next section).

✏️ Worked example: how fast does a feedback loop fill an eval set?
  • 50,000 answers a day; 2% of users click a thumb → 1,000 ratings a day; 15% of those are 👎 → 150 complaints a day.
  • An engineer triages 30 a day into categories (retrieval miss, wrong policy, tone, refusal). Within a week the top two categories are obvious.
  • Each triaged example, with the correct answer written in, becomes a golden-set case: 30 × 20 working days ≈ 600 new eval cases a month, all drawn from real traffic. That's how an eval set stays representative (Lesson 5.1).

AI product metrics: measure the outcome, not the activity

MetricDefinitionThe trap
Task success / resolution rateShare of tasks truly completed (e.g. no re-contact within 7 days)Needs a follow-up window; slower to measure
Deflection rateShare of conversations closed without a humanRises when the bot gives up users or answers wrongly; never report it alone
Time savedMinutes per task before vs after, measured, not surveyedIgnores time spent fixing AI errors
Cost per resolved taskAll AI + human cost ÷ tasks truly resolvedPer-request cost hides unresolved retries
Guardrail hit rateShare of requests blocked or rewritten by safety checksA sudden rise = an attack or a broken prompt; a fall to zero = a broken guardrail
Escalation rate, time to humanHow often and how fast people take overLow escalation with low satisfaction = users trapped

The chain that matters: offline eval score → online success → user behaviour → business value. Each link can break. A golden set that doesn't look like real traffic makes the first link lie; a deflection metric makes the last one lie.

✏️ Worked example: cost per resolved task

100,000 conversations × $0.10 of model cost = $10,000. If 40,000 are truly resolved (no re-contact within a week), AI cost per resolved conversation = $10,000 ÷ 40,000 = $0.25, not the $0.10 per conversation on the invoice. Add the humans who handled the other 60,000 and you have the number to compare with the old process. This is also how some vendors now price: Intercom charges for its Fin agent per outcome ($0.99 per outcome on its 2026 pricing page), not per message.

Staged rollout1% → 5% → 25% → 100%, behind a feature flagPre-agreed success metrics and stop conditions (guardrail hit rate, escalations, complaints)
Kill switch, always—Turn the AI off (fall back to search, a form, or a human) in seconds, without a deploy

When AI is the wrong solution. When a rule or a lookup gives the exact answer (“what's my order status?” is a database query); when errors are unacceptable and users can't check the output; when you can't build an eval, so you'll never know if it works; when the cost per task beats the value; or when a decision legally needs an explainable reason. Google's Rules of Machine Learning opens with this: don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Streaming in every provider SDK (Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini) and UI kits such as the Vercel AI SDK; feedback and online evals in Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust or Arize; feature flags and staged rollouts with LaunchDarkly, Unleash or OpenFeature; product analytics (PostHog, Amplitude) for the implicit signals.

import os, anthropic
from langfuse import get_client

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
langfuse = get_client()

def answer_stream(question):
    if not flags.is_enabled("ai_answers"):   # kill switch from your feature-flag service
        yield from search_results(question)     # the non-AI fallback
        return
    with client.messages.stream(
        model=os.environ["CHAT_MODEL"], max_tokens=800,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}],
    ) as stream:
        for text in stream.text_stream:
            yield text                             # first words on screen in under a second

def on_thumb(trace_id, up, reason=None):        # wire this to the 👍/👎 buttons
    langfuse.create_score(trace_id=trace_id, name="user-feedback",
                          value=1 if up else 0, data_type="BOOLEAN", comment=reason)

flags and search_results stand for your feature-flag client and existing search. The trace ID links each thumb to the exact prompt, retrieved chunks and output, which is what makes triage possible.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

Ship a narrow feature at a low autonomy level to real users in weeks; read every trace yourself; a thumbs button and an edit log are your first eval pipeline. Price and measure per outcome early: cost per resolved task tells you whether there's a business.

Big tech

Launch reviews ask for the risk tier, autonomy level, eval results, a rollout plan with stop conditions, and a kill switch. Experiments run on 1% of traffic with guardrail metrics. Human-AI interaction guidelines (Microsoft's, Google's) are checklists in design review, and legal reviews anything the AI says on the company's behalf.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our support bot's deflection went from 50% to 70%. Is that good?” A senior answer:

  • Asks how deflection is defined, and checks true resolution (no re-contact), CSAT and complaints over the same period.
  • Looks at what changed (threshold? prompt? traffic mix?) and samples conversations, especially closed-without-human ones.
  • Proposes a metric tree: offline evals → online success → user behaviour → cost per resolved task, with guardrail metrics that can stop a rollout.
  • Matches the bot's autonomy to the cost of errors, and names the kill switch.

How: 18 generally applicable design guidelines for how AI systems should behave with people, validated through multiple rounds of evaluation including a user study.

Why this method: they turn “make it trustworthy” into a checklist a design review can actually walk through; Microsoft's HAX Toolkit packages them with examples.

A guidebook for AI product teams 🏢 Google PAIR People + AI Guidebook

How: Google's People + AI Research team publishes guidance, patterns and worksheets for designing human-centred AI products.

Why this method: it gives product, design and engineering a shared vocabulary for trust, feedback and failure, so those questions come up in design review rather than after launch.

Acceptance rate as the usefulness signal 🧪 GitHub · 2022 Productivity Assessment of Neural Code Completion

How: surveyed Copilot users about productivity and compared their answers with logged usage data.

Why this method: the rate at which shown suggestions are accepted predicted perceived productivity better than more detailed metrics such as how long completions survived in the code: a cheap implicit signal beat a sophisticated one.

Pricing per outcome 🏢 Intercom · 2026 Fin AI Agent pricing

How: the support agent is billed at $0.99 per outcome, with one outcome counted per conversation, rather than per message or seat.

Why this method: it ties the vendor's revenue to the metric customers care about, resolved conversations, the same “cost per resolved task” you should track for your own features.

Rule #1: launch without ML 🏢 Google Rules of Machine Learning

How: Martin Zinkevich's guide starts with “don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning”, then insists on metrics and simple pipelines first.

Why this method: a heuristic or a search box that ships now gives you users, data and a baseline, which is exactly what you need to prove later that the AI version is better.

🤯 Fun fact · the 55% experiment

In GitHub's 2022 controlled experiment, 95 professional developers were split into two groups and asked to write an HTTP server in JavaScript. The group with Copilot finished on average in 1 hour 11 minutes; the group without took 2 hours 41 minutes, which GitHub reported as 55% faster. It's one of the few early AI productivity claims backed by a randomised experiment rather than a survey.

Check yourself

1. An AI assistant costs $0.08 per conversation in model calls and handles 50,000 conversations a month; 20,000 are truly resolved. What's the AI cost per resolved conversation?
50,000 × $0.08 = $4,000 of model cost ÷ 20,000 resolved = $0.20. The unresolved 30,000 still cost money (and usually a human too), which per-request cost hides.
2. After lowering the confidence threshold, your bot's deflection rose from 50% to 70%, but repeat contacts and complaints rose too. What's going on, and what should you track?
Deflection counts conversations closed without a human, including wrong answers that bounce back later. A lower threshold raises it mechanically. True resolution and cost per resolved task capture whether the problem was solved; the threshold should maximise those.
3. An agent can cancel customer subscriptions. Evals show it picks the right action 97% of the time. Which UX fits best?
Cancelling is costly and awkward to undo, and 3% of a large volume is a lot of wrong cancellations. A clear confirmation keeps the speed while catching most errors; manual-only throws away the value. The confirmation and the log are also your best feedback data.
📝 TL;DR
  • Your AI will be wrong at scale; design what happens then: sources, “I don't know”, drafts, confirmation, undo, handoff.
  • Set autonomy by cost of error × reversibility × whether users can check, inside an error budget.
  • Stream: first words in under a second; show agent progress steps.
  • Turn thumbs, edits, accepts and escalations into eval cases; measure true resolution and cost per resolved task, never deflection alone.
  • Launch in stages behind a flag with a kill switch, and sometimes the right answer is no AI.
