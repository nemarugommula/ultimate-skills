Phase 9 · Lesson 1

AI system design: end-to-end case studies

A demo takes a weekend. A system that is right, safe, affordable and measurable takes design. Here is a framework for any AI design question, three complete case studies, and a canvas to practise on.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Token cost math: cost = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price, prices quoted per million tokens (Lesson 0.3).
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): find the relevant pieces of your documents, put them in the prompt, then answer (Lessons 3.1–3.6). Permissions and freshness at scale: Lesson 4.7.
  • Evals: a golden dataset of real questions with known good answers, scored automatically on every change (Lessons 5.1–5.6).
  • Agents: an LLM in a loop that picks tools until the task is done; guardrails and human approval keep it safe (Lessons 6.1 and 6.6).
  • Gateways, fallbacks and guardrails: one front door for model calls, with retries, backup models, cost caps and safety checks (Lessons 8.1–8.3).
🎯 Hook

LinkedIn's team wrote up how they built their generative-AI assistant for job seekers. They reached 80% of the experience they wanted within the first month. Then they spent four more months trying to get past 95%.

The last stretch was all system design: splitting the work into routing, retrieval and generation steps; small models for routing and big ones for writing; separate evals for each step; and fixing the ~10% of structured outputs that broke their parser until errors fell to about 0.01%. The model was never the hard part. The system around it was.

Numbers first: 45 minutes, eight steps

An AI design interview (or a real design review) has the same shape as any system design: requirements, estimates, architecture, deep dives. AI adds three things you must say out loud: the quality bar, the risk of a wrong answer, and the token budget. A good split of 45 minutes:

StepMinutesWhat you say or draw
1. Requirements, quality bar, risk5Who uses it, for which task. What “good” means as a number (e.g. ≥ 90% of answers correct and cited). What a wrong answer costs: annoying, expensive or dangerous.
2. Data sources4Where the knowledge lives, how fresh it must be, who may see what, personal data (PII).
3. Estimates5Requests per day and at peak, tokens per request, cost per month, a latency budget.
4. Architecture & model choice10The simplest design that can meet the bar: one call → RAG → fixed workflow → agent. Which model tier for each step.
5. Evals5Golden set, metrics per step, the gate a change must pass before it ships.
6. Safety4Prompt injection, data leaks, harmful actions; what's enforced in code, not in the prompt.
7. Deep dives & failure modes8The 2–3 hardest parts, and what happens when each piece fails.
8. Rollout & monitoring4Shadow mode, beta, staged rollout, kill switch, tracing, online evals, cost alerts.
💡 Big idea An AI system is a normal distributed system with one probabilistic component in the middle. Everything around the model exists to make its output measurable (evals), bounded (permissions, guardrails, limits in code), affordable (routing, caching, batching) and recoverable (fallbacks, human handoff, undo). Design those four, and the model choice becomes a detail you can change later.
🧠 Analogy: staffing a help desk with a brilliant new hire

Your new hire is fast, well read and confident, and is wrong a few times in a hundred without noticing. You wouldn't give them every key in the building. You'd give them a library card for the right documents (retrieval), a badge that opens only the rooms the visitor may enter (permissions), a checklist to test them on before their first day (evals), a supervisor who signs off anything involving money (human approval), and a budget. That's AI system design.

Step 4 in detail: climb the complexity ladder only as far as you must

Anthropic's guide to agents puts it plainly: find the simplest solution possible, and only increase complexity when needed. Each rung costs more tokens, more latency and more ways to fail.

Model choice is per step, not per product: a small, fast model for routing, classification and extraction; a larger model only where reasoning or writing quality shows up in your evals. Open models you host yourself (Lesson 7.3) are an option when data can't leave your network or volume makes per-token prices painful.

Case study 1 · The enterprise knowledge assistant (RAG with permissions)

Prompt: “Design an assistant that answers employees' questions from the company wiki, shared drives and support tickets. 10,000 employees.”

Requirements. Answers with citations; says “I don't know” rather than guessing; never shows a document the asker can't open; content updated within an hour; first words on screen in under 1.5 s. Quality bar: ≥ 90% correct and cited on a golden set, zero permission leaks (a hard gate, not an average). Risk tier: information only, no actions.

✏️ Worked example: the estimate, out loud
  • Traffic: 10,000 employees × 30% active per day × 5 questions = 15,000 questions a day. Over an 8-hour working day that's ≈ 0.5 per second, so plan for ≈ 1.6 per second at a 3× peak. Small, and that's normal for internal tools: cost and quality dominate, not QPS.
  • Tokens per question: system prompt 800 + chat history 1,000 + 8 retrieved chunks × 400 + question 50 = 5,050 input; answer ≈ 350 output.
  • Cost with an illustrative mid-tier model at $3 / $15 per million tokens: 5,050 × $3/M = $0.01515, plus 350 × $15/M = $0.00525, so ≈ $0.020 per question.
  • Month: 15,000 × 22 working days = 330,000 questions × $0.0204 ≈ $6,700 a month, about $0.67 per employee. Caching the 800-token system prompt (cache reads bill at about a tenth of the input price, Lesson 1.4) saves ≈ $700 of that.
  • Index: 500,000 documents × 2,000 tokens = 1 billion tokens ≈ 2.5 million 400-token chunks; at 1,024 float32 dimensions that's ≈ 10 GB of vectors. One machine's worth, not a cluster.
  • Latency: permission lookup 20 ms + hybrid search 80 ms + rerank 150 ms + time to first token 600 ms ≈ 0.85 s to first word; streaming 350 tokens at 60 per second takes ≈ 6 s more, which is fine because the user is already reading.

Deep dives.

  • Permissions are enforced at retrieval, in code. Each chunk carries the access list of its source (groups that can read it), synced from the source systems. The search filters on the asker's groups before ranking, so a forbidden chunk never reaches the prompt. Never ask the model to “not mention” documents: it can't keep secrets it was shown (Lesson 4.7).
  • Freshness: connectors listen to change feeds and re-index changed documents; a deleted file or revoked permission must disappear in minutes, not at the next nightly job.
  • Grounding: answer only from retrieved text, cite every claim, abstain when retrieval is weak (Lesson 3.5).
  • Evals: ~200 real questions per department with expected sources; recall@k for retrieval, faithfulness for answers (Lesson 5.4); and a separate permission test suite: users who must not see a document ask about it, and any leak fails the build.

Leak through a shared cacheAn intern gets an answer built from the HR folder because a manager asked first.Key caches by the asker's permission set, or don't cache answers at all.
Stale permissionsSomeone moved teams last week and still sees the old team's docs.ACL sync on change events + a nightly full reconciliation.

Case study 2 · The customer-support agent (tools, escalation, evals)

Prompt: “Design an AI agent for an online store that resolves customer chats: order status, returns, refunds. 600,000 conversations a month.”

Requirements. Resolve common issues end to end; follow the refund policy exactly; hand off to a human with a summary when stuck or asked; a first reply (or a visible “checking your order…” status) within a few seconds. Quality bar: task success on simulated conversations ≥ 80%, zero refunds outside policy. Risk tier: actions with money. That tier changes everything: limits live in code, and big actions need a human.

✏️ Worked example: an agent's tokens multiply
  • A conversation has ~4 customer turns; the agent makes ~2.5 LLM calls per turn (think, call a tool, answer), so 10 calls per conversation.
  • Each call sends system prompt + tool definitions (2,500 tokens, the same every time), history (~1,500) and tool results (~800): 4,800 input, and writes ~200 output.
  • Per conversation: 10 × 4,800 = 48,000 input and 2,000 output tokens. With the 2,500-token prefix cached (billed at a tenth): 23,000 × $3/M + 25,000 × $0.30/M + 2,000 × $15/M = $0.069 + $0.0075 + $0.030 ≈ $0.11. Without caching: $0.17.
  • Month: 600,000 × $0.1065 ≈ $64,000 (vs ≈ $104,000 uncached). Peak load: 20,000 conversations a day × 10 calls ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 2.3 calls/s, ≈ 7 at a 3× peak.
  • Value check: if a human-handled chat costs ~$5 (illustrative) and the agent resolves 60%, AI cost per resolved conversation is $64,000 ÷ 360,000 ≈ $0.18. That ratio, not the raw bill, decides whether the project lives (Lesson 9.2).

Deep dives.

  • Read tools vs write tools. get_order and track_parcel are safe to call freely. issue_refund goes through a policy engine in code: amount ≤ $50 and within the return window → automatic; above → a human approves. The prompt explains the policy; the code enforces it. Write tools take an idempotency key so a retry never pays twice (Lesson 6.2).
  • Escalation is a feature. Hand off when the customer asks, after two failed attempts, on angry sentiment, or on any policy edge. The human gets a summary and the tool history, so the customer never repeats themselves (Lesson 6.6).
  • Loop limits: at most ~8 steps and a token budget per conversation; past it, escalate.
  • Evals: simulated customers with goals (“return the blue jacket, it's day 25”) talk to the agent in a sandbox; score whether the final database state is right and whether every tool call obeyed policy, and run each scenario several times, because a reliable agent passes all of them, not just one (the τ-bench idea, Lesson 5.5).

Case study 3 · The document-processing pipeline (workflow, not agent)

Prompt: “An insurer receives 300,000 claim documents a month (PDFs and scans, ~6 pages). Extract the fields into the claims system.”

Requirements. Field-level accuracy ≥ 98% on the fields that move money (amounts, dates, policy number); results within 24 hours; uncertain documents go to a human; personal data handled under retention rules. Nobody is waiting on a screen, so this is a batch workflow: the steps are known, and an agent would only add cost and variance.

✏️ Worked example: batch halves the bill
  • Extraction input: 6 pages × ~800 tokens + 600 tokens of instructions and JSON schema = 5,400; output ≈ 400 tokens of JSON. Mid tier: 5,400 × $3/M + 400 × $15/M = $0.0222.
  • A small model first classifies the document type from page 1 (1,000 tokens in, 10 out at $1 / $5 per M): ≈ $0.001.
  • Both through a batch API (OpenAI and Anthropic both offer 50% off for results within 24 hours): ≈ $0.0116 per document, × 300,000 ≈ $3,500 a month.
  • People: if 8% of documents go to review at 3 minutes each, that's 1,200 hours a month, versus 30,000 hours to key in everything by hand at 6 minutes each. The review queue is part of the design, sized like any other component.

Deep dives. Structured output against a JSON schema (Lesson 1.3); validation in code (line items sum to the total, dates are real, the policy number exists); a confidence score per field, with documents below the threshold routed to review; evals as field-level precision and recall on ~500 labelled documents per type, plus a 1% random human audit in production to catch drift when a new template appears. The same shape fits a coding pipeline such as an automated code-review bot: diff → retrieve related code → LLM review → run tests and linters in a sandbox → a human merges. There the eval is “do the tests pass?”, the idea behind SWE-bench (2023), which scores models on real GitHub issues by running each repository's tests.

import os
from litellm import Router

# One front door for every model call: retries, then a backup model
router = Router(
    model_list=[
        {"model_name": "primary", "litellm_params": {"model": os.environ["PRIMARY_MODEL"]}},
        {"model_name": "backup",  "litellm_params": {"model": os.environ["BACKUP_MODEL"]}},
    ],
    fallbacks=[{"primary": ["backup"]}],   # provider down or rate-limited → backup
    num_retries=2,
    timeout=20,
)
resp = router.completion(model="primary", messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}])

# The estimate you say out loud, as code (prices per million tokens)
def monthly_cost(tasks_per_day, calls, in_tok, out_tok, p_in, p_out, cached=0.0, days=30):
    per_call = in_tok * ((1 - cached) * p_in + cached * p_in * 0.1) + out_tok * p_out
    return tasks_per_day * days * calls * per_call / 1e6

monthly_cost(2000, 10, 4800, 200, 3, 15, cached=2500/4800)   # ≈ $6,390 for 2,000 chats a day

Model IDs come from configuration (e.g. anthropic/… or openai/… strings), so swapping models is a config change and an eval run, not a code change.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startup

One strong hosted model behind a thin gateway, managed vector DB or pgvector, a 50-question golden set from day one, tracing from the first user. Ship the narrowest version with a human fallback, then let traces tell you what to build. Your scarce resource is engineering time, so buy everything that isn't your product.

Big tech

A platform team owns the gateway, the eval service, guardrails and permission-aware retrieval as shared services; product teams compose them. Reviews ask for the risk tier, data classification, eval results and rollout plan before launch. Volume makes routing, caching, fine-tuned small models and self-hosting worth the engineering.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a chatbot over our internal docs.” What separates a senior answer:

  • Asks for the quality bar and the risk before drawing anything, and turns them into numbers (correct-and-cited rate, leak rate = 0).
  • Estimates tokens, cost per request, cost per month and latency by hand, and uses them to choose the design.
  • Starts on the lowest rung of the ladder that can work and says what evidence would justify climbing.
  • Puts permissions, limits and policies in code, not in the prompt.
  • Names the eval plan and failure modes unprompted, and ends with rollout: shadow, beta, staged, kill switch.
🏭 In the real world
A pipeline of small and big models 🏢 LinkedIn · 2024 Musings on building a Generative AI product

How: a three-step pipeline (route the question, retrieve, generate), smaller models for routing and retrieval, larger ones for generation, and evaluation pipelines per step.

Why this method: routing and retrieval behave like classification and are cheap to test separately; generation took most of the work, and per-step evals showed where.

Permission-trimmed retrieval 🏢 Microsoft · 2026 Data, privacy and security for Microsoft 365 Copilot

How: Copilot only surfaces organizational data that the individual user has at least view permission for, using the existing permission models (for example, SharePoint).

Why this method: reusing the source systems' permissions means the assistant can't become a back door; the flip side, which Microsoft stresses, is that those source permissions must actually be right.

A support assistant at scale 🏢 Klarna · 2024 AI assistant handles two-thirds of customer service chats

How: an assistant built on OpenAI models handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of Klarna's service chats, in 35+ languages.

Why this method: Klarna reported resolution time falling from 11 minutes to under 2 and a 25% drop in repeat inquiries: business metrics, not model metrics, are what justified it.

Testing agents with simulated users 🧪 Research · 2024 τ-bench (Sierra)

How: an LLM plays the customer; the agent has domain tools and a policy; success is judged by comparing the final database state with the goal state, over repeated trials (pass^k).

Why this method: even strong function-calling agents succeeded on under 50% of tasks and were inconsistent across retries (pass^8 under 25% in retail), which is why support agents need limits and escalation.

The token bill of agents 🏢 Anthropic · 2025 How we built our multi-agent research system

How: a lead agent plans and spawns parallel sub-agents that search, each with its own context window.

Why this method: it beat a single agent by 90.2% on their internal research eval, but agents used ~4× and multi-agent systems ~15× the tokens of chats, so it only pays for high-value tasks.

🤯 Fun fact · tokens explain performance

In Anthropic's analysis of their research agent on the BrowseComp benchmark, token usage alone explained 80% of the variance in performance. Spending more tokens bought better answers, which is exactly why the budget is a design input, not an afterthought.

Check yourself

1. An agent makes 10 LLM calls per conversation, each with 5,000 input tokens and 200 output tokens, at $3 / $15 per million tokens, with no caching. Roughly what does one conversation cost?
Input: 10 × 5,000 = 50,000 tokens × $3/M = $0.15. Output: 10 × 200 = 2,000 × $15/M = $0.03. Total $0.18. Note the input dominates: agents resend the whole context every step, which is why caching the fixed prefix is the first fix.
2. Your knowledge assistant filters by permissions at retrieval. After adding a semantic answer cache to save money, a junior employee gets an answer that quotes the executive compensation folder. What's the root cause?
Any cache that sits after the permission check must be keyed by the permission set (or by user). Otherwise it's a side door around your ACL filter. Leak tests in the eval suite catch this before users do.
3. An interviewer asks: “Design an AI system that extracts fields from 300,000 invoices a month.” What's the strongest opening move?
The steps are known (parse → classify → extract → validate → route), nobody is waiting, and errors move money. That points to the lowest rung that works: a batch workflow (half price), code validation, confidence routing to humans, and field-level evals.
📝 TL;DR
  • Framework: requirements with a quality bar and risk tier → data → estimates → simplest architecture → evals → safety → deep dives → rollout.
  • Estimate tokens × calls × price per request, then per month; agents multiply calls, so cache the fixed prefix and route easy steps to small models.
  • Put permissions, limits and policies in code; the prompt only explains them.
  • Climb the ladder (prompt → RAG → workflow → agent) only when evals show you must; design the human fallback as a real component.
