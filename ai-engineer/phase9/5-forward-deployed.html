Phase 9 · Lesson 5

Forward deployed engineering: shipping AI with customers

Most enterprise AI doesn't fail in the model. It fails in the gap between a demo and a customer's real data, systems, people and politics. Forward deployed engineers are the people paid to close that gap.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • AI system design: requirements, architecture, cost and latency budgets (Lesson 9.1).
  • Product metrics: measure the outcome, not the activity (Lesson 9.2).
  • Evals and golden sets: fixed test cases you score every change against (Lessons 5.1, 5.6).
  • RAG in production: permissions, freshness, connectors to real systems (Lesson 4.7).
🎯 Hook

In August 2025, a report from MIT's NANDA initiative, based on 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments, found that only about 5% of enterprise generative-AI pilots achieved rapid revenue acceleration. The rest mostly stalled, with little or no measurable impact on profit and loss.

The report's diagnosis wasn't model quality. It was a “learning gap” and flawed enterprise integration: generic tools that don't learn or adapt to how a company actually works. It also found that buying from specialised vendors and building with partners succeeded about two-thirds of the time, while internal builds succeeded only a third as often. Treat the exact percentages with care (it's one report with a narrow definition of success), but the pattern matches what practitioners see: pilots die of integration, data access and missing success metrics. That's the job description of a forward deployed engineer.

💡 Big idea A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer embedded with a customer whose job is the customer's outcome, not a ticket queue. They scope the problem, prototype on real data, take it to production inside the customer's systems, and feed what they learn back into the product, so the next customer needs less custom work.
🧠 Analogy: the field engineer for a new kind of engine

A company sells a powerful new engine. The engine works on the test bench. But every customer's vehicle is different: different mounts, different fuel lines, different mechanics who don't trust it yet. The field engineer goes to the customer's garage, fits the engine to their vehicle, trains their mechanics, notices that every customer struggles with the same bracket, and phones the factory to make the bracket standard. The engine is the model. The vehicle is the enterprise. The bracket is your next product feature.

Where the role comes from

The title is Palantir's. Their engineering blog describes two main engineering roles: Software Engineers (“Devs”), who build the platform, and Forward Deployed Software Engineers (“Deltas”), who deploy and customise it for customers, sit in business development and measure success by impact on the customer's goal. Their shorthand: a Dev's focus is one capability for many customers; a Delta's is one customer with many capabilities. They also insist a Delta isn't a consultant, because a consultant typically delivers a one-time analysis while a Delta builds lasting software.

The model spread to AI labs and startups once LLMs made “the product works, but each customer must be fitted” the normal state of enterprise AI. As of late October 2026, OpenAI's job board listed 28 roles with “Forward Deployed” in the title and Anthropic's listed six. OpenAI describes its FDE team as partnering with customers to turn research breakthroughs into production systems, with engineers who embed with strategic customers, write scopes for both prototypes and production, code side by side with customer teams on their infrastructure, and travel up to 50%. Anthropic's staff FDE posting stresses anticipating security and procurement gates, handing over systems the customer can run, and codifying repeatable deployment patterns for the product team.

Running a pilot that can succeed

  • Agree the success metric up front, in writing, with its current baseline: “median handling time for billing tickets falls from 14 to 9 minutes with no drop in CSAT (customer satisfaction score)”. Not “users like it”.
  • Request data access on day 1, through the customer's real process. Prototype on a sample while approvals run.
  • Build the eval set from the customer's data: 50–150 real cases, labelled by their experts, including the ugly ones. It catches failures early, settles arguments, and lets you swap models safely mid-pilot.
  • Demo weekly to the users and the sponsor. Weekly demos are cheap insurance against the champion leaving or the scope drifting.
  • Define exit criteria: what result means production, what means stop. A pilot without an exit becomes “pilot purgatory”.
✏️ Worked example: is the pilot passing?

The customer's experts labelled 120 real support tickets. The agreed bar: the AI's suggested resolution is acceptable in at least 85% of cases, and it must never suggest a refund above policy.

  • Week 5 run: 96 acceptable out of 120 = 80%. Below the bar: 0.85 × 120 = 102 needed, so 6 more cases must pass.
  • Error analysis: 14 of the 24 failures come from one cause, tickets that reference an order number the bot can't look up. One connector (the order system) fixes most of them.
  • Week 7 run after the connector: 107 / 120 = 89.2%, zero policy violations. You can say exactly why it passed, which is what a sponsor needs to sign.

    Change management and adoption

    A system that works and isn't used has the same business value as one that doesn't work. Adoption is part of the job:

    • Fit the workflow: put the AI where people already work (the ticket view, the CRM record), not in a new tab.
    • Find champions among the users, train them first, and let them train their peers.
    • Make it safe to distrust: show sources, make overriding easy, and visibly act on feedback (Lesson 9.2).
    • Measure usage and outcome weekly, by team. A team at 10% usage is telling you something; go and sit with them.
    • Talk honestly about jobs: people resist tools they think will replace them; tell them what changes in their day.

    Turning one-off work into product

    The risk of forward deployment is becoming a consulting firm with a software logo: every customer gets a bespoke fork nobody can maintain. The habits that prevent it:

    • Build on the platform, not beside it. Custom logic lives in configuration, prompts, tools and connectors that the product supports.
    • The third-time rule: the first time, build it for the customer; the second time, notice; the third time, turn it into a product feature (a connector, a template, an eval pack) and hand it to the product team.
    • Write it down: a short field report after each engagement (what we built, what broke, what the product should change) is the FDE's most valuable deliverable for the company.

    When to say no

    Saying no early is cheaper than failing slowly. Push back, re-scope or walk away when: there's no data access path; there's no owner or sponsor on the customer side; nobody will agree a metric; the task is better solved with rules or classic software (Lesson 0.6); the error cost is too high for current reliability with no review step (Lesson 9.4); or the customer wants a custom fork that can never become product.

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Startup

    Founders and early engineers are the FDEs. Every engagement is also product discovery: the third-time rule decides the roadmap. Keep scope narrow, charge for pilots so customers commit people and data, and refuse engagements that can't become product.

    Big tech / AI lab

    FDE is a dedicated function next to sales, solutions architects and customer success, focused on strategic accounts, with playbooks, reference architectures and partner firms for delivery. The hard part is the feedback loop: making sure lessons from the field actually change the platform.

    🎤 Interview angle

    FDE loops mix coding, system design and customer scenarios. Common questions and what a strong answer covers:

    • “Walk me through your first two weeks at a new customer.” Shadow users, pick one workflow, write metric + baseline, request access day 1, gather 100 real cases, weekly demo cadence, a risk list.
    • “The pilot is stalled at week 8. What do you do?” Diagnose (access, quality, adoption, sponsor), go back to the written metric, show the eval, re-scope in writing or stop.
    • “The customer wants a feature only they need.” Find the underlying need, check if it fits the platform (configuration, tool, connector), price or decline a fork, record it for product.
    • “Explain to their CISO (chief information security officer) how data flows.” SSO, permissions-aware retrieval, where data is stored and for how long, the model provider's terms, private networking, audit logs.
    • Practical: build a working prototype on a messy dataset in a few hours, then present it to a non-technical “customer”. They grade judgement and communication as much as code.
    🏭 In the real world
    Devs and Deltas 📜 History · Palantir, 2019 Dev versus Delta: Demystifying engineering roles at Palantir

    How: Forward Deployed Software Engineers sit in business development, support one customer, and deploy and customise the platform against that customer's goals, while Devs build the platform for many customers.

    Why this method: complex data platforms only create value once fitted to each organisation's data and problems; the Delta role makes that fitting an engineering job measured by customer impact.

    FDEs at a frontier lab 🏢 OpenAI · 2026 Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting

    How: engineers embed with strategic customers, prepare scopes of work for proofs of concept and production, code alongside customer teams on their infrastructure, and feed product feedback to research and product teams.

    Why this method: turning model capability into production systems inside large organisations is the bottleneck, and the abstractions built in one engagement speed up the next.

    Owning engagements end to end 🏢 Anthropic · 2026 Staff Forward Deployed Engineer posting

    How: lead discovery and scope before a build, anticipate security and procurement gates, build production applications and agent components inside customer systems, and hand over systems the customer can operate.

    Why this method: codifying repeatable deployment patterns and sending them back to product is how forward deployment scales beyond one account.

    A pilot with a QA loop, then system-wide 🏢 Kaiser Permanente · 2024 Quality assurance informs large-scale use of ambient AI

    How: a 10-week pilot in early 2024 with more than 1,000 physicians, clinician feedback on accuracy and usability, and a QA team of physicians, informaticists, safety and quality leaders, before deploying across 40 hospitals and 600 offices.

    Why this method: a bounded pilot with real users and an explicit quality loop is what earned the go-ahead to scale.

    Private connectivity to a model 🏢 AWS · docs Amazon Bedrock interface VPC endpoints (PrivateLink)

    How: an interface endpoint lets workloads in a VPC call the model service without an internet gateway, NAT, VPN or public IP addresses; model providers similarly offer regional data residency (OpenAI's, for example, is set per project, documented here).

    Why this method: regulated customers often require that traffic and data stay on private networks or in specific regions; knowing these options turns a security blocker into a configuration task.

    🤯 Fun fact · why “Delta”?

    “Dev” is obvious. “Delta”, Palantir explains, is a leftover from its early days, when each team in business development was named after a letter of the NATO phonetic alphabet. The name stuck to the role that would be copied across the AI industry.

    Check yourself

    1. The customer's experts labelled 150 cases. The agreed bar is 90% acceptable. Your system passes 126. How many more cases must pass?
    0.90 × 150 = 135 needed; 135 − 126 = 9. Then do error analysis on the 24 failures: they usually cluster into a few causes (often a missing connector or data field), and fixing the biggest cluster is the fastest way over the bar.
    2. Week 6 of a 12-week pilot: your champion moves to another team, and the new manager says “I'm not sure this is what we need.” What's the best move?
    Stakeholder change is one of the most common pilot killers. The written metric and weekly evidence let a newcomer decide quickly. Silently continuing risks a “no” at week 12; stacking scopes guarantees running out of time.
    3. You've now built a custom ServiceNow connector with permission filtering for three different customers. What should happen next?
    That's the third-time rule: repeated field work is a product signal. Productising it lowers the cost of the next deployment and stops three diverging forks from becoming permanent maintenance.
    📝 TL;DR
    • FDEs embed with customers and own the outcome: discovery → prototype on real data → production in their systems → feedback to product.
    • The role comes from Palantir (“Deltas”: one customer, many capabilities) and is now common at AI labs and startups.
    • Pilots succeed with one workflow, a written metric and baseline, data access requested on day 1, an eval set from the customer's data, and weekly demos.
    • Integration (SSO, SCIM, permissions, CRMs, ticketing, residency, VPC) is most of the work. Productise repeats; say no to work that can't succeed or can't become product.
