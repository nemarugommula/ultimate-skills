Phase 9 · Lesson 4

Vertical AI: building for legal, health, finance & other domains

A horizontal AI product serves everyone a little. A vertical one serves one profession deeply: its data, its workflows, its regulators and its idea of what a mistake costs. Most of the engineering is not the model.

🎯 Hook

In 2023 a New York lawyer with more than 30 years of practice used ChatGPT for research in Mata v. Avianca, a personal-injury suit against an airline. The brief cited court decisions that didn't exist. When he asked the chatbot whether one case was real, it assured him the case “does indeed exist” and could be found on Westlaw and LexisNexis. Judge P. Kevin Castel fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000, writing that using AI wasn't “inherently improper”, but that lawyers have a gatekeeping role to ensure their filings are accurate.

A year later, Stanford researchers tested the legal industry's own retrieval-based research tools, built specifically to avoid this. They hallucinated less than general chatbots, but still 17% to 33% of the time. Building for a profession means building that gatekeeping into the product.

Numbers first: the same error rate, very different bills

Take one system with a 2% error rate doing 10,000 tasks a month: 200 wrong outputs. What those 200 errors cost depends entirely on the vertical (illustrative orders of magnitude):

TaskWhat one error looks likeRough cost per error200 errors a month

Horizontal products can treat errors as an annoyance. Vertical products are priced, regulated and judged on the bottom two rows. That changes the architecture: citations, review steps, audit trails and expert evals stop being nice-to-haves and become the product.

💡 Big idea A vertical AI product wins on four things a general model can't download: proprietary data (the documents, records and outcomes of the field), the workflow (built into the tools experts already use), trust machinery (citations, review, audit, compliance), and expert-defined quality (evals written by the profession). The model is a component you will swap several times.
🧠 Analogy: the general contractor vs the hospital builder

A general contractor can build a house anywhere. A hospital builder needs the same bricks, plus medical-gas lines, infection-control rules, inspectors who sign off each stage, and drawings that every trade can trace. Both use the same cement. The hospital builder's value is everything around the cement, and nobody hires a contractor who “usually” remembers the oxygen lines.

Four patterns that cover most vertical products

  1. Domain RAG with citations. Every claim links to a source span the expert can open in one click. Answers without support are refused or flagged. Add a citation verifier: a deterministic check that each cited ID exists in what was retrieved, and that the quoted text really appears in it.
  2. Structured extraction from documents. Contracts, claims, medical records and filings become fields in a schema (party, date, amount, diagnosis code), each with a source location and a confidence. Validate with rules (dates parse, totals add up) and send low-confidence fields to a person.
  3. Workflow automation with human review. The AI drafts; a professional reviews, edits and signs; the system records who approved what. Measure the edit rate: it's your best production quality signal.
  4. Copilots inside existing tools. Professionals live in their system of record. A side panel in the EHR or a Word add-in beats a separate website, and it inherits the tool's permissions and audit logs.
✏️ Worked example: is the review step worth it?

An ambient clinical-note product drafts 10,000 notes a month. The model gets something material wrong in 2% of them: 10,000 × 0.02 = 200 flawed drafts.

  • No review: 200 flawed notes go into patient records every month.
  • The clinician reviews each draft before signing and catches 95% of the errors: 200 × 0.05 = 10 slip through. Cost: 10,000 × 1.5 minutes = 15,000 minutes = 250 clinician-hours a month.
  • Make review easier (each sentence links to the moment in the visit transcript, uncertain items highlighted) and catch 99%: 200 × 0.01 = 2 slip through, for the same review time.

Improving the model from 2% to 1% halves the errors. Improving the review UI from 95% to 99% catch rate cuts what reaches the record by 5×. In verticals, the review experience is often a bigger lever than the model.

Compliance: the short version an engineer needs

You are not the lawyer; you are the person who makes the controls real. High level, as of 2026, not legal advice:

RuleWhat it isWhat it means for your system
HIPAA (US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)Protects identifiable health information (PHI, protected health information). A vendor that handles PHI for a provider is a “business associate” and may use it only as its contract allows.A BAA (business associate agreement) with the customer and with every downstream vendor that sees PHI, including your model provider (OpenAI, for example, requires a signed BAA before PHI is sent to its API). Minimum necessary data, access controls, logs, no PHI in debug traces.
SOC 2An independent auditor's report (under AICPA criteria) on your controls for security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy.Not a law, but enterprise buyers ask for the report in the security review. Start the controls early: access reviews, change management, incident response, vendor management.
GDPR (EU General Data Protection Regulation)Rules for personal data of people in the EU. Processors must give “sufficient guarantees” under a contract (Article 28). People have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects (Article 22), with exceptions.Data processing agreements down the vendor chain, data residency where promised, deletion and access requests that reach your logs and indexes, and a human in decisions like credit or hiring.
Financial record-keeping (e.g. US SEC Rule 17a-4 for broker-dealers)Firms must keep business communications for set periods (often 3–6 years), stored either with an audit trail that can re-create the original or in non-rewriteable, non-erasable form.If AI drafts client communications, the drafts, approvals and sent versions may be records. Immutable logs with retention, and compliance review before sending.

Two habits make all of these easier: an audit trail for every AI output (what went in, which sources and versions were retrieved, which model and prompt version, what came out, who edited and approved it, when) and data minimisation (send the model only what the task needs).

Evaluating with domain experts

You cannot judge whether a contract summary is good enough for a partner to sign. So in vertical AI the eval set is co-owned with experts: they write the cases from real (de-identified) work, define a rubric with weighted requirements and penalties, and grade a calibration set that your LLM judge must agree with (Lesson 5.3). Harvey's public BigLaw Bench is a good model: tasks drawn from real billable work, lawyer-written rubrics with negative points for hallucinations, and a separate score for how many correct statements are backed by an accurate source. In production, the expert's edits to drafts are free labels: track edit rate per task type, and turn heavily edited outputs into new eval cases (Lesson 9.2).

How much to automate

The right level of autonomy depends on two numbers: how much one error costs, and how many tasks there are. Low cost, high volume: automate fully and monitor. High cost: put an expert in the loop, or keep the AI as an assistant to an expert who stays in charge. Decisions with legal or similarly significant effects on a person need a human regardless of the arithmetic.

Model of the modes (per month): full automation = $15k fixed (monitoring, evals, on-call) + errors × cost; human-in-the-loop = $5k fixed (review tooling) + an expert reviews every output for the review time and catches 95% of errors; assist-only = $1k fixed + the expert does the task with AI suggestions, taking 3× the review time and catching 99%. Real systems mix them, for example automating high-confidence items and reviewing the rest.

Things to try: “Tag support tickets” sits deep in the automation zone; at the far left, volume is too low for automation's fixed costs to pay off, so an expert with an AI assistant is cheapest. “Draft clinical note” needs review; push the error rate to 0.2% and it still does, because one error is so expensive. “Approve a consumer loan” is forced out of full automation by the legal-effect rule no matter what the arithmetic says. Then drag review minutes down: faster review (better UI, linked evidence) grows the human-in-the-loop zone, which is how good vertical products make review affordable.

import hashlib, json, datetime

def audit_record(user, task, inputs, retrieved, model, prompt_version, output, reviewer=None, final=None):
    """One immutable record per AI output: enough to reconstruct exactly what happened."""
    rec = {
        "ts": datetime.datetime.now(datetime.timezone.utc).isoformat(), "user": user, "task": task,
        "input_sha256": hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(inputs, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest(),  # raw PHI stays in the secure store
        "sources": [{"doc_id": d["id"], "version": d["version"], "span": d["span"]} for d in retrieved],
        "model": model, "prompt_version": prompt_version, "output": output,
        "reviewer": reviewer, "final": final, "edited": final is not None and final != output,
    }
    append_to_worm_store(rec)                                   # object-lock / WORM bucket with a retention period
    return rec

def unsupported_citations(answer_citations, retrieved):
    """Deterministic gate: every cited source must be one we retrieved, and its quote must appear in it."""
    by_id = {d["id"]: d["text"] for d in retrieved}
    return [c for c in answer_citations if c["doc_id"] not in by_id or c["quote"] not in by_id[c["doc_id"]]]

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Vertical startup

Pick one painful, expensive workflow (demand letters, visit notes, due-diligence review) and own it end to end. Recruit practitioners as early employees and design partners. Get SOC 2 and the BAA/DPA chain sorted before the first big customer asks. Your moat is the workflow, the outcome data you accumulate and the trust record, not a model.

Incumbent / big tech

Already owns the system of record (EHR, legal research database, trading platform) and the licensed data, so it adds AI where users already are. Its risks are slower iteration and the temptation to bolt a chatbot onto the side instead of redesigning the workflow. Partnerships between the two are common.

🎤 Interview angle

Vertical-AI interview questions, and what a strong answer covers:

  • “Design an AI that drafts clinical notes from visits.” Consent, ASR (speech recognition) tuned to medical speech, structured note sections, each sentence linked to transcript evidence, clinician sign-off, BAA chain, PHI-free logs, clinician-graded eval, edit-rate monitoring.
  • “How do you stop a legal assistant from inventing citations?” Retrieve only from a licensed database, require citations by document ID, verify quotes deterministically, block or flag unsupported claims, lawyer review before filing, and measure with an expert rubric that penalises hallucinations.
  • “What's your moat when the base model gets better?” Workflow integration, proprietary and outcome data, expert evals, compliance posture; a better base model should make your product better, not obsolete.
  • “A customer asks you to fully automate a high-stakes decision.” Do the error-cost × volume maths, check Article 22-style rules, propose staged autonomy with confidence routing.
🏭 In the real world
A law firm rolls out legal AI firm-wide 🏢 A&O + Harvey · 2023 A&O announces partnership with Harvey

How: Allen & Overy trialled Harvey (built on OpenAI's GPT-4) from November 2022 with an innovation team of lawyers and developers; by the end of the trial about 3,500 lawyers had asked it around 40,000 questions for client work, and it rolled out across 43 offices.

Why this method: a long internal trial with practising lawyers, led by the firm itself, before firm-wide use: the pilot-then-scale path most vertical products follow.

Expert rubrics with penalties for hallucination 🏢 Harvey · 2024 Introducing BigLaw Bench

How: tasks modelled on real billable work, designed by the company's attorney team; bespoke rubrics award points for each requirement and subtract points for hallucinations and other failures; a separate “source score” measures how many correct statements cite an accurate source.

Why this method: multiple-choice benchmarks don't capture what lawyers actually produce; scoring traceability separately rewards answers an expert can verify.

Ambient clinical notes at health-system scale 🏢 Kaiser Permanente + Abridge · 2024 Kaiser Permanente press release

How: Abridge's ambient listening tool drafts clinical notes across Kaiser's 40 hospitals and 600+ medical offices; it requires patient consent, and clinicians review the notes before they enter the medical record. Abridge says clinicians can validate each part of a note against the transcript and audio.

Why this method: consent, expert sign-off and linked evidence are exactly the controls that make generative AI acceptable in a medical record.

Full documents and full citations for finance 🏢 Hebbia · 2025 OpenAI customer story: Hebbia

How: Hebbia's Matrix breaks complex questions into steps, routes them to several models in parallel, processes whole documents rather than excerpts, and returns answers with full citations for investors, bankers and lawyers.

Why this method: in data rooms and filings the answer is often spread across a document rather than in one chunk, and professionals must check every figure.

A choice of AI-only or lawyer-reviewed drafts 🏢 EvenUp · 2026 EvenUp

How: personal-injury firms draft demand letters with AI, choosing instant AI demands or legal-reviewed demands by case type; the company describes a dataset of 250,000+ verdicts and settlements and says it is SOC 2-audited and HIPAA-attested.

Why this method: the review level is a product option priced by risk, and the proprietary outcome data is what a general model can't reproduce.

🤯 Fun fact · asking the liar if it lied

In Mata v. Avianca, the lawyer did try to verify a citation, by asking ChatGPT itself. It apologised for “confusion” and confirmed the invented case was real and could be found in legal databases. Verification has to come from a system that can check, not from the one that generated the claim.

Check yourself

1. A note-drafting product handles 20,000 visits a month. The model makes a material error in 3% of drafts, and clinician review catches 90% of errors. How many flawed notes reach the record each month?
20,000 × 0.03 = 600 flawed drafts; 10% slip past review: 600 × 0.1 = 60. Raising the catch rate to 99% (better review UI, linked evidence) brings it to 6, a bigger effect than halving the model's error rate.
2. Your legal research assistant sometimes cites cases that don't exist. Which change actually fixes this?
Instructions and more training reduce the rate but can't guarantee anything (even legal RAG tools hallucinated 17–33% in the Stanford study). A deterministic citation check plus the professional's sign-off is the gatekeeping the judge in Mata described.
3. A US clinic wants to send visit transcripts to your AI service, which calls a hosted model API. What must be true first?
Under HIPAA, a vendor handling PHI for a provider is a business associate and may use it only as its contract permits; that applies down the chain. Encryption is necessary but not sufficient, and LLMs are used with PHI routinely once the agreements and controls are in place.
📝 TL;DR
  • Vertical AI = one profession's data, workflow, rules and quality bar. The same error rate costs pennies in one domain and sanctions or harm in another.
  • Four patterns: RAG with verified citations, structured extraction, drafting with expert review, copilots inside the system of record.
  • Compliance is engineering: BAAs, SOC 2, GDPR agreements, record retention, audit trails and data minimisation.
  • Experts write the evals; edits are your production signal; automate by error cost × volume, and keep humans on decisions with legal effect.
