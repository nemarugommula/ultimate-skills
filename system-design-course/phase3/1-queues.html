Phase 3 · Lesson 1

Message queues & pub/sub

Put slow or spiky work in a line and let workers take it at their own pace. Queues decouple services, soak up traffic spikes and survive crashes, which is why almost every large system has one in the middle.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Latency = how long one request takes; users notice anything over a few hundred milliseconds (Lesson 0.3).
  • Stateless workers can be added or removed freely behind something that hands them work (Lesson 1.1).
  • Rate limiting = saying “not now” when load is too high, instead of falling over (Lesson 1.5).
🎯 Hook

Every Slack message post, push notification, link preview, calendar reminder and billing calculation goes through one job queue. On its busiest days, Slack reported, that queue processes over 1.4 billion jobs, peaking at 33,000 per second.

None of that work happens while you wait for your message to send. It's put in a line and done a moment later. That one design choice is what this lesson is about, along with the day the line itself broke.

Numbers first: why not just do it now?

A user signs up. Your code saves the account (about 5 ms) and sends a welcome email through an email provider's API (typically 300 ms, sometimes seconds, sometimes an error).

Sign-up latency5 + 300 = 305 ms (or a timeout)5 + ~2 ms to enqueue = 7 ms

The queue turns “do it now, in the user's request” into “do it soon, somewhere else”. That's called making the work asynchronous (async).

💡 Big idea A message queue sits between a producer (who creates work) and consumers (who do it). It decouples them in time and speed: the producer never waits, the consumer works at its own pace, and if either side is down for a while, messages simply wait in line.
🧠 Analogy: the ticket rail in a restaurant kitchen

Waiters (producers) clip orders on a rail and go back to their tables. Cooks (consumers) pull the next ticket when they're free. A rush of orders makes the rail longer, not the waiters slower. If a cook burns a dish and walks off, the ticket goes back on the rail for another cook. And a ticket nobody can cook (“a dish we don't make”) gets pinned to a separate board for the manager. That separate board is a dead-letter queue.

The words you need

TermPlain meaning
MessageA small piece of data describing work or an event: {"type":"welcome_email","user":42}. Typically a few KB; SQS allows up to 1 MiB.
Ack (acknowledgement)The consumer telling the queue “done, delete it”. No ack = the queue assumes the consumer failed.
Visibility timeoutWhile a consumer works on a message, it's hidden from others. If no ack arrives in time (SQS default: 30 s), it reappears for someone else.
RedeliveryThe same message delivered again after a crash or timeout. This is why queues are at-least-once: you may see a message twice (Lesson 3.3 fixes that).
Dead-letter queue (DLQ)Where a message goes after failing too many times (SQS calls the limit maxReceiveCount), so one bad message can't loop forever.
BackpressurePushing back on producers when consumers can't keep up: a full queue rejects new work, or producers slow down.

Spikes: the queue as a shock absorber

A queue can't create capacity. It only moves work from a busy moment to a quieter one. The math is simple subtraction:

backlog growth = arrival rate − processing rate “Each second, the line grows by what came in minus what the workers finished.” If arrivals stay above processing for good, the line grows forever.
arrivals: messages per second inprocessing: workers × jobs per worker per second
✏️ Worked example: the TV ad

Sign-ups normally arrive at 100 per second. Each worker sends 10 emails per second (100 ms each), and you run 15 workers: capacity = 15 × 10 = 150 per second. A TV ad brings 500 per second for 60 seconds.

  • During the ad the backlog grows by 500 − 150 = 350 per second. After 60 s: 350 × 60 = 21,000 messages waiting.
  • After the ad, spare capacity is 150 − 100 = 50 per second, so draining takes 21,000 ÷ 50 = 420 s = 7 minutes.
  • The unlucky last sign-up waits about 21,000 ÷ 150 = 140 s for its email. Nobody's sign-up failed.

Two knobs to reduce the wait: more workers (scale on queue depth, Lesson 5.3) or accepting the delay. The playground starts with exactly these numbers.

One message, one worker, or one message, everyone?

There are two shapes of messaging, and mixing them up is a classic design mistake:

Work queue (point-to-point)Pub/sub (publish/subscribe, fan-out)
Who gets a messageExactly one of the competing workersEvery subscriber gets its own copy
Use it forSpreading work: resize images, send emails, charge cardsAnnouncing events: “order placed” → email, inventory, analytics
Scale byAdding workers to the same queueAdding subscribers, each with its own queue and workers
ExamplesSQS queue, RabbitMQ queue, NATS queue groupSNS topic → SQS queues, RabbitMQ fanout exchange, Kafka topic (Lesson 3.2)

The detail that bites: is a subscriber's copy stored while it's offline? Redis Pub/Sub and core NATS are at-most-once: a subscriber that isn't connected at that moment never gets the message. SNS → SQS, RabbitMQ queues and Kafka store it.

🔥 Real outage: when the queue itself fills up

Slack's original job queue kept jobs in Redis, an in-memory store. In the outage that triggered its redesign, database contention slowed the workers, jobs piled up, and Redis ran out of memory. With no memory, the web app couldn't enqueue new jobs, so everything that depended on the queue failed. Worse, Redis needed a little free memory even to dequeue, so the queue stayed stuck after the database recovered and needed lots of manual work.

The fix: Slack put Kafka (a durable, disk-based log, Lesson 3.2) in front of Redis as a buffer and rate-limited the relay into Redis. A backlog now sits on disk instead of exhausting memory. Lesson: a queue's own capacity is a limit too. Monitor depth and age, and decide what happens when it's full.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Managed: AWS SQS (queues) + SNS (fan-out topics), Google Cloud Pub/Sub, Azure Service Bus. Self-hosted: RabbitMQ (flexible routing, per-message acks), Redis Streams (when you already run Redis), NATS (very lightweight; add JetStream for persistence), and Kafka for high-volume event streams (next lesson). A Python SQS worker with boto3:

import json, boto3
sqs = boto3.client("sqs")
url = sqs.get_queue_url(QueueName="welcome-emails")["QueueUrl"]

while True:
    resp = sqs.receive_message(QueueUrl=url, MaxNumberOfMessages=10,
                               WaitTimeSeconds=20,      # long polling (max 20 s): fewer empty calls
                               VisibilityTimeout=60)    # hidden from others while we work
    for m in resp.get("Messages", []):
        send_email(json.loads(m["Body"]))           # if this crashes, no delete ...
        sqs.delete_message(QueueUrl=url,                # ... so the message comes back: at-least-once
                           ReceiptHandle=m["ReceiptHandle"])

The same idea in Redis Streams, from redis-cli:

XGROUP CREATE jobs workers $ MKSTREAM                 # a consumer group: workers share the stream
XADD jobs * type welcome_email user 42                # producer appends a message
XREADGROUP GROUP workers w1 COUNT 10 BLOCK 5000 STREAMS jobs >   # worker w1 takes new ones
XACK jobs workers 1718000000000-0                      # done: remove from the pending list
XPENDING jobs workers                                  # what's been taken but never acked?

Try it locally: docker run -p 6379:6379 redis and paste the commands above into redis-cli, or docker run -p 5672:5672 -p 15672:15672 rabbitmq:management for RabbitMQ with a web UI on port 15672. A DLQ in SQS is one setting on the source queue: a redrive policy with a maxReceiveCount.

⚖️ Trade-offs: when a queue helps and when it hurts
✅ Use a queue when…❌ Think twice when…
The user doesn't need the result right now (emails, thumbnails, reports)The user is waiting for the answer (a search result, a login)
Traffic is spiky and the work can wait a littleArrivals are above capacity all the time: the queue only delays the failure
The downstream is slow or flaky (third-party APIs)Strict global ordering matters (most queues don't promise it; see FIFO queues and Kafka partitions)
Several teams need to react to the same event (fan-out)You can't make consumers handle duplicates. At-least-once means duplicates will happen.

Costs you take on: one more system to run and monitor, harder debugging (the work happens “somewhere, later”), and eventual consistency: the email arrives a moment after the sign-up, not with it.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Use a managed queue (SQS, Cloud Pub/Sub) or a job library on the database or Redis you already run (Sidekiq, Celery, BullMQ). Make every job safe to run twice. Set a DLQ and an alert on its size from day one. Don't run Kafka for a few hundred jobs a second.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Queues per workload with separate worker pools so one noisy job type can't starve the rest, autoscaling on queue depth and age, priority lanes, rate-limited relays to fragile dependencies, DLQ triage tooling and replay, and durable buffers (often Kafka) in front of anything in memory, as Slack did.

🎤 Interview angle

“Put it on a queue” is a start, not an answer. A senior answer covers:

  • Why async here: which work leaves the request path and what latency the user now sees.
  • Delivery guarantee: at-least-once, so consumers are idempotent (safe to repeat, Lesson 3.3).
  • Capacity math: arrival vs processing rate, how big the backlog gets in a spike, how long it drains.
  • Failure handling: visibility timeout vs job duration, retries with backoff, a DLQ and who looks at it.
  • Shape: work queue or fan-out, and whether subscribers need their own durable queue.

Common follow-ups: “What if the consumer crashes halfway?” “How do you keep order for one user?” “What happens when the queue is full?” “How do you know you're falling behind?” (Answer: alert on the age of the oldest message, not just depth.)

🏭 In the real world
A durable buffer in front of an in-memory queue 🏢 Slack · 2017 Scaling Slack's Job Queue

How: web apps write jobs to Kafka; a relay (JQRelay) moves them into the existing Redis-based queue at a controlled rate, and workers run them. Over 1.4 billion jobs on the busiest days.

Why this method: Kafka stores the backlog on disk, so a slow-down no longer exhausts Redis memory and blocks new jobs. They kept the existing workers and rolled it out one job type at a time.

A queue at retail scale 🏢 Amazon · 2021 Amazon SQS: 15 Years and Still Queueing

How: during Prime Day 2021, traffic to SQS peaked at 47.7 million messages per second, according to AWS. Producers and consumers never talk to each other directly; they each talk to the queue.

Why this method: a managed queue absorbs a once-a-year spike without each team sizing its own brokers for the peak.

Visibility timeouts and dead-letter queues 🏭 In production SQS visibility timeout (AWS docs)

How: a received message is hidden for the visibility timeout (default 30 s, extendable up to 12 hours). If it isn't deleted in time it becomes visible again. A redrive policy moves it to a DLQ after maxReceiveCount receives.

Why this method: the queue never needs to know whether a consumer crashed; a missing delete is enough. The DLQ keeps one bad message from looping forever.

Fire-and-forget vs stored messages 🧰 Open source Redis Pub/Sub delivery semantics

How: Redis documents Pub/Sub as at-most-once: once sent, a message is never sent again, so an offline subscriber misses it. Redis Streams persist messages and support at-least-once with consumer groups and acks.

Why this method: fire-and-forget is cheap and fast for things like live presence; anything you can't lose needs storage and acks.

Queue groups for load balancing 🧰 Open source NATS queue groups

How: subscribers that join the same queue group share a subject; each message goes to exactly one member, so adding members spreads the work.

Why this method: the same subject can serve both shapes: plain subscribers get every message (fan-out), a queue group splits the work.

🤯 Fun fact · a very old cloud service

According to AWS, the first beta of SQS was announced “with little fanfare” in late 2004, and the original one-line description of a reliable, highly scalable hosted queue still applies today. Queues are one of the oldest building blocks of the cloud.

🌉 How it connects

Backpressure is rate limiting applied to producers (Lesson 1.5). Next, Kafka turns the queue into a replayable log (Lesson 3.2). At-least-once delivery means duplicates, which idempotency fixes (Lesson 3.3). Publishing a message reliably after a database write needs the outbox (Lesson 3.5). Workers autoscale on queue depth (Lesson 5.3), and the notification system design is mostly queues (Lesson 7.8). In AI systems, batch inference and document-embedding jobs usually run from a queue so GPU workers stay busy without being flooded.

Check yourself

1. Jobs arrive at 200 per second at peak. Each worker finishes 25 jobs per second. What's the minimum number of workers so the backlog doesn't grow at peak?
Capacity must at least match arrivals: 200 ÷ 25 = 8 workers. In practice you'd run a few more (say 10) for headroom, crashes and deploys. With fewer than 8, the backlog grows by (200 − 25 × workers) per second for as long as the peak lasts.
2. A video-transcoding worker takes about 90 seconds per job. The SQS queue uses the default 30-second visibility timeout. What goes wrong?
The queue can't tell a slow worker from a dead one. Without a delete in 30 s, the message reappears and gets processed again. Set the timeout above the job's real duration, or have the worker extend it while it runs (ChangeMessageVisibility). And make the job idempotent anyway.
3. When an order is placed, three teams need to react: send an email, reserve stock, update analytics. Analytics is sometimes down for maintenance. Which design fits?
A single work queue gives each order to only one service. Redis Pub/Sub fans out but is at-most-once, so Analytics misses everything while it's down. Fan-out to durable per-subscriber queues gives every service its own copy and lets Analytics catch up later.
📝 TL;DR
  • A queue decouples producers from consumers: fast requests, survivable outages, spikes turned into backlog.
  • backlog growth = arrivals − processing. A queue absorbs spikes; it can't fix permanently missing capacity.
  • Acks + visibility timeout give at-least-once delivery: crashes cause redelivery, so consumers must handle duplicates.
  • Work queue = one consumer per message; pub/sub = every subscriber gets a copy (store it if they can be offline).
  • Always have a DLQ, a cap or backpressure plan, and alerts on depth and oldest-message age.
