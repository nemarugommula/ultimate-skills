Phase 5 · Lesson 5

Cloud building blocks & serverless

Regions, availability zones, networks, compute, managed databases, queues and permissions: the parts every company rents from AWS, Google Cloud or Azure. Knowing which block to pick, and how each one fails, is most of real-world system design.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Availability and the nines: 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year; redundancy multiplies nines only if failures are independent (Lesson 0.5).
  • Replication and failover: a standby copy of the database takes over when the primary dies (Lesson 2.3).
  • Containers, Kubernetes and Terraform: how you package, run and describe it all (Lessons 5.1–5.4).
🎯 Hook

At 9:37 AM PST on February 28, 2017, an AWS engineer debugging the S3 billing system ran a command from an established playbook. One input was entered incorrectly, and a much larger set of servers was removed than intended, including servers behind two core S3 subsystems. Both needed a full restart, something AWS hadn't done in its larger regions “for many years”.

S3 in us-east-1 (Northern Virginia) was down or degraded until 1:54 PM PST, over four hours. EC2 launches, EBS volumes restored from snapshots and Lambda in the region were hit too, because they depend on S3. Even AWS's status dashboard couldn't be updated, since it depended on S3. One region, one typo, a big chunk of the internet. This lesson is about building on the cloud knowing that any one piece of it can fail.

Numbers first: regions and availability zones

  • A region is a geographic area (us-east-1 = Northern Virginia, eu-west-1 = Ireland). AWS runs over 100 availability zones across its regions.
  • An availability zone (AZ) is one or more data centres with independent power, cooling and security. Every AWS region has at least three. They're “many kilometres” apart but within 100 km (60 miles) of each other, linked by low-latency fibre fast enough for synchronous replication.

So the AZ is your basic failure domain. If one AZ is up 99.9% of the time and two AZs failed independently, both being down at once would be 0.001 × 0.001 = one in a million: “six nines”. Real failures are not fully independent (shared software, shared control planes, the same bad config pushed everywhere), so the real number is lower. But spreading over AZs is still the cheapest big availability win there is.

💡 Big idea The cloud is a menu of building blocks that differ in who runs what. The more managed a block (a function, a managed database), the less you operate and the less you control. A good design picks the most managed block that meets the requirements, spreads it across failure domains (AZs, sometimes regions), and knows the price curve, because the cheapest option at 10 requests per second is rarely the cheapest at 10,000.
🧠 Analogy: places to stay

A VM is an unfurnished flat: total freedom, but you buy the furniture and fix the boiler. A container platform (ECS, Cloud Run) is a serviced apartment: bring your suitcase (the image), they handle the building. A function (Lambda) is a hotel room by the night: nothing to look after, you pay per night, and on a long stay it costs more than renting. A managed database is a bank vault: you could build your own safe, but the bank does backups, guards and locks. And just like a tenant, you still lock your own door: that's the shared responsibility model.

Serverless: when you don't want servers at all

Serverless means you never see or size a server: you deploy code (functions) or images (Cloud Run, Fargate) and the provider runs as many copies as traffic needs, down to zero when idle (Cloud Run scales to zero instances by default). The catches:

  • Cold starts. When no warm copy is free, the platform must start one first. AWS says cold starts typically happen in under 1% of invocations and take from under 100 ms to over 1 second. Fine for most APIs; noticeable for strict latency targets (fixes: provisioned concurrency, min instances, smaller packages).
  • Limits. A Lambda invocation runs for up to 15 minutes by default, with memory and payload caps. Long jobs belong elsewhere.
  • Cost at steady high load. You pay for wall-clock time, including time spent waiting on the database. A server waiting on I/O can serve other requests meanwhile; a function instance handles one at a time.
✏️ Worked example: when do functions get expensive?

Lambda's pricing page lists $0.20 per million requests and $0.0000166667 per GB-second (the x86 rates used in the page's own examples at the time of writing; check the page). An API call with 512 MB memory running 100 ms:

  • Compute: 0.5 GB × 0.1 s × $0.0000166667 = $0.00000083. Plus the request: $0.0000002. Total ≈ $1.03 per million requests.
  • At 10 req/s all month (10 × 3,600 × 730 = 26.3 million requests): ≈ $27/month.
  • At 100 req/s: ≈ $272/month. At 1,000 req/s: ≈ $2,716/month.
  • The same pricing page quotes an m7g.xlarge VM (4 vCPU, 16 GB, Arm-based Graviton3) at $0.1632/hour on demand in US East (N. Virginia): ≈ $119/month, or $238 for two (one per AZ).

Lambda equals two VMs at about 88 req/s of steady traffic. Below that, functions win (and cost nothing at zero traffic). Well above it, servers win, if they can handle the load and someone is willing to run them. Try it with your own numbers:

Designing for a zone failure

The classic production layout: a load balancer across 2 or 3 AZs, app servers in each, and a database with a synchronous standby in another AZ (RDS Multi-AZ, Cloud SQL high availability). The question that separates juniors from seniors: if one AZ disappears, is what's left big enough? Running 3 AZs at 100% of what you need means losing one leaves you at 67%. Sizing for it (“N+1”) costs more servers, all the time.

🔥 Real outage · Datadog, March 8, 2023

A security update to systemd was applied automatically to many of Datadog's VMs. On restart, the network daemon deleted routes that the container-networking plugin (Cilium) depended on, and nodes dropped off the network. It hit tens of thousands of nodes in five Datadog regions, spanning dozens of availability zones on three different cloud providers, and took the service down for users in all of them.

Lesson: multiple zones, regions and even clouds protect you from hardware and data-centre failures, not from one change applied everywhere at once. Roll changes out zone by zone and region by region, including the “automatic” ones from your OS (Lesson 6.2).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The AWS CLI / gcloud / az CLIs, consoles for exploring, Terraform for everything real (Lesson 5.4), and for serverless the Serverless Framework, AWS SAM or SST. A few everyday commands, and a least-privilege IAM policy:

aws ec2 describe-availability-zones --region us-east-1     # the zones you can spread over
aws s3 cp ./build s3://acme-assets/ --recursive            # upload static files
aws lambda invoke --function-name resize-image out.json    # run a function once
gcloud run deploy api --image=us-docker.pkg.dev/acme/api/api:a81b7c0 \
  --region=us-central1 --min-instances=1 --max-instances=50 --concurrency=80
  # one warm instance (no cold start), up to 50, 80 requests at once per instance

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Pick one cloud and one region. Run your API on Cloud Run, ECS Fargate or a PaaS, a managed Postgres with Multi-AZ on, S3 for files, a queue for background jobs, functions for glue. All in Terraform, billing alarms on, IAM least privilege from the start. Kubernetes can wait until you have many services and someone to own it.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Many accounts (per team and environment) under central guardrails, every service across 3 AZs sized N+1, some critical paths multi-region, reserved or committed-use pricing for the steady base load, a FinOps team watching cost per request, and regular game days that switch off a zone on purpose (Lesson 6.4).

🎤 Interview angle

In a design interview, the cloud is where your boxes actually live. A senior answer:

  • Names the failure domains: “3 AZs, N+1 sized, Multi-AZ database; a region outage is accepted, or here's the multi-region plan and its cost.”
  • Chooses managed services by default and says why a self-run component is worth it when it is.
  • Estimates cost roughly and knows where serverless stops being cheap.
  • Mentions IAM and network boundaries: private subnets for data, least-privilege roles per service.

Common follow-ups: “What happens when us-east-1 has a bad day?” “Why not Lambda for everything?” “How long does your database failover take, and what do users see during it?”

🏭 In the real world
A typo and a regional outage 🏢 AWS · 2017 Summary of the Amazon S3 Service Disruption in us-east-1

How: a mistyped input removed too much capacity; restarting index and placement subsystems took hours; dependent services in the region failed with it. AWS changed its tool to remove capacity more slowly and to refuse going below a minimum.

Why this method: the fix is a guardrail in the tool, not “be more careful”: the same idea as plan-before-apply (Lesson 5.4).

A managed NoSQL database at peak 🏢 Amazon · 2023 Prime Day 2023: all the numbers

How: DynamoDB served Alexa, the Amazon.com sites and the fulfilment centres, peaking at 126 million requests per second with single-digit-millisecond responses. On the same day 5,835 Aurora database instances processed 318 billion transactions.

Why this method: a managed service that scales by design lets product teams survive the biggest day of the year without running database clusters themselves.

Global, consistent SQL 🏢 Google · 2012 Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database (OSDI)

How: data is synchronously replicated across data centres, with externally consistent distributed transactions made possible by a time API that exposes clock uncertainty.

Why this method: applications get SQL transactions that stay correct across data centres and regions, instead of choosing between scale and consistency.

A checklist for cloud designs 🧰 AWS docs AWS Well-Architected Framework

How: six pillars to review a design against: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability, plus industry “lenses”.

Why this method: a shared vocabulary for design reviews, and a good structure for the end of an interview answer.

🤯 Fun fact · close, but not too close

AWS places the AZs of a region many kilometres apart, far enough that a fire, flood or local power cut shouldn't hit two at once, yet all within 100 km (60 miles) of each other, close enough for synchronous database replication. The distance is a deliberate trade between independence and latency.

Check yourself

1. A webhook handler gets about 5 requests per second on average, in bursts. Each runs 200 ms at 512 MB. Roughly what does it cost per month on Lambda (ignore the free tier)?
5 × 3,600 × 730 ≈ 13.1 million requests. Compute: 0.5 GB × 0.2 s × $0.0000166667 ≈ $0.00000167 per request ≈ $21.9; requests: 13.1 × $0.20 ≈ $2.6. Total ≈ $25/month, well under two always-on VMs, and bursts cost nothing extra to absorb.
2. You run 4 app servers in each of 3 AZs, and you need 10 servers' worth of capacity at peak. One AZ fails at peak. What do you have left, and is that enough?
Losing one of 3 AZs removes a third: 12 → 8. You need 10. N+1 sizing for 3 AZs means each AZ holds half the peak (5 servers), so any two can carry it: 15 servers in total.
3. A startup with 3 engineers is building its first API (Postgres-backed, unpredictable traffic). Which setup fits best?
The most managed blocks that meet the need: no cluster to run, automatic scaling (to zero if needed), a database with backups and failover handled for you, and everything reproducible. The single VM is cheap but is one failure away from losing data; self-run Kubernetes across regions is far more than three people can operate.
📝 TL;DR
  • Region = geography; AZ = independent data centre(s) within ~100 km. The AZ is your basic failure domain: spread over 2–3 and size for losing one (N+1).
  • Building blocks: VPC + security groups, load balancers, VMs / containers / functions, managed SQL and NoSQL, object storage, queues, IAM.
  • Pick the most managed block that fits; you still own data, IAM and network rules (shared responsibility).
  • Serverless: scale to zero, cold starts (<1%, up to ~1 s+), pay for wall-clock time; cheapest when traffic is low or bursty, not at high steady load (~$1 per million 100 ms / 512 MB calls).
  • Redundancy only multiplies nines for independent failures: one bad change pushed everywhere beats every zone.
