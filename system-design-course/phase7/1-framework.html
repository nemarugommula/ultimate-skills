Phase 7 · Lesson 1

The design framework: from vague prompt to architecture

A repeatable six-step path from “design Instagram” to a defended architecture in about 45 minutes. It's how senior engineers run real design reviews, and exactly what system design interviews test.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Back-of-the-envelope math: a day has 86,400 seconds, so 1 million actions a day is about 12 per second (Lesson 0.3).
  • The basic building blocks: load balancers spread traffic over servers (Lesson 1.2), caches keep hot answers in memory (Lesson 1.3), CDNs serve files near users (Lesson 1.4).
  • Thinking in trade-offs: every choice buys something and costs something (Lesson 0.1).
🎯 Hook

In December 2011, Instagram's engineers described how a startup with a small team had scaled to 14 million+ users in a little over a year. Their three rules for picking any system: keep it very simple, don't reinvent the wheel, and go with proven, solid technologies when you can.

No exotic architecture. Just the right boxes for the numbers they actually had. This lesson gives you the method to find those boxes for any prompt, and to explain why each one is there.

Numbers first: 45 minutes, six steps

A design interview, or a first design review, gives you about 45 minutes. People who fail usually don't lack knowledge. They spend 30 minutes drawing boxes and never get to the hard part. A typical budget:

Notice where the time goes: a third of it is deep dives. The high-level diagram is the entry ticket. The deep dives are where you show seniority.

💡 Big idea Every box in your diagram should be justified by a requirement or a number. “We need a CDN because 17,000 photo views per second at 200 KB is 28 Gbps” is a design. “We need a CDN because big sites have one” is a guess.
🧠 Analogy: the architect and the house

A good architect doesn't start by drawing walls. They ask: how many people live here, do you work from home, what's the budget, is it in a flood zone? Then they sketch the rough floor plan, then they work out the hard bits (the staircase, the load-bearing wall), and finally they tell you what it will cost to add a floor later. Clarify, estimate, sketch, deep dive, wrap up. Same order.

Step 1 · Clarify requirements

Prompts are vague on purpose. “Design a photo sharing app” could mean a family album for 1,000 people or Instagram. You split what you learn into two lists:

  • Functional requirements: what the system does. “Users upload photos. Users follow others. Users see a feed.” Pick the 3–4 core ones and say out loud what you're leaving out.
  • Non-functional requirements: how well it does it. Scale (users, requests), latency (how fast, usually as a percentile like p99, the time 99% of requests beat), availability (how often it's up, Lesson 0.5), consistency (is a slightly stale answer OK? Lesson 2.6), durability (can we ever lose data?).

Good non-functional requirements have numbers. Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) gives a typical service target as: respond within 300 ms for 99.9% of requests at a peak of 500 requests per second. That one sentence tells you what to optimise and how to test it.

The questions a senior asks are the ones whose answers change the design:

QuestionWhy it changes the design
How many users, and how many are active at once?One database or a sharded fleet (Lesson 2.4)
What's the read:write ratio?Read-heavy → caches and replicas. Write-heavy → queues, log-structured stores (Lessons 1.3, 2.2)
Can users see slightly stale data?Eventual consistency and caching are allowed, or every read must hit the source of truth
Real time or eventually?Push over WebSockets (Lesson 0.4) vs polling vs batch
Can we ever lose data?Replication and backups, or “best effort” is fine
One region or global?Latency to far users, data residency, multi-region writes

Questions like “which programming language?” rarely change the architecture. Assume those out loud and move on. Practice telling the two kinds apart in the game further down.

Step 2 · Estimate (by hand, out loud)

Three formulas cover most prompts:

QPS = daily actions ÷ 86,400 · peak ≈ 2–3 × average · storage = items per day × size × days kept “Requests per second is the number of actions per day divided by the seconds in a day. Peak traffic is typically two to three times the average. Storage is how many items you add per day, times their size, times how long you keep them.”

QPS means queries (requests) per second. Round hard: 86,400 ≈ 100,000 is fine for a first pass.

✏️ Worked example: a photo sharing app

Assume (and say you're assuming): 10 million daily active users. Each uploads 0.1 photos a day and views 50. Photos are about 2 MB stored and 200 KB as a feed-sized version.

  • Uploads: 10M × 0.1 = 1M per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 12 per second, peak (×3) ≈ 35/s.
  • Views: 10M × 50 = 500M per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 per second, peak ≈ 17,400/s.
  • Read:write ratio = 500M : 1M = 500 : 1. Very read-heavy → caching and a CDN are the story.
  • Storage: 1M × 2 MB = 2 TB per day, about 730 TB a year. That's object storage (Lesson 2.7), not a database.
  • Metadata (who, when, caption) at about 1 KB per photo: 1M × 1 KB = 1 GB a day, 365 GB a year. One well-indexed SQL database holds that for years.
  • Bandwidth if your servers send every view: 17,400/s × 200 KB ≈ 3.5 GB/s ≈ 28 Gbps. That's why the images go on a CDN.

Five minutes of arithmetic just decided four boxes: object storage, a CDN, a cache, and “one SQL database is fine for metadata”.

Step 3 · API and data model

Write the 3–5 calls the client makes. It forces you to agree on what the system does before you draw anything:

POST /v1/photos              {caption}  → 201 {photo_id, upload_url}   # client uploads bytes straight to object storage
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=…       → 200 {photos:[…], next_cursor}            # cursor pagination (Lesson 7.3)
POST /v1/users/{id}/follow   → 204
GET  /v1/photos/{id}         → 200 {url_on_cdn, caption, likes}

Then the main tables: users(id, name), photos(id, user_id, created_at, caption, object_key), follows(follower_id, followee_id). Name the primary key and the index your hottest query needs (Lesson 2.2): here, photos by (user_id, created_at).

Step 4 · The high-level design

Now draw boxes, and trace one read and one write through them. Try it on three prompts: pick components, and the checker tells you what's missing, what's overkill, and what your design does to the load numbers.

    Step 5 · Deep dives: where seniority shows

    You can't go deep on everything. Pick the 2–3 parts where:

    • The numbers are biggest: the 500:1 read path, the 28 Gbps of images, the celebrity with 100 million followers (Lesson 7.3).
    • Correctness is hardest: two buyers and one seat, a payment retried twice (Lesson 7.7), a message delivered out of order (Lesson 7.4).
    • The interviewer leans in: if they ask “what happens when…”, that's the deep dive they want.

    A good deep dive has the same shape every time: 2–3 options → a table of trade-offs → your choice for these numbers → how it fails and how you'd notice (metrics and alerts, Lesson 6.3).

    Step 6 · Wrap up

    In the last five minutes, find your own weak spots before the interviewer does:

    • Bottlenecks: “At 10× traffic the single database is the first thing to go; I'd add read replicas, then shard by user ID.”
    • Failure: “If the cache dies, the database sees full load for a few minutes; it can take about 5,000 QPS, so I'd warm the cache gradually.”
    • Trade-offs you made: “I chose eventual consistency for the feed; a new post may take a few seconds to show up.”
    • What's next: monitoring, cost, abuse, the features you parked.

    Practice step 1: which questions matter?

    🎮 Playground · The requirements clarifier

    Prompt: “Design a ticket sales site for a big concert.” You have 4 minutes of clarifying time, and every question costs one minute. Pick the questions whose answers change the design. Each answer updates the spec, the numbers and the diagram.

    minutes used 0 / 4
    design-changing answers 0
    peak load at open unknown
    score 0 / 10

    The best four questions uncover a flash crowd (20 fans per seat in one minute), assigned seats (no double booking), holds that expire, and payments that can fail. Those four answers are the design. “Which language?” and “dark mode?” cost you minutes and change nothing.

    🧰 Tools you'll actually use

    For real design work: a design doc (Lesson 8.1) in Google Docs, Notion or Markdown in the repo; diagrams in Excalidraw, draw.io (diagrams.net) or Mermaid; and a Python REPL for the estimates. Keep the estimate as code so anyone can rerun it when the assumptions change:

    # estimate.py: rerun when an assumption changes
DAU            = 10_000_000
uploads_per    = 0.1
views_per      = 50
photo_bytes    = 2e6
feed_img_bytes = 200e3
PEAK           = 3                      # peak ≈ 3× average (an assumption)

day = 86_400
up_qps   = DAU * uploads_per / day
view_qps = DAU * views_per / day
print(f"uploads {up_qps:.0f}/s, peak {PEAK*up_qps:.0f}/s")
print(f"views   {view_qps:,.0f}/s, peak {PEAK*view_qps:,.0f}/s")
print(f"storage {DAU*uploads_per*photo_bytes*365/1e12:.0f} TB/year")
print(f"egress  {PEAK*view_qps*feed_img_bytes*8/1e9:.0f} Gbps without a CDN")

    Try it locally: save it and run python3 estimate.py. It prints 12/s and 35/s for uploads, 5,787/s and 17,361/s for views, 730 TB a year, and 28 Gbps. A Mermaid diagram is just text: flowchart LR; U[Users]-->CDN; U-->LB-->App-->DB.

    ⚖️ Trade-offs: using a framework
    ✅ It helps when…❌ It hurts when…
    The prompt is vague and you need structure under pressureYou follow it like a script and ignore the interviewer steering you
    You need to show you can estimate and prioritiseYou spend 15 minutes on estimates nobody uses. Only estimate what changes a decision.
    Writing a real design doc: the same sections reviewers expectThe problem is tiny. A one-page note beats a six-section doc for a cron job.
    Comparing options fairly (same requirements, same numbers)You present only one option. Seniors show the road not taken and why.
    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Founding engineer (0 → 1)

    Same six steps, compressed into a one-page doc and an afternoon. Requirements are guesses, so design for 10× today, not 1000×. Pick boring managed services (one Postgres, one Redis, S3, a CDN), like Instagram's “keep it very simple”. Write down which number would force the next redesign.

    Big tech (1 → 100)

    The steps become a formal design review: a written doc (Lesson 8.1), reviewers from security, privacy and SRE (site reliability engineering), capacity numbers checked against real dashboards, a launch plan and rollback. The hard part is rarely the boxes; it's migrations, dependencies on other teams and operating it for years.

    🎤 Interview angle

    What's evaluated: problem exploration (did you find what matters?), a working design, depth on the hard parts, trade-offs, and communication (can I follow you?).

    • Common mistakes: drawing before asking; naming technologies without reasons (“Kafka, Cassandra, Kubernetes”); estimating and then ignoring the numbers; going silent while thinking; never mentioning failure.
    • Say the plan at the start: “I'll spend a few minutes on requirements, do quick estimates, sketch the design, then go deep on the two hardest parts.” It shows you drive.
    • Likely follow-ups: “What breaks first at 10× traffic?”, “What if this component goes down?”, “How would you make this cheaper?”, “What would you build first with two engineers?”
    🏭 In the real world
    Simple, proven parts first 🏢 Instagram · 2011 What Powers Instagram

    How: most data (users, photo metadata, tags) in PostgreSQL, photos in Amazon S3 behind the CloudFront CDN, and Redis powering the main feed and sessions. The post lists the three principles from the hook.

    Why this method: a small team can only operate a few systems well. Proven parts let them spend their time on the product and on the few things that really needed custom work.

    Non-functional requirements as percentiles 🏢 Amazon · 2007 Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store (SOSP)

    How: the paper states service targets like “a response within 300 ms for 99.9% of its requests for a peak client load of 500 requests per second”, and explains why Amazon measures the 99.9th percentile rather than the average.

    Why this method: an average hides the slowest customers. A percentile target at a stated peak load is testable and tells designers exactly what to optimise.

    A step-by-step design method 🏢 Google · 2018 The SRE Workbook: Non-Abstract Large System Design

    How: Google SRE teaches design in two phases. Basic design asks “Is it possible?” and “Can we do better?”; scaling up asks “Is it feasible?” (given money and hardware), “Is it resilient?” and “Can we do better?”, with real numbers at every step.

    Why this method: “non-abstract” means every design ends with concrete machine counts and costs, so a design that only works on a whiteboard gets caught early.

    Estimate before you build 📜 Google · Jeff Dean Software Engineering Advice from Building Large-Scale Distributed Systems (Stanford talk)

    How: the talk calls the ability to estimate a design's performance “without actually having to build it” an important skill, and gives the “Numbers Everyone Should Know” table (memory reference 100 ns, disk seek 10 ms, a datacenter round trip 0.5 ms).

    Why this method: a minute of arithmetic rules out designs that could never meet the target, before anyone writes code.

    🤯 Fun fact · 30 thumbnails, two designs

    In that same talk, Jeff Dean estimates a page of 30 image thumbnails. Reading the images one after another: 30 disk seeks × 10 ms + reading 30 × 256 KB at 30 MB/s ≈ 560 ms. Issuing the reads in parallel: about 18 ms (he notes variance makes it more like 30–60 ms). Same hardware, 10–30× faster, decided on the back of an envelope.

    Check yourself

    1. 50 million daily users each open the home page 8 times a day. Roughly what's the average request rate?
    50M × 8 = 400M a day. 400M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 4,600/s, so about 5,000. Peak is typically 2–3× that, so plan for 10,000–15,000/s.
    2. The interviewer says “Design a chat app.” What's the best first move?
    The answers decide the design: 1:1 only or 1,000-person groups, history forever or 30 days, online-only or offline push. Naming technologies without requirements is the most common way to lose points.
    3. You have 15 minutes left on a photo sharing design. Which deep dive is most worth it?
    Deep dives go where the numbers are biggest or correctness is hardest. The read path is 500× the write path and decides cost and latency. Frameworks and column lists rarely change the architecture.
    📝 TL;DR
    • Six steps in about 45 minutes: clarify (5–7) → estimate (3–5) → API + data (5) → high-level (10) → deep dives (10–15) → wrap up (3–5).
    • Ask the questions whose answers change the design: scale, read:write, staleness, real time, durability, regions.
    • QPS = daily actions ÷ 86,400; peak ≈ 2–3×; storage = items/day × size × days. Every box must be justified by a number or a requirement.
    • Seniority shows in the deep dives and the wrap-up: options, trade-offs, failure modes, and what breaks at 10×.
