Phase 7 · Lesson 5

Design video streaming (YouTube/Netflix)

Take a creator's raw file, turn it into many small, ready-to-play pieces at several qualities, and deliver them from servers near every viewer. Video is the biggest consumer of internet bandwidth, so every design choice here is really a cost choice.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The design framework: clarify → estimate → API and data model → high-level design → deep dives → wrap-up (Lesson 7.1).
  • Object storage (S3 and friends): cheap, durable storage for big files, addressed by key (Lesson 2.7). CDNs (content delivery networks) keep copies of files in cities near users (Lesson 1.4).
  • Queues hand work to background workers so the request path stays fast (Lesson 3.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2021 YouTube said that, on average, more than 500 hours of video are uploaded every minute. Every one of those hours has to be converted into several qualities and formats before anyone can watch it smoothly.

Doing that on ordinary servers got so expensive that YouTube designed its own chip for it, the Video (trans)Coding Unit, and reported 20–33× better compute efficiency than its previous optimized system. By the end of this lesson you'll know why that conversion step exists, why it's split into hundreds of small jobs, and how a player on a shaky train connection keeps playing.

Step 1 · Clarify the requirements

“Design YouTube” is too big for 45 minutes. A senior candidate narrows it first. Questions worth asking:

  • Upload, watch, or both? Both, but the deep dives will be on processing and playback, not comments or recommendations.
  • Video on demand or live? On demand (pre-recorded). Live streaming has a different latency budget; mention it as an extension.
  • Which devices? Phones, browsers and TVs, on networks from fibre to patchy mobile. That forces several qualities.
  • How fast must an upload become watchable? Minutes, not hours, for a typical 10-minute video.
  • Global? Yes, viewers everywhere. That forces a CDN.

Step 2 · Numbers first

Two numbers drive this whole design: how much storage each video costs, and how much bandwidth watching costs. Start with one video.

Video quality is measured in bitrate: how many bits per second of video. 5,000 kbps (kilobits per second) = 5 Mbps. A player can only play smoothly if the network delivers at least the bitrate.

We'll produce several versions of each video, called renditions. The list of them is the bitrate ladder. An example ladder (typical values, rounded):

RenditionBitrate10-minute video
240p400 kbps30 MB
360p800 kbps60 MB
480p1,400 kbps105 MB
720p2,800 kbps210 MB
1080p5,000 kbps375 MB
Whole ladder10,400 kbps780 MB
size = bitrate × duration ÷ 8 “Size in bytes is the bitrate in bits per second, times the seconds, divided by 8 bits per byte.”
✏️ Worked example: YouTube-sized storage and bandwidth
  • One 1080p rung, 10 minutes: 5,000,000 bits/s × 600 s ÷ 8 = 375,000,000 bytes = 375 MB.
  • The whole ladder for one hour: 10,400 kbps × 3,600 s ÷ 8 = 4.68 GB per hour of video.
  • Uploads per day at 500 hours/minute: 500 × 60 × 24 = 720,000 hours per day.
  • New ladder storage per day: 720,000 × 4.68 GB ≈ 3.4 PB per day (a petabyte is a million GB). Keeping the original upload too (assume 10 Mbps sources, 4.5 GB/hour) adds about 3.2 PB more.
  • Watching: assume 100 million viewers watch 60 minutes a day at an average 3 Mbps. That's 108 × 3,600 s × 3 Mbps ÷ 8 = 135 PB served per day, an average of 12.5 terabits per second.

Conclusions you can say out loud: storage grows by petabytes a day, so storage tiers matter. Egress (bytes sent out) is even bigger, so the design lives or dies on the CDN. And the request rate for metadata (titles, view counts) is small by comparison.

💡 Big idea Never stream one big file. Cut each video into small segments (a few seconds each), encode every segment at several bitrates, and let the player pick the next segment's quality based on how fast its network is right now. Small, immutable files are what make CDNs, parallel encoding and adaptive playback all work.
🧠 Analogy: a buffet, not a set menu

A restaurant that serves one giant plate to everyone wastes food on small eaters and starves big ones. A buffet puts out small portions in several sizes, and each diner takes what they can manage, one plate at a time. Segments are the small plates. The bitrate ladder is the choice of sizes. The player is the diner deciding, plate by plate, how hungry (how fast) it is.

Step 3 · API and data model

The upload API never carries the video bytes itself. Your servers hand out presigned URLs: short-lived links that let the client upload straight into object storage without any cloud credentials of its own.

Step 4 · High-level design

Two paths that barely touch: a write path (upload → process) that can be slow, and a read path (watch) that must be fast and is almost entirely files on a CDN.

Step 5 · Deep dives

Deep dive 1: resumable upload and the transcoding pipeline

A 2 GB upload over a phone connection will fail somewhere. If failure means “start again”, big uploads never finish. So the client splits the file into parts (S3 calls this multipart upload: up to 10,000 numbered parts, recommended for anything 100 MB or larger) and retries only the part that failed. Google's YouTube Data API has its own resumable upload protocol for the same reason.

When the upload completes, object storage emits an event and a workflow starts. It's a DAG (directed acyclic graph: a set of jobs where arrows say “this must finish before that starts”):

StageWhat it doesParallel?
InspectIs the file valid? Duration, resolution, frame ratePer chunk
SplitCut into chunks at keyframes (full pictures the decoder can start from)One job
EncodeEach chunk × each rung of the ladder (and each codec, the compression format, like H.264 or AV1)Yes: chunks × rungs jobs
Assemble + packageJoin chunks, cut into 2–6 s segments, write manifests (HLS/DASH, below)Per rung
Side jobsThumbnails, captions, content checksAlongside encode
PublishSet status = READY in the databaseLast

Why chunk? A 10-minute video in 1-minute chunks × 5 rungs = 50 independent jobs. If one 1080p chunk takes about 2 minutes to encode (an assumption for software encoding), 50 workers finish in roughly the time of the slowest chunk plus split and assembly, instead of the hour or so one machine would need. And a crashed worker only loses one chunk, which the queue hands to someone else (at-least-once delivery, Lesson 3.1; make each job idempotent, safe to run twice, Lesson 3.3).

Deep dive 2: adaptive bitrate streaming (HLS and DASH)

ABR (adaptive bitrate) streaming is the trick that makes playback smooth. The two standard formats are Apple's HLS (HTTP Live Streaming, published as RFC 8216) and MPEG-DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP). Both work the same way: a manifest (a small text file) lists the renditions, and each rendition's playlist lists its segments.

# master.m3u8: the menu
#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=800000,RESOLUTION=640x360
360p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=2800000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
720p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=5000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
1080p/index.m3u8

# 720p/index.m3u8: the segments of one rendition
#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-TARGETDURATION:4
#EXTINF:4.0,
seg_00001.m4s
#EXTINF:4.0,
seg_00002.m4s

The player keeps a buffer (seconds of video downloaded but not yet played). Before each segment it decides which rung to fetch. Fetch too high and the buffer drains: rebuffer. Fetch too low and the picture is needlessly blurry. Two families of rules:

  • Throughput-based: measure how fast the last few segments downloaded and pick the highest rung below, say, 80% of that.
  • Buffer-based: look at the buffer. Nearly empty? Lowest rung. Comfortably full? Highest. In between, step up gradually.

What you should see: a fixed high bitrate works only while the network beats it; the moment it dips, the buffer empties and the video stalls. Adaptive rules trade a few seconds of lower quality for no spinning wheel, which viewers care about far more. Real players (hls.js, Shaka Player, ExoPlayer, AVPlayer) use more refined versions of these same rules.

Deep dive 3: CDN delivery and storage tiers

At 12.5 Tbps no single data centre can serve everyone. Segments are perfect CDN content: small, immutable (a segment never changes, so caches never go stale), and requested with plain HTTP. Popularity is lopsided: a small share of videos gets most views, so edge caches hit very often for them, while the long tail is fetched from origin on demand (Lesson 1.3's skew, Lesson 1.4's edge).

Netflix goes further: its own CDN, Open Connect, puts Netflix's cache servers inside internet service providers' networks; it says it partners with over a thousand ISPs. Storage gets the same lopsided treatment: hot renditions in standard object storage, old unwatched ones moved to cheaper “infrequent access” or archive classes, and expensive extras (4K, newer codecs) produced only for videos that turn out to be popular.

One more: counting views

A viral video can get millions of view events per minute. Don't run UPDATE videos SET views = views + 1 for each: that's a hot row (Lesson 2.4). Send view events to a stream (Kafka, Lesson 3.2), aggregate per video every few seconds (Lesson 3.6), and write the totals in batches. Viewers accept a count that's a few seconds behind; deduplicate and filter bots before the count becomes “official”.

A two-rung HLS ladder with FFmpeg:

ffmpeg -i source.mp4 \
  -map 0:v -map 0:a -map 0:v -map 0:a \
  -c:v libx264 -c:a aac -g 48 -keyint_min 48 \          # fixed keyframe spacing so rungs line up
  -b:v:0 800k  -s:v:0 640x360 \
  -b:v:1 2800k -s:v:1 1280x720 \
  -f hls -hls_time 4 -hls_playlist_type vod \
  -hls_segment_filename "out/%v/seg_%05d.ts" \
  -master_pl_name master.m3u8 \
  -var_stream_map "v:0,a:0 v:1,a:1" out/%v/index.m3u8

s3 = boto3.client("s3")
url = s3.generate_presigned_url("put_object",

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Don't build a transcoder. Presigned uploads to S3, then Mux, Cloudflare Stream or MediaConvert, behind a managed CDN, with a 3–4 rung ladder. Spend your time on the product. Watch two numbers: encoding cost per uploaded hour and egress per viewer-hour.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Own chunked encoding fleet (or custom chips), per-title/per-shot ladders, several codecs chosen per device, storage tiering, lazy renditions, multi-CDN or embedded ISP caches, and player teams tuning ABR with A/B tests on rebuffer rate and startup time (Lesson 6.3).

🎤 Interview angle

What interviewers listen for:

  • You separate the write path from the read path and say the read path is “files on a CDN”.
  • You do the storage and egress math and conclude that cost, not QPS, is the main constraint.
  • You explain segments + bitrate ladder + ABR and who decides the quality (the player).
  • You make the pipeline chunked, parallel and retryable, with a status field the API exposes.

Common mistakes: streaming one MP4 from an app server; uploading through your API servers; one big “transcode” box with no failure story; forgetting the CDN. Likely follow-ups: “How would you support live streaming?” (shorter segments, low-latency HLS, no time to encode a full ladder ahead), “A video goes viral in a country with weak networks; what changes?”, “How do you count views without a hot row?”, “How do you stop people downloading paid content?” (DRM, digital rights management: encrypted segments plus licence servers).

🏭 In the real world
Per-title encoding 🏢 Netflix · 2015 Per-Title Encode Optimization

How: instead of one fixed ladder whose top rung was 5,800 kbps for 1080p, Netflix analyses each title and builds its own ladder. For one simple title the top 1080p rung dropped to 4,640 kbps, a 20% bitrate saving at the same quality.

Why this method: a cartoon needs far fewer bits than grainy film, so a one-size ladder either wastes bandwidth or looks blocky.

Custom transcoding chips 🏢 YouTube · 2021 Reimagining video infrastructure to empower YouTube

How: YouTube built the Video (trans)Coding Unit, hardware dedicated to transcoding, and reported 20–33× compute-efficiency improvements over its previous optimized software system. The same post cites more than 500 hours uploaded per minute.

Why this method: at that upload volume, transcoding is a fleet-sized bill, so specialised hardware pays for itself.

A CDN inside ISPs 🏢 Netflix · Open Connect Netflix Open Connect

How: Netflix places its own cache appliances inside ISP networks and peers openly at interconnection points; it says it partners with over a thousand ISPs.

Why this method: serving video from inside the viewer's ISP shortens the path, cuts transit costs for both sides and makes playback faster.

The HLS standard 📜 RFC 8216 · 2017 HTTP Live Streaming (RFC 8216)

How: defines the playlists (manifests), media segments and the tags like EXT-X-STREAM-INF and EXT-X-TARGETDURATION that every HLS player reads.

Why this method: everything is ordinary HTTP files, so any web server or CDN can deliver video with no special streaming servers.

Multipart, resumable uploads 🏭 In production · AWS S3 Uploading and copying objects using multipart upload

How: a file is uploaded as up to 10,000 numbered parts that can be sent in parallel, paused and resumed; AWS recommends it for objects of 100 MB or more. Presigned URLs let clients upload parts without AWS credentials.

Why this method: a network error only costs one part, not the whole multi-GB upload.

🤯 Fun fact · the strangest number in the HLS spec

The example playlist in RFC 8216 lists segments of 9.009 seconds. That isn't a typo: 9.009 s is exactly 270 frames of video at 29.97 frames per second, the frame rate inherited from North American broadcast TV. Segment boundaries have to land on whole frames (ideally keyframes), so real playlists are full of numbers like this.

Check yourself

1. One hour of video is encoded into a ladder whose bitrates add up to 10 Mbps. Roughly how much storage does the ladder take?
10,000,000 bits/s × 3,600 s ÷ 8 = 4,500,000,000 bytes = 4.5 GB. Multiply by hundreds of thousands of uploaded hours a day and storage tiering stops being optional.
2. Viewers on trains complain the video keeps freezing, though it looks great when it plays. You serve one 1080p MP4 file. What's the best fix?
The bottleneck is the viewer's last-mile network, not your servers. With several renditions in small segments, the player drops to a lower bitrate when the train enters a bad patch and climbs back up after. Longer segments of one bitrate still stall.
3. Why do clients upload video straight to object storage with presigned URLs instead of through your API servers?
Streaming gigabytes through your API ties up servers for minutes per upload and makes failures expensive. A presigned URL is a short-lived permission slip for one object; the bytes go where they'll live anyway, part by part.
📝 TL;DR
  • Size = bitrate × duration ÷ 8. A 5-rung ladder is about 4.7 GB per hour; at YouTube scale that's petabytes a day, and egress is bigger still.
  • Write path: presigned, multipart uploads → a chunked DAG of retryable encode jobs → segments + manifests in object storage.
  • Read path: the player fetches small segments from a CDN and picks each one's bitrate (ABR) from its buffer and throughput.
  • The main constraint is cost: tier storage, encode expensive renditions lazily, tune the ladder per title, push bytes to the edge.
