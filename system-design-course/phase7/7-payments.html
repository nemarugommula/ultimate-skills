Phase 7 · Lesson 7

Design a payment system

Move money between a customer, your company and the people you pay, correctly every time, even when networks time out and messages arrive twice. The traffic is small; the cost of one bug is a real person charged twice.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Idempotency: doing an operation twice has the same effect as doing it once; retries need it (Lesson 3.3).
  • Transactions: several writes that all happen or none do (ACID, Lesson 2.5). Sagas and the outbox for steps across services (Lesson 3.5).
  • Timeouts and retries with backoff, and why a timeout doesn't mean “it failed” (Lesson 4.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2019 Airbnb's payments engineers published “Avoiding Double Payments in a Distributed Payments System”. Their goal, in one phrase: the guest is charged at most once, however many retries happen across their payment microservices.

They built a shared idempotency library called Orpheus for every payments service, and one section of the post is titled, bluntly, “A duplicate payment created as a result from replica lag”. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to point at the exact moment a double charge happens, and the two ideas that stop it: idempotency keys and a double-entry ledger.

Step 1 · Clarify the requirements

Questions a senior candidate asks:

  • Who's paying whom? Let's design a marketplace (like the ride app in Lesson 7.6): riders pay, we keep a fee, drivers get paid out weekly.
  • Do we touch card numbers? No. A PSP (payment service provider, such as Stripe, Adyen or Braintree) collects card details in its own form and gives us a token. That keeps us out of most PCI DSS scope (the card industry's security standard).
  • Charge now, or hold and charge later? Hold at request, charge at trip end (authorize and capture, below).
  • Currencies, refunds, disputes? One currency for now; refunds yes; disputes as a follow-up.
FunctionalNon-functional

Step 2 · Numbers first (they're small)

✏️ Worked example: how big is “big” for payments?
  • Volume: 10,000,000 payments a day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 116 per second on average. Assume a 5× peak: about 580 per second. One well-tuned Postgres primary can take that (Lesson 2.5).
  • Ledger rows: each payment creates about 4 ledger lines (charge, fee, driver share, settlement) × 200 bytes = 800 B. 10,000,000 × 800 B = 8 GB a day, about 2.9 TB a year. Big enough to plan partitioning, small next to video.
  • Latency: a PSP call typically takes a few hundred milliseconds to a couple of seconds, because it goes to the card network and the customer's bank. Your own code is not the slow part.

So the hard part isn't scale. It's correctness under partial failure: a call that times out after the money moved, a webhook delivered twice, a crash between “charged” and “recorded”.

How a card payment actually flows

StepWhat happensMoney moves?
1. AuthorizeThe PSP asks the card network and the issuing bank (the customer's bank) to approve and hold the amountNo, it's reserved. Stripe's docs list about 7 days for most online card holds
2. CaptureYou tell the PSP to actually take the held amount (all of it or less)Yes, it's now a charge
3. SettleThe PSP batches captured charges and pays your bank account, minus its feesYes, later (often days)
4. PayoutYou pay sellers/drivers what you owe themYes, from your bank

The PSP reports what happened by calling your server: a webhook (an HTTP POST to a URL you registered, like payment_intent.succeeded). Webhooks are at-least-once: Stripe's docs warn an endpoint may receive the same event more than once, retries for up to three days, and doesn't guarantee order.

💡 Big idea A payment is a state machine you persist before every external call, and every external call carries an idempotency key. Then a timeout is not a mystery: it's the UNKNOWN state, and the fix is to ask again with the same key, never to charge again with a new one.
🧠 Analogy: the cloakroom ticket

You hand over your coat and get ticket #47. If you come back and say “I gave you a coat”, the attendant might hand you any coat, or a second one. If you say “ticket #47”, they check: either your coat is on the hook (here it is) or it isn't. The ticket is the idempotency key: it turns a vague “do this” into “do this specific thing, once”. And the attendant's book where every coat in and out is written down, never erased, is the ledger.

Step 4 · High-level design

The payment service owns the state machine. It writes to its database first, then calls the PSP, then records the answer. The PSP's webhook is a second, independent path to the same truth, so a lost response can still be resolved.

Step 5 · Deep dives

Deep dive 1: idempotency keys, end to end

The client creates a key once per intent (“pay for order 8812”), not per HTTP request, and sends it with every retry. The server does this, in one database transaction where possible:

  1. Claim the key: INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING. If the key already exists with a stored response, return that response. If it exists and is still in progress, return 409 “try again shortly”.
  2. Record intent: payment row in PROCESSING, committed before calling the PSP (Airbnb's Orpheus calls this the pre-RPC phase, RPC meaning remote procedure call: the network call to another service).
  3. Call the PSP with the same idempotency key (Stripe accepts an Idempotency-Key header), so even our retry can't create a second charge there.
  4. Record the outcome (post-RPC): SUCCEEDED / FAILED + the stored response. On a timeout: UNKNOWN, and a background job re-asks the PSP with the same key or looks the payment up.

Read the keys from the primary database, never a replica: a lagging replica (Lesson 2.3) can say “never seen this key” for a payment that's already in flight. That's exactly the duplicate Airbnb describes.

Deep dive 2: the double-entry ledger

A single balance column that you UPDATE can't explain itself: when it's wrong, nobody knows why. Accountants solved this centuries ago. Every movement of money is a journal entry with at least two lines: debits on one side, credits on the other, and they must be equal. Money never appears or disappears; it moves between accounts. Balances are just sums of lines.

Rider pays $20; driver earns $16; we keep $4DebitCredit
PSP receivable (the PSP owes us)20.00
Driver payable (we owe the driver)16.00
Platform revenue4.00
Total20.0020.00 ✅

Rules engineers add: lines are append-only (a mistake is fixed by a new reversing entry, never an edit), each entry is written in one transaction, and an hourly check asserts every entry sums to zero.

Deep dive 3: reconciliation

Even with perfect code, three records of the same money exist: your ledger, the PSP's report, and the bank statement. Every day a reconciliation job matches them line by line (by PSP reference and amount) and raises every mismatch: a charge the PSP has but you don't (a lost response you never resolved), one you have twice (a double-processed webhook), a settlement short by a fee you didn't expect. It's the safety net that catches whatever the state machine missed. Big companies have whole teams whose dashboards are reconciliation breaks.

Webhooks, done safely

  • Verify the signature (PSPs sign webhook bodies with a shared secret) so nobody can fake “payment succeeded”.
  • Dedupe by event id: insert into processed_events first; a unique-key violation means “already handled, return 200”.
  • Don't assume order: a refund event can arrive before the charge event. Apply events as state-machine transitions, and ignore ones that would move backwards.
  • Return 200 fast, then do the work from a queue (Lesson 3.1), so the PSP doesn't time out and retry.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

PSPs: Stripe, Adyen, Braintree, Checkout.com; ledger products and open source: Modern Treasury, TigerBeetle (an open-source financial transactions database), Formance; plain Postgres for most companies. A charge with an idempotency key:

curl https://api.stripe.com/v1/payment_intents \
  -u sk_test_…: \
  -H "Idempotency-Key: ord_8812-attempt" \
  -d amount=5000 -d currency=usd -d capture_method=manual   # authorize now, capture later
-- claim the key: exactly one request wins
INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (key, request_hash, status)
VALUES ($1, $2, 'started') ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING RETURNING key;

-- every journal entry must balance: this query must always return zero rows
SELECT entry_id, SUM(amount_cents) FROM ledger_lines
 GROUP BY entry_id HAVING SUM(amount_cents) <> 0;

Try it locally: docker run -e POSTGRES_PASSWORD=dev -p 5432:5432 postgres, create the two tables, then run the same INSERT … ON CONFLICT twice: the second returns no row. Stripe test mode with sk_test_ keys lets you replay the same Idempotency-Key and see the identical response.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Use a PSP's hosted checkout; never store card numbers. On day one: idempotency keys on every money-moving endpoint, webhook dedupe, integer cents, and a double-entry ledger table even if it feels like overkill. Retrofitting a ledger onto years of UPDATE balance is one of the most painful migrations there is.

Big tech (1 → 100)

A payments platform team: multiple PSPs and acquirers with smart routing, a dedicated ledger service, automated reconciliation and break dashboards, fraud models before authorization, payouts in dozens of currencies, and regulators and auditors as stakeholders in every design review.

🎤 Interview angle
  • Say early that the problem is correctness, not QPS (show the small numbers).
  • Explain authorize vs capture and the role of the PSP; keep card data out of your systems.
  • Draw the state machine, including UNKNOWN, and how it's resolved.
  • Idempotency keys from client to your service to the PSP; storage on the primary.
  • Double-entry ledger, append-only, plus reconciliation; webhooks deduped and order-independent.

Common mistakes: “retry on timeout” with no key; treating a timeout as a failure and asking the user to pay again; a mutable balance column; floats for money; trusting webhook order. Likely follow-ups: “The PSP is down: what does checkout do?”, “How do you pay out 1 million drivers every Monday?” (a batch saga with per-payout idempotency), “How do refunds and partial captures fit the ledger?”, “How do you handle a currency conversion?”

🏭 In the real world
Idempotency keys in a public API 🏢 Stripe · 2017 Designing robust and predictable APIs with idempotency

How: clients send a unique Idempotency-Key header; the server recognises retries of the same request. The post pairs this with exponential backoff and random jitter for client retries.

Why this method: after a dropped connection the outcome is ambiguous to the client; the key makes retrying safe instead of risky.

A shared idempotency library 🏢 Airbnb · 2019 Avoiding Double Payments in a Distributed Payments System

How: Orpheus splits every request into Pre-RPC (record the request), RPC (call the external service) and Post-RPC (record the response and whether an error is retryable), with leases on keys and reads from the primary only.

Why this method: a library let every payments team get the same guarantees without each engineer becoming a distributed-systems expert.

An immutable double-entry database 🏢 Square · 2019 Books: an immutable double-entry accounting database service

How: money moves between “books” only through journal entries that must balance to zero, so each cent lost is matched by a cent gained; books are immutable except through those entries.

Why this method: the ledger records not just what changed but why, which is what finance and auditors need.

Accounting explained for engineers 🏢 Modern Treasury · 2022 Accounting for Developers, Part I

How: walks through accounts, debits and credits with a Venmo-style transfer; the power of double entry is that the sum of debit-normal balances always equals the sum of credit-normal balances.

Why this method: engineers building money movement need the accountant's invariants, not just database ones.

At-least-once webhooks 🏭 In production · Stripe docs Receive Stripe events in your webhook endpoint

How: endpoints may receive the same event more than once (log processed event ids), events aren't guaranteed in order, and failed deliveries are retried for up to three days with exponential backoff.

Why this method: at-least-once with dedupe on the receiver is far easier to make reliable than exactly-once delivery over the internet (Lesson 3.3).

🤯 Fun fact · even the errors are idempotent

Stripe's API docs say that it saves the status code and body of the first request made with an idempotency key, and retries with the same key return that saved response, including 500 errors. That sounds odd until you think like a payments engineer: a retry must never be a second roll of the dice. (The same docs say keys can be removed automatically once they're at least 24 hours old, so a key is a safety window, not forever.)

Check yourself

1. A marketplace processes 50 million payments a day, with a peak of 5× the average. Roughly what peak rate must the payment service handle?
50,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 579 per second on average; × 5 ≈ 2,900 per second. Busy, but well within a sharded SQL setup. The difficulty in payments is correctness under failure, not raw throughput.
2. Your call to the PSP times out after 10 seconds. You don't know whether the card was charged. What should the payment service do?
A timeout means “I don't know”, not “no”. Asking the customer to pay again or sending a fresh key are both how double charges happen. The same key makes the retry safe: the PSP returns the original result.
3. A payment_intent.succeeded webhook arrives twice and your handler shipped the order twice. What's the fix?
Webhooks are at-least-once by design, so the receiver must be idempotent. A unique processed_events(event_id) row, inserted in the same transaction as the side effects, makes the second delivery a harmless no-op.
📝 TL;DR
  • Payments are low QPS, high stakes: design for correctness under timeouts, retries and duplicate messages.
  • Persist a state machine before every external call; a timeout is UNKNOWN, resolved by retrying with the same idempotency key.
  • Record money in an append-only, double-entry ledger in integer cents: every entry's debits equal its credits.
  • Webhooks are at-least-once and unordered: verify, dedupe by event id, apply as state transitions. Reconcile daily against the PSP and bank.
