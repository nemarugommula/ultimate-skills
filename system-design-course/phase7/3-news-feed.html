Phase 7 · Lesson 3

Design a news feed (fan-out)

Show each user the latest posts from everyone they follow, in milliseconds, for hundreds of millions of users. The core decision, doing the work when a post is written or when the feed is read, shapes the whole system.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Redis keeps data in memory: lists and sorted sets answer in about a millisecond (Lesson 1.3).
  • Queues and workers do slow work in the background instead of in the request (Lesson 3.1).
  • p99 = the latency 99% of requests beat; asking many servers at once means waiting for the slowest (Lessons 0.5 and 6.3).
🎯 Hook

At QCon London 2014, a Twitter engineer showed the timeline numbers: more than 240 million active users and 300,000 queries per second of timeline reads, answered in 1 ms at the median and 4 ms at p99, out of a Redis timeline cache filled by a “fanout” service.

Four milliseconds to assemble a personal feed from hundreds of followed accounts is only possible because the feed was mostly built before anyone asked for it. This lesson is about when to do that work, and the celebrity who breaks it.

Step 1 · Requirements

Functional: users publish posts (text, photo links); users follow other users; the home feed shows recent posts from followed accounts, newest or most relevant first, 20 at a time, with infinite scroll.

Non-functional: feed loads fast (target p99 under about 200 ms end to end); very read-heavy; eventual consistency is fine (a new post can take a few seconds to show up in followers' feeds); highly available (a stale feed beats an error page).

Step 2 · Estimates (by hand)

✏️ Worked example: the feed's numbers
  • Feed reads: 200M × 10 = 2B a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 23,000/s, peak (×2.5) ≈ 58,000/s.
  • Posts: 10% of users post once a day = 20M a day ≈ 230/s. Reads outnumber posts 100 to 1.
  • Fan-out on write: each post copied into ~200 followers' feeds = 20M × 200 = 4B inserts a day ≈ 46,000 inserts/s.
  • Fan-out on read instead: each feed load asks ~200 followed accounts for recent posts: 58,000 × 200 ≈ 11.6 million lookups/s at peak.
  • Feed storage: keep 500 post IDs × 8 bytes per active user = 4 KB; × 200M ≈ 800 GB of raw data (Redis overhead can roughly double it): a cluster of a few dozen big Redis nodes.
  • One celebrity with 100M followers posts. Fan-out on write = 100M inserts for one post. At 1M inserts/s of worker capacity, that's 100 seconds of backlog, and everyone else's posts wait behind it.

Step 5 · High-level design and the core decision

There are two ways to build a feed, and the names say where the copying happens:

Fan-out on write (push)Fan-out on read (pull)
When a post is publishedA worker inserts its ID into every follower's timelineJust store it
When a feed is readRead one precomputed list: fastAsk every followed account for recent posts, merge, sort: slow
Cost scales withPosts × followers (writes)Feed reads × followings (reads)
Breaks onCelebrities (one post → 100M writes)Users who follow thousands; tail latency
Wasted workWriting to feeds of users who never log inNone, but repeated on every refresh
💡 Big idea A feed is a trade between work at write time and work at read time. Reads outnumber writes 100:1, so pushing work to write time usually wins, except for the few accounts whose single write fans out to millions. So big systems do both: push for normal accounts, pull for celebrities.
🧠 Analogy: newspapers vs a newsstand

Fan-out on write is home delivery: the moment the paper is printed, a copy goes into every subscriber's mailbox, so reading it in the morning is instant. Fan-out on read is the newsstand: nothing is delivered, and each reader walks around collecting the papers they like. Home delivery is great, until one paper has 100 million subscribers and the vans are still out at noon. So the giant papers stay at the newsstand, and your morning routine is: open the mailbox, then grab two papers on the way.

Deep dive 1 · The celebrity problem

Follower counts are wildly lopsided: most accounts have a few hundred followers, a few hundred accounts have millions. With pure fan-out on write, those few accounts cause:

  • Write storms: one post = tens of millions of inserts, delaying everyone's posts behind it (the backlog you just saw).
  • Hot shards: fan-out workers all hammer the timeline shards at once.
  • Waste: many of those followers won't open the app today; their feeds get written anyway.

The hybrid: accounts above a follower threshold are not fanned out. Their recent posts sit in one small, heavily cached list per celebrity. At read time, the feed service fetches your precomputed timeline plus the recent posts of the few celebrities you follow, and merges them. Where should the threshold go?

🎮 Playground · Pick the hybrid threshold

A model of 200M accounts whose follower counts follow a power law (the top account has 100M followers, the median account about 100), with about 236 follows per user. The sweet spot is usually somewhere around a few hundred thousand to a million followers: only a few thousand accounts or fewer are pulled, the biggest burst drops from 100M to under a million inserts, and each feed load merges just a handful of extra lists. Those celebrity lists are hot keys, so they get replicated across cache servers (Lesson 1.3).

Deep dive 2 · Storing, hydrating and paging the feed

  • Store IDs, not posts. A timeline is a Redis list or sorted set of post IDs, capped at ~500 with LTRIM. On read: take 20 IDs, hydrate them with one multi-get from the post cache, drop deleted or blocked ones. The Twitter slides show this shape: get timeline tweet IDs, then get each tweet.
  • Only active users. Skip fan-out for users who haven't logged in for weeks; rebuild their feed on demand (pull) when they return.
  • Pagination with a cursor, not an offset. “Page 2 = items 21–40” breaks when 5 new posts arrive while you scroll: you see 5 duplicates. Instead the client sends “older than post_id X”. Time-sortable IDs make that a range query.
  • Ranking runs after collecting candidates: gather a few hundred candidate posts (timeline + celebrities + recommendations), compute features (author affinity, engagement, freshness), score them with a model, re-rank, then page. Facebook's Multifeed does ranking and filtering in its aggregators at read time.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Redis (lists or sorted sets for timelines), Kafka or SQS for the fan-out queue, PostgreSQL, Cassandra or DynamoDB for posts, a graph store for follows (Facebook built TAO), and managed feed services such as Stream (getstream.io) if you'd rather not build it. 🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Fan-out on read with one indexed SQL query and a short cache. It's correct, easy to change (ranking experiments are just a different ORDER BY), and fine for the first hundreds of thousands of users. Move to Redis timelines when the query shows up in your p99 dashboards, not before.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Hybrid push/pull, timelines only for active users, machine-learned ranking over hundreds of candidates, separate teams for the graph, timelines, ranking and integrity (spam, abuse). Facebook goes further toward pull: its feed is assembled per viewer at read time from an in-memory index, because privacy checks depend on who is looking.

🎤 Interview angle

What's evaluated: the push vs pull trade-off with numbers, the celebrity problem and the hybrid, storing IDs and hydrating, cursor pagination, and where ranking fits.

  • Common mistakes: picking push or pull without estimating both sides; storing full posts in every timeline (edits and deletes become fan-outs too); offset pagination; forgetting inactive users; no plan for a celebrity.
  • Likely follow-ups: “A user unfollows someone: what happens to their feed?” (filter at read time or remove lazily), “How do deletes propagate?”, “What if the timeline cache cluster is lost?” (rebuild from posts + follows, pull mode meanwhile), “How do you add ads or recommendations?”, “How fresh must ranking features be?”
🏭 In the real world
Push into Redis timelines 🏢 Twitter · 2012–2014 Timelines at Scale (Raffi Krikorian, QCon San Francisco 2012, on InfoQ)

How: the talk explains how Twitter delivers tweets into home timelines. A later QCon London 2014 deck by Twitter's Brian Degenhardt (slides) shows the write path (Write API → Fanout → Redis timeline cache) and reports 300k QPS of timeline reads at 1 ms p50 / 4 ms p99.

Why this method: reads vastly outnumber tweets, so precomputing timelines makes each read a single in-memory lookup.

Pull, because every viewer sees something different 🏢 Facebook · 2013 TAO: Facebook's Distributed Data Store for the Social Graph (USENIX ATC)

How: because every item is privacy-checked for the current viewer, “we resolve data dependencies and check privacy each time the content is viewed. As much as possible we pull the social graph, rather than pushing it.” TAO serves “a billion reads and millions of writes each second”.

Why this method: when the answer depends on who is asking, precomputing it per follower doesn't work; instead, make reads extremely cheap.

Ranking at read time 🏢 Facebook · 2015 Serving Facebook Multifeed

How: “leaf” servers keep recent News Feed actions indexed in memory; “aggregators” fan each query out to the leaves, then rank, filter and return results. Splitting the two onto separate machines brought a reported 40% efficiency gain.

Why this method: aggregation is CPU-heavy and indexing is memory-heavy, so scaling them separately fits each one to the right hardware.

Redis feeds, then a ranked feed 🏢 Instagram · 2011 & 2016 See Posts You Care About First in Your Feed

How: in 2011 Instagram wrote that Redis “powers our main feed” (What Powers Instagram). In March 2016 it announced the feed would be ordered by how likely you are to care about each post, rather than purely by time.

Why this method: people were missing on average 70% of their feeds; ranking puts the posts they care about where they'll see them.

🤯 Fun fact · nobody reads the whole feed

When Instagram announced its ranked feed in 2016, it said people miss on average 70 percent of their feeds. That changes the design: if most of a feed is never seen, spending a lot to deliver every post instantly to everyone is wasted work, and spending on choosing the right 30% pays off.

🌉 How it connects

The timeline cache is Lesson 1.3's cache-aside at huge scale; fan-out runs through queues (Lesson 3.1) or Kafka (Lesson 3.2); timelines are sharded by user ID (Lesson 2.4); post IDs are time-sortable like the Snowflake IDs in Lesson 7.2. Chat (Lesson 7.4) faces the same fan-out question for group messages, and notifications (Lesson 7.8) fan out too. Ranking is where AI enters: candidate generation and learned scoring are the same retrieve-then-rank pattern used in search and recommendation systems.

Check yourself

1. 10 million posts a day, and authors have 300 followers on average. With fan-out on write, roughly how many timeline inserts per second?
10M × 300 = 3 billion inserts a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 34,700/s. That's the price of making every feed read a single lookup, and peaks (and celebrities) push it far higher.
2. A star with 80M followers posts, and for two minutes everyone's posts are delayed. What's the standard fix?
The hybrid. Only a few thousand accounts are above the threshold, so each feed read merges a handful of hot, well-cached lists, while normal accounts stay on fast push. Pure pull would make every read slow; scaling workers for a rare 80M burst is very expensive.
3. Users scrolling their feed see the same posts twice when new posts arrive. What's wrong?
With offsets, 5 new posts at the top push everything down by 5, so “items 21–40” repeats 5 you've seen. A cursor anchored to the last post ID you saw is stable no matter what arrives on top.
📝 TL;DR
  • 200M users → ~58k feed reads/s vs ~230 posts/s: make reads cheap.
  • Fan-out on write = precompute timelines (fast reads, write storms); fan-out on read = merge at read time (cheap writes, slow tail-heavy reads).
  • Hybrid: push for normal accounts, pull for celebrities above a threshold; merge a handful of hot lists per read.
  • Timelines store capped lists of post IDs in Redis; hydrate with a multi-get; paginate with cursors; rank after collecting candidates.
