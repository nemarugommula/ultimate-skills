Phase 7 · Lesson 6

Design ride sharing (Uber): geo at scale

Track a million moving cars, find the closest free one to a rider in milliseconds, and make sure no car is ever sent to two riders. It's the classic “geospatial plus real-time plus consistency” design.

🎯 Hook

Uber divides the world into hexagons. Its 2018 post on H3, its open-source hexagonal grid, explains that it calculates surge pricing by measuring supply and demand in hexagons, and uses the grid to optimise pricing and dispatch.

And the apps used to ask the server “anything new?” over and over. Uber wrote that at one point 80% of requests to its backend API gateway were polling calls, which led it to build a push platform. By the end of this lesson you'll know why both choices (hexagons and push) fall straight out of the numbers.

Step 1 · Clarify the requirements

Questions a senior candidate asks before drawing anything:

  • Which flows? Rider requests a ride, we match a nearby driver, both see each other move, the trip ends and is paid for. (Payments are Lesson 7.7; we'll call that service.)
  • How fresh must locations be? A few seconds old is fine. A car moves about 50 m in 4 s at city speed.
  • What does “nearest” mean? Closest by driving time (the ETA, estimated time of arrival), not straight-line distance. A driver across a river is not close.
  • Scale? Global, but every match is local: a rider in Paris never needs a driver in Lima.
  • Surge, pooling, scheduled rides? Mention surge; leave pooling and scheduling out of scope.

✏️ Worked example: where the load really is
  • Location writes: 1,000,000 ÷ 4 s = 250,000 writes per second. Bandwidth: 250,000 × 100 B = 25 MB/s. Easy to receive, hard to index if every write touches a disk-backed B-tree (Lesson 2.2).
  • Ride requests: 20,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 231 per second on average; assume 3× at the evening peak ≈ 700 per second. Each needs one “drivers near here” query.
  • Ratio: about 350 location writes for every match query. This is a write-heavy system, and the writes are ephemeral: only the latest position matters.
  • History (for trip receipts, fraud, ML): if every online driver's updates were kept all day, 1,000,000 × (86,400 ÷ 4) × 100 B = 2.16 TB per day, an upper bound. That goes to a cheap append-only store, not the matching path.

Conclusion: keep current locations in memory, sharded by geography; stream the history to cheap storage asynchronously.

💡 Big idea Turn a 2-D question (“who's near this point?”) into a 1-D lookup. Cut the map into cells with ids, keep a list of drivers per cell, and search the rider's cell and its neighbours, ring by ring. Every geospatial index (geohash, quadtree, S2, H3) is a different way of cutting the map.
🧠 Analogy: finding a friend at a festival

You don't ask all 80,000 people “are you Sam?”. The festival map has numbered zones. You check your own zone first, then the zones touching it, then the next ring out. And you'd rather the zones were hexagons than squares: from a hexagon, all six neighbours are the same distance away, so “one ring out” means the same thing in every direction.

Step 5 · Deep dives

Deep dive 1: the geospatial index

Why not just SQL? SELECT … WHERE lat BETWEEN a AND b AND lng BETWEEN c AND d can use an index on one column, but then filters a whole strip of the planet on the other. And rewriting 250,000 rows a second in a disk-backed index is the wrong tool. The four common ways to cut the map:

IndexHow it cuts the mapGood atWatch out for
GeohashRepeatedly halve lat/lng; interleave the bits into a string. Nearby points share a prefix (u09tv…)Simple; works in any key-value store; Redis GEO uses itTwo points across a cell edge can have very different prefixes: always check neighbours
QuadtreeSplit a square into 4 whenever it holds too many pointsAdapts to density: small cells downtown, big ones in the countrysideA tree in memory; rebalancing as cars move
S2 (Google)Project the globe onto a cube, then a quadtree of squares on each face, ordered along a space-filling curvePrecise coverings of any shape; tested on Google's geographic dataSquares: diagonal neighbours are farther than edge neighbours
H3 (Uber)Hexagons on an icosahedron, 16 resolutions, each about 7× finer than the lastAll 6 neighbours equidistant; great for “rings” and for aggregating supply/demandA few cells aren't hexagons: 12 pentagons per resolution (placed in the oceans)

Sizes to know (H3's published averages): resolution 7 cells are about 5.2 km², resolution 8 about 0.74 km², resolution 9 about 0.11 km² (a few hundred metres across). Matching typically searches at a fine resolution; surge and analytics aggregate at coarser ones.

Deep dive 2: matching with an expanding radius

The algorithm: look up the rider's cell, collect available drivers in it; if too few, add the ring of neighbours (k = 1), then k = 2, and so on up to a maximum radius. With hexagons, k rings cover 1 + 3k(k + 1) cells: 1, 7, 19, 37, 61. Then rank the candidates by ETA from a routing service (road graph + live traffic), not by straight-line distance, and offer the trip to the best one.

Subtle bug: the first ring that contains a driver doesn't necessarily contain the nearest driver. A driver just across the edge of the next ring can be closer than one in a far corner of this ring. Fix: once you've found enough candidates, search one more ring, then sort by real distance (or ETA). Try to catch it:

Deep dive 3: the trip state machine and “one driver, one rider”

Every trip moves through explicit states, and every transition is a conditional write (“move to X only if currently Y”):

REQUESTED→MATCHING→DRIVER_OFFERED→DRIVER_ASSIGNED→DRIVER_ARRIVED→IN_PROGRESS→COMPLETED CANCELLED (from any state before IN_PROGRESS)

The dangerous moment is the offer. Two matchers, serving two nearby riders, both see driver d_77 as available. If both just write “d_77 → OFFERED”, the driver gets two trips. The fix is a compare-and-set: an atomic write that succeeds only if the current value is what you expected.

-- SQL: only one of two concurrent matchers gets rowcount = 1
UPDATE drivers SET status = 'OFFERED', trip_id = 't_123', offer_expires = now() + interval '15 seconds'
 WHERE driver_id = 'd_77' AND status = 'AVAILABLE';

# Redis: a lease that disappears by itself if the driver never answers
SET driver:d_77:lock t_123 NX EX 15

The offer has a timeout: the EX 15 is a TTL (time to live), so if the driver doesn't accept in 15 s, the lease expires, the driver returns to AVAILABLE, and matching moves to the next candidate. The same pattern (state machine + conditional writes + timeouts) shows up again in payments (Lesson 7.7).

Live updates: push, not poll

During a trip both phones want the other party's position every few seconds. If a million riders polled every 4 s, that's another 250,000 requests a second that mostly answer “nothing new”. Instead, keep one persistent connection per phone (WebSocket, gRPC stream, or MQTT, a lightweight messaging protocol built for phones and devices) and push updates to it, with mobile push notifications (Lesson 7.8) as a fallback when the app is in the background.

Surge (mention, don't design unless asked): count open requests and available drivers per coarse hexagon every minute or so; where demand far exceeds supply, raise the price multiplier for that area. It's a stream-processing job (Lesson 3.6) over the same location and request events.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Launch in one city with Postgres + PostGIS or Redis GEO, a hosted maps API for ETA, polling every few seconds, and a conditional UPDATE for dispatch. That handles thousands of drivers. Spend your effort on the trip state machine and payments: those bugs cost money and trust.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Hexagon-indexed in-memory location services sharded across a ring of nodes, Kafka everywhere, a push platform holding millions of connections, in-house routing and ETA models, batched marketplace matching, surge computed per hexagon, and cell-based regional isolation so one city's outage doesn't spread.

🎤 Interview angle
  • Do the estimate and say the punchline: location writes dominate (hundreds of thousands per second) and are ephemeral, so they belong in memory.
  • Name a geo index and explain how a “nearby” search works, including neighbour cells and expanding rings.
  • Rank by ETA, not distance.
  • Show the trip state machine and how a conditional write + offer timeout prevents double dispatch.
  • Push instead of polling for live updates; shard by geography and handle hot regions.

Common mistakes: storing every location in SQL and querying with BETWEEN; ignoring cell edges; no story for two matchers grabbing the same driver. Likely follow-ups: “A stadium empties: 50,000 requests in one hexagon. What happens?”, “A location shard dies: how fast do you recover?” (drivers resend every 4 s: rebuild from the stream), “How would you add pooled rides?”, “How do you compute surge?”

🏭 In the real world
Hexagonal grid for pricing and dispatch 🏢 Uber · 2018 H3: Uber's Hexagonal Hierarchical Spatial Index

How: the world is covered by hexagons at 16 resolutions; Uber measures supply and demand per hexagon to calculate surge pricing and to optimise dispatch.

Why this method: hexagons have equidistant neighbours and minimise the error when people move through a city, so rings and gradients behave the same in every direction.

Push instead of polling 🏢 Uber · 2020 Uber's Real-Time Push Platform

How: at one point 80% of requests to Uber's backend API gateway were polling calls. Its push platform (RAMEN) later ran with more than 1.5 million concurrent connections, pushing over 250,000 messages per second.

Why this method: polling drained batteries, slowed the app and loaded the backend with “nothing new” answers; one long-lived connection carries only real changes.

A consistent hash ring for app servers 🧰 Open source · Uber Ringpop

How: a library that keeps a consistent hash ring on top of a membership protocol and forwards each request to the node that owns its key.

Why this method: it lets a stateful in-memory service (like “who owns this area's drivers”) be spread over many nodes that agree on ownership as nodes come and go (Lesson 2.4).

Spherical geometry on a cube 🧰 Open source · Google S2 Geometry

How: a library for cells, coverings and spatial indexing on the sphere, tested extensively on Google's geographic data.

Why this method: working on the sphere avoids the distortions of flat lat/lng math, and cell ids turn regions into ranges of integers any database can index.

Geo search in a cache 🏭 In production · Redis Redis GEOSEARCH

How: members are stored in a sorted set whose scores are geohashes (latitude and longitude bits interleaved into one 52-bit integer); GEOSEARCH … BYRADIUS … ASC COUNT returns the closest ones, in O(N + log M) time.

Why this method: one command gives a startup a working “drivers near me” in memory, long before it needs its own index.

🤯 Fun fact · you can't tile a ball with hexagons

It's mathematically impossible to cover a sphere with hexagons alone. So every H3 resolution contains exactly 12 pentagons, one at each corner of the underlying icosahedron (a 20-sided die). H3 places those corners over the oceans, so they almost never matter for a ride in a city. A football makes the same compromise: 12 black pentagons among the hexagons.

Check yourself

1. 2,000,000 drivers are online and each sends a location every 5 seconds. How many location writes per second must the system absorb?
2,000,000 ÷ 5 s = 400,000 writes/s. That's why current locations live in memory, sharded by geography, and only the history goes to disk, asynchronously.
2. Your matcher returns the first driver found while expanding hexagon rings. Riders sometimes get a car that's farther than another available one. Why, and what's the fix?
Rings are a coarse filter. Cells are hundreds of metres wide, so the first ring with a driver bounds the answer but doesn't settle it. One extra ring plus a real ranking (ideally by ETA) fixes it cheaply. Squares would make the problem worse, not better.
3. Two matchers, serving two riders, both read driver d_77 as AVAILABLE and both send an offer. What's the right design?
Reading twice still races. A compare-and-set makes the check and the write one atomic step; a TTL on the offer frees the driver if they never answer. This is the same idea as optimistic concurrency in Lesson 2.5.
📝 TL;DR
  • 1M drivers × one update per 4 s = 250k writes/s of ephemeral data: keep current locations in memory, history in cheap append-only storage.
  • Cut the map into cells (geohash, quadtree, S2, H3) and search the rider's cell and neighbouring rings; search one extra ring, then rank by ETA.
  • Trips are an explicit state machine; a conditional write + offer timeout guarantees one driver per rider.
  • Push over persistent connections instead of polling; shard by geography and plan for hot regions.
