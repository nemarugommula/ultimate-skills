Phase 7 · Lesson 8

Design a notification system

One service that every team calls to reach users by push, email or SMS: reliably, in the right order of urgency, and without annoying people into turning notifications off. At scale, the hard part is as much “don't send” as “send”.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Queues and Kafka: producers drop work, workers pick it up at their own pace (Lessons 3.1 and 3.2).
  • Retries, backoff and idempotency: retry safely without doing the work twice (Lesson 3.3); circuit breakers stop calling a dead dependency (Lesson 4.4).
  • Rate limiting with token buckets (Lesson 1.5), and fan-out from one event to many recipients (Lesson 7.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2018 LinkedIn described why it built Air Traffic Controller (ATC), a single platform every LinkedIn notification goes through. Repetitive, excessive and low-quality notifications, the post says, made members file complaints, disable notifications, and use the app less.

ATC was designed around what LinkedIn calls the “5 Rights”: the right message, to the right member, through the right channel, at the right time, with the right frequency. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to build a pipeline that does all five, and survives a provider outage while doing it.

Step 1 · Clarify the requirements

Questions a senior candidate asks:

  • Which channels? Mobile push (Apple's APNs, Apple Push Notification service, and Google's FCM, Firebase Cloud Messaging), email, SMS, and an in-app inbox.
  • What kinds of messages? Very different urgencies: a login code (seconds matter), a chat message (seconds), “someone liked your post” (minutes are fine), a weekly newsletter (hours are fine).
  • Who triggers them? Other services, through an API or events. Also bulk campaigns to millions.
  • User control? Per-category, per-channel opt-outs, quiet hours in the user's time zone, unsubscribe links (legally required for marketing email in many countries).
  • Guarantees? At least once for important messages; never the same notification twice in a row on screen.

Step 2 · Numbers first

✏️ Worked example: average, burst, and storage
  • Average: 200 million users × 5 notifications a day = 1 billion a day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 11,600 per second.
  • Burst: breaking news to 50 million users, all within 5 minutes: 50,000,000 ÷ 300 s ≈ 167,000 per second, about 14× the average. Bursts, not averages, size this system.
  • Device tokens (the address APNs/FCM gives each app install): 200 M users × 1.5 devices × about 200 bytes ≈ 60 GB. Fits in a sharded key-value store.
  • Notification log (for dedupe, inbox and analytics): 1 billion × 500 bytes = 500 GB a day; 90 days of retention ≈ 45 TB.
  • Payload limit: an APNs notification body can be at most 4 KB. You send a short message and an id, never the whole document.
💡 Big idea Treat every notification as a request that must pass gates before it may be sent: dedupe, preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps, aggregation. Then route what survives into separate queues by priority, and send with retries and failover. Most of the value is in the gates.
🧠 Analogy: a good personal assistant

A bad assistant interrupts you for every email. A good one knows that a call from your child's school goes straight through, that ten “someone liked your post” messages become one note at lunch, that you're not to be disturbed at 3 a.m., and that if your phone is off, a text is better than nothing. The notification service is that assistant, for 200 million people at once.

Step 4 · High-level design

Producers call the notification service. It runs the gates, renders the template, and puts the message on a priority queue. Channel workers send through providers (APNs, FCM, an email service, an SMS gateway) and record the result.

Step 5 · Deep dives

Deep dive 1: priorities and fan-out

One queue for everything means a 60,000-message campaign sits in front of a login code. Fix: separate queues (or Kafka topics) per priority, with workers that always drain the higher ones first, or with dedicated worker pools per priority so the low one can never starve the high one. LinkedIn's ATC post mentions exactly this: high-priority Kafka topics for member messages.

Fan-out for a campaign or breaking news: don't build 50 million messages in one request. A campaign job pages through the audience (say 1,000 users per page), enqueues one batch job per page, and batch workers expand each into individual sends. Spread the send over a window (and over time zones) so providers and your own servers see a ramp, not a wall. Providers rate-limit you too: plan for per-provider token buckets (Lesson 1.5).

Deep dive 2: reliability: retries, dedupe, failover

  • At-least-once + dedupe: a worker that crashes after sending but before acknowledging the queue will send again. So every notification carries a dedupe key (event id + user id); before sending, SET dedupe:{key} 1 NX EX 86400 in Redis. If it already exists, skip.
  • Retry with exponential backoff + jitter for timeouts and 5xx answers; don't retry 4xx errors like a bad token. After N tries, move the message to a DLQ (dead-letter queue) for inspection.
  • Failover: for channels where it matters (SMS codes, transactional email), integrate two providers. A circuit breaker (Lesson 4.4) on provider A opens after a burst of failures, and traffic switches to B.
  • Token hygiene: APNs returns 410 when a device token is no longer active. Delete it, or you'll keep paying to send into the void.
  • Push isn't storage: if a phone is offline, APNs keeps only one notification per app (usually the latest). The in-app inbox is the durable record; push is just the doorbell.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Providers: APNs, FCM, Amazon SNS / SES, SendGrid, Twilio; all-in-one notification platforms like OneSignal, Knock or the open-source Novu; queues: Kafka, SQS, RabbitMQ; Redis for dedupe and caps. Sending one push through APNs over HTTP/2, with a collapse id:

curl --http2 https://api.push.apple.com/3/device/$DEVICE_TOKEN \
  -H "authorization: bearer $APNS_JWT" \
  -H "apns-topic: com.example.app" -H "apns-push-type: alert" \
  -H "apns-priority: 10" \                          # 10 = send now; 5 = may be batched to save battery
  -H "apns-collapse-id: post:p_7" \                 # newer pushes for this post replace older ones
  -d '{"aps":{"alert":{"title":"New comments","body":"Asha and 41 others commented"}}}'

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Use FCM + an email API + Twilio from a job queue, or a hosted platform. Do three things from day one: a separate high-priority queue for login codes and receipts, an idempotency/dedupe key on every send, and a preferences table with an unsubscribe link. Those are painful to add later.

Big tech (1 → 100)

A central notification platform every team must use, so one place enforces caps and quiet hours across all products. Stream processing per user (LinkedIn's ATC runs on Samza with RocksDB state), machine-learning-ranked relevance and send-time optimisation, multiple providers per channel, and opt-out rate as a company-level health metric.

🎤 Interview angle
  • Size for bursts, not averages, and say how campaign fan-out is spread out.
  • Priority separation so critical messages never wait behind bulk ones.
  • At-least-once + dedupe keys, retries with backoff, a DLQ, provider failover, token cleanup.
  • The user-protection gates: preferences, quiet hours in local time, frequency caps, aggregation.
  • An inbox as the durable copy; push as best-effort.

Common mistakes: one queue for everything; calling providers synchronously from the producer's request; no dedupe; ignoring time zones; “exactly-once push” (impossible across APNs/FCM). Likely follow-ups: “How do you send to 50 million users in 5 minutes?”, “The SMS provider is down; what happens to login codes?”, “How do you stop a buggy producer from spamming every user?” (per-producer quotas, a global kill switch), “How would you measure whether a notification was worth sending?”

🏭 In the real world
One platform for every notification 🏢 LinkedIn · 2018 Air Traffic Controller: Member-First Notifications at LinkedIn

How: ATC, built on Samza with RocksDB state, routes all notifications for a member to the same task, then applies channel selection, aggregation (e.g. grouping invitation reminders into one email), delivery-time optimisation (not while the member is sleeping) and high-priority Kafka topics for messages.

Why this method: excessive, repetitive notifications drove complaints and opt-outs; one platform can see everything a member is about to receive and decide as a whole.

Near-real-time fan-out 🏢 LinkedIn · 2018 Concourse: Generating Personalized Content Notifications in Near-Real-Time

How: replaced batch jobs that ran a few times a day with a streaming pipeline: content events flow through Kafka, partitioned by the member who created the content, and are fanned out to candidate recipients.

Why this method: the batch version could notify people hours late, when the content was no longer relevant.

The push gateway's rules 🏭 In production · Apple Sending notification requests to APNs

How: an HTTP/2 POST per notification, payloads up to 4 KB, apns-priority 10 (immediate) vs 5 or 1 (may be grouped and delivered in bursts), apns-collapse-id, and an expiration after which APNs stops retrying.

Why this method: the OS, not your server, decides how to spend the phone's battery; priorities and collapse ids are how you cooperate with it.

Normal vs high priority on Android 🏭 In production · Google FCM: setting message priority

How: FCM attempts to deliver high-priority messages immediately, even when the device is in Doze mode (Android's deep-sleep state), and are meant for time-sensitive, user-visible content; normal ones may be delayed while the app is in the background.

Why this method: if every app sent everything as urgent, phones would never sleep; your priority queue should map onto the platform's.

A WebSocket proxy for live delivery 🏢 Netflix · 2024 Pushy to the Limit

How: Pushy holds hundreds of millions of concurrent WebSocket connections and delivers hundreds of thousands of messages per second to Netflix apps.

Why this method: for apps that are open, a persistent connection you control is faster and richer than going through APNs/FCM.

🤯 Fun fact · APNs keeps only one

If a phone is offline and you send it ten notifications for the same app, Apple's documentation says APNs stores only one (in most cases the latest) and delivers it when the device comes back. The other nine are gone. That's why serious apps keep an in-app inbox or sync on open: push is a doorbell, not a mailbox.

🌉 How it connects

This design is queues and Kafka (Lessons 3.1, 3.2), idempotent retries (Lesson 3.3), circuit breakers (Lesson 4.4) and token buckets (Lesson 1.5) assembled into one service. In AI products, the same gates decide when an agent should interrupt a human, and when to batch its updates into a summary.

Check yourself

1. A product team wants to notify 20 million users within 10 minutes. What send rate must the pipeline (and its providers) sustain?
20,000,000 ÷ 600 s ≈ 33,300 per second, roughly 3× this lesson's daily average. Check the providers' rate limits too, and spread the send over time zones if “within 10 minutes” isn't really required.
2. Every time marketing launches a campaign, users complain their login codes arrive minutes late. What's the best fix?
More workers shrink the delay but a big enough campaign always wins. Priority separation makes the guarantee structural: bulk work only uses capacity that critical work doesn't need. Night-only campaigns break quiet hours for half the planet.
3. A worker crashes after calling APNs but before acknowledging the queue message. The message is redelivered. How do you avoid a duplicate push?
Queues are at-least-once; “exactly once” end to end is achieved by making the effect idempotent (Lesson 3.3). The dedupe key turns the second attempt into a no-op. Never retrying would lose important messages instead.
📝 TL;DR
  • 1 billion notifications a day is ~11.6k/s, but a breaking-news burst can be 167k/s: size for bursts and spread fan-out.
  • Every send passes gates: dedupe, preferences, quiet hours, caps, aggregation. Fewer, better notifications keep users opted in.
  • Separate queues by priority; retry with backoff, dead-letter what keeps failing, fail over between providers.
  • Push is best-effort (APNs keeps one per app when offline); the inbox is the durable copy.
