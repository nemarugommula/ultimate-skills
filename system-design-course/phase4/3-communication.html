Phase 4 · Lesson 3

Service communication, discovery & service mesh

How services find each other when machines come and go, when to call and wait versus send an event and move on, and how a mesh of proxies gives every service encryption, retries and metrics for free.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • REST and gRPC: two ways to make a request–response call between programs (Lesson 0.4).
  • Queues and events: a producer drops a message and moves on; consumers process it later (Lessons 3.1 and 3.2).
  • Availability multiplies along a chain of required services (Lessons 0.5 and 4.1).
  • DNS turns a name like api.example.com into an IP address (Lesson 0.2).
🎯 Hook

Back when Lyft had about 30 services, its engineers faced such constant, sporadic network and service-call failures that, as Matt Klein later wrote, most developers were afraid to put high-volume service calls in critical paths. Nobody could tell whether a problem was in the code, the cloud network or the load balancer.

So Lyft built Envoy: a proxy that runs next to every service and handles all its network traffic. By the time they open-sourced it in September 2016, it ran on thousands of machines and over 100 services, handling over 2 million requests per second. Envoy became the heart of the “service mesh” idea you'll learn here.

Numbers first: wait for it, or send it and move on?

A checkout request needs: payment, stock reservation, a confirmation email, and an analytics event. Each service is up 99.9% of the time. Typical times: payment 200 ms, stock 20 ms, email 300 ms, analytics 15 ms.

Async removes services from the user's critical path. Its price: the email arrives a little later, and you need idempotent consumers because messages can be delivered twice (Lesson 3.3).

💡 Big idea Call synchronously only when the caller needs the answer to respond (“is this card OK?”). Everything that can happen after you respond (emails, search indexing, analytics, telling other teams) should be an event. Every sync call you remove takes a service off the critical path.

🧠 Analogy: phone call vs text message

A sync call is a phone call: you get the answer right away, but only if the other person picks up, and you're stuck on the line until they finish. An event is a text: you send it and get on with your day; they reply when they can. You call your bank when a payment is declined. You text a friend that you've arrived.

Service discovery is the phone book (people change numbers all the time). A service mesh is giving everyone a personal assistant who places calls for them, redials if the line drops, scrambles the conversation so nobody can eavesdrop, and keeps a log of every call.

Service discovery: who is at which address right now?

With autoscaling and deploys, instances of a service appear and vanish many times a day, each with a new IP address. Hard-coding addresses fails within hours. Service discovery keeps a live list of healthy instances. Two shapes:

Client-side discoveryServer-side discovery
HowThe caller asks a registry for the list of instances and picks one itself (with its own load balancing)The caller sends to one stable name or virtual IP; a load balancer or proxy picks the instance
ExamplesNetflix Eureka with client libraries; Consul's APIKubernetes Services (orders.shop.svc.cluster.local), AWS load balancers, Consul DNS (orders.service.consul)
ProsSmart, per-request choices; no extra hopCallers stay simple and language-agnostic
ConsA client library per language, kept up to dateOne more component in the path; DNS caching can point at dead instances

Either way, instances register (themselves, or the platform does it, as Kubernetes does) and send health checks, so dead instances drop out of the list within seconds (Lesson 1.2).

The service mesh: a proxy next to every service

Every service needs the same plumbing: encryption, timeouts, retries, load balancing, metrics, tracing. With 5 languages, that's 5 libraries to build and keep in sync. A service mesh moves the plumbing out of the app into a proxy that runs next to each instance, called a sidecar. The app talks plain HTTP to localhost; the sidecar does the rest.

  • Data plane: the sidecar proxies (usually Envoy; Linkerd uses its own small proxies written in Rust). All traffic between services passes through them.
  • Control plane: pushes configuration to all proxies (Istio's is called istiod): routes, retry rules, certificates.
  • What you get: mTLS (mutual TLS: both sides prove who they are with certificates, and traffic is encrypted; Lesson 4.6), retries and timeouts, traffic splitting for canaries (Lesson 6.2), and the same metrics for every service.
  • What you pay: two extra proxy hops per call, plus CPU and memory for every sidecar. Istio's own benchmark for version 1.24: one sidecar handling 1,000 requests per second (1 KB each) used about 0.20 vCPU (a fifth of one virtual CPU core) and 60 MB of memory.

Things to try: with 10% random failures and no retries, a 3-deep request succeeds only 0.93 ≈ 73% of the time. One retry lifts each hop to 99%, so the request succeeds about 97% of the time, for about 10% more load. That's the mesh's best trick. But retries only help with random failures. If B is failing because it's overloaded, every retry adds load to a service that's already drowning (Lesson 4.4). Note that Istio already retries 2 times by default on connection failures, so retries in your code stack on top of the mesh's.

✏️ Worked example: what does the mesh cost?

A company runs 400 pods (instances), each with a sidecar. Total internal traffic is 200,000 requests per second, and every request passes through 2 sidecars (caller's and callee's).

  • Sidecar work: 200,000 × 2 = 400,000 proxied requests/s.
  • Using Istio's figure of about 0.20 vCPU per 1,000 requests/s (and assuming it scales roughly linearly, which you'd confirm with a load test): 400,000 ÷ 1,000 × 0.20 = 80 vCPU.
  • Memory: 400 sidecars × about 60 MB ≈ 24 GB (more if each proxy holds a lot of routing config).

That's a few big machines' worth, in exchange for encryption, retries and metrics everywhere without touching app code. Worth it for 400 pods; probably not for 4.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Calls: gRPC with protobuf, or REST with an OpenAPI spec. Discovery: Kubernetes Services and DNS (the default if you're on Kubernetes), HashiCorp Consul, Netflix Eureka. Meshes: Istio (Envoy sidecars, or the newer sidecar-less “ambient” mode) and Linkerd. A Kubernetes Service plus an Istio retry rule:

# A stable name for all "orders" pods: orders.shop.svc.cluster.local
apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata: { name: orders, namespace: shop }
spec:
  selector: { app: orders }       # any pod with this label is a live instance
  ports: [{ port: 80, targetPort: 8080 }]
---
# Istio: timeouts and retries for calls to orders, with no app code changes
apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1
kind: VirtualService
metadata: { name: orders, namespace: shop }
spec:
  hosts: [orders.shop.svc.cluster.local]
  http:
  - route: [{ destination: { host: orders.shop.svc.cluster.local } }]
    timeout: 3s                   # give up on the whole call after 3 s
    retries:
      attempts: 2                 # at most 1 + 2 = 3 tries
      perTryTimeout: 1s
      retryOn: connect-failure,refused-stream,unavailable

Try it cheaply: kind create cluster (Kubernetes in Docker), deploy two pods behind a Service, then kubectl run -it --rm dbg --image=busybox -- nslookup orders.shop.svc.cluster.local to see discovery in action. Skip the mesh until you have many services.

Choice✅ When it fits❌ When it doesn't

Async eventsSide effects, fan-out to many consumers, smoothing spikesThe user needs the result now; you can't tolerate any delay
Client-side discoveryOne or two languages, need smart routingMany languages (a library each)

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

One monolith plus a queue for background jobs. If you have a few services, use your platform's built-in discovery (Kubernetes Services, or your cloud's load balancer) and HTTP/JSON. Put timeouts on every call. No mesh.

Big tech (1 → 100)

gRPC internally with schemas in a shared registry, events on Kafka between domains, and a mesh (or a shared RPC framework doing the same job) giving every call mTLS, retries, deadlines and uniform metrics. A platform team owns the mesh like a product.

🎤 Interview angle

When you draw arrows between services, say what each arrow is:

  • Sync or async, and why: “payment is sync because we need the decline before responding; email is an event.”
  • How it's found: “services discover each other through Kubernetes DNS; instances drop out on failed health checks.”
  • What protects it: timeouts, bounded retries, and where they live (app code or mesh, not both stacked blindly).
  • The availability math of your critical path, and which services you moved off it.

Common follow-ups: “What happens if the queue is down?” · “Why gRPC and not REST here?” · “What does a sidecar cost you?”

🏭 In the real world
A proxy next to every service 🏢 Lyft · 2016 Announcing Envoy: C++ L7 proxy and communication bus

How: Envoy runs alongside every application server; apps talk to their local Envoy, and the Envoys form a mesh that handles discovery, load balancing, retries, circuit breaking and statistics.

Why this method: Lyft ran services in PHP and Python with a mishmash of libraries. Doing networking in one out-of-process proxy gave every language the same behavior and the same metrics.

Data plane and control plane 🧰 Open source · Istio Istio architecture

How: Envoy sidecars (the data plane) carry all traffic between microservices and report telemetry; istiod (the control plane) configures them and handles certificates. Istio publishes its overhead: about 0.20 vCPU and 60 MB per sidecar at 1,000 requests/s in version 1.24 (performance page).

Why this method: separating config from traffic lets one team change retry or security policy for thousands of proxies at once.

Client-side discovery 🏢 Netflix · open source Netflix Eureka

How: Eureka is a REST service registry used in AWS for discovery, load balancing and failover of middle-tier servers; instances register and clients fetch the list.

Why this method: in the cloud, instances come and go constantly, so callers need a live list of healthy instances instead of fixed addresses, plus failover when some disappear.

Server-side discovery built in 🧰 Open source · Kubernetes Kubernetes Service

How: a Service gives a set of pods one stable name and address; Kubernetes DNS resolves names like my-svc.my-namespace.svc.cluster.local (DNS docs).

Why this method: the docs state the aim directly: you shouldn't need to modify your app to use an unfamiliar discovery mechanism.

🤯 Fun fact · from one company's proxy to an industry standard

Envoy was open-sourced by Lyft in September 2016, accepted into the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF) in September 2017, and reached “graduated” status in November 2018. Today it is the proxy inside Istio's sidecars, among many other uses.

🌉 How it connects

Retries without limits cause the outages in Lesson 4.4; the mesh is often where timeouts and circuit breakers are configured. mTLS identities are the base of service-to-service auth (Lesson 4.6). Queues and Kafka from Phase 3 carry the async half. Kubernetes Services come back in Lesson 5.2, and the mesh's uniform metrics feed the dashboards in Lesson 6.3. AI systems use the same patterns: a gateway calls model servers over gRPC, and slow jobs (batch embedding, fine-tuning) go through queues.

Check yourself

1. A request synchronously calls 6 services in a row, each 99.9% available. Roughly how many minutes per month does it fail? (A month ≈ 43,200 minutes.)
0.9996 ≈ 0.994, so it fails about 0.6% of the time: 0.006 × 43,200 ≈ 259 minutes. Six times one service's 43 minutes. Moving services off the sync path is the fix.
2. After an order is placed you must update search, send an email and award loyalty points. How should the order service do it?
None of the three is needed to answer the user. One event decouples them: a slow email service can't slow checkout, and new consumers can subscribe without changing the order service. Polling another service's database creates a shared-database coupling (Lesson 4.2).
3. A startup with 4 services in one language asks whether to adopt a service mesh. What's the best answer?
A mesh pays off when the same plumbing would otherwise be rebuilt across many services and languages, or when you need mTLS everywhere. For 4 services in one language, it's a lot of new machinery to run and debug. “Never” is also wrong: at scale it's often the cheapest way to get uniform security and telemetry.
📝 TL;DR
  • Sync only when you need the answer now; everything else is an event. Each removed sync hop raises availability (aN) and cuts latency.
  • Discovery keeps a live list of healthy instances: client-side (Eureka) or server-side (Kubernetes Services, load balancers, DNS).
  • A service mesh puts a sidecar proxy next to every instance for mTLS, retries, timeouts and metrics, at the cost of extra hops, CPU and complexity.
  • Retries help random failures and hurt overload; know where they're configured so they don't stack.
