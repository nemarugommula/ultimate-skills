Phase 4 · Lesson 2

Service boundaries & domain-driven design

Where you draw the lines between services decides whether you get independent teams or a “distributed monolith” that has all the costs of microservices and none of the benefits.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Microservices = independently deployable services, each owning its data; every call between them crosses the network (Lesson 4.1).
  • Transactions = several writes that succeed or fail together; they only work inside one database (Lesson 2.5).
  • Sagas = a chain of local transactions with “undo” steps, used when one business action spans services (Lesson 3.5).
🎯 Hook

By 2020 Uber had grown to around 2,200 critical microservices. Their engineering blog describes an investigation where engineers had to work through around 50 services across 12 teams just to find the root cause of one problem.

Their fix wasn't fewer services. It was better boundaries: they grouped the 2,200 services into 70 domains, each with one entry point (a “gateway”). Teams that used to call many services now call one, and Uber reports that onboarding a new feature onto a platform got 25–50% faster. Drawing the lines well is the whole game.

Numbers first: the price of a bad line

Take an online shop doing 1,000 product-page views per second. The page needs the product, its price, its reviews and whether it's in stock.

DesignServices the page touchesNetwork calls per secondTypical added latency
Product, price and reviews together; stock elsewhere21 × 1,000 = 1,000~1 call (a few ms)
One service per table (“entity services”)43 × 1,000 = 3,000~3 calls, and 3 more things that can fail

Three times the network traffic, and the page now fails if any of four services is down. The data didn't change. Only the lines did. The rule that falls out:

cross-service calls/s = Σactions rate × (services touched − 1) “For each user action, multiply how often it happens by how many extra services it must reach. Good boundaries keep that sum small for the frequent actions.”

Domain-driven design in five words

Domain-driven design (DDD, from Eric Evans' 2004 book) is a way to model software around the business. Five ideas matter for service boundaries:

TermPlain meaningExample in a shop
DomainThe business area the software servesOnline retail
Ubiquitous languageThe exact words the business uses, used in the code too“Order”, “refund”, “backorder”, not tbl_ord_2
Bounded contextA boundary inside which each word has one meaning and one model“Product” in Catalog = photos and descriptions; in Inventory = a SKU (stock-keeping unit) in a warehouse bin; in Shipping = weight and box size
AggregateA cluster of objects that must change together in one transaction; one “root” guards itAn Order and its OrderLines: you never save half an order
Context mapA diagram of how contexts relate and translate between modelsOrdering sends “OrderPlaced”; Shipping turns it into a shipment

The service rule of thumb: one bounded context ≈ one service (or a few), owned by one team. And never split an aggregate across services, or you've turned one transaction into a distributed one.

💡 Big idea Split by business capability (catalog, ordering, payments, shipping), not by technical layer or by table. Things that change together and are read together belong together. A good boundary is one most user actions don't cross.
🧠 Analogy: departments in a company

A company isn't split into “the people who use spreadsheets” and “the people who use email” (technical layers). It's split into Sales, Warehouse, Accounting. Each department has its own files and its own meaning for words: to Sales a “customer” is a lead with a phone number; to Accounting it's an account with a credit limit.

Departments talk through clear requests (“please ship order 42”), not by walking into each other's filing cabinets. That's a bounded context with a private database.

Database per service

If two services read and write the same tables, they are coupled no matter how many repos they live in. Rename a column and both break; one team's slow query locks the other team's rows. The pattern on microservices.io is blunt: keep each service's persistent data private to that service, reachable only through its API.

  • Private tables per service in a shared database server: cheapest; enforce with database permissions.
  • Schema per service: stronger separation, still one server to run.
  • Database server per service: full isolation, and each service can pick the right store (Lesson 2.1).

The price: no JOIN across services. You either call the other service, or keep a read-only copy of the data you need, updated by events (Lesson 3.2). Copies are fast but a little stale.

The distributed monolith trap

A distributed monolith is a system split into services that still can't change independently. You pay the network and ops costs of Lesson 4.1 and keep the coordination costs of a monolith. Symptoms:

SymptomWhat it tells you
Releasing service A requires releasing B and C at the same timeThe interface between them isn't stable (Lesson 4.5)
Several services read and write the same tablesThe real boundary is the database, not the services
One page load runs a synchronous chain of 6+ servicesThe lines cut through a frequent action
Every feature needs pull requests in 4 repos and 3 teamsBoundaries follow layers or tables, not business capabilities

Entity services look tidy on a slide and are a disaster: over 2,000 calls per second, and Order is split from its OrderLines. Bounded contexts cut the calls nearly in half (about 2,150/s down to about 1,190/s), keep both aggregates whole, and the remaining sagas are real business processes (checkout touches money, stock and the order). Services: Catalog (Product, Price, Review), Inventory (Stock), Ordering (Cart, Order, OrderLine), Payments (Payment, Invoice), Shipping (Shipment), Customers (Customer, Address).

Total ≈ 1,193 calls/s, and 1,000 of them come from one action. That tells you exactly where to spend effort: give Catalog an event-fed copy of “in stock / out of stock” and the biggest line disappears. Focus on the frequent actions, not the rare ones.

Getting there: the strangler fig

You almost never draw boundaries on a blank page. You have a monolith that works and earns money. A big-bang rewrite (build the new system for a year, switch over in one night) is famously risky: no value until the end, and one giant cutover.

The alternative is named after the strangler fig, a vine Martin Fowler saw in the Queensland rainforest that grows around a host tree until it can stand on its own. In software: put a routing layer in front of the monolith, rebuild one capability at a time as a service, move its traffic over gradually, and repeat. Each step is small and can be rolled back by flipping a route.

Two things make the strangler fig safe. Every step moves a small share of traffic, and every step is reversible in seconds (change the route back). Press “Bug in new Catalog!” during the 5% canary step and only 3% of all requests are affected until you roll back. In a big-bang cutover, the same bug hits everyone.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Finding boundaries: Event Storming (a workshop with sticky notes for every business event, like “OrderPlaced”, that surfaces contexts), and context maps. Routing for the strangler fig: any reverse proxy or API gateway (Nginx, Envoy, Kong, AWS API Gateway; Lesson 1.6). Moving data: change data capture with Debezium streams the monolith's row changes into Kafka so the new service can build its own copy (Lesson 3.2).

# nginx.conf: the strangler fig's routing layer
split_clients "${remote_addr}" $catalog_backend {
    5%   catalog_service;            # canary: 5% of users get the new service
    *    legacy_monolith;            # everyone else: unchanged
}
upstream catalog_service { server catalog.internal:8080; }
upstream legacy_monolith { server monolith.internal:8080; }

server {
    location /api/products/ { proxy_pass http://$catalog_backend; }   # migrated path
    location /              { proxy_pass http://legacy_monolith; }   # everything else
}

Rolling back is one line: set the canary to 0% and reload (nginx -s reload). Try it locally with docker run -p 8080:80 -v $PWD/nginx.conf:/etc/nginx/conf.d/default.conf nginx and two tiny backends.

Choice✅ Gains❌ Costs

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Use DDD lightly: name modules after business capabilities, keep each module's tables private to it, and keep the business's words in the code. Those are your future service boundaries, for free. When you do extract the first service, use a strangler-fig route, not a rewrite.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Boundaries are an org-design question: domains map to groups of teams, each domain exposes one gateway, and architecture reviews look for chatty or shared-database designs. Migrations off legacy systems run for years with routing layers, shadow traffic and change data capture.

🎤 Interview angle

When you draw boxes in a design interview, interviewers are checking:

  • Do boundaries follow business capabilities, and does each service own its data?
  • Do the frequent requests stay inside one or two services? Where they don't, do you use read copies or caching?
  • Which actions span services, and how do you keep them consistent (saga, outbox; Lesson 3.5)?
  • If there's a legacy system: how do you migrate incrementally (strangler fig, shadow traffic, canary)?

Common follow-ups: “Two services both need customer data. Who owns it?” · “Your checkout touches four services. What happens if payment succeeds and the stock reservation fails?” · “How would you know a boundary is wrong?”

🏭 In the real world
Domain-oriented microservices 🏢 Uber · 2020 Introducing Domain-Oriented Microservice Architecture

How: Uber grouped around 2,200 microservices into 70 domains, arranged domains in layers that limit who may depend on whom, and gave each domain a single gateway as its entry point. The post also estimates that half of Uber's microservices churn every 1.5 years.

Why this method: with that much churn, callers that depend on one stable gateway per domain don't have to change every time services behind it are rewritten.

The strangler fig 📜 Martin Fowler · updated 2024 Strangler Fig

How: gradually build new capabilities around the edges of a legacy system, moving behavior and traffic piece by piece until the old system can be retired. The 2024 update explains the botanical name.

Why this method: each step delivers value and teaches you something, and none of them requires betting the business on one cutover night.

Database per service 🧰 Pattern catalog microservices.io: Database per service

How: each service's data is private and reachable only through its API, implemented as private tables, a private schema or a private database server.

Why this method: a shared database is a hidden contract: schema changes and slow queries in one service break others.

Bounded contexts 📜 Martin Fowler · 2014 BoundedContext

How: a large domain is divided into contexts, each with its own model and language, with explicit relationships between them.

Why this method: one unified model for a whole big business becomes contradictory (“customer” means different things in different departments); separate models stay consistent inside their boundary.

🤯 Fun fact · it used to have a scarier name

Fowler's original post was called just “Strangler Application”. In the 2024 update he notes that people kept saying “strangler” and forgetting the botany, so it's now “Strangler Fig”: a plant that starts in a nook of a host tree and slowly grows its own roots to the ground.

🌉 How it connects

Boundaries decide how much services talk (Lesson 4.3) and how much one service's failure can hurt another (Lesson 4.4). Actions that cross boundaries need sagas and the outbox (Lesson 3.5); read copies are fed by event streams (Lesson 3.2). In the design problems of Phase 7, the first boxes you draw are bounded contexts. In AI systems, the same thinking separates the model-serving context from the product context so each can evolve on its own.

Check yourself

1. A home page is viewed 2,000 times per second and touches 4 services. A redesign makes it touch 2. How many cross-service calls per second does that save?
Before: 2,000 × (4 − 1) = 6,000 calls/s. After: 2,000 × (2 − 1) = 2,000 calls/s. Saved: 4,000 calls/s, and two fewer services that can break the home page.
2. A team wants an “Order service” and a separate “OrderLine service”. What's the main problem?
An aggregate is what must change together in one transaction. Splitting it turns every order save into a saga and every order read into two calls. Keep aggregates whole; split between contexts, not inside them.
3. You must move a busy payments flow out of a 10-year-old monolith with zero downtime. Which plan is best?
That's the strangler fig: small, reversible steps with real traffic. The big-bang cutover bets everything on one night. Writing into the monolith's tables creates a shared-database distributed monolith.
📝 TL;DR
  • Draw boundaries around business capabilities (bounded contexts), not tables or technical layers; never split an aggregate.
  • Each service owns its data. Need someone else's? Call them, or keep an event-fed read copy and accept a little staleness.
  • Score a design by Σ rate × (services touched − 1): keep the frequent actions inside one service.
  • Watch for the distributed monolith: lockstep releases, shared tables, long synchronous chains.
  • Migrate with the strangler fig: route, extract, canary, grow, repeat.
