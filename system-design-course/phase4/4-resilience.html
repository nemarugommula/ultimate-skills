Phase 4 · Lesson 4

Resilience: timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, bulkheads

In a system of many services, something is always slow or broken. These patterns stop one sick dependency from taking everything else down with it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Thread pool: a server handles each request on a worker from a fixed pool; when all are busy, new requests wait or fail.
  • Idempotency: an operation that's safe to repeat, so a retry can't charge a card twice (Lesson 3.3).
  • Sync calls put services on your critical path, and meshes can retry for you (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

On December 7, 2021, at 7:30 AM PST, an automated scaling activity inside AWS's US-EAST-1 region triggered unexpected behavior from a large number of internal clients. The resulting surge of connections overwhelmed the network devices between AWS's internal network and its main network.

The delays caused errors, and the errors caused even more connection attempts and retries, which kept the devices congested. AWS's summary describes all network devices fully recovering at 2:22 PM PST, almost seven hours later. Retries are meant to help. Without limits, they are how a small problem becomes a big one.

Numbers first: how one slow service eats your threads

Service A has 200 worker threads and gets 500 requests per second. Every request calls B (10 ms). One in five also calls C (normally 20 ms). How many threads are busy? Use Little's law: things in the system = arrival rate × time each one stays.

threads busy = requests per second × seconds each request holds a thread “If 100 requests arrive every second and each one keeps a thread for 5 seconds, 500 threads are busy at any moment.”

That's a cascading failure: a failure that spreads because each failing part pushes more load onto the next. Google's SRE (site reliability engineering) book defines it as a failure that grows over time through positive feedback.

💡 Big idea Every call to another service needs a limit: a limit on time (timeout), on attempts (retry budget), on concurrency (bulkhead), and on calling something that's already down (circuit breaker). Unlimited waiting is how one slow dependency becomes your outage.
🧠 Analogy: the ship, the fuse box and the phone queue

A bulkhead is the wall between compartments of a ship: one flooded compartment doesn't sink the ship. A circuit breaker is the one in your fuse box: when a device shorts, it cuts the power right away instead of letting the wires overheat, and you try flipping it back later. A timeout is hanging up after 2 minutes on hold. A retry with backoff and jitter is calling back later, but not all at the same second as everyone else who got cut off.

The circuit breaker's three states

  • Closed (normal): calls go through; the breaker counts failures over a sliding window of recent calls.
  • Open: the failure rate crossed the threshold. Calls fail immediately (or use a fallback) without touching the sick service. This gives it room to recover and keeps your threads free.
  • Half-open: after the cooldown, a few trial calls go through. If they succeed, back to closed; if not, open again.
🎮 Playground · A circuit breaker vs a flaky dependency

Your service sends 50 calls per second to a dependency. At t = 10 s it goes down: every call hangs until a 1-second timeout. Set how long the outage lasts and tune the breaker. Without a breaker, every call during the outage waits a full second and the sick service keeps getting hammered.

Things to try: with the breaker on, the sick service gets a small trickle of trial calls instead of the full stream, and callers wait about a millisecond instead of a second. Now make the cooldown 30 s: the breaker stays open long after the dependency recovers, so you serve fallbacks for nothing. A 1 s cooldown sends more trial calls into the outage. Tuning is a trade-off between protecting the dependency and recovering quickly.

✏️ Worked example: retries multiply

A user request goes through 5 layers of services, ending at a database. Each layer tries each call up to 3 times. The database starts failing.

  • Layer 5 tries the database 3 times per call from layer 4. Layer 4 tries layer 5 three times. And so on up.
  • Worst case: 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 35 = 243 database calls for one user request. AWS's Builders' Library uses this exact example.
  • With Google's per-client budget (retries allowed only while they're under 10% of requests), the growth is capped near 1.1× instead.

The fix is structural: retry at one layer (usually the edge or the mesh), give every client a retry budget, and back off with jitter: wait a random time between 0 and min(cap, base × 2attempt).

🎮 Playground · Cascading failure

Service A: 200 threads, 500 requests/s. All requests call B (10 ms); 20% also call C. At t = 5 s, C becomes slow. Run it with no protection and watch A die, including the requests that never needed C. Then add a timeout, a bulkhead (a separate limit on concurrent C calls), and retries.

With no protection, A's 200 threads fill with requests stuck on C within about 2 seconds, and from then on A rejects most requests, whatever they need: a slow C has become a broken A. A timeout alone saves A here. A bulkhead protects A even with no timeout, because C can never hold more than its share of threads. And “Timeout + 2 retries” shows the trap from the hook: each retry holds the thread for another full timeout, and C, already struggling, gets three times the calls.

🔥 Real outage · AWS US-EAST-1, December 7, 2021

What happened: an automated scaling activity triggered a surge of connection activity from many clients on AWS's internal network. Network devices between the internal and main networks became congested; the resulting delays and errors led to more connection attempts and retries, which kept them congested. Internal monitoring was also affected, which slowed diagnosis.

Impact: several AWS services were impaired for hours; for example, the Route 53 API was impaired from 7:30 AM to 2:30 PM PST (existing DNS answers kept working).

Lesson: clients that retry without backoff, jitter and budgets turn a capacity problem into a feedback loop. The AWS summary lists the scaling activity as disabled and further protections added.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Libraries: Resilience4j (Java; the recommended successor to Netflix Hystrix), Polly (.NET), tenacity (Python retries), cockatiel (Node). In the proxy or mesh: Envoy and Istio have timeouts, retries, outlier detection and circuit-breaking limits built in (Lesson 4.3). Testing: fault injection (Istio can inject delays and errors), and chaos experiments (Lesson 8.4).

    payments:
      maxAttempts: 3
      waitDuration: 100ms
      enableExponentialBackoff: true
      exponentialBackoffMultiplier: 2
# Python: exponential backoff with "full jitter"
import random, time
def call_with_retries(fn, attempts=3, base=0.1, cap=2.0):
    for i in range(attempts):
        try:
            return fn(timeout=0.5)                 # always a timeout
        except TransientError:
            if i == attempts - 1: raise
            time.sleep(random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** i)))  # spread retries out

Try it cheaply: run any small HTTP service, then add tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem delay 2000ms inside its container (Linux) or use Toxiproxy (docker run -p 8474:8474 ghcr.io/shopify/toxiproxy) to inject latency, and watch whether your caller's timeouts actually fire.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

The cheap 80%: a timeout on every outbound call (HTTP clients often default to none or to minutes), retries only at one layer with jittered backoff, and idempotency keys on payment calls. Add a fallback for the one feature that can degrade (recommendations, avatars).

Big tech (1 → 100)

Resilience is platform policy: deadlines propagated through every RPC, retry budgets in the client library or mesh, breakers and outlier detection in the proxy, load shedding by request priority, and regular fault-injection and chaos tests (Lesson 8.4) to prove the fallbacks work.

🎤 Interview angle

“What happens when this service is slow?” is one of the most common follow-ups. A senior answer:

  • Names a timeout with a number and where it comes from (the callee's p99.9), and a whole-request deadline.
  • Says where retries happen (one layer), how many, with backoff + jitter, and that the call is idempotent.
  • Adds a circuit breaker and a bulkhead for risky dependencies, and a fallback the product team agreed to.
  • Explains load shedding: under overload we reject low-priority work early so the rest succeeds.

Common follow-ups: “Your retry made it worse. Why?” · “How do you pick the timeout value?” · “What does the user see when the breaker is open?”

🏭 In the real world
Circuit breakers and bulkheads as a library 🏢 Netflix · open source Netflix Hystrix

How: Hystrix wrapped calls to dependencies with timeouts, isolated thread pools (bulkheads), circuit breakers and fallbacks. It is now in maintenance mode (final version 1.5.18); Netflix recommends Resilience4j for new projects.

Why this method: with hundreds of dependencies, some are always failing; isolating each one kept a single failure from consuming the whole service.

Timeouts, retries and jitter 🏢 Amazon · Builders' Library Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter

How: Amazon sets timeouts from the downstream service's latency percentile matching an acceptable false-timeout rate, retries at a single layer, and limits retries locally with a token bucket (added to the AWS SDK, Amazon's client libraries, in 2016). The jitter math is in Marc Brooker's 2015 post Exponential Backoff and Jitter.

Why this method: retries at every layer multiply (35 = 243×), and synchronized retries arrive as waves; budgets and jitter break both.

Retry budgets and load shedding 🏢 Google · SRE book Addressing Cascading Failures

How: limit retries per request, use a server-wide retry budget, shed load, and degrade gracefully. The companion chapter Handling Overload describes at most 3 attempts per request and a 10% per-client retry ratio, which keeps growth near 1.1×.

Why this method: cascading failures are feedback loops; every pattern here cuts the loop somewhere.

The circuit-breaker state machine 🧰 Open source · Resilience4j Resilience4j CircuitBreaker

How: a finite state machine with CLOSED, OPEN and HALF_OPEN states over a count-based or time-based sliding window. Defaults: 50% failure threshold, window of 100 calls, 60 s wait in open.

Why this method: a sliding window reacts to recent behavior only, so one bad minute last hour doesn't keep the breaker open.

🤯 Fun fact · the pattern came from a book about production

Martin Fowler credits Michael Nygard's book Release It! with popularizing the circuit breaker in software (CircuitBreaker, 2014). And when Netflix retired Hystrix, it said its focus had shifted to adaptive approaches, like concurrency limits that react to real-time performance instead of hand-tuned thresholds.

🌉 How it connects

Retries are only safe with idempotency (Lesson 3.3). Rate limiting (Lesson 1.5) is load shedding at the front door. Queues give backpressure a place to live (Lesson 3.1). Meshes configure these patterns centrally (Lesson 4.3). Alerting on breaker trips and timeouts is Lesson 6.4, and proving all of this works is chaos engineering (Lesson 8.4). For AI systems the same applies to model APIs: set timeouts, retry rate-limit errors with jittered backoff, and fall back to a smaller model when the big one is slow.

Check yourself

1. A service gets 200 requests/s that call a dependency, and the dependency now takes 3 s per call. With no timeout, how many threads are tied up waiting on it?
Little's law: 200 requests/s × 3 s = 600 threads. A 200-thread pool would be exhausted in a second. A 300 ms timeout would cut it to 60.
2. A recommendations service is timing out under heavy load. Every caller retries 3 times immediately. What's the best change?
The failures come from overload, so retries add load to the thing that's drowning. A breaker stops the flood and a fallback keeps the page useful; budgets and jitter limit and spread the remaining retries. Removing the timeout would freeze the callers' threads.
3. Image thumbnails and checkout both run in one service. A thumbnail library starts hanging and checkout requests begin failing. Which pattern most directly prevents this?
The hang consumes shared threads. A bulkhead caps how many threads thumbnails can hold, so checkout keeps its own. A bigger pool only delays exhaustion; retries add load.
📝 TL;DR
  • Little's law: threads busy = rate × time held. A slow dependency with no timeout exhausts your pool and takes down unrelated requests.
  • Every call gets a timeout (from the callee's p99.9) and a deadline; retries happen at one layer, with backoff, jitter and a budget, only for idempotent calls.
  • Circuit breakers (closed → open → half-open) fail fast and give sick services room; bulkheads cap what one dependency can consume.
  • Plan the degraded experience: fallbacks, load shedding, backpressure.
