Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Monolith vs microservices (and when not to split)

One deployable app, or many small services that each own their data? The choice decides how fast a growing team can ship, and how many new ways the system can fail.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Latency numbers: a function call takes nanoseconds; a round trip inside one datacenter takes about half a millisecond (Lesson 0.3).
  • Availability multiplies: if a request needs two parts that are each up 99.9% of the time, it succeeds about 99.8% of the time (Lesson 0.5).
  • p99 = the 99th percentile latency: 99% of requests are faster than this, 1% are slower (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

In March 2023, Amazon's Prime Video team published a post about the tool that checks the quality of every live stream customers watch. They had built it as distributed serverless pieces glued together by AWS Step Functions (Amazon Web Services' workflow tool). It hit a hard scaling limit at around 5% of the expected load.

So they moved all the pieces into a single process, a monolith, running on ordinary virtual servers and containers. Data now passed between components in memory instead of over the network. Their result: infrastructure cost down by over 90%, and it scaled further. Amazon, the company famous for small independent teams, chose a monolith. This lesson is about why that can be the right call.

Numbers first: what a network hop costs

Splitting an app into services turns function calls into network calls. Here is what that does, with typical numbers:

Inside one process (monolith)Between services
Time per callnanosecondsabout 0.5 ms round trip in one datacenter, plus serializing the data to JSON or protobuf and back
Can it fail halfway?No: it returns or throwsYes: timeouts, lost packets, the other side restarting mid-call
TransactionsOne database transaction covers everythingNo shared transaction; you need sagas (Lesson 3.5)
Things to run1 build, 1 deploy pipeline, 1 set of dashboardsOne of each per service, plus discovery, tracing and on-call for each

Availability multiplies too. If one page needs 10 services in a row and each is up 99.9% of the time, the page works 0.99910 ≈ 99.0% of the time. That is about 7 hours of failure a month instead of 43 minutes.

availabilitychain = a1 × a2 × … × aN “A request that needs every service in the chain only succeeds if all of them succeed, so you multiply their availabilities.” More hops, lower availability.

So why does anyone split? Because the costs of a monolith grow with the number of engineers, not the number of users. Let's define the three options first.

“Microservice” has no official size. What matters is independent deployability: a team can release its service without coordinating with anyone else.

💡 Big idea Microservices are mostly a tool for scaling teams, not for scaling traffic. A well-built monolith can serve millions of users. What it can't do well is let 300 engineers ship 50 times a day without stepping on each other.
🧠 Analogy: one kitchen vs a food court

A monolith is one restaurant kitchen. Cooks pass plates by hand, share one fridge, and one head chef runs the line. With 5 cooks it's fast. With 80 cooks, everyone queues for the same stove.

Microservices are a food court: many small kitchens, each with its own fridge and menu, opening and closing on its own. Great for 80 cooks. But a meal that needs dishes from five stalls now involves walking between counters, and if the drinks stall is closed, the combo meal fails.

A modular monolith is one big kitchen with clearly marked stations and a rule: you ask the pastry station for dessert, you don't walk in and grab their tools.

When splitting pays off

  • Team count. Past a few dozen engineers in one codebase, merges collide, the test suite slows, and one bad change blocks everyone's release.
  • Independent deploys. The checkout team wants to ship 10 times a day; the billing team ships weekly with extra review. One deploy pipeline forces one speed.
  • Different scaling profiles. Video transcoding needs big machines with many CPU cores; the profile API needs many small ones. In a monolith you scale everything to fit the hungriest part.
  • Fault isolation. A memory leak in a recommendations feature shouldn't crash checkout. Separate processes contain the damage (if you add the defences in Lesson 4.4).

Conway's law ties the first point together. In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that organizations produce designs whose structure is “a copy of the organization's communication structure.” Three teams tend to produce three components. Big tech uses this on purpose: design the teams you want, and the architecture follows. This is sometimes called the inverse Conway maneuver.

✏️ Worked example: the deploy train

A monolith with 200 engineers, each merging about 1 change a day. The deploy pipeline takes 30 minutes, so there are about 16 deploys in an 8-hour day.

  • Changes per deploy = 200 ÷ 16 = 12.5.
  • Suppose 2% of changes are bad enough to force a rollback. A deploy is clean only if all 12.5 changes are fine: 0.9812.5 ≈ 78%.
  • So about 1 deploy in 5 gets rolled back, and the other 11 or so changes in it wait for the next train, even though they were fine.

With 20 engineers the same math gives 0.981.25 ≈ 98% clean deploys. The pain grows with headcount. Play with it below.

Things to try: with 5 engineers, microservices burn about half of every week on platform work, which is why a founding team should almost never start there. Slide to 450 and the monolith's deploy train is a mess: fewer than 6 in 10 deploys stick. Now raise the ops cost per service to 15 hours (no shared platform, every team hand-rolls its own pipeline and dashboards) and watch the microservices advantage shrink. Good platform tooling is what makes microservices affordable.

The costs nobody puts on the slide

  • Network calls. Every hop adds latency and a new way to fail. You'll need timeouts, retries and circuit breakers (Lesson 4.4).
  • Data consistency. No more JOIN across services and no single transaction. “Place order and charge card” becomes a saga that can half-finish (Lesson 3.5).
  • Debugging. One user request might touch 15 services. Without distributed tracing (Lesson 6.3) you're reading 15 log files and guessing.
  • Ops. Each service needs a pipeline, alerts, dashboards, on-call, dependency upgrades and security patches.
  • The tail. When one request fans out to many services, the slowest one sets the pace. This one surprises people most. Try it.
🎮 Playground · The tail at scale

One user request calls N backend services. Each call usually takes about 8 ms, but a small share are slow (250–1,000 ms: a garbage-collection pause, a cold cache, a busy neighbour on the same machine). Fan-out calls them all in parallel and waits for the slowest. Chain calls them one after another. We simulate 4,000 requests. Then turn on hedged requests: if a call hasn't answered in 20 ms, send a backup copy to another replica and take whichever answers first.

Try N = 100 with 1% slow calls. A single service is fast for 99 calls in 100. But a request that fans out to 100 of them hits at least one slow call 63% of the time: 1 − 0.99100 ≈ 0.63. The rare tail of each service becomes the common case of the whole request. Hedging attacks it directly: a call is now slow only if both copies are slow (1% × 1% = 0.01%), at the price of about 1–2% extra calls.

P(request is slow) = 1 − (1 − p)N “The chance that at least one of N calls is slow is one minus the chance that all N are fast.”
p: share of slow calls per serviceN: services one request touches
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Modular monolith: Shopify's open-source Packwerk (Ruby) checks in CI that one package only uses the packages it declares. Java teams use ArchUnit or Spring Modulith; Python teams use import-linter. Microservices: Kubernetes to run them (Lesson 5.2), a service catalog like Backstage to track who owns what, and OpenTelemetry for tracing (Lesson 6.3).

# components/checkout/package.yml  (Packwerk)
enforce_dependencies: true      # fail CI if checkout uses a package it didn't declare
dependencies:
  - components/payments              # checkout may call payments' public API
  - components/platform
metadata:
  owner: "checkout-team"              # who to page, who reviews changes

# in CI:
$ bin/packwerk check                 # lists every reference that crosses a boundary illegally

Try it cheaply: in any codebase you own, draw the folders as boxes and the imports between them as arrows (madge --image graph.svg src/ for JavaScript, pydeps for Python). If the picture is a hairball, splitting into services will only make the hairball slower.

⚖️ Trade-offs: when to split and when to stay together
✅ Split into services when…❌ Stay a (modular) monolith when…
Many teams (roughly 5+) block each other's releasesOne or two teams; you're still finding product–market fit
Parts have very different scaling or hardware needs (graphics processors, big memory)Load is similar everywhere; one autoscaled app handles it
Boundaries are stable and well understood (Lesson 4.2)You don't yet know where the boundaries are. A wrong split is far costlier to fix than a wrong module
You have (or can buy) a platform: CI/CD (automated build, test and deploy), Kubernetes, tracing, on-callEvery service would need hand-built pipelines and dashboards

Middle paths: a modular monolith; or a monolith plus 2–3 services carved out for parts with truly different needs (video processing, search, ML inference).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Start with one well-structured monolith, one database, one deploy. Draw module boundaries from day one (folders with public interfaces, a lint rule against reaching into another module's internals). Pull out a service only when a concrete pain appears: a part that needs a GPU (graphics processor, for ML models), a background job that keeps starving the web servers.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Hundreds or thousands of services, each owned by one team, on a shared platform (Kubernetes, service mesh, tracing, a service catalog). Teams are designed around business domains on purpose (Conway's law). The hard problems are boundaries (Lesson 4.2), resilience (Lesson 4.4) and API evolution (Lesson 4.5), not writing services.

🎤 Interview angle

“I'd use microservices” is not an answer. A senior answer covers:

  • Why split at all: team count, independent deploys, different scaling profiles. Tie it to the scenario's numbers.
  • The costs you accept: network latency, partial failure, no cross-service transactions, harder debugging, ops per service.
  • The tail: fan-out amplifies p99, so you limit fan-out, set timeouts, and consider hedging.
  • A path, not a leap: start modular, extract services along stable boundaries, migrate gradually (strangler fig, Lesson 4.2).

Common follow-ups: “How many services would you start with?” · “How do you keep data consistent across them?” · “What would make you merge two services back together?”

🏭 In the real world
Back to a monolith, 90% cheaper 🏢 Amazon Prime Video · 2023 Scaling up the Prime Video audio/video monitoring service (archived)

How: the stream-monitoring tool was built from serverless pieces orchestrated by Step Functions, passing data between them through S3 (Amazon's object storage). The team moved all components into a single process on EC2 and ECS (Amazon's virtual machines and container service), so data moved in memory. The post reports infrastructure cost reduced by over 90% and better scaling.

Why this method: the expensive parts were the orchestration and the data passed between distributed components. For a tightly coupled pipeline, network hops were pure cost. The post itself says the choice has to be made case by case.

Goodbye microservices 🏢 Segment · 2018 Goodbye Microservices: From 100s of problem children to 1 superstar

How: Segment had a separate service (and queue) per data destination. Shared libraries drifted between codebases, and 3 full-time engineers spent most of their time keeping the system alive. They consolidated over 140 services into one.

Why this method: the destinations did nearly the same work, so separate services added overhead without independent teams or scaling needs to justify it.

The modular monolith 🏢 Shopify · 2019–2020 Deconstructing the Monolith

How: Shopify's core is one of the largest Ruby on Rails codebases, worked on by over a thousand developers (by 2020, over 2.8 million lines of Ruby). Instead of microservices, they kept one codebase and enforced component boundaries, later with their open-source tool Packwerk (Under Deconstruction).

Why this method: they wanted clear ownership and boundaries without paying the network, consistency and ops costs of splitting.

Fan-out makes the tail common 📜 Google · 2013 The Tail at Scale (Dean & Barroso, CACM)

How: the paper shows that if a server is slow (over 1 second) for 1 in 100 requests, a request that needs 100 such servers in parallel is slow 63% of the time. It proposes hedged requests: in one BigTable benchmark, a backup request after 10 ms cut the 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for just 2% more requests.

Why this method: you can't remove every source of slowness on thousands of machines, so you design the request so that it doesn't wait on the unlucky one.

🤯 Fun fact · the 1968 law

Conway's law comes from a short 1968 article in Datamation magazine called “How Do Committees Invent?”. Martin Fowler's later advice, Monolith First (2015), is its practical cousin: he notes that almost all successful microservice stories he heard began as a monolith that grew too big and was broken up.

Check yourself

1. A page calls 50 backend services in parallel. Each is slow on 1% of calls. Roughly what share of page loads hit at least one slow call?
1 − 0.9950 ≈ 1 − 0.605 = 0.39, so about 40%. Each service's rare tail becomes common for the whole page. Fixes: call fewer services, set tight timeouts, or hedge slow calls.
2. A 4-person startup is building its first product. The CTO wants 12 microservices “so we can scale later”. What's the best advice?
With 4 people, the fixed costs (pipelines, tracing, on-call, network failures) eat a big share of the week, and the boundaries are still guesses. A modular monolith keeps the option to split along boundaries you've learned are stable. No structure at all makes a later split much harder.
3. Which is the strongest reason to move a part of a monolith into its own service?
Independent scaling, different hardware and a separate owning team are real reasons to split. Clean code can be had with modules, and a network call is always slower than a function call, never faster.
📝 TL;DR
  • Microservices scale teams (independent deploys, ownership), and cost you network calls, partial failures, lost transactions and ops per service.
  • Availability multiplies along a chain (0.99910 ≈ 99.0%), and fan-out makes the tail common: 1 − (1 − p)N.
  • Default: a modular monolith with enforced boundaries; extract services for different scaling needs or team independence.
  • Conway's law: your architecture will mirror your org chart, so design both together.
