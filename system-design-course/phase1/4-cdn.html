Phase 1 · Lesson 4

CDNs & the edge

A content delivery network keeps copies of your files in hundreds of cities, so users fetch them from down the road instead of across an ocean. It's the cheapest latency fix there is, and it can absorb most of your traffic bill.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Caching: keep copies of popular answers; hit ratio = share answered by the copy; TTL = how long a copy is trusted (Lesson 1.3).
  • RTT (round-trip time) = how long a message takes to get there and back. A new HTTPS connection needs a few round trips before the first byte (Lesson 0.2).
  • Light in fiber covers roughly 200 km per millisecond (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

Netflix doesn't send your movie from a data center in another country. It built its own CDN, Open Connect, and offers its caching servers to internet providers at no charge, so they can sit inside the provider's own network. Netflix says it partners with over a thousand ISPs this way.

Why give hardware away? Because the cheapest, fastest byte is one that never crosses the internet. That's the whole idea of a CDN.

Numbers first: Sydney meets Virginia

Your servers (the origin) are in Virginia. A user in Sydney is about 15,700 km away.

  • Light in fiber: 15,700 km ÷ 200 km/ms ≈ 79 ms one way, so at best 157 ms there and back. Real cables wander, so call it ~240 ms RTT.
  • A fresh HTTPS request needs about 3 round trips (TCP handshake, TLS handshake, the request itself): 3 × 240 ≈ 720 ms before your server even starts. Plus 20 ms of work: about 0.74 s.
  • Now put a cache in Sydney, ~10 ms away: 3 × 10 = 30 ms if it has the file. About 24× faster, and your origin never sees the request.

You can't make light faster. You can only move the answer closer.

💡 Big idea A CDN is the cache from Lesson 1.3, copied into points of presence (PoPs: small data centers in many cities). It wins twice: popular files come from nearby (hits), and even requests it can't cache are faster, because the slow handshakes happen with a nearby edge server that keeps a warm connection to your origin.
🧠 Analogy: the central library and its branches

The central library (your origin) has every book. Driving there takes an hour. So the city opens branch libraries in every neighborhood (PoPs) that stock the popular books. Most people find what they want at their branch. If not, the branch orders it from central over its own van service (the warm connection), and keeps a copy for the next reader. When a new edition comes out, central has to tell the branches to throw out the old one: that's a purge.

How a request finds the nearest edge

  • DNS steering: when the browser looks up cdn.example.com (Lesson 0.2), the CDN's DNS server answers with the IP address of a PoP near the user.
  • Anycast: every PoP announces the same IP address, and the internet's routers deliver each packet to the closest one. Cloudflare's network is built this way; it lists data centers in more than 330 cities.

What a CDN can cache (and what it shouldn't)

ContentCache at the edge?Typical rule
Images, video segments, fonts✅ Yes, for a long timeLong TTL
JS / CSS with a content hash in the name (app.3f9a2c.js)✅ Yes, “forever”max-age=31536000, immutable: a new version gets a new name
Public HTML, public API responses⚠️ BrieflyA few seconds to minutes, plus purge on change
Personalized pages, account data❌ Never in a shared cacheprivate or no-store

You control this with the Cache-Control response header. The directives that matter most:

DirectiveMeans
public, max-age=300Anyone (browser and CDN) may reuse this for 300 s
s-maxage=60Shared caches (the CDN) use 60 s; browsers use max-age
privateOnly the user's browser may store it, never the CDN
no-storeDon't store it anywhere
stale-while-revalidate=30Serve the old copy for up to 30 s while fetching a fresh one (the stampede fix from Lesson 1.3)

Defaults differ by CDN. Cloudflare, for example, does not cache HTML or JSON unless you tell it to; it caches by file extension (images, CSS, JS, video and so on).

Three more edge tricks

  • Origin shield: one extra cache layer in front of the origin. Without it, a file that's missing in 300 PoPs means up to 300 origin fetches; with it, the PoPs ask the shield and the shield asks the origin once. AWS CloudFront offers this as “Origin Shield”.
  • Purge (invalidation): tell every PoP to drop a file now, instead of waiting for its TTL. Better still, use versioned file names so you rarely need to.
  • Signed URLs: a link with an expiry time and a cryptographic signature, so the CDN only serves paid or private files (a course video, a download) to people you gave the link to.

✏️ Worked example: what the hit ratio does to your bill

A media site sends 500 TB a month of images and video.

  • That's 500 × 1012 bytes × 8 bits ÷ (30 × 86,400 s) ≈ 1.5 Gbps on average, and peaks are several times that.
  • With a 95% CDN hit ratio, the origin only serves 5%: 25 TB a month. Your servers and your outbound bandwidth shrink 20×.
  • Going from 95% to 99% cuts origin traffic again, from 25 TB to 5 TB. Like Lesson 1.3: the last few points of hit ratio are worth the most.

🔥 Real outage · Fastly, 8 June 2021

On 12 May, Fastly deployed software containing a hidden bug that only a specific customer configuration could trigger. On 8 June, a customer pushed a perfectly valid configuration change that did exactly that, and 85% of Fastly's network started returning errors, taking a large share of its customers' websites with it.

Fastly detected it within one minute, found and disabled the configuration, and within 49 minutes 95% of the network was back to normal. Lesson: a CDN puts a huge number of sites behind one shared system. That's why some companies keep a second CDN they can switch to, and why CDN configuration deserves the same care as a code deploy.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Managed CDNs: Cloudflare, AWS CloudFront, Fastly, Akamai, Google Cloud CDN. Your job is mostly sending the right headers and naming files well. Headers from nginx at the origin:

# fingerprinted assets (app.3f9a2c.js): cache for a year, never revalidate
location /assets/ {
    add_header Cache-Control "public, max-age=31536000, immutable";
}
# public HTML: CDN keeps it 60 s, browsers revalidate; serve stale while refreshing
location / {
    add_header Cache-Control "public, max-age=0, s-maxage=60, stale-while-revalidate=30";
}
# anything personal: never in a shared cache
location /account/ {
    add_header Cache-Control "private, no-store";
}

Purging a file from every CloudFront edge:

aws cloudfront create-invalidation --distribution-id E2EXAMPLE123 --paths "/index.html" "/img/hero.jpg"

Try it: curl -sI https://www.example.com/ | grep -iE 'cache-control|age|cf-cache-status|x-cache' on any big site. Headers like age, cf-cache-status: HIT or x-cache: Hit from cloudfront tell you a CDN answered, and how old its copy is.

⚖️ Trade-offs
✅ A CDN shines when…⚠️ Think harder when…
Users are spread across countriesAll users are in one city next to your servers
Content is the same for many users (media, assets, public pages)Every response is personal: the gain is only the nearby handshake
You want protection: the CDN soaks up traffic spikes and attacks (Lesson 6.6)Stale content is dangerous (prices at checkout, stock levels)
Egress is a big part of your billYour hit ratio is low; you may pay twice

The hidden cost is correctness: a wrong Cache-Control can show one user's page to another. Make private the default for anything behind a login.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Put a managed CDN in front of the whole site on day one: it's minutes of setup and often has a free tier. Fingerprint your static files, set long max-age on them, keep HTML short-lived, mark logged-in pages private. Done.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Multiple CDNs with traffic steering between them, custom edge logic (edge functions for auth, A/B tests, image resizing), tiered caching with origin shields, purge pipelines wired to deploys, and for the biggest video companies, their own CDN inside ISPs (Netflix Open Connect).

🎤 Interview angle

For any read-heavy or global design (video, images, news feed), a senior answer:

  • Puts static and media content on a CDN early and estimates the hit ratio and the resulting origin load.
  • Explains cache keys and invalidation: versioned URLs for assets, short TTLs plus purge for HTML.
  • Separates public vs private content, and uses signed URLs for paid or private media.
  • Mentions what happens if the CDN fails or a popular file is purged (origin shield, request collapsing, a second CDN).

Common follow-ups: “How does a user reach the nearest PoP?” (DNS steering or anycast) · “You deployed a new JS file but users still get the old one. Why?” · “Would you cache API responses at the edge?”

🏭 In the real world
A CDN inside the ISPs 🏢 Netflix · Open Connect Netflix Open Connect

How: Open Connect Appliances are embedded in ISP networks (provided at no charge to qualifying partners), with the same capabilities as the appliances in Netflix's 60+ data centers, plus open peering at interconnection points.

Why this method: video is huge and very cacheable; serving it from inside the user's own ISP is faster for viewers and cheaper for everyone.

Many machines, one IP address 🏢 Cloudflare · 2011 A Brief Anycast Primer

How: every data center announces the same IP addresses, so routers deliver each user's packets to the nearest one. The post shows a traceroute from Northern California ending in San Jose.

Why this method: speed, plus resilience: Cloudflare can take a whole data center offline and traffic flows to the next closest one. It also spreads attack traffic over many data centers.

A shield in front of the origin 🏭 AWS CloudFront Using Amazon CloudFront Origin Shield

How: an extra caching layer: edge locations and regional edge caches fetch from Origin Shield, which can retrieve each object with a single request to your origin.

Why this method: fewer simultaneous requests to the origin, which matters most for live video and after purges.

Private content with signed URLs 🏭 AWS CloudFront Serve private content with signed URLs and signed cookies

How: users can only fetch the files through CloudFront URLs or cookies that you signed, and you block direct access to the origin (for example an S3 bucket).

Why this method: paid or private files still get CDN speed, without making them public.

One header controls every cache 🧰 Web standard HTTP Cache-Control (MDN)

How: max-age, s-maxage, private, no-store, immutable and stale-while-revalidate tell browsers and CDNs what to keep and for how long.

Why this method: the origin stays in charge; every cache between it and the user follows the same rules.

🤯 Fun fact · same address, many places

In that 2011 anycast primer, Cloudflare explained that wherever you were in the world you'd get the same IP addresses for a site, yet 12 data centers were all listening on them. Today its network page lists data centers in more than 330 cities, still answering on shared addresses.

🌉 How it connects

A CDN is caching (1.3) spread across the planet, often with load balancing (1.2), rate limiting (1.5) and gateway features (1.6) running at the edge too. It's the backbone of video streaming (Lesson 7.5) and a key DDoS defense (Lesson 6.6). Object storage (2.7) is the usual origin for media. AI products use the edge too: model files and app assets are served from CDNs, while the inference itself still runs in a few regions with GPUs.

Check yourself

1. You serve 200 TB a month through a CDN with a 90% hit ratio. About how much does your origin send?
Misses are 10%: 0.1 × 200 = 20 TB. At 99% it would be 2 TB. 180 TB is what the CDN serves from its caches.
2. You deployed a fixed app.js, but users keep getting the old one for hours. What's the best long-term fix?
A new version gets a new URL, so no cache can serve the old one, and old versions can be cached forever. no-store throws away all the CDN's benefit; purging everything works but causes an origin stampede on every deploy.
3. Which response must never be stored in the CDN's shared cache?
A shared cache could hand one user's orders to the next visitor. Personal responses get Cache-Control: private (browser only) or no-store.
📝 TL;DR
  • A CDN is a cache in hundreds of cities. Distance costs ~1 ms of RTT per 100 km of fiber, paid several times per new connection.
  • It helps even uncacheable requests: handshakes end at a nearby edge, which keeps warm connections to the origin.
  • Cache-Control decides everything: long max-age + immutable for fingerprinted assets, short s-maxage for public HTML, private for anything personal.
  • Origin traffic = (1 − hit ratio) × total. Origin shields cut purge stampedes; signed URLs protect private files.
