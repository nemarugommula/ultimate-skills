Phase 6 · Recap

Recap: shipping & running it

Designing a system is half the job. This phase was the other half: getting changes into production safely, seeing what the system is doing, responding when it breaks, sizing and paying for it, and keeping attackers out.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🔁

6.1 CI/CD

P(false red) = 1 − (1 − p)n. Cheap checks first, build once and promote the same artifact, trunk-based with flags, measure DORA (lead time, deploy frequency, recovery time, change fail rate). GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, Argo CD, Bazel.

🏷️

6.1b Versioning & releases

P(surprise break) = 1 − (1 − b)N·r·d. SemVer for libraries (^ = minors, ~ = patches, 0.x = anything), commit the lockfile + npm ci, move it with bot PRs (cooldown, grouping, automerge on green), Conventional Commits → semantic-release. Dependabot, Renovate, semantic-release, Keep a Changelog.

🐤

6.2 Deploying safely

Damage ≈ traffic × share × error rate × time to undo. Canary with automated analysis vs a baseline, feature flags hashed by user ID, expand and contract migrations. Argo Rollouts, Spinnaker + Kayenta, Unleash, LaunchDarkly.

🔭

6.3 Observability

Metrics say that, traces say where, logs say why. p99, not averages; fan-out makes tails common; RED per service, USE per resource; bounded label cardinality. Prometheus, Grafana, OpenTelemetry, Jaeger.

📟

6.4 Incidents

Burn rate = error rate ÷ allowed rate; 14.4× for 1 h = 2% of a month's budget. Page on symptoms, mitigate first, IC + ops + comms + scribe, blameless postmortems. Alertmanager, PagerDuty, Statuspage.

📈

6.5 Capacity & cost

L = λW; latency ≈ S ÷ (1 − ρ). Plan peak at 50–70%, pre-scale for known events, load-test with an arrival rate, track cost per request, mix reserved / on-demand / spot. k6, Locust, HPA, Kubecost.

🛡️

6.6 Security

Assume breach; shrink the blast radius. Authorize every request, parameterize queries, secrets never in git (short-lived, least privilege), SBOMs and scans, DDoS protection and a WAF at the edge, audit logs. Vault, KMS, Trivy, Cloudflare, AWS WAF.

🗣️ Teach it back

An interviewer asks: “Walk me through what happens from the moment you merge a change until you're confident it's healthy for every user. And what happens at 3 a.m. if it isn't?”

Say it out loud in two minutes. A strong answer touches: the pipeline stages and the artifact, a staged rollout gated by metrics, feature flags, the SLIs you watch (p99, error rate), burn-rate alerts, the incident roles and “roll back first,” and the postmortem that follows. Bonus: why the database migration was safe, and what a leaked deploy key could reach.

🏗️ Design it yourself: run a flash-sale checkout

You're the lead engineer for a ticketing company. A huge concert goes on sale Saturday at 10:00. Marketing expects about 500,000 fans in the first ten minutes. Your normal peak is 2,000 requests/s. You have three weeks. Using only this phase's ideas, plan how you'll ship the changes, size the system, watch it, defend it, and respond if it breaks.

Reveal a strong answer

1. Requirements

  • Fans can browse, join a queue, pick seats and pay. SLO for the sale window: 99.9% of checkout requests succeed, p99 under 1 s.
  • No ticket sold twice; no payment taken without a ticket. Bots must not crowd out humans.

2. Numbers

  • 500,000 fans × about 20 requests each in 10 minutes = 10 million requests ÷ 600 s ≈ 16,700 req/s on average; plan for a 3× burst in the first minute: about 50,000 req/s, 25× your normal peak.
  • Little's law at 100 ms per request: 50,000 × 0.1 = 5,000 requests in flight.
  • If a pod safely does 250 req/s and you want it at 60%: 50,000 ÷ 150 ≈ 334 pods for the front end. Autoscaling can't add that in a minute, so pre-scale Friday night.
  • Checkout writes: if the database comfortably handles about 1,500 orders/s, the waiting room must admit buyers at that rate, not let 50,000 in at once.

3. Shipping the changes (6.1, 6.2)

  • All new code (waiting room, seat locks) merged behind feature flags weeks ahead, off by default; pipeline under 15 minutes so a fix during the sale can ship fast.
  • Each change goes out by canary with automated analysis; schema changes are expand and contract. Change freeze for non-sale code from Friday.
  • Kill switches for anything non-essential (recommendations, reviews) to shed load instantly.

4. Proving it (6.5)

  • Breakpoint test with k6 at arrival rates up to 60,000 req/s against a production-sized environment: find the first bottleneck (likely the seats database or payment provider limits).
  • Spike test from 2,000 to 50,000 req/s in one minute; soak test for 2 hours at 20,000 req/s for leaks.
  • Cost: 334 pods for a few hours is cheap compared with a failed sale; use on-demand (not spot) for the sale fleet.

5. Watching it (6.3, 6.4)

  • A sale dashboard: RED for queue, seats and payment services; p99 checkout latency; waiting-room admit rate; database saturation (USE).
  • Burn-rate alerts tuned for a 2-hour event: a 1-hour window is too slow, so use short windows (5 min) with a high burn threshold, plus a war room with a named incident commander, ops lead and comms lead from 09:30.
  • Pre-written runbooks: “payment provider slow” → pause admissions; “seats DB hot” → lower the admit rate; “bad deploy” → roll back.

6. Defending it (6.6)

  • CDN + DDoS protection in front, WAF and per-account / per-IP rate limits plus bot detection at the waiting room.
  • Authorization checked on every order endpoint (no IDOR on /orders/{id}); payment keys in a secrets manager, least privilege for each service.

7. Classic mistakes

  • Trusting autoscaling for a 25× jump in one minute.
  • Load-testing with a fixed number of users that wait for replies (hides overload), or only testing the home page.
  • Letting everyone into checkout at once instead of metering admission to what the database can take.
  • Shipping new code on the morning of the sale, or with no rollback plan.
  • Alerts on 1-hour windows for a 10-minute rush: the sale is over before the page fires.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. 5,000 requests/s. A release fails 4% of requests. A 1% canary is caught after 10 minutes. About how many requests failed?
10 × 60 × 5,000 × 1% × 4% = 1,200. At 100% for the same 10 minutes it would be 120,000: the canary cut the damage 100×.
2. Your SLO is 99.95%. Over the last hour (and the last 5 minutes) the error rate is 0.72%. What should happen?
The allowed error rate is 0.05%, so 0.72 ÷ 0.05 = 14.4×: about 2% of the month's budget per hour, the SRE workbook's fast-burn paging threshold.
3. 1,200 requests/s, 250 ms each. Each pod handles 30 requests at once, and you want pods at most 60% busy. How many pods?
Little's law: 1,200 × 0.25 = 300 in flight; 300 ÷ 30 = 10 pods at 100%; 10 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 16.7, so 17 pods. 300 is the concurrency, not the pod count.
4. You're the only backend engineer at a 5-person startup and want safer deploys this week. What gives the most safety for the effort?
Fast, practiced rollback shrinks “time to undo,” and flags shrink “share exposed,” for almost no cost. Automated canary analysis needs real traffic volume and tooling; manual gates slow everything and batch changes into riskier releases.
5. A critical vulnerability is announced in a widely used library. What turns “are we affected?” into a one-hour question instead of a two-week hunt?
An SBOM lists every component inside each artifact, including transitive dependencies. Query them all, then patch and redeploy through the pipeline. A WAF rule can buy time, but it doesn't tell you where the library runs.
