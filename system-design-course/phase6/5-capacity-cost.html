Phase 6 · Lesson 5

Capacity planning, load testing & cost

How many servers do you need, how do you know before launch day, and what does each request cost? Get it wrong one way and you fall over; get it wrong the other way and you burn money.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Back-of-the-envelope estimation: QPS (queries per second), peak vs average (Lesson 0.3).
  • Horizontal scaling and the Kubernetes HPA (horizontal pod autoscaler, adds pods when a metric is high) (Lessons 1.1 and 5.3).
  • Saturation and p99 latency from your metrics (Lesson 6.3).
🎯 Hook

When Pokémon GO launched in 2016, Niantic and Google's reliability engineers had planned for a baseline traffic target, with a worst-case estimate of about 5× that. Real player traffic surged to 50× the target: ten times the worst case. Google provisioned extra capacity on the fly, and before the launch in Japan the teams even upgraded the game's Kubernetes cluster to allow more than a thousand extra nodes.

You can't always predict demand. But you can know your limits, test them, and keep enough headroom and automation to react. That's capacity planning.

Numbers first: Little's law

Your API gets 2,000 requests per second, and each takes 200 ms (0.2 s) to finish. How many requests are in progress at any moment?

L = λ × W = 2,000 /s × 0.2 s = 400 requests in flight “The average number of things in the system equals the rate they arrive times how long each one stays.” It works for requests, database connections, threads, even people in a coffee shop.
L: requests in progress (concurrency)λ (lambda): arrival rateW: time each spends inside

So if each server can handle 50 requests at once (say, 50 worker threads or database connections), you need at least 400 ÷ 50 = 8 servers. At 100% busy. Which is exactly where you must never run, as the next section shows.

Little's law also explains a classic outage: a dependency slows from 200 ms to 2 s. Same 2,000 requests per second, but now 4,000 are in flight. Your thread pools and connection pools fill up, and the whole service stops, even though traffic didn't change.

Why “100% utilized” means “broken”

Utilization (written ρ, “rho”) is the share of time a server is busy. In a simple queue where requests arrive at random, the average time in the system is:

W = S ÷ (1 − ρ) “Response time is the service time S divided by the idle share.” At 50% busy, requests take 2× their service time; at 90%, 10×; at 99%, 100×.
Utilization ρ50%80%90%95%99%
Slowdown 1 ÷ (1 − ρ)2×5×10×20×100×

This is the textbook single-server queue with random arrivals (called M/M/1). Real systems differ in the details, but the shape is universal: a knee where latency stops growing gently and shoots up. Requests that arrive while the server is busy wait in line, and near 100% the line almost never empties.

💡 Big idea Capacity is the load where latency is still acceptable, not the load where things crash. Plan to run well below the knee at peak (often around 50–70% utilization), so you have room for spikes, for a lost server or zone, and for the minutes autoscaling needs to react.
🧠 Analogy: the supermarket checkout

One cashier, customers arriving at random. If the cashier is busy half the time, you rarely wait. At 90% busy, a short lull never quite clears the line, and one slow customer with coupons backs it up for everyone. Opening a second till before the line forms is capacity planning. Calling someone in from home after the line reaches the door is autoscaling with lead time.

Check the last two numbers: the measured average number of requests in the system matches λ × W, whatever the utilization. That's Little's law, live. And notice p99: near the knee, the slowest requests suffer far more than the average.

Headroom: what you keep in reserve

  • Peak, not average. Daily peaks are often 2–3× the average; launches and sales are bigger.
  • Lose a zone. Running in 3 availability zones and want to survive losing one? The other two must carry everything, so at peak each zone should sit at no more than about two thirds of its safe capacity (often called N+1).
  • Autoscaling lead time. A new server needs time to boot, pull its image, start and warm up: often a few minutes. Your headroom has to absorb growth during that time.
✏️ Worked example: headroom for a launch

During a launch, traffic grows 5% per minute. New pods take 4 minutes to become ready.

  • By the time new capacity arrives, traffic has grown 1.054 ≈ 1.22×.
  • So the current fleet must already handle 22% more than today's load: run at most about 1 ÷ 1.22 ≈ 82% of safe capacity, before any other margin.
  • Add the zone-loss margin (each zone ≤ 67%) and the stricter one wins: plan for about 65% at peak.

For a known event (a sale, a launch, a ticket on-sale), don't rely on autoscaling: pre-scale the day before, and load-test at the expected peak.

Load testing: find the limit before your users do

A load test sends synthetic traffic at your system to measure how it behaves. The k6 documentation names the common shapes:

TestLoadDurationAnswers
SmokeMinimalShortDoes the script and system work at all?
Average-loadTypical trafficMidDo we meet our SLO on a normal day?
StressAbove averageMid (5–60 min)What happens at peak and beyond?
SoakAverageHoursMemory leaks, filling disks, slow degradation
SpikeVery high, suddenA few minutesDo we survive a flash crowd? Does autoscaling keep up?
BreakpointIncreases until it breaksAs long as neededWhere exactly is the limit, and which part breaks first?

One subtle point: generate load as an arrival rate (“1,000 new requests per second”), not as a fixed number of users who each wait for a reply before sending the next. With a fixed number of waiting users, a slow server automatically gets fewer requests, which hides the very overload you're testing for. k6 calls the arrival-rate style an “open model.”

🎮 Playground · Find the breaking point

A service with app pods (each safely handles about 250 req/s) in front of one database (about 1,800 queries/s, one query per request). Requests time out after 2 s. Pick a test shape, size the fleet, and see where it breaks, and which part breaks. Try adding pods past the database's limit.

Things to try: on the ramp, go from 4 to 8 pods: the breaking point moves up, then stops at the database. More app pods can't fix a database limit (that's when you reach for caching, read replicas or sharding, Lessons 1.3, 2.3, 2.4). On the spike, turn on autoscaling with a 3-minute lead time: the new pods arrive after the damage is done. Then try pre-scaling with more pods instead.

Cost: the other half of capacity

Every extra server is money. The useful unit is cost per request (or per user, per order), because it tells you whether growth makes you richer or poorer.

✏️ Worked example: cost per million requests

Say 10 servers at about $0.10 per hour each (an illustrative price for a small cloud VM; check your provider) serve an average of 800 requests/s.

  • Cost per hour: 10 × $0.10 = $1.00. Requests per hour: 800 × 3,600 = 2.88 million.
  • Cost per million requests: $1.00 ÷ 2.88 ≈ $0.35.
  • If nights are quiet and autoscaling halves the fleet for 12 hours a day, the average drops to about $0.26 per million, a 25% saving with no code change.
Pricing modelWhat it isGood for
On-demandPay by the second or hour, no commitmentSpiky or unknown load; the baseline price
Reserved / savings plansCommit to 1 or 3 years of usage for a discount; AWS advertises up to 72% off on-demand for Savings PlansThe steady base load you're sure you'll have
Spot / preemptibleSpare capacity at a deep discount (AWS: up to 90% off), but it can be taken back; AWS gives a two-minute interruption noticeBatch jobs, CI runners, stateless workers that tolerate being killed

A common mix: reserved for the base, on-demand for the daily peak, spot for batch work. FinOps is the practice of making engineers see and own these costs: the FinOps Foundation calls it an operational framework and cultural practice that creates financial accountability through collaboration between engineering, finance and business teams.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Load testing: k6 (JavaScript scripts, Go engine), Locust (Python), Gatling, JMeter, vegeta; managed: Grafana Cloud k6, Azure Load Testing. Cost: AWS Cost Explorer, GCP billing reports, Kubecost/OpenCost for per-team Kubernetes costs. A k6 breakpoint-style test with pass/fail thresholds:

import http from 'k6/http';

export const options = {
  scenarios: {
    ramp: {
      executor: 'ramping-arrival-rate',   // open model: new requests per second, whatever the latency
      startRate: 100, timeUnit: '1s',
      preAllocatedVUs: 200, maxVUs: 2000,
      stages: [
        { target: 500,  duration: '2m' },
        { target: 2000, duration: '8m' },   // keep climbing until something breaks
      ],
    },
  },
  thresholds: {
    http_req_failed:   ['rate<0.01'],     // under 1% errors
    http_req_duration: ['p(95)<500'],     // 95% of requests under 500 ms
  },
};

export default function () {
  http.get('https://staging.example.com/api/products');
}
# Kubernetes HPA: keep average CPU near 60%, between 4 and 40 pods
apiVersion: autoscaling/v2
kind: HorizontalPodAutoscaler
metadata: { name: shop }
spec:
  scaleTargetRef: { apiVersion: apps/v1, kind: Deployment, name: shop }
  minReplicas: 4        # the floor is your headroom for sudden spikes
  maxReplicas: 40
  metrics:
    - type: Resource
      resource:
        name: cpu
        target: { type: Utilization, averageUtilization: 60 }

Try it locally: k6 run load.js, or docker run --rm -i grafana/k6 run - <load.js. Locust: write a HttpUser class with @task methods, then locust -f locustfile.py --headless -u 500 -r 50 --host https://staging.example.com. Never load-test production or someone else's site without permission.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Know your one bottleneck (it's usually the database) and roughly what load breaks it: run a 30-minute k6 breakpoint test before every big launch. Set billing alerts on day one. Use managed and serverless services that scale for you, and buy reserved capacity only once your base load is boringly predictable.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Quarterly capacity plans built from growth forecasts, per-service load models (requests → CPU, memory, storage), regular production load tests and “game days,” region-level N+1 or N+2, organization-wide reserved-capacity buying, and FinOps dashboards that show each team its cost per request.

🎤 Interview angle

Capacity math is where design interviews get concrete. A senior answer:

  • Estimates peak QPS, then concurrency with Little's law, then servers needed at a target utilization (not 100%).
  • Names the first bottleneck (often the database or a single hot shard) and what happens beyond it.
  • Explains headroom: zone loss, autoscaling lead time, pre-scaling for known events.
  • Mentions how you'd verify it (a load test) and what it costs.

Follow-ups: “Latency doubled but traffic is flat. Why?” (a dependency got slower: Little's law means more requests in flight, pools fill up). “Why not run at 95% CPU to save money?” (the queueing knee: latency and the risk of collapse explode).

🏭 In the real world
Demand 10× the worst case 🏢 Niantic + Google Cloud · 2016 Bringing Pokémon GO to life on Google Cloud

How: the game's logic ran on Google Container Engine (GKE, Kubernetes). Traffic reached 50× the initial target, ten times the worst-case estimate, and Google's Customer Reliability Engineering team provisioned extra capacity and upgraded the cluster to add over a thousand more nodes before the Japan launch.

Why this method: when forecasts are wrong by 10×, what saves you is elastic infrastructure and people who know where the limits are.

A traffic peak nobody planned for 🏢 Ticketmaster · 2022 Taylor Swift | The Eras Tour Onsale Explained

How: Ticketmaster wrote that bot attacks and fans without presale codes drove 3.5 billion total system requests during the on-sale, 4× its previous peak.

Why this method: capacity plans are built on past peaks; a launch that attracts bots and a huge fan base can break that assumption, which is why systems also need admission control (queues, rate limits, Lesson 1.5).

Named load-test shapes 🧰 Open source · Grafana k6 k6: load test types

How: the guide defines smoke, average-load, stress, soak, spike and breakpoint tests by their load level and duration, and when to run each.

Why this method: each shape answers a different question; a team that only runs one kind finds the others' failures in production.

Spare capacity at a discount 🏭 In production · AWS Amazon EC2 Spot Instances

How: Spot Instances run on spare EC2 capacity for up to 90% off on-demand prices; AWS sends an interruption notice two minutes before it stops or terminates one.

Why this method: fault-tolerant, stateless work (batch, CI, queue workers) can checkpoint or retry, so a cheap machine that might vanish is a great deal.

The queueing formula 📜 History · 1961 Little: A Proof for the Queuing Formula L = λW (Operations Research)

How: John D. C. Little's short paper proved that the average number in a queueing system equals the arrival rate times the average time spent, under very general conditions.

Why this method: it lets you turn throughput and latency (which you measure) into concurrency (which you must provision: threads, connections, memory).

🤯 Fun fact · a formula from 1961 in your thread pool

Little's law is older than the internet, and MIT still describes its author, John Little, as best known for his proof of L = λW. Every time you size a database connection pool or a Kubernetes concurrency limit, you're using a result from a 5-page operations research paper.

Check yourself

1. A service handles 3,000 requests/s with an average latency of 50 ms. Each pod has a pool of 20 database connections, and each request holds one for its whole duration. How many pods do you need at minimum (before headroom)?
Little's law: L = 3,000 × 0.05 = 150 requests in flight, each holding a connection. 150 ÷ 20 = 7.5, so 8 pods at 100% busy. With headroom for the queueing knee and zone loss, you'd plan for 12 or more.
2. Your servers run at 70% CPU at peak and someone proposes cutting the fleet so they run at 95% to save money. What happens to latency, roughly?
Latency isn't linear in utilization. In the simple queue model the slowdown goes from 1 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 3.3× to 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20×, about six times worse, and any spike pushes it over the edge.
3. A big ticket sale starts at 10:00 sharp; traffic will jump 20× within a minute. Your autoscaler needs about 4 minutes to add pods. What's the best plan?
Autoscaling reacts after the fact; a 20× jump in one minute is over before new pods are ready. For known events, pre-scale to a capacity you've verified with a spike test, and protect the system with admission control so excess users wait instead of everyone failing.
📝 TL;DR
  • Little's law: concurrency L = λ × W. It sizes threads, connections and pods, and explains why slow dependencies cause outages.
  • Latency ≈ S ÷ (1 − ρ): run well below 100% (often 50–70% at peak) for spikes, zone loss and autoscaling lead time.
  • Load test with an arrival rate: smoke, average, stress, soak, spike, breakpoint. Find the first bottleneck before users do.
  • Track cost per request; mix reserved (base), on-demand (peaks) and spot (batch).
