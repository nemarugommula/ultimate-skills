Phase 6 · Lesson 4

Alerting, on-call, incidents & postmortems

Things will break. What separates good teams is how fast they find out, how calmly they stop the bleeding, and how honestly they learn from it, so the same thing doesn't break twice.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • SLO (service level objective): a target such as “99.9% of requests succeed over 30 days”; the error budget is the 0.1% you're allowed to fail (Lesson 0.5).
  • Metrics like error rate and p99 latency, queried with PromQL (Lesson 6.3).
  • Rollback is usually the fastest fix for a bad release (Lesson 6.2).
🎯 Hook

On January 31, 2017, a GitLab engineer was trying to fix database replication. Trying to wipe the database directory on the secondary server, they ran the command on the primary. They stopped it a second or two later, but about 300 GB of data was already gone. Then the team found that the regular pg_dump backups weren't there: the storage bucket was empty.

GitLab.com was down for about 18 hours and permanently lost the database changes made between 17:20 and midnight UTC that day. Then GitLab did something unusual: it published a detailed, blameless postmortem, focused on the broken processes rather than the person. This lesson is about everything around that night: alerts, on-call, incident roles, and the postmortem.

Numbers first: how much failure is allowed?

A 99.9% availability SLO over 30 days allows 0.1% of requests to fail. If every request failed at once, that's:

30 days × 24 h × 60 min × 0.1% = 43.2 minutes of total outage per month

That's the error budget. Now the key idea for alerting: burn rate, how fast you're spending it.

burn rate = current error rate ÷ error rate the SLO allows “A burn rate of 1 spends the budget in exactly 30 days. A burn rate of 14.4 spends 2% of a 30-day budget in one hour.” 14.4 × 1 hour ÷ 720 hours = 2%.

💡 Big idea Page on symptoms users feel, not on causes. “Checkout errors are eating the error budget fast” deserves a 3 a.m. phone call. “CPU is at 85%” doesn't: it might be fine, and if it isn't, a user-facing symptom will tell you. Every page should be urgent, real and actionable; everything else becomes a ticket or a dashboard.
🧠 Analogy: a smoke alarm that cries wolf

A smoke alarm that goes off every time you make toast gets its battery pulled. Then the real fire comes. Alerts work the same way: each false page trains people to ignore pages. A good alert goes off for every real fire, early enough to matter, and almost never for toast.

Tuning an alert: four things you trade

The SRE workbook judges an alert on four properties:

  • Precision: of the alerts that fire, how many were real problems? (Low precision = false pages.)
  • Recall: of the real problems, how many caused an alert?
  • Detection time: how long until it fires.
  • Reset time: how long it keeps firing after the problem is gone.

A naive rule like “error rate above 0.1% for 5 minutes” has great detection time and terrible precision: every brief blip pages someone. A burn-rate rule over 1 hour ignores short blips but catches real outages within minutes. Adding a short window (1/12 of the long one: 5 minutes for a 1-hour rule) makes it stop firing soon after the problem ends.

✏️ Worked example: when does the 14.4× alert fire?

SLO 99.9%, so the allowed error rate is 0.1%. The fast-burn rule pages when the 1-hour average error rate is at least 14.4 × 0.1% = 1.44% (and the 5-minute average is too).

  • A full outage at 30% errors: the 1-hour average reaches 1.44% after 1.44 ÷ 30 × 60 min ≈ 2.9 minutes.
  • A partial outage at 2% errors: 1.44 ÷ 2 × 60 ≈ 43 minutes. Slower, because it's burning slower.
  • A blip at 5% for 8 minutes: the 1-hour average peaks at 5 × 8 ÷ 60 ≈ 0.67%. Never pages. Good: it cost under 1% of the month's budget.
  • A slow leak at 0.8% (burn rate 8) never reaches 1.44%. That's why there's a second rule: 6× over 6 hours, which fires after 6 × 0.6 ÷ 0.8 = 4.5 hours.

Notice the pattern: when it fires, about 2% of the budget is gone, whatever the error rate. That's the design.

On-call: humans are part of the system

Someone has to answer the page. Healthy on-call is designed, not hoped for:

  • A rotation: a primary and a secondary on call, usually a week at a time, handing over with notes.
  • A load limit: Google's SRE book estimates each incident takes about 6 hours of work counting analysis, fixes and the postmortem, so it sets a maximum of 2 incidents per 12-hour shift, and caps on-call at 25% of an SRE's time.
  • Runbooks: for each alert, a page explaining what it means, what to check and how to mitigate. An alert without a runbook is a riddle at 3 a.m.
  • Severity levels: SEV1 (many users down, all hands), SEV2 (degraded), SEV3 (minor). Severity decides who gets woken up and how often updates go out.

During an incident: roles, then mitigate, then debug

When things are on fire, confusion is the enemy. Google's incident management (based on the firefighters' Incident Command System) and PagerDuty's public incident response guide both split the work into roles:

RoleJobDoes NOT
Incident commander (IC)Owns the incident: decides, delegates, keeps the big picture. Holds every role not yet given away.Type commands into production
Ops lead / subject matter expertsThe only people changing the system: rollback, failover, scalingWrite status updates
Communications (internal and customer liaison)Regular updates to stakeholders and the status pageDebug
Scribe / planningKeeps the live incident document and timeline; files follow-ups, arranges handoffsMake decisions

The first goal is mitigation, not understanding. Roll back the last deploy, shift traffic away from the bad region, turn off the feature flag, add capacity. You can find the root cause tomorrow with the system healthy. Debugging in production while users are failing is the most common way a 10-minute incident becomes a 2-hour one.

After the incident: the blameless postmortem

A postmortem is a written record of an incident: what happened, the impact, the timeline, the contributing causes, and the action items to prevent it or make it smaller next time. Google's SRE book calls blameless postmortems a tenet of SRE culture: it must focus on identifying the contributing causes “without indicting any individual or team.”

Why blameless? Because if people get punished for mistakes, they hide them, and you lose the information you need. The GitLab engineer typed a command on the wrong server; the real problems were a confusing tool with no warning, untested backups, and no clear sign of which server you were on. Those are fixable. “Be more careful” isn't.

✏️ Worked example: a postmortem skeleton
  • Summary: 14:02 deploy of checkout v2.31 caused 8% of checkout requests to fail for 20 minutes.
  • Impact: 20 × 4,800 = 96,000 failed checkout requests: about 3.7% of the month's error budget (a 30-day month at 1,000 req/s is 2.59 billion requests; 0.1% of that is 2.59 million).
  • Timeline: 14:02 deploy → 14:13 fast-burn alert pages (the 1-hour error average crossed 1.44%) → 14:15 IC declared, rollback started → 14:22 errors back to normal.
  • Contributing causes: discount code crashed on carts with no items in stock; no test covered it; the deploy went to 100% at once.
  • Action items (each with an owner and a date): add the missing test; move checkout to canary deploys; add a kill-switch flag around discounts.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Alert rules: Prometheus + Alertmanager (routing, grouping, silences), Grafana Alerting, Datadog monitors. Paging and on-call schedules: PagerDuty, Opsgenie, incident.io, Grafana OnCall. Status pages: Atlassian Statuspage, Instatus. A multiwindow burn-rate alert for a 99.9% SLO as a Prometheus rule, following the SRE workbook:

# recording rules first: the error ratio over several windows
groups:
- name: slo-checkout
  rules:
  - record: job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1h
    expr: sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",status=~"5.."}[1h]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))
  # ... same for 5m, 30m and 6h windows
  - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetBurn
    expr: |
      (job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1h{job="checkout"} > (14.4 * 0.001)
       and job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate5m{job="checkout"} > (14.4 * 0.001))
      or
      (job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate6h{job="checkout"} > (6 * 0.001)
       and job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate30m{job="checkout"} > (6 * 0.001))
    labels:
      severity: page
    annotations:
      summary: "Checkout is burning its error budget fast"
      runbook_url: "https://wiki.example.com/runbooks/checkout-errors"

Try it locally: Prometheus and Alertmanager both run as single containers (prom/prometheus, prom/alertmanager); promtool check rules slo.yml validates a rule file before you deploy it. Tools like Sloth or Pyrra generate these rules from a short SLO definition.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Two or three pages total: the site is down (an external uptime check), checkout or sign-up error rate is burning budget, and the database is nearly out of disk. Route them to a free-tier pager. Write a one-page postmortem for every user-facing outage, even when it's just you. A shared status page builds trust with early customers.

Big tech (1 → 100)

SLOs per service with burn-rate alerts generated from config, follow-the-sun rotations across time zones, trained incident commanders, severity definitions tied to customer impact, a postmortem review meeting, and action items tracked to completion. Error budgets connect alerts to release decisions (Lesson 8.4).

🎤 Interview angle

Two places this shows up: “how would you know it's broken?” in design rounds, and “tell me about an incident” in behavioral rounds. A senior answer covers:

  • SLIs and SLOs first, then burn-rate alerts on them; symptoms page, causes go to dashboards or tickets.
  • Mitigate first (rollback, failover, flag off), debug later.
  • Incident roles and communication cadence.
  • A blameless postmortem with owned, dated action items, and how you checked they actually got done.

Behavioral tip: tell an incident story in the shape of a postmortem: impact, timeline, what you did to mitigate, root causes, and what changed afterwards. Interviewers listen for ownership without blame.

🏭 In the real world
Burn-rate alerting 🏢 Google · SRE workbook Alerting on SLOs

How: the chapter walks through six alerting approaches, from a simple threshold up to multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts, and recommends paging at 2% of budget in 1 hour and 5% in 6 hours, with a ticket at 10% in 3 days. The short window is 1/12 of the long one.

Why this method: it balances precision, recall, detection time and reset time at once: big outages page in minutes, slow burns page in hours, and blips don't page at all.

Blameless postmortems 🏢 Google · SRE book Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure

How: define postmortem triggers in advance (any stakeholder may also request one), write them blamelessly, review them, and visibly reward people for writing good ones.

Why this method: a culture that punishes mistakes hides them; a blameless one surfaces the process and tooling gaps that let a single slip become an outage.

Incident command 🏢 Google · SRE book Managing Incidents

How: roles modelled on the Incident Command System: an incident commander, an ops lead (the only group modifying the system), a communications role, and a planning role; plus a live incident state document.

Why this method: separating responsibilities stops several people from changing production at once, and keeps stakeholders informed without interrupting the responders.

An open incident playbook 🏭 In production · PagerDuty PagerDuty incident response: roles

How: PagerDuty publishes its own process, with roles including incident commander, deputy, scribe, subject matter expert, customer liaison and internal liaison, plus training material for each.

Why this method: a written, rehearsed process means nobody has to invent one at 3 a.m.

A postmortem in public 🏢 GitLab · 2017 Postmortem of database outage of January 31

How: the postmortem covers the database setup, a detailed timeline, why each backup method failed (the pg_dump bucket was empty), a “5 whys” analysis, and the improvements. It estimates roughly 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments and 700 new user accounts were affected.

Why this method: backups you've never restored are not backups. Publishing the analysis rebuilt trust and taught the whole industry that lesson.

🤯 Fun fact · the most-watched database restore

During the 2017 outage, GitLab kept its working notes in a public Google document and streamed the recovery on YouTube. According to its postmortem, the stream peaked at around 5,000 viewers and was the #2 live stream on YouTube for several hours.

🌉 How it connects

Alerts are built on the SLOs from Lesson 0.5 and the metrics from Lesson 6.3. The fastest mitigations are the rollbacks and feature flags from Lesson 6.2. Lesson 8.4 turns error budgets into a policy (freeze risky launches when the budget is gone) and adds chaos engineering, which practices incidents on purpose. Check yourself

1. Your SLO is 99.9% over 30 days. An incident fails 5% of requests. What's the burn rate, and how long until the whole month's budget is gone?
5% ÷ 0.1% = 50. At 50× the budget lasts 30 days ÷ 50 = 0.6 days ≈ 14.4 hours. That's well above the 14.4× paging threshold: wake someone up.
2. 14:13, you're paged: checkout errors at 8%, starting right after a 14:02 deploy. What's the best first move?
Mitigate first. A rollback takes minutes and is almost always safe; finding and fixing the bug under pressure can take an hour while users keep failing. Debug tomorrow, on a healthy system.
3. Your team gets 40 pages a week, mostly “CPU above 80%” and “error rate above 0.1% for 5 minutes,” and most need no action. What do you change?
Pages that need no action destroy trust in the pager. Burn-rate alerts fire only when users are hurt badly enough to matter; CPU and short blips belong on dashboards or in tickets.
📝 TL;DR
  • Error budget = 1 − SLO; burn rate = error rate ÷ allowed rate. 14.4× for 1 hour = 2% of a month's budget.
  • Page on symptoms with multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts; send causes to dashboards and tickets.
  • In an incident: roles (IC, ops, comms, scribe), mitigate first (rollback, flag off, failover), debug later.
  • Blameless postmortems with owned, dated action items turn incidents into improvements.
