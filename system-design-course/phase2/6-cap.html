Phase 2 · Lesson 6

CAP, PACELC & consistency models

When copies of your data live in different places, you must choose: during a network split, refuse some requests or let the copies disagree; and the rest of the time, wait for distant copies or answer fast with possibly old data.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Replication lag, read-your-writes and quorums (W + R > N) (Lesson 2.3).
  • A round trip across a continent takes tens of milliseconds; light in fibre can't go faster (Lesson 0.3).
  • Isolation levels: how much concurrent transactions can see of each other (Lesson 2.5).
🎯 Hook

Google wanted a database that behaves like a single machine, with transactions in the order they really happened, while its copies sit on different continents. Clocks on different servers never agree exactly, so Spanner (2012) puts a set of time servers in every datacenter, most with GPS receivers and the rest with atomic clocks, behind an API, TrueTime, that returns the time with an error bar: in production, typically between about 1 and 7 ms, about 4 ms most of the time.

Then, before a transaction's commit becomes visible, Spanner simply waits out the uncertainty. Google bought consistency across the planet with hardware and a few milliseconds of waiting. Most of us pay with latency or with availability. This lesson is about which one, when.

Numbers first: two regions, one network cable

Your app runs in US-East and EU-West, each with a copy of the data. A round trip between them is typically about 70–80 ms. A local write takes about 2 ms.

The bottom row is the CAP choice. The top row is the everyday cost that PACELC adds. You pay one of them; physics doesn't let you pay neither.

💡 Big idea CAP: when a network Partition separates replicas, a system must either refuse some requests to stay Consistent, or answer them and stay Available while copies drift. PACELC adds: Else (no partition), it trades Latency against Consistency. Partitions are rare; the latency trade-off is paid on every request.
🧠 Analogy: two shops, one stock ledger

A bakery has two shops across town sharing one stock count, kept in sync by phone. The phone line goes dead. The shops can stop selling the last cakes until the line is back (consistent, not available), or keep selling and sort it out later, maybe apologizing for an oversold cake (available, not consistent). Even with the phone working, calling the other shop before every sale makes the queue slower: that's the latency side.

CAP, said precisely

  • Consistency (in CAP) means linearizable: every read sees the latest completed write, as if there were one copy. It's not the C in ACID (Lesson 2.5), which is about your rules holding.
  • Availability: every request to a node that's still running gets a real answer, not an error.
  • Partition tolerance: the system keeps working when messages between nodes are lost. Networks do fail (GitHub's 43 seconds, Lesson 2.3), so you don't get to opt out of P.

Eric Brewer, who proposed CAP, wrote in 2012 that the “2 of 3” framing was always misleading: partitions are rare, so there's little reason to give up C or A when there's no partition, and the choice can be made differently for different operations and data in the same system.

Things to try: in CP mode, cut the link and add an item in the EU: refused, because the EU copy can't confirm it has the latest cart. In AP with last-write-wins, both sides accept, and on heal one side's whole cart replaces the other's: items vanish silently. AP with merge keeps every added item, which is what Amazon's Dynamo did for carts; the Dynamo paper notes the catch: an “add to cart” is never lost, but deleted items can resurface.

PACELC: the trade-off you pay every day

Daniel Abadi's 2012 article argued that CAP describes only failures, while the bigger day-to-day cost of consistency is latency: keeping replicas in sync means waiting for them. PACELC reads: if Partition, choose A or C; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. His classifications at the time:

ClassMeaningExamples given (defaults, 2012)
PA/ELAvailable during partitions, fast otherwiseDynamo, Cassandra, Riak
PC/ECConsistent always, pays in availability and latencyVoltDB/H-Store, Megastore, BigTable/HBase
PA/ECConsistent normally, available during partitionsMongoDB

Many modern databases let you choose per request: Cassandra's consistency level, DynamoDB's ConsistentRead, MongoDB's read and write concerns.

The consistency ladder

“Consistent” isn't one thing. From strongest to weakest:

ModelPromiseTypical costExample
Linearizable (strong)Acts like one copy: every read sees the latest writeA round trip to a leader or quorum on every operationSpanner (externally consistent), DynamoDB strongly consistent reads, etcd
CausalIf B depends on A (a reply to a message), everyone sees A before BTrack dependencies; no global waitingSome geo-replicated stores and research systems
Session (read-your-writes, monotonic reads)You see your own writes; your view never goes backwardsSticky sessions or tracking log positionsMost apps on read replicas, done right (Lesson 2.3)

✏️ Worked example: picking per feature, not per company

A global marketplace, users in the US and EU, typical RTT 75 ms between regions.

  • Payments and stock (overselling costs money): strong consistency, data homed in one region per seller. A buyer abroad pays ~75 ms extra on checkout, a few times per order. Fine.
  • Product pages and reviews: eventual. Read locally in ~2 ms; a new review appearing 1 second later in the other region harms nobody.
  • “My orders” page: session consistency (read-your-writes), so a buyer always sees the order they just placed.
  • During a partition: checkout in the cut-off region fails with a clear error (CP); browsing keeps working (AP).

That's Brewer's point: the choice happens many times inside one system.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The knobs, per request: DynamoDB ConsistentRead and global tables, Cassandra/ScyllaDB consistency levels (ONE, QUORUM, LOCAL_QUORUM), MongoDB write and read concerns, Postgres synchronous_commit. Globally consistent SQL: Spanner, CockroachDB. And Jepsen for testing what a database really guarantees.

# DynamoDB: eventually consistent by default (half the read cost); ask for strong per call
aws dynamodb get-item --table-name Carts --key '{"user_id":{"S":"u42"}}' --consistent-read

-- Cassandra (cqlsh): strong-ish inside one region, async to the others
CONSISTENCY LOCAL_QUORUM;
INSERT INTO carts (user_id, item) VALUES (42, 'book');

// MongoDB: wait for a majority of replicas before "OK"
db.orders.insertOne({ user: 42, total: 30 }, { writeConcern: { w: "majority" } })
db.orders.find({ user: 42 }).readConcern("majority")

A rule worth remembering: with replicas spread evenly over several regions, Cassandra's QUORUM counts replicas in every region, so writes usually wait on a cross-region round trip; LOCAL_QUORUM counts only the local region and accepts that other regions lag.

⚖️ Trade-offs
Choose consistency (CP / EC) for…Choose availability and speed (AP / EL) for…
Money, balances, stock, seats, unique usernamesLikes, views, feeds, recommendations, presence
Anything where a wrong answer is worse than no answerAnything where no answer is worse than a slightly old one
Cost: errors during partitions, round trips on every writeCost: conflicts to merge, anomalies to explain to users

Conflict handling is the hidden work of AP: last-write-wins is simple but silently drops updates; merging (like Dynamo's carts, or CRDTs, data types designed to merge automatically) keeps data but needs care with deletes. How replicas detect that two writes were concurrent (vector clocks) is Lesson 3.4b.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

One region, one primary database: you're strongly consistent by default and CAP barely applies. Use read replicas only for reads that tolerate lag. Don't go multi-region for “availability” before you need to; a second region doubles the operational work and forces exactly these choices.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Multiple regions, with a deliberate consistency choice per data type: home regions for user data, strongly consistent stores for money, eventually consistent global tables for caches and social data. Teams document what happens during a region partition, test it in game days (Lesson 8.4), and measure replication lag as an SLO (Lesson 0.5).

🎤 Interview angle

“It's AP because CAP” is a weak answer. What a senior answer covers:

  • For each kind of data, which consistency it needs and why (what's the harm of a stale read?).
  • What happens during a partition: who gets errors, who gets old data, how copies reconcile afterwards.
  • The latency cost of strong consistency across regions, with a number.
  • How conflicts are resolved in AP parts (last-write-wins, merge, CRDTs) and what's lost.

Common follow-ups: “Two users in different regions grab the same username during a partition. What happens?” “Is your system CP or AP?” (good answer: “it depends on the operation, here's the table”).

🏭 In the real world
CAP, revisited by its author 📜 History · 2012 CAP Twelve Years Later (Eric Brewer)

How: Brewer explains that “2 of 3” was misleading, that partitions are rare, and that designers should detect partitions, operate in a limited mode during them, and recover (merge) afterwards.

Why this method: treating a partition as a mode you plan for gives better systems than picking a letter once.

Latency is the everyday trade-off 📜 History · 2012 Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design (Abadi, IEEE Computer)

How: introduces PACELC and classifies systems: Dynamo, Cassandra and Riak as PA/EL by default; VoltDB/H-Store and Megastore as PC/EC; MongoDB as PA/EC.

Why this method: CAP says nothing about normal operation, where most of the cost of consistency (waiting for replicas) is actually paid.

Global consistency with clocks 🏢 Google · 2012 Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database (OSDI)

How: TrueTime exposes clock uncertainty (kept small with GPS and atomic clocks); commit wait makes sure a transaction's timestamp has passed everywhere before it's visible. F1, Google's ad backend, ran on five replicas spread across the US.

Why this method: externally consistent transactions at global scale, paid for with special hardware and a few milliseconds per commit.

Consistency as a per-read choice 🏢 Amazon Web Services DynamoDB read consistency

How: reads are eventually consistent by default and cost half as much as strongly consistent reads, which you request per call. Reads from global secondary indexes and streams are always eventually consistent.

Why this method: most reads tolerate a little staleness, so the cheap, fast option is the default and you pay for strength only where needed.

Both choices across regions 🏢 Amazon Web Services DynamoDB global tables: how it works

How: the default mode (MREC) replicates asynchronously, typically within a second, and resolves concurrent writes with last writer wins. The strong mode (MRSC) synchronously replicates each change to at least one other Region before returning, and must be deployed in exactly three Regions.

Why this method: AP/EL by default for speed, with a CP-style option for data that must never diverge.

🤯 Fun fact · a keynote that became a theorem

CAP began as a conjecture in Brewer's keynote at the 2000 ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), and that talk led to a formal proof. A slide in a keynote became one of the most cited, and most misquoted, ideas in distributed systems.

🌉 How it connects

Quorums (Lesson 2.3) are how you dial along this spectrum. Consensus protocols like Raft (Lesson 3.4) are how CP systems agree on one leader without split brain. Idempotency and retries (Lesson 3.3) make AP systems safe to retry. Chat (Lesson 7.4) needs causal ordering of messages; payments (Lesson 7.7) need strong consistency for the ledger. In AI systems, a vector index that lags the source documents by a few seconds is a classic eventual-consistency choice.

Check yourself

1. Replicas sit in Frankfurt, Virginia and Oregon. A Frankfurt user writes with a majority quorum. Frankfurt–Virginia is about 90 ms round trip, Frankfurt–Oregon about 150 ms. Roughly how long does the write take?
A majority of 3 is 2: the local replica plus the nearest remote one, Virginia. So the write waits about one 90 ms round trip. Waiting for all three would cost the 150 ms one; eventual consistency would answer in ~2 ms.
2. During a partition between regions, which feature should refuse requests rather than risk inconsistency?
Two people could claim the same username on opposite sides of the partition, and there's no good merge afterwards. A like count that's off for a few minutes, or a slightly old feed, is harmless, so those should stay available.
3. A database is described as PA/EL. What does that mean?
PACELC: if Partition, choose A or C (here A); Else, choose Latency or Consistency (here L). Abadi gave Dynamo, Cassandra and Riak (default settings) as PA/EL examples.
📝 TL;DR
  • CAP: during a partition, refuse requests (C) or let copies diverge (A). Partitions are rare; choose per operation.
  • PACELC: otherwise, strong consistency costs a round trip to other replicas on every write (tens to hundreds of ms across regions).
  • Consistency ladder: linearizable → causal → session (read-your-writes) → eventual.
  • Strong for money, stock and uniqueness; eventual for counts, feeds and caches; plan how AP data merges (last-write-wins loses updates).
