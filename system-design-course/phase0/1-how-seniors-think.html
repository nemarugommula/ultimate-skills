Phase 0 · Lesson 1

What system design is (and how seniors think)

System design is choosing the shape of servers, databases and networks that meets a product's needs at a given scale. The code for a feature barely changes between 1,000 and 100 million users; the shape around it changes completely.

🎯 Hook

On February 9th, 2016, Stack Overflow's load balancers handled 209 million HTTP requests. That's one of the busiest sites on the web. It ran on 9 primary web servers, and a single application served every Q&A site in the network.

Ask a junior engineer to “design Stack Overflow” and you often get forty boxes: microservices, Kafka, Kubernetes, three kinds of database. Ask a senior and the first answer is a question: “How many requests per second, and how many of them are writes?” This lesson is about that habit.

Numbers first: the same feature at three sizes

Take one feature: a comments box under news articles. Assume each user loads 10 pages a day, and 2% of users post one comment. Traffic isn't flat over a day, so we plan for a busy-hour peak of about 3× the average (a common rule of thumb; Lesson 0.3 shows real spikes can be far bigger).

A DAU is a daily active user. QPS means queries (requests) per second: how hard a component is being hit.

Daily active usersPeak readsPeak writesWhat it needs
1,0000.35 QPS~0.001 QPSOne small server + one database. Anything works.
1,000,000347 QPS0.7 QPSStill one database. Two app servers behind a load balancer so one can fail.
100,000,00034,722 QPS69 QPSA cache and a CDN (content delivery network: copies of files in cities near users) for reads, several app servers, read copies of the database. Writes are still small.

Look at the last row. Reads grew 100,000×, writes stayed tiny. So the design problem at 100 M users is a read problem, and the fix is copies of data (caches, replicas), not a fancy write pipeline. You only learn that by doing the arithmetic.

peak QPS ≈ DAU × requests per user per day ÷ 86,400 × peak factor “Users times requests each, divided by the seconds in a day, times how much busier the busiest hour is.” 86,400 is the number of seconds in a day.
💡 Big idea System design is mostly about non-functional requirements: how many, how fast, how reliable, how fresh, how cheap. Write those down with numbers first. The architecture then falls out of them, and every box you draw should point back to a number that needed it.

Two kinds of requirements

Functional requirements say what the system does. Non-functional requirements say how well it must do it. Interviews and design reviews are won on the second list.

Functional (what)Non-functional (how well)
Users can post a commentScale: 100 M daily users, 35 k reads/s at peak
Users see comments, newest firstLatency: p99 under 200 ms. p99 means 99 of every 100 requests are faster than this; the slowest 1% is where users get annoyed.
Users can like a commentAvailability: 99.9% of the time it works (about 8.8 hours of downtime a year; Lesson 0.5)
Moderators can delete a commentConsistency: how fresh must data be? Seeing a like count that's 5 seconds old is fine. Seeing a deleted comment 5 minutes later is not.
Durability, cost, security: never lose a posted comment; stay under a budget; block spam
🧠 Analogy: the menu vs the kitchen

A restaurant's menu is its functional requirements: burgers, fries, shakes. A food truck and a stadium kitchen can serve the exact same menu. What differs is everything else: 50 meals a day or 50,000, a 5-minute wait or a 30-second one, open on match days only or every day of the year, and what happens when the fryer breaks. Those are the non-functional requirements, and they decide whether you build a truck or a stadium kitchen. Nobody builds the stadium kitchen to serve 50 meals.

The questions seniors ask first

Every one of these questions changes the design. That's what “it depends” means: it depends on these answers.

QuestionWhy the answer changes the design
How many users, and how many requests each per day?Sets QPS: one server, ten, or ten thousand (Lesson 0.3).
What's the read : write ratio?Read-heavy → copies of data (cache, CDN, replicas). Write-heavy → queues, partitioning the data (sharding, Lesson 2.4).
How fresh must reads be?“A few seconds stale is OK” unlocks caches and replicas. “Must be exact” (money, stock levels) forbids them on that path.
How fast? (p99 target)Under 100 ms for users worldwide means servers near users. Seconds OK means you can do work later in the background.
Where are the users?One country → one region. Worldwide → CDNs, maybe several regions, and the speed of light starts to matter (Lesson 0.2).
How bad is downtime?A blog can be down an hour. A payment system can't. Each extra “nine” costs real money (Lesson 0.5).
How much data, for how long?Gigabytes fit on one machine. Petabytes need object storage and planning (Lesson 2.7).
What's the team and budget?Three engineers can't run 40 services. The best design is one your team can operate at 3 a.m.

✏️ Worked example: from vague prompt to first shape

Prompt: comments for a news site with 5 million daily users. Each views 10 articles a day; 2% post a comment.

  • Reads: 5,000,000 × 10 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 579 per second on average. Peak ×3 ≈ 1,736 per second.
  • Writes: 5,000,000 × 2% ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1.2 per second. That's nothing for any database.
  • Ratio: about 500 reads per write. Read-heavy, and a few seconds of staleness is fine for comments.
  • First shape: two app servers behind a load balancer (so one can die), one Postgres database, and a cache for “comments of article X”. That's it. No queue, no sharding, no microservices.
  • What would change it: a viral article getting 50,000 reads/s (one hot cache key, Lesson 1.3), or a new requirement like live-updating comments (WebSockets, Lesson 0.4).

    “It depends”, made concrete

    “It depends” is only a senior answer if you say on what and what you'd pick for each case. Almost every design choice trades one of four things against the others:

    ChoiceYou gainYou pay
    Add a cacheLower latency, less database loadStale data, one more system to run
    Add database read copies (replicas)More read capacity, survives one database failingCopies lag behind by a moment; more cost
    Run in a second regionSurvives a whole data center failing; faster for far usersAbout double the cost, much harder to keep data consistent
    Wait for every copy before confirming a writeEveryone always sees the latest dataSlower writes (one round trip to the farthest copy)
    Split into microservicesTeams deploy independentlyNetwork hops, more ways to fail, lots of operations work (Lesson 4.1)

    🧰 Tools you'll actually use

    Senior engineers design in writing before they build. The everyday tools: a design doc (Google Docs, Notion, Confluence or Markdown in the repo), a diagram tool (Excalidraw, draw.io, or diagrams-as-code with Mermaid, which GitHub renders inside Markdown), and a spreadsheet or notebook for the numbers. A skeleton that follows the structure Google engineers describe:

    # Design: Article comments
## Context and scope     what exists today, what we're building
## Goals and non-goals    goals: 5M DAU, p99 < 200 ms, 99.9%
                           non-goals: live updates (later), threads deeper than 2
## Numbers                reads ~1.7k/s peak, writes ~1.2/s, 30 GB/yr
## The design             diagram + APIs + data model
## Alternatives considered NoSQL store (rejected: writes are tiny), ...
## Cross-cutting concerns  security, privacy, monitoring, cost

    ⚖️ Trade-offs: how much design up front?
    ✅ Design carefully up front when…❌ Keep it light when…
    The decision is hard to undo (database choice, data model, public API shape)The decision is easy to change later (which cache library, how many servers)
    Many teams depend on it, or money and data are at stakeYou're still finding out whether anyone wants the product
    The numbers say you're near a limit (one database at 70% CPU)The numbers say you're 100× below any limit. Build the simple thing.

    The classic mistake both ways: over-engineering for scale you don't have (forty services for 1,000 users), and ignoring a number that's about to bite (a table growing 10 GB a day on a 500 GB disk).

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Founding engineer (0 → 1)

    The biggest risk is building something nobody wants, not traffic. Pick boring, managed tools (one Postgres, one app, a managed host), write a one-page design note for anything hard to undo, and do the scale arithmetic so you know roughly when you'll need to change things.

    Big tech (1 → 100)

    Every design goes through a written doc and a review with other senior engineers. Requirements come with SLOs (Lesson 0.5), capacity plans (Lesson 6.5) and a list of teams that depend on you. The skill is aligning many people on one trade-off, and writing down why (Lesson 8.1).

    🎤 Interview angle

    The first five minutes decide most system design interviews. What a senior answer covers:

    • Restates the problem and splits functional from non-functional requirements, and agrees which features are out of scope.
    • Asks for, or proposes, numbers: users, read : write ratio, data size, latency and availability targets. Then does the QPS math out loud.
    • Starts with the simplest shape that works, then scales the part the numbers say will break.
    • Names trade-offs unprompted: “I'm choosing X, which costs Y; if Z changed I'd switch.”

    Common follow-ups: “What if traffic grows 10×?” “What breaks first?” “What if this must work across regions?” “What would you build first with two engineers?”

    🏭 In the real world
    Design docs with goals and non-goals 🏢 Google Design Docs at Google (Malte Ubl)

    How: engineers write relatively informal documents before building. A common structure: context and scope, goals and non-goals, the design, alternatives considered, and cross-cutting concerns like security and privacy.

    Why this method: writing down the trade-offs early is cheap; discovering them after the code exists is expensive. The doc also becomes the memory of why a decision was made.

    Non-functional requirements as pillars 🏭 AWS AWS Well-Architected: the six pillars

    How: AWS reviews architectures against six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability. Functional requirements are treated as a separate concern.

    Why this method: a checklist of “how well” questions stops teams from shipping something that works on day one but can't be run, secured or afforded.

    A modular monolith instead of microservices 🏢 Shopify · 2019 Deconstructing the Monolith

    How: Shopify kept all of its code in one codebase but defined and enforced boundaries between components, rather than splitting into microservices.

    Why this method: their own experience told them there's no one-size-fits-all architecture and microservices bring their own problems. The right design depends on the phase of growth: “it depends”, applied.

    A few servers for a huge site 🏢 Stack Overflow · 2016 Stack Overflow: The Architecture, 2016 Edition

    How: 209 million HTTP requests on the measured day, handled by 9 primary web servers running one multi-tenant application, plus a handful of SQL Server, Redis and Elasticsearch machines.

    Why this method: a simple, well-tuned shape with heavy caching was enough for their numbers, and far cheaper to run than a sprawling one.

    🤯 Fun fact · more reliable isn't always better

    Google's SRE book argues that past a point, extra reliability makes a service worse: it slows down features and raises cost, and users can't tell. Its example: a user on a 99% reliable smartphone can't tell the difference between a 99.99% and a 99.999% reliable service. Requirements are about what users actually notice.

    Check yourself

    1. A to-do app has 2 million daily users. Each makes 30 requests a day; plan for a peak of 3× the average. Roughly what peak QPS do you design for?
    2,000,000 × 30 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 694 per second on average; × 3 ≈ 2,083 QPS at peak. That's a few app servers and one database: no exotic infrastructure needed.
    2. Which of these is a non-functional requirement?
    The other two describe what the system does. A p99 latency target describes how well it must do it, and it's the kind of requirement that changes the architecture (caches, CDNs, servers near users).
    3. A startup with 3 engineers expects 5,000 daily users at launch. One engineer proposes 12 microservices on Kubernetes “so we're ready to scale”. What's the senior response?
    5,000 users is well under 1 request per second on average. The real risks are speed of building and operating load. Keep clear module boundaries (like Shopify's modular monolith) so splitting later is possible if the numbers ever demand it.
    📝 TL;DR
    • System design = picking the shape (servers, data, network) that meets the non-functional requirements: scale, latency, availability, freshness, cost.
    • Numbers first: peak QPS ≈ DAU × requests/user ÷ 86,400 × peak factor. Split reads from writes.
    • “It depends” is only senior when you say on what, and what you'd choose in each case.
    • Start with the simplest shape; scale only the part the numbers say will break; name what each choice costs.
