Availability, SLIs, SLOs, SLAs & the nines

“Highly available” only means something with a number attached: what share of requests succeed, measured how, promised to whom. Those numbers decide how much redundancy you build, how fast you can ship, and what an outage costs.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Non-functional requirements: availability is one of them, next to scale and latency (Lesson 0.1).
  • Handy time facts: a year ≈ 525,600 minutes; a 30-day month = 43,200 minutes (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

At 9:37 AM PST on February 28th, 2017, an engineer on Amazon S3 (Simple Storage Service, AWS's file storage) ran an established playbook command to remove a few servers from a billing subsystem in AWS's Northern Virginia region. One input was typed wrong, and a much larger set of servers was removed, including the ones that track where every S3 object lives. S3 in that region couldn't serve requests until the subsystems restarted; new EC2 server launches, EBS disks and Lambda functions were hit too. Full recovery: 1:54 PM, about 4 hours 17 minutes later.

For a service promising 99.99% a year, the whole year's allowance is 52.6 minutes. One typo spent nearly five years of it. Even AWS's own status dashboard couldn't be updated at first: its admin console depended on S3.

Numbers first: what each “nine” allows

Availability is the share of time (or of requests) the service works. Each extra nine cuts allowed downtime by 10×:

AvailabilityDowntime per yearPer 30-day monthPer dayTypically means…
99% (“two nines”)3.65 days7.2 hours14.4 minInternal tools, batch jobs
99.9% (“three nines”)8.76 hours43.2 min1.44 minMost software-as-a-service products and APIs
99.95%4.38 hours21.6 min43.2 sImportant user-facing services
99.99% (“four nines”)52.6 min4.32 min8.64 sCore cloud services, payments; needs automatic failover
99.999% (“five nines”)5.26 min25.9 s0.87 sRare and very expensive; no human can react that fast

At four nines, a monthly outage can last 4 minutes. That's less time than it takes to get paged, open a laptop and log in. So four nines isn't something you achieve by working hard during incidents; it has to be designed in: redundancy, automatic failover, and safe deploys.

💡 Big idea Availability is a budget, not a virtue. Pick the target users actually need, measure it honestly, and spend the allowed failures on shipping faster. 100% is the wrong goal: each extra nine costs roughly 10× more effort, and past a point users can't tell the difference.
🧠 Analogy: old Christmas lights

Old-style Christmas lights were wired in series: one dead bulb and the whole string goes dark. A request that must pass through a load balancer, an app, a cache and a database is a string of bulbs in series: every one must work. A car's spare tyre is the opposite, parallel: you're only stuck if the tyre and the spare both fail. Good designs keep the series string short and put spares next to every bulb that matters.

SLI, SLO, SLA: measure, target, promise

TermWhat it isExample
SLI (service level indicator)A measured number about the service% of HTTP requests that returned non-5xx in under 300 ms
SLO (service level objective)Your internal target for an SLI, over a window99.9% of requests good, over a rolling 30 days
SLA (service level agreement)A contract with customers, with consequences if missedBelow 99.9% in a month → 10% refund as service credits

The SLA is usually looser than the SLO, so you get warned (SLO missed) before you owe money (SLA missed). Good SLIs measure what users feel: successful requests and latency, ideally from the load balancer or the client, not “the server process is running”.

The error budget

The error budget is 1 − SLO: the failures you're allowed. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows 0.1% bad requests, the equivalent of 43.2 minutes of full outage. At 1,000 requests/s that's about 2.6 million failed requests a month. While budget remains, teams ship freely; when it's spent, they slow down and fix reliability. It turns “should we ship?” from an argument into a number.

Chains and spares: the two formulas

in series: Atotal = A × B   ·   in parallel: Atotal = 1 − (1 − A) × (1 − A′) “When a request needs every part, multiply their availabilities: the whole is worse than the weakest part. When any one copy is enough, multiply the chances they're all down at once, and subtract from one.”

Two 99.9% parts in a chain: 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.8%, worse than either. Two 99.9% copies where either can serve: 1 − 0.001 × 0.001 = 99.9999%. The catch: the parallel formula assumes the copies fail independently. Two servers in the same rack, the same availability zone (one data center within a cloud region) or running the same bad deploy fail together, and then the spare is worth much less.

MTBF and MTTR: fail less, or recover faster

MTBF is the mean time between failures; MTTR is the mean time to recovery. Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR). A service that fails once a month (720 hours) and takes 1 hour to fix is 720 ÷ 721 = 99.86%. Cut recovery to 30 minutes and it's 99.93%, without preventing a single failure. Fast rollbacks and automatic failover are often the cheapest nines.

✏️ Worked example: the checkout path

Checkout needs a load balancer (99.99%), an app server (99.95%), the database (99.9%) and a third-party payment API (99.9%), all in series.

  • As drawn: 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.74% → about 22.8 hours of downtime a year.
  • Two app servers: the app becomes 1 − 0.0005² ≈ 99.99998%, total 99.79% → 18.4 hours.
  • Plus a database standby that takes over (1 − 0.001² = 99.9999%): total 99.89% → 9.6 hours.
  • Plus “degrade gracefully” on the payment API: if it's down, accept the order and charge it a little later, so the API leaves the critical chain: total 99.99% → about 53 minutes a year.

The last step gained the most, and it wasn't a server: it was a design decision to stop depending on something you don't control.

The same zone outage is a shrug at 99% (2% of the budget), a quarter of the month at 99.9%, and more than the whole month's budget at 99.99%. Burn rate is how fast you're spending: 1× means you'd use exactly the budget over the window; a full outage at a 99.9% SLO burns at 1,000×, spending a month's budget in 43 minutes. Alerting on burn rate is how SRE teams page on what matters (Lesson 6.4).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

SLIs come from metrics: Prometheus + Grafana (open source), or Datadog, Google Cloud Monitoring and AWS CloudWatch SLOs. Tools like Sloth and Pyrra generate SLO dashboards and burn-rate alerts for Prometheus. External uptime checks (UptimeRobot, Pingdom, Checkly) test from outside, the way users see you.

(1 - sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code!~"5.."}[1h]))
     / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))) / (1 - 0.999)

⚖️ Trade-offs: how many nines?
TargetWhat it takesCost
99%–99.9%Two app servers, managed database with backups, an on-call person, rollback buttonLow: what any decent setup gives you
99.95%–99.99%Redundancy in every layer across availability zones, automatic failover, canary deploys (Lesson 6.2), graceful degradation of non-critical dependenciesRoughly 2× the infrastructure and much more engineering
99.999%Multiple regions active at once, no single dependency, heavy automation, constant failure testingVery high, and it slows every change

Remember the chain: you can't be more available than the things you depend on in series. Promising 99.99% on top of a provider's 99.9% only works if that provider isn't on the critical path.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Aim for about 99.9%, honestly measured with an external uptime check. Get there with managed services, two app instances, automatic database backups you've actually restored, and one-click rollback. Don't promise customers an SLA you can't measure.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Every service has written SLOs and an error budget policy; dependencies are graded by criticality; teams run multi-zone by default and multi-region where needed; SLAs are contracts with credits. Reliability work is prioritised by budget burn, not by who complains loudest (Lesson 8.4).

🎤 Interview angle

When you say “highly available”, expect “how available?”. A senior answer:

  • Proposes a number and translates it into downtime (“99.95% is about 22 minutes a month”).
  • Defines the SLI: which requests count as good, measured where.
  • Walks the critical path, multiplies availabilities, and removes single points of failure with redundancy across zones.
  • Makes non-critical dependencies soft and mentions MTTR: fast detection, rollback and failover.

Common follow-ups: “What happens if this region goes down?” “Your database has 99.95%; how do you offer 99.99%?” “How would you alert on this SLO?”

🏭 In the real world
Error budgets 🏢 Google SRE SRE book: Embracing Risk

How: product and SRE teams agree a quarterly error budget from the SLO; monitoring measures actual uptime, and releases continue while budget remains. Their example: with a 99.999% SLO, a problem that fails 0.0002% of queries spends 20% of the quarter's budget.

Why this method: it removes the politics between “ship faster” and “be reliable”: both sides watch the same number.

An error budget policy 🏢 Google SRE SRE workbook: Example Error Budget Policy

How: if the service has used up its error budget, the team halts changes and releases other than urgent (P0) issues and security fixes until it's back within its SLO.

Why this method: the policy is written and agreed in advance, so a freeze is a rule everyone signed up for, not a fight during a crisis.

An SLA with service credits 🏭 AWS Amazon EC2 Service Level Agreement

How: for EC2 instances spread across two or more availability zones in a region, AWS commits to 99.99% monthly uptime; below that, customers get credits of 10%, 30% or 100% depending on how far below.

Why this method: the promise depends on your design (multiple zones), which is exactly the parallel formula: one zone can fail, two rarely do at once.

A hidden serial dependency 🏢 AWS · 2017 Summary of the Amazon S3 Service Disruption

How: a mistyped input removed far more servers than intended; S3's index and placement subsystems needed a full restart. Services built on S3 in the region failed too, and the status dashboard couldn't be updated because its console depended on S3.

Why this method: AWS's fixes were classic availability moves: safeguards so capacity can't be removed too fast, splitting the index into smaller pieces to recover faster (lower MTTR), and running the dashboard console across multiple regions.

🤯 Fun fact · Google breaks its own lock service on purpose

Chubby, Google's distributed lock service, was so reliable that other teams started assuming it would never go down, and their services broke badly when it rarely did. So SRE made sure Chubby meets its SLO but doesn't greatly exceed it: if a quarter goes by without a real outage, they take it down deliberately, flushing out services that depend on it too much.

Check yourself

1. Your SLO is 99.95% over a 30-day month. How much full outage can you afford in a month?
43,200 minutes × 0.0005 = 21.6 minutes. Four minutes is 99.99%; about 4.4 hours is 99.95% over a whole year.
2. A request passes through three services in series, each 99.9% available. What's the availability of the whole request?
0.999³ ≈ 0.997, about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 8.8. Chains are worse than their weakest link; 99.9999% would be three parallel copies (but you'd need 1 − 0.001³ = 99.9999999% for that).
3. A product page calls a recommendations service that's only 99.5% available. The page must be 99.95%. What's the best design?
In series, the page can't beat 99.5%. Taking a non-essential dependency off the critical path (timeout + fallback) removes it from the multiplication. Endless retries make outages worse by piling more load on a struggling service (Lesson 4.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Nines: 99.9% ≈ 8.8 h/year (43 min/month); 99.99% ≈ 53 min/year (4.3 min/month).
  • SLI = what you measure; SLO = your target; SLA = the contract. Error budget = 1 − SLO, spent on shipping.
  • Series: multiply (A × B). Parallel: 1 − (1 − A)(1 − B), only if failures are independent.
  • Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR): faster recovery and soft dependencies are often the cheapest nines.
