Phase 8 · Lesson 3

The founding-engineer playbook: 0 → 1 → 100

How to build a product that can grow to millions of users without building for millions on day one: start boring, measure everything, and add each component only when a signal tells you to.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Peak QPS (queries per second) from users × requests per day ÷ 86,400 × a peak factor (Lesson 0.3).
  • The scaling toolbox: stateless servers behind a load balancer (1.1, 1.2), caches (1.3), indexes (2.2), read replicas (2.3), queues (3.1).
  • Innovation tokens: spend new technology only on your differentiator (Lesson 8.2).
🎯 Hook

In December 2011, Instagram described its whole stack in a blog post. It had grown to 14 million+ users in a little over a year, and the post said: “We've only got 3 engineers.”

Their first principles were plain: keep it very simple, don't reinvent the wheel, use proven technologies. The stack was Django app servers, PostgreSQL, Redis, Amazon S3 with a CDN, and a task queue, all on Amazon's cloud. No exotic database, no custom infrastructure. That's the founding-engineer playbook in one company.

Numbers first: you need much less than you think

Say your app reaches 1 million registered users. Typical consumer numbers: about 20% use it on a given day, and each active user makes about 50 requests:

  • Daily active users: 0.2 × 1,000,000 = 200,000
  • Requests per day: 200,000 × 50 = 10 million → 10,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116 per second on average
  • Peak (about 3× average): ~350 requests per second

A single well-indexed Postgres server typically handles thousands of simple queries per second, and a couple of app servers handle a few hundred requests per second. So at 1 million users, a well-built product can still run on two app servers and one database. Even at 10 million users the same math gives about 3,500 requests per second at peak: real work, but not Google.

peak QPS ≈ users × daily-active share × requests per active user ÷ 86,400 × peak factor “Peak requests per second is roughly your users, times the share active each day, times how many requests each one makes, divided by the seconds in a day, times how much busier the peak is than the average.”
💡 Big idea Start with the simplest architecture that works, instrument it from day one, and add each new component only when a measured signal says so. Every component you add early costs money, on-call time and speed, and buys nothing until the signal appears.
🧠 Analogy: the food truck

You don't build a 200-seat restaurant before anyone has tasted your food. You start with a food truck: one cook, a short menu, a cash box. When the line goes around the block, you hire a second cook (another app server). When the fridge is too far from the stove, you put the popular ingredients on the counter (a cache). You only build the restaurant once you know people want the food, and you always keep the health inspector happy (backups and monitoring).

Do things that don't scale

Paul Graham's 2013 essay of that name is about founders recruiting users by hand: the Stripe founders didn't send a sign-up link, they asked for your laptop and installed it on the spot. The engineering version is the same mindset: run the monthly invoice job by hand from a script, answer support from a SQL console, build a simple admin page instead of a workflow engine. Automate a task when doing it by hand starts to hurt, and by then you'll know exactly what to automate.

✏️ Worked example: reading the signals at 300,000 users

Your dashboard shows the database at 90% CPU at peak, and pages are slowing down. Before buying anything, do the math:

  • Peak requests: 300,000 × 0.2 × 50 ÷ 86,400 × 3 ≈ 104 per second, about 2 queries each ≈ 210 queries per second.
  • A healthy Postgres handles thousands of indexed queries per second. 210 should be nothing. So the database isn't too small: some queries are doing too much work.
  • The slow-query log shows SELECT … FROM orders WHERE user_id = $1 scanning all 3 million rows each time. One CREATE INDEX turns each of those scans into a few page reads (Lesson 2.2).

Result: CPU drops from 90% to under 10%, for $0. Replicas or a cache would have cost money every month and hidden the real problem. Measure, then fix the cheapest cause.

Observability and backups from day one

Two things are not “scale problems” and can't wait: seeing what's happening (error tracking, request rate, error rate, latency, Lesson 6.3) and recovering from mistakes (automated backups that you have actually restored). One fat-fingered DELETE at 10 users is a bad day; at 100,000 users without a working backup, it can end the company. Lesson 8.4 tells the story of a well-known company whose backups turned out to be empty on the day it needed them.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A PaaS (Render, Fly.io, Heroku) or managed containers (AWS ECS/Fargate, Google Cloud Run); managed Postgres (RDS, Cloud SQL, Neon, Supabase); a job queue (Sidekiq, Celery, BullMQ); error tracking (Sentry); uptime checks; and a metrics stack (Lesson 6.3). Your whole day-one stack, locally:

# docker-compose.yml: app + Postgres + Redis (for the job queue)
services:
  app:
    build: .
    ports: ["8000:8000"]
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://app:app@db:5432/app
      REDIS_URL: redis://redis:6379/0
    depends_on: [db, redis]
  worker:                       # same code, runs background jobs
    build: .
    command: "celery -A app worker"
    depends_on: [db, redis]
  db:
    image: postgres:16
    environment: { POSTGRES_USER: app, POSTGRES_PASSWORD: app, POSTGRES_DB: app }
  redis:
    image: redis:7
# restore-drill.sh: run monthly. A backup you haven't restored is a hope, not a backup.
pg_dump -Fc "$PROD_URL" -f nightly.dump             # or use your provider's snapshot
createdb drill_$(date +%Y%m%d)
pg_restore -d drill_$(date +%Y%m%d) nightly.dump
psql -d drill_$(date +%Y%m%d) -c "SELECT count(*) FROM users;"  # sanity check: is the data really there?

Try it locally: docker compose up with any small app, then run the drill against the local database. Time how long the restore takes: that's your recovery time (Lesson 8.4).

⚖️ Trade-offs
✅ Stay simple when…⚠️ Build ahead when…
You don't yet know what users want (most startups)A launch with a known huge audience is scheduled (a TV spot, a big partner)
Your metrics show headroom (under ~70% at peak)The change is a one-way door that's cheap now and brutal later: IDs, the tenant key in every table, data residency
The fix can be added in days when the signal appearsLead time is long: a sharding migration or a new region takes months

The trap on the other side: “we'll fix it later” for things that are cheap only now. Use UUIDs or another non-sequential ID scheme from day one, put a tenant or customer ID in every multi-tenant table, and keep modules separate in your monolith. Those cost hours now and months later.

🚀 Founding engineer vs joining at scale

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

Your job is speed with a safety net. One monolith, one database, bought commodities, tested backups, error tracking. Keep a written list of the shortcuts you took and the signal that will force each fix. Hire for people who like shipping.

Joining at 1 → 100

You'll inherit those shortcuts. Don't sneer at them: they're why the company survived. Map them, measure them, and fix the ones whose signals are firing, in the order of the table above. That's what a senior engineer at a growing company spends most of a year doing.

🎤 Interview angle

Startup interviews often ask: “You're our first engineer. Design the MVP for X; we launch in 6 weeks.” A strong answer:

  • Starts with the smallest architecture that meets the requirements, with rough peak numbers to prove it fits.
  • Says what you'd buy and what you'd deliberately not build yet.
  • Names the signals that would trigger each next step (“at 70% DB CPU at peak I'd look at slow queries, then a cache”).
  • Never skips backups, monitoring and security basics.

Big-tech interviews flip it: they give you 100M users. The same skill applies: show which parts of the simple design break first, and why.

🏭 In the real world
A boring stack for 14 million users 🏢 Instagram · 2011 What Powers Instagram

How: Django on 25+ stateless app servers, PostgreSQL in a primary–replica setup with frequent snapshot backups, Redis for feeds and sessions, photos in S3 behind CloudFront, about 200 Python workers on a Gearman task queue, and monitoring with Munin, Pingdom, PagerDuty and Sentry.

Why this method: with 3 engineers, proven technologies they didn't have to invent let them spend their time on the product.

Do things that don't scale 📜 Paul Graham · 2013 Do Things that Don't Scale

How: founders recruit early users by hand (Stripe installing on people's laptops, Airbnb going door to door in New York) and give them unusually attentive service.

Why this method: a startup has to get its engine started before it can grow on its own; the manual work teaches you what to automate.

From monolith to modular monolith 🏢 Shopify · 2019 Deconstructing the Monolith

How: one of the largest Ruby on Rails codebases, worked on by more than a thousand developers, kept in one codebase with enforced boundaries between components instead of being split into microservices.

Why this method: it increased modularity without adding deployment units or network calls between services.

Splitting too far, then merging back 🏢 Segment · 2018 Goodbye Microservices

How: the team consolidated over 140 services into a single service after 3 full-time engineers were spending most of their time just keeping the system alive.

Why this method: the operational overhead of many small services outweighed their benefits for that team and that problem.

🤯 Fun fact · the “Collison installation”

Y Combinator has a name for the Stripe founders' trick of saying “give me your laptop” instead of “we'll send you a link”: the Collison installation, after brothers Patrick and John Collison. It's in Paul Graham's essay, and it's the purest example of doing something that doesn't scale on purpose.

Check yourself

1. Your app has 500,000 users. About 20% are active daily, each makes 30 requests, and peak is 3× average. Roughly what peak load do you need to handle?
0.2 × 500,000 = 100,000 active users × 30 = 3 million requests a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 35 per second, × 3 ≈ 104 per second. One or two app servers and one database handle that comfortably.
2. Database CPU is at 85% at peak. Reads dominate, and one query accounts for 70% of database time. What's your first move?
Measure, then fix the cheapest cause. One query using 70% of the time is almost always a missing index or an N+1 pattern (Lesson 2.2). Replicas would cost money every month and multiply the waste; a migration is a one-way door aimed at the wrong problem.
3. A founder says: “We have a read replica in another zone, so we don't need backups.” What's wrong?
Replication protects against a machine dying, not against bad writes (Lesson 2.3). Backups are point-in-time copies you can go back to, and they only count once you've restored one (Lesson 8.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Do the math: 1M users is typically a few hundred requests per second at peak. Two app servers and one database go a long way.
  • Day one: modular monolith, managed Postgres, a job queue, object storage + CDN, bought commodities, CI/CD, monitoring and restored backups.
  • Add components on signals, cheapest fix first: indexes → cache → replicas → sharding; queue for slow work; modules before services.
  • Over-engineering is a failure too: it costs money, on-call hours and speed before it buys anything.
