Phase 8 · Lesson 5

The senior system design interview

How the interview is really scored, how senior and staff answers differ, how to spend your 45 minutes, and how to practise until it feels like a normal design conversation.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The design framework: requirements → estimates → API and data → high-level design → deep dives → trade-offs (Lesson 7.1).
  • Back-of-the-envelope math: 86,400 seconds a day; 1 million a day ≈ 12 per second (Lesson 0.3).
  • Trade-off language: reversibility, cost, “what would change my mind” (Lesson 8.2).
🎯 Hook

Amazon publishes its interview guide for mid-level engineers (SDE II). The loop is four 55-minute interviews, and you should expect at least one on software systems design. Its tips read like this lesson's table of contents: clarify vague requirements and treat your interviewer as a customer; compare trade-offs, pick one and justify it; cover operations and resilience, not just the happy path.

One line stands out: they're looking for “broad comprehension of system design and expertise in one area”. Breadth to sketch the whole thing, depth to prove you've built something real. That's the target.

Numbers first: where the 45 minutes go

Most system design interviews give you 45 to 60 minutes. Here's a typical budget for a 45-minute round (this course's advice; adjust to the interviewer):

PhaseMinutesWhat you produce
1. Requirements~5Users, core features, scale, what matters most (latency? consistency?), explicit non-goals
2. Estimates~4Peak QPS, storage per year, read:write ratio: 3–4 numbers that drive decisions
3. API + data model~53–5 endpoints, the main tables or keys
4. High-level design~9A diagram that handles every requirement end to end
5. Deep dives~15The 1–2 hardest parts, with options and trade-offs
6. Wrap-up~7Bottlenecks, failure modes, monitoring, what you'd do at 10×

Notice the biggest slice is the deep dive. Candidates who spend 25 minutes drawing boxes never get to the part that separates senior from mid-level: going deep on the hard problem.

💡 Big idea The interview isn't a quiz with one right architecture. It's a simulation of working with you on a design: does the interviewer leave thinking “I'd trust this person to own a system”? You show that by scoping a vague problem, quantifying it, making trade-offs out loud, and driving the conversation to what matters.
🧠 Analogy: the house tour with a new client

An architect meeting a client doesn't start by drawing walls. They ask who lives there, the budget, what they hate about their current house. Then a rough floor plan, then the tricky part (the staircase, the plumbing), and they explain why: “I put the kitchen here because you said you cook every night.” The client's questions aren't attacks; they're the job. Your interviewer is that client.

What changes with level

Every company has its own ladder, but the signals interviewers listen for follow a common shape. This is the course's summary:

SignalMid-levelSeniorStaff
ScopeSolves the stated problemScopes it: asks what matters, sets non-goalsReframes it: questions whether it's the right problem, proposes phases
NumbersSome, when promptedDrives decisions with estimatesAlso cost, team effort and risk
Trade-offsNames one optionCompares options against the requirements and picks oneAlso reversibility, migration paths, what would change the decision
DepthKnows the componentsGoes deep in 1–2 areas, explains failure modesDeep where it matters, and knows where depth doesn't matter
DrivingFollows the interviewerLeads the structure, checks inRuns it like a design review with a peer
OperationsRarely mentionedMonitoring, failure handling, rolloutSLOs, multi-team ownership, evolution over years

At staff level, the interview often feels less like answering and more like leading. Will Larson's Staff Engineer research describes several shapes of staff work (tech lead, architect, solver, right hand); behavioural rounds will probe which ones you've actually done.

Communication habits that score

  • Think out loud. Google's interview tips say interviewers look at your thought process more than the answer itself. Silence gives them nothing to score.
  • State assumptions and choices. Google also suggests telling the interviewer when you're making an assumption or going deep instead of broad, and why.
  • Write things down: requirements, numbers and the diagram where both of you can see them.
  • Check in at phase changes: “That's the high level. I'd like to go deep on fan-out next, unless you'd prefer storage?”
  • Treat hints as gifts. “What about a celebrity with 50 million followers?” means “go there now”.
  • Say what you don't know. Amazon's guide says that if you're asked about an unfamiliar topic, tell the interviewer and suggest exploring a familiar one instead.

✏️ Worked example: “Design the like button” (45 minutes, senior level)

Minutes 0–5, requirements. “Users like and unlike posts; everyone sees a like count; I see whether I liked it. Is it OK if counts lag by a few seconds? (Yes.) My own like must show immediately. 100 million daily active users. Non-goals: who-liked-this lists, notifications.”

Minutes 5–9, estimates. 100M DAU × 10 likes a day = 1 billion likes a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11,600 writes/s, peak ×3 ≈ 35,000/s. Each user views about 200 posts a day, each showing a count: 20 billion count reads a day ≈ 231,000 reads/s. “So reads beat writes 20 to 1, and writes alone are too much for one database primary. That decides two things: counts must be cached, and like records must be sharded.”

Minutes 9–14, API and data. POST /posts/{id}/like, DELETE /posts/{id}/like, counts returned with the feed. Table likes(post_id, user_id, created_at) with a unique key on (post_id, user_id), which makes a retried like harmless (idempotent, Lesson 3.3). Shard by post_id (Lesson 2.4).

Minutes 14–23, high level. API servers → write the like row → publish an event to a log (Lesson 3.2) → counter workers add up events per post and update counts in Redis; the feed reads counts from Redis (Lesson 1.3).

Minutes 23–38, deep dive: the hot post. “A celebrity post can get a million likes in a few minutes, so one Redis key becomes a hot key.” Options: split the counter into N sub-keys and sum on read; or batch increments in the workers (add 500 at once instead of 500 times). “I'd batch, because it also cuts write load, and accept a few seconds of lag, which we agreed is fine.”

Minutes 38–45, wrap-up. “If Redis loses a counter, rebuild it from the likes table. Monitor consumer lag and the error rate on the like endpoint. At 10× I'd look first at the likes table shards.”

Why this scores well: every component traces back to a number or a requirement, there's a real trade-off decision, and the deep dive went where the problem is actually hard.

    ⚖️ Trade-offs inside the interview
    TensionLean one way when…Lean the other when…
    Breadth vs depthEarly: cover every requirement end to end firstAfter minute ~20: go deep on the hardest part
    Standard design vs novel ideaDefault: use the well-known pattern and say soThe requirements genuinely break the standard pattern; explain why
    Leading vs followingYou have a plan: drive it, check in at phase changesThe interviewer steers: follow at once; they're telling you what they need to score
    Precision vs speed in mathRound hard: 86,400 ≈ 100,000 is fineA number decides a design choice: be accurate within 2×
    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Founding engineer (0 → 1)

    Startup loops are less standard: often a practical take-home or pairing session, a design of their actual product, and long conversations with founders. They listen for judgment under constraints: what you'd build in 6 weeks, what you'd buy, what you'd skip (Lesson 8.3). Ask them about their stack and their biggest technical risk.

    Big tech (1 → 100)

    Structured loops: several interviewers, each scoring specific competencies against a shared rubric, then a hiring committee or debrief. Google says it assesses every candidate with clear rubrics, the same for everyone in that role. Consistency matters: one weak round on structure can outweigh a brilliant deep dive.

    🎤 Interview angle: the questions behind the questions
    • “How would you scale this to 10×?” → Do you know what breaks first, and why?
    • “What if this component goes down?” → Have you thought about failure, or only the happy path?
    • “Why not use X?” → Can you compare options fairly, or only defend your own?
    • “How would you know it's working?” → Do you think in SLIs, SLOs and dashboards (Lessons 0.5, 6.3)?
    • “How would you roll this out?” → Have you shipped real systems safely (Lesson 6.2)?
    • Behavioural, for senior+: “Tell me about a design decision you got wrong.” Have the story ready, with what you changed afterwards.
    🏭 In the real world
    Published system design expectations 🏢 Amazon SDE II interview prep

    How: a loop of four 55-minute interviews with at least one systems design question; tips include clarifying requirements, treating the interviewer as a customer, comparing trade-offs and justifying a choice, and covering metrics, bottlenecks, failure points, redundancy and logs.

    Why this method: designing at Amazon's size and speed of change is part of the job, so the interview simulates it.

    Behavioural answers in STAR form 🏢 Amazon Leadership Principles

    How: the SDE II guide asks candidates to prepare examples tied to Amazon's Leadership Principles and suggests the STAR method (situation, task, action, result) to structure them.

    Why this method: specific past behaviour is easier to evaluate fairly than hypothetical answers.

    Thought process over the answer 🏢 Google Interview tips

    How: questions are designed to leave room for clarification; candidates are encouraged to say when they're making an assumption or going deep instead of broad, and why.

    Why this method: the interviewer is looking at how you think, which predicts how you'll work far better than a memorised answer.

    Structured interviewing with shared rubrics 🏢 Google How we hire

    How: every candidate is assessed with clear rubrics, the same for everyone being considered for a role; open-ended questions replaced brainteasers.

    Why this method: evaluating everyone on the same criteria makes results comparable and fairer.

    The shapes of staff-level work 📜 Will Larson Staff archetypes (StaffEng)

    How: describes four common staff-plus archetypes: the Tech Lead, the Architect, the Solver and the Right Hand, based on interviews with staff engineers.

    Why this method: “staff” means different jobs at different companies; knowing the archetypes helps you tell your stories in the shape the role needs.

    🤯 Fun fact · goodbye, golf balls in a school bus

    Google's hiring page answers the famous question directly: it stopped asking brainteasers years ago, because its own data showed they didn't predict how well someone would do on the job. Open-ended design questions, scored on clear rubrics, replaced them.

    Check yourself

    1. Mid-interview, you need write throughput: 50 million daily users each make about 20 writes a day. Roughly what average rate is that?
    50M × 20 = 1 billion writes a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11,600 per second, so about 35,000 at a 3× peak. Shortcut: 1 billion a day ≈ 12,000 per second. Then say what it decides: more than one database primary comfortably handles, so plan for sharding or batching.
    2. The interviewer asks you to go deep on Cassandra's compaction strategies, which you've never used. What's the best response?
    Amazon's guide recommends exactly this: tell the interviewer and suggest exploring a familiar topic instead. Honest limits plus real depth elsewhere beat a bluff that falls apart at the next follow-up.
    3. Which behaviour is the strongest staff-level signal?
    Staff engineers reframe the problem, think in phases and risks, and know which decisions deserve care (one-way doors, Lesson 8.2). Detail and internals knowledge matter, but they're senior-level table stakes.
    📝 TL;DR
    • Budget the time: ~5 requirements, ~4 estimates, ~5 API/data, ~9 high level, ~15 deep dive, ~7 wrap-up (for 45 minutes).
    • Senior = scope, numbers that decide things, real trade-offs, depth in 1–2 areas, operations. Staff adds reframing, phases, reversibility and leading the room.
    • Think out loud, state assumptions, check in, treat hints as gifts, and say what you don't know.
    • Practise with a timer and a rubric, record yourself, and prepare STAR stories for the behavioural rounds.
