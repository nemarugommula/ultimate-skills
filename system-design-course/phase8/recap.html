🧾 Phase 8 cheat sheet

📝

8.1 Design docs & ADRs

Write when cost < P(catch) × cost later. Context, goals & non-goals, design, alternatives, cross-cutting, rollout, open questions. ADRs: 1–2 pages, superseded, never edited away. Google Docs, adr-tools, MADR.

⚖️

8.2 Trade-offs & build vs buy

One-way doors: slow and written; two-way doors: decide today. ~3 innovation tokens, spent on your core. Debt interest = cost per change × change rate. Compare 3–5 year TCO. TCO sheet, debt register, tech radar.

🚀

8.3 Founding-engineer playbook

1M users ≈ a few hundred req/s at peak. Monolith + Postgres + queue + object storage + CDN, bought commodities, restored backups. Add on signals: indexes → cache → replicas → shards. PaaS, managed Postgres, Sentry.

🛡️

8.4 SRE culture

Budget = (1 − SLO) × window: 99.9%/30 days ≈ 43 min. Freeze when spent; page at 14.4× burn for 1 h. Toil < 50%. Chaos: steady state → hypothesis → inject → compare, tiny blast radius. Know RPO/RTO. Prometheus, Sloth, Chaos Mesh, FIS.

🎤

8.5 The interview

45 min ≈ 5 requirements · 4 estimates · 5 API/data · 9 high level · 15 deep dive · 7 wrap-up. Think out loud, state assumptions, compare and choose, cover failures. Staff = reframe, phase, reversibility. Excalidraw, timer, rubric.

🗣️ Teach-back: the staff-level question

“You've just joined as the first senior engineer at a 20-person startup. The product works, users are growing 15% a month, and the CTO asks: what should we change in our architecture this year, and what should we leave alone? Walk me through how you'd decide.”

A great answer measures first (peak load, DB CPU, error rate, deploy pain, cloud bill), maps each signal to the cheapest fix (Lesson 8.3), separates one-way from two-way doors (8.2), sets one SLO with an error-budget policy (8.4), writes the plan as a short design doc with ADRs (8.1), and says explicitly what it will not do yet, and which signal would change that.

🏗️ Design it yourself: the final problem

✏️ The problem

You're the founding engineer of TicketDrop, a startup selling concert tickets. Most days, 10,000 people browse. Next month, a huge artist goes on sale: about 500,000 fans will arrive in the first 10 minutes for 50,000 seats. A seat must never be sold twice, nobody may be charged twice, and the on-sale must keep a 99.9% success SLO even if a zone fails. You have 4 engineers and 4 weeks.

Design it: requirements, numbers, architecture, what you build vs buy, how you'll prove it works before the day, and what you'll write down.

Reveal a strong answer

Requirements. Functional: browse events, join the on-sale, hold seats for a few minutes, pay, get a ticket by email. Non-functional: no double-selling (correctness beats speed), no double charges, fair ordering, 99.9% SLO during the on-sale, survive a zone loss. Non-goals for v1: resale marketplace, seat maps with live animation, multiple currencies.

Numbers. 500,000 fans × ~20 requests in 10 minutes = 10 million requests ÷ 600 s ≈ 17,000 requests per second at the door, against a normal day's handful. But 50,000 seats ÷ 600 s ≈ only ~83 purchases per second. The trick is to absorb the crowd cheaply and let only a trickle reach the inventory.

Architecture.

  • CDN + virtual waiting room (Lessons 1.4, 1.5): static pages from the edge; fans get a signed queue token and are admitted at a controlled rate (say a few thousand per minute). Buy this or build the simplest version; it's the part that takes the 17,000 req/s.
  • The monolith (Lesson 8.3) with an inventory module, behind a load balancer across 3 zones with N+1 capacity (Lessons 1.2, 8.4).
  • Postgres as the source of truth for seats: a hold is a conditional update, UPDATE seats SET held_by = $user, held_until = now() + '5 min' WHERE id = $seat AND (held_by IS NULL OR held_until < now()); one row changes or none does, so a seat can't be held twice (Lesson 2.5). Expired holds free themselves.
  • Payments: buy (a payment provider), with an idempotency key per order so retries never double-charge (Lessons 3.3, 7.7, 8.2).
  • Queue + workers for ticket PDFs and emails (Lessons 3.1, 7.8), so purchases return fast.
  • Redis for queue positions and cached event pages, never for the final seat decision (Lesson 1.3).

Proving it before the day. A load test at 2× the expected peak (Lesson 6.5); a chaos experiment losing a zone and killing the DB primary (Lesson 8.4); a game day with the on-call team; an SLO with burn-rate alerts on the purchase path (Lessons 0.5, 6.3); a feature flag to close the waiting room's gate instantly (Lesson 6.2); a restore drill of the orders database.

What you write down. A design doc with alternatives (Lesson 8.1): waiting room vs autoscaling everything, Postgres conditional updates vs a Redis-based seat lock. ADRs for the one-way doors: the order ID and idempotency scheme, the seat-hold model.

Trade-offs. The waiting room trades a worse experience (waiting) for correctness and survival. Short holds free seats faster but frustrate slow payers. Admitting fans faster sells out sooner but risks overload; the admission rate is your dial.

Classic mistakes. Letting all 500,000 fans hit the database; deciding seat availability from a cache; retrying payments without idempotency keys; splitting into microservices four weeks before launch; never load-testing; no plan for the payment provider being slow.

🏆 Boss quiz: the whole course

1. A photo app has 20 million daily users, each uploading 5 photos of about 2 MB a day. Roughly how much new storage per day?
20M × 5 = 100 million photos × 2 MB = 200 million MB = 200 TB a day, about 73 PB a year before replication. That number decides the design: object storage with lifecycle tiers and a CDN (Lessons 0.3, 1.4, 2.7), never a database.
2. A service gets 50,000 reads per second. You add a cache with a 95% hit rate. How many reads per second still reach the database?
Database load = (1 − hit rate) × QPS = 0.05 × 50,000 = 2,500 (Lesson 1.3). The flip side: if the cache dies, the database suddenly sees 20× its normal load, which is why stampede protection and cold-start plans matter.
3. A mobile client times out on “Pay” and retries. Sometimes users get charged twice. What's the fix?
Networks fail, so retries are necessary; idempotency makes them safe (Lessons 3.3 and 7.7). Removing retries loses payments; longer timeouts just make the duplicates rarer and slower.
4. Your SLO is 99.95% over 30 days. One incident took the service fully down for 15 minutes. Roughly how much of the error budget is gone?
Budget = 0.05% × 43,200 min = 21.6 minutes; 15 ÷ 21.6 ≈ 69%. Under the example policy (Lesson 8.4) it's far above the 20% threshold, so it needs a postmortem with a top-priority action item, and the team should slow risky launches for the rest of the window.
5. Your B2B startup must choose between all customers in shared tables (with a tenant ID) and a separate database per customer. How should you decide?
Tenancy touches every table, query and migration, so changing it later means moving all customer data: a one-way door (Lesson 8.2). It deserves the written trade-offs (8.1), it can't be deferred (every table needs it on day one, Lesson 8.3), and the ADR tells future engineers why.

📚 Where to go next

📚 Books
  • Book Designing Data-Intensive Applications (Martin Kleppmann, O'Reilly). The deepest single book on the ideas behind Phases 2 and 3: storage, replication, partitioning, consistency, streams.
  • Book Google's SRE books: Site Reliability Engineering and The Site Reliability Workbook (free to read online), plus Building Secure & Reliable Systems and a new second edition. The source for Lesson 8.4.
  • Book Software Engineering at Google (Winters, Manshreck, Wright, 2020). How a huge engineering organisation keeps code healthy over decades.
  • Book Staff Engineer: Leadership beyond the management track (Will Larson). What the job after senior actually looks like, with many real stories on staffeng.com.
🎓 Courses & references
  • Course MIT 6.5840 Distributed Systems. Lectures, papers and labs where you build Raft and a sharded key-value store yourself (Lessons 3.4, 2.4).
  • Repo The System Design Primer. A huge, free index of topics and practice problems for interview prep.
  • Articles The Amazon Builders' Library. In-depth articles by Amazon's most senior engineers on how systems are designed, released and run at scale, including the trade-offs behind them.
🏭 Engineering blogs worth following
  • Blog Netflix Tech Blog: resilience, chaos engineering, streaming at scale.
  • Blog Cloudflare Blog: networking, the edge, DDoS, and detailed outage write-ups.
  • Blog Discord Engineering: real-time messaging and databases at huge scale.
  • Blog Shopify Engineering: one of the largest Rails monoliths, and how they keep it modular.
  • Blog Stripe Engineering and Engineering at Meta: payments correctness, and infrastructure at planet scale.
