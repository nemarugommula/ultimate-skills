Phase 8 · Lesson 1

Design docs, RFCs & decision records

Write the design down before you build it, so people can argue with a document instead of with production. At scale, writing is how one engineer's judgment reaches fifty teams.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The design framework: requirements → estimates → high-level design → deep dives → trade-offs (Lesson 7.1). A design doc is that framework, written down.
  • SLOs = the reliability targets you promise, like “99.9% of requests succeed” (Lesson 0.5).
  • Canary rollouts and feature flags let you ship a change to a small slice of users and undo it fast (Lesson 6.2).
🎯 Hook

Amazon doesn't use slide decks in meetings. In his 2017 shareholder letter, Jeff Bezos explained that teams write six-page narrative memos instead, and everyone reads one silently at the start of the meeting, “in a kind of study hall”.

He also wrote that a great memo probably takes a week or more: written, rewritten, shared with colleagues, set aside, edited again. One of the biggest companies on earth makes its decisions on documents. By the end of this lesson you'll know how to write one people actually want to read.

Numbers first: why writing is cheap

Imagine your team must choose a database for a new service. Here's what a design doc costs, with typical numbers:

Typical effortEngineer-days
Write the docabout 3 days for one engineer3
Review it6 reviewers × 2 hours each = 12 hours1.5
Total4.5

Now the cost of getting it wrong. Discovering after launch that the database can't handle your access pattern means a migration: typically 3 engineers for 10 weeks, so 3 × 10 × 5 = 150 engineer-days, plus the risk of the migration itself.

That's 33 times the cost of the doc. If writing it gives you even a 1-in-10 chance of catching that mistake, the expected saving is 0.1 × 150 = 15 engineer-days, more than three times what the doc cost.

worth writing if   cost of doc < P(catch a mistake) × cost of the mistake later “A design doc pays for itself when the chance it catches a mistake, times what that mistake would cost after launch, is bigger than the time spent writing and reviewing it.” That's why tiny, obvious changes don't need one, and big, hard-to-undo ones always do.
💡 Big idea A design doc is the cheapest place to be wrong. Its job isn't to describe code; it's to write down the trade-offs you made, so other people can find the holes before the code exists.
🧠 Analogy: the renovation plan

Moving a wall on the architect's drawing costs an eraser. Moving it after it's built costs a demolition crew. A design doc is the drawing: your neighbours (other teams), the electrician (security) and the building inspector (the SRE, or site reliability engineer, who'll be paged at night) all look at it while changes are still free.

What goes in a design doc

Every company has its own template, but the good ones share a shape. This is close to the one Google engineers describe publicly:

SectionWhat it answersCommon mistake
Context & scopeWhat exists today, what's broken, with numbers. Facts only.Selling the solution before explaining the problem.
Goals & non-goalsWhat success means, and what you're deliberately not doing.Non-goals that are just negated goals (“shouldn't crash”). A real non-goal is something that could reasonably be a goal, like “no video in v1”.
The designOverview diagram, then APIs, data model, key flows, capacity estimates.Pages of code. Show the shape and the decisions, not the implementation.
Alternatives consideredOther designs that could work, and why you didn't pick them.A “strawman” alternative nobody would choose. Reviewers notice.
Cross-cutting concernsSecurity, privacy, observability (Lesson 6.3), cost.Skipping it, then failing the security review a week before launch.
Risks, rollout & rollbackWhat could go wrong, how you ship gradually, how you undo it.“We'll deploy it” as the whole plan.
Open questionsThe decisions you need from reviewers.Hiding them, so the review argues about the wrong things.

Length: for a larger project, about 10–20 pages is the sweet spot in Google's experience. Beyond that, split the problem. For small changes, a 1–3 page “mini design doc” with the same sections works well.

The life of a design doc

  1. Draft and iterate with a few people who know the area best. Most big changes happen here.
  2. Review: share it widely. Lightweight version: send it to the team and wait for comments. Heavier version: a design review meeting with senior engineers.
  3. Implement, and update the doc when reality disagrees with it.
  4. Maintain: months later, it's how a new engineer learns why the system looks the way it does.

RFCs and ADRs: the doc's cousins

Sizea few to ~20 pagessimilar, often shorter1–2 pages
PurposeDescribe a system and its trade-offsPropose a change and collect comments from a wide audienceRecord one decision: context, decision, consequences
Lives inGoogle Docs, Notion, ConfluenceA shared folder, mailing list or GitHub repoThe code repository, next to the code (docs/adr/0007-….md)
LifetimeMostly read during the projectClosed with a decisionForever. Never edited away, only superseded by a newer ADR

“RFC” comes from the internet's standards documents, and many companies use the word for their internal proposal process. Open-source projects run theirs in public: Rust's RFCs, Python's PEPs (Python Enhancement Proposals) and Kubernetes' KEPs (Kubernetes Enhancement Proposals) are all readable on the web, and they're the best free examples of design writing you'll find.

✏️ Worked example: writing the goals section

Your app has 2 million users and you're adding push and email notifications. The goals section should carry numbers a reviewer can check:

  • Volume: about 10 million notifications per day. 10,000,000 ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 116 per second on average. Evenings are busier, so plan for about 3× that: ~350 per second at peak (Lesson 0.3).
  • Goal: 99% of notifications delivered to the provider within 30 seconds of the event.
  • Goal: users can turn off each notification type.
  • Non-goal: SMS in v1 (it's a reasonable goal, which is why it's worth saying no out loud).
  • Non-goal: exactly-once delivery. A rare duplicate is acceptable; we'll make sends idempotent (Lesson 3.3) to keep it rare.

A reviewer can now ask sharp questions: “350 per second is fine for one queue, but what about a breaking-news blast to all 2 million users at once?” That question, found in a doc, costs nothing. Found in production, it's an outage.

    Decision records: the memory of a codebase

    Design docs go stale. Two years later, a new engineer asks “why on earth do we use a queue here?” and nobody remembers. In 2011 Michael Nygard proposed a tiny fix: for every significant decision, write a short ADR with five parts:

    • Title: a short noun phrase, numbered. “ADR 9: LDAP for multitenant integration.”
    • Context: the forces at play (technical, political, social), stated as plain facts.
    • Decision: full sentences, active voice. “We will …”
    • Status: proposed, accepted, deprecated, or superseded (with a link to its replacement).
    • Consequences: all of them, good, bad and neutral.

    ADRs are numbered in order and numbers are never reused. If you reverse a decision, you don't delete the old ADR; you mark it superseded. The log becomes a history of how the system got its shape. Build one:

        🧰 Templates & tools you'll actually use

        Docs live in Google Docs, Notion or Confluence (comments are the point). ADRs live in the repo as Markdown, often with adr-tools (a small shell CLI) or the MADR template. A design-doc skeleton you can copy:

        # Design: <short name>            Author(s) · Reviewers · Status: Draft
## Context and scope        what exists, what hurts, with numbers
## Goals                    measurable: "p99 upload < 2 s at 50 uploads/s"
## Non-goals                reasonable things you are NOT doing
## Design                   diagram first, then API, data model, flows, estimates
## Alternatives considered  2+ real options and why not
## Cross-cutting concerns   security, privacy, observability, cost
## Risks and rollout        feature flag, canary %, rollback, migration plan
## Open questions           decisions you need from reviewers

        And an ADR (Nygard's format):

        # 7. Use PostgreSQL as the primary database
Status: Accepted (supersedes / superseded by …)
Context: forces in tension, as facts
Decision: We will …
Consequences: good, bad and neutral

        Try it locally: install adr-tools (it's in Homebrew), then adr init doc/architecture/decisions, adr new Use PostgreSQL, and later adr new -s 2 Use Aurora, which marks ADR 2 as superseded for you.

        ⚖️ Trade-offs: when to write, and how much
        ✅ Write a doc when…❌ Skip it (or write a one-pager) when…
        The decision is hard to undo (data model, public API, a new datastore)It's a two-way door you can reverse in a day (Lesson 8.2)
        Several teams are affected, or the work takes more than a few weeksOne person, a few days, an obvious solution
        There are real alternatives with different trade-offsThe doc would just be the implementation manual. Writing is overhead too
        You'll need security, privacy or SRE sign-off anywayA quick prototype would answer the question faster than prose

        The real risk is the opposite extreme: review processes so heavy that engineers avoid writing. Keep reviews time-boxed, and say up front which decisions you need.

        🚀 Startup vs big tech

        Founding engineer (0 → 1)

        No formal review board, but write a one-page doc for anything that touches the data model or money, and keep ADRs in the repo from day one. They cost minutes, and when engineer #10 joins they replace a week of “why is it like this?” questions.

        Big tech (1 → 100)

        Docs are how work gets funded and how seniority is judged. Expect templates with mandatory security, privacy and reliability sections, formal design reviews, and promotion packets that cite the docs you wrote. Writing clearly is a core senior skill there.

        🎤 Interview angle

        A system design interview is a design doc spoken out loud: requirements are the goals and non-goals, estimates are the numbers section, and “why not X?” is the alternatives section. Behavioural rounds for senior roles often ask about writing directly:

        • “Tell me about a design you wrote that got significant pushback. What changed?”
        • “How do you get a decision made when two senior engineers disagree?” (Write both options down with their trade-offs, agree on the criteria, then decide.)
        • “How do you make sure a decision is still understood a year later?” (ADRs, linked from the code.)
        🏭 In the real world
        Design docs as engineering culture 🏢 Google · 2020 Design Docs at Google (Malte Ubl)

        How: relatively informal docs, mostly in Google Docs, with context and scope, goals and non-goals, the design, alternatives considered and cross-cutting concerns such as security, privacy and observability. About 10–20 pages for larger projects, or a 1–3 page mini doc.

        Why this method: design issues are found while changes are still cheap, and the docs scale senior engineers' knowledge across the organisation.

        The original ADR 📜 History · 2011 Documenting Architecture Decisions (Michael Nygard)

        How: one or two pages per decision with title, context, decision, status and consequences, stored in the repository, numbered sequentially and never reused; reversed decisions are kept and marked superseded.

        Why this method: without the rationale, newcomers can only blindly accept or blindly change past decisions, and both are dangerous.

        RFCs from tens to thousands of engineers 🏢 Uber · 2018 Scaling Engineering Teams via RFCs (Gergely Orosz)

        How: engineers write planning docs before building and share them widely for anyone to comment on; the post describes the process growing from tens of engineers to a couple of thousand.

        Why this method: writing creates accountability and more thorough decisions, and it gives every team visibility into what others are building.

        Public RFCs with a final comment period 🧰 Open source The Rust RFC process

        How: “substantial” changes go through an RFC pull request; when the trade-offs have been discussed enough, a sub-team member proposes a final comment period with a disposition: merge, close or postpone.

        Why this method: a community of thousands can't decide in a meeting; a written proposal with a clear closing step lets everyone be heard and still reach a decision.

        Production-readiness built into the template 🧰 Open source Kubernetes KEP template

        How: every KEP has goals and non-goals, risks and mitigations, a test plan, graduation criteria (alpha, beta, GA) and a production readiness questionnaire: how to roll back, which metrics show it's working, what its SLOs are.

        Why this method: putting rollback and monitoring questions in the template means nobody can forget them, across hundreds of contributors.

        🤯 Fun fact · the ADR that documents itself

        Nygard's 2011 post is itself written as an ADR. Its headings are Context, Decision, Status (“Accepted”) and Consequences. The proposal for decision records is a decision record. It also contains the memorable aside “Bullets kill people, even PowerPoint bullets”, his argument for full sentences.

        Check yourself

        1. A design doc costs about 4 engineer-days to write and review. You estimate a 20% chance it catches a mistake that would cost 60 engineer-days to fix after launch. Is it worth writing?
        0.2 × 60 = 12 engineer-days expected saving against 4 spent. Docs win whenever cost < P(catch) × cost later. The less reversible the decision, the bigger the “cost later”, which is why big-tech templates insist on docs for data models and public APIs.
        2. You're writing the non-goals for a new photo-upload service. Which one is a useful non-goal?
        A good non-goal is something that could reasonably be a goal, so writing it down prevents a real argument later. “Should not lose photos” and “should not be slow” are just goals stated negatively.
        3. Two years ago, ADR 12 chose a self-hosted queue. You're now moving to a managed one. What do you do with ADR 12?
        ADRs are an append-only history. Keeping ADR 12 (marked superseded) preserves why the old choice made sense then, which helps the next person judge whether today's context really changed. Numbers are never reused.
        📝 TL;DR
        • A design doc is the cheapest place to be wrong: write one when cost < P(catch a mistake) × cost of the mistake later.
        • Sections: context, goals & non-goals, design, alternatives, cross-cutting concerns, risks & rollout, open questions. 10–20 pages max; often 1–3.
        • RFCs collect comments from a wide audience and end in a decision; ADRs record one decision in 1–2 pages, in the repo, superseded, never edited away.
        • Write numbers reviewers can check, real alternatives, and honest downsides.
