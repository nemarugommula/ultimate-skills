Phase 8 · Lesson 4

SRE culture: error budgets & chaos engineering

Site reliability engineering turns reliability from a feeling into a number you can spend: an error budget that decides when to ship and when to fix, plus deliberate failure practice so real failures are boring.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • SLI and SLO: an SLI (service level indicator) measures something users feel, like the share of successful requests; an SLO (objective) is the target for it, like 99.9% (Lesson 0.5).
  • Incidents and blameless postmortems (Lesson 6.4), and canary deploys that expose a change to a few users first (Lesson 6.2).
  • Availability zones: separate data centers within one cloud region (Lesson 5.5).
🎯 Hook

On 21 April 2011, a large Amazon Web Services outage in the US-East region took down or badly hampered a number of popular websites. Netflix ran in the same region. Its engineers wrote afterwards that the service “ran without intervention”, with higher error rates and latency than normal, and no noticeable increase in customer service calls.

Netflix had designed for failure: stateless services, data copied across zones, and Chaos Monkey, a service that deliberately kills its own production servers so teams get used to failure. They'd practised. This lesson is about building that culture.

Numbers first: what “99.9%” lets you spend

An SLO is also a budget. If 99.9% of minutes must be good, then 0.1% may be bad. Over a 30-day month (30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes):

SLOAllowed bad time per 30 daysThat's about…
99%432 minutes7.2 hours: a bad afternoon every month
99.5%216 minutes3.6 hours
99.9%43.2 minutesone slow rollback
99.95%21.6 minutesone quick incident
99.99%4.32 minutesbarely time to get paged and open a laptop

Partial outages count partially. A bad deploy that fails 5% of requests for 20 minutes costs 0.05 × 20 = 1 full-outage minute, or 1 ÷ 43.2 ≈ 2.3% of a 99.9% budget. With request counts it's the same idea: at 1 billion requests a month, 99.9% allows 1 million failed requests.

error budget = (1 − SLO) × window     burn rate = (budget used ÷ budget) ÷ (time passed ÷ window) “The error budget is the share of failures your SLO allows, over the window. The burn rate says how fast you're spending it: a burn rate of 1 uses exactly the whole budget by the end of the window; 2 runs out halfway.”
💡 Big idea 100% reliability is the wrong target: users can't tell the difference past a point, and chasing it stops all change. Pick an SLO users actually need, and treat the gap to 100% as a budget to spend on shipping. Budget left: ship faster. Budget gone: fix reliability first. The data decides, not the loudest person in the room.
🧠 Analogy: a monthly allowance

You get an allowance at the start of each month. Spend it on fun things (new features, risky launches) as you like. If it runs out on the 10th, there are no more treats until the next refill, and you'd better work out why it went so fast. Nobody yells at you; the rule was agreed in advance. That's an error-budget policy.

Error budgets in practice

Google's SRE book describes the loop: product and SRE teams agree on a quarterly error budget from the SLO. As long as measured reliability is above the SLO, new releases can be pushed. If the budget is spent, releases are temporarily halted while effort goes into testing and resilience. Even outages that aren't your fault (a network failure, a data center problem) eat the budget, which keeps everyone honest.

The SRE Workbook publishes an example error-budget policy you can copy. Its key rules:

  • If the service has exceeded its error budget over the preceding four weeks, halt all changes and releases except urgent (P0) fixes and security fixes, until it's back within its SLO.
  • If a single incident consumes more than 20% of the budget over four weeks, the team must write a postmortem with at least one top-priority action item.
  • It's explicitly not a punishment: it gives teams permission to focus on reliability when the data says it matters most.

Burn-rate alerts turn the budget into pages. The Workbook's recommended starting point for a 99.9% SLO: page if you burn 2% of the 30-day budget in 1 hour, a burn rate of 14.4 (because 0.02 × 720 hours = 14.4); page at 5% in 6 hours; open a ticket at 10% in 3 days.

✏️ Worked example: will we make it to the end of the month?

Day 10 of a 30-day window, and you've used 60% of the budget.

  • Time passed: 10 ÷ 30 = 33% of the window. Burn rate = 60% ÷ 33% = 1.8.
  • At this pace you've used 6% per day, so the budget runs out at 100 ÷ 6 ≈ day 16.7, two weeks early.
  • Under the policy: you aren't frozen yet, but the team should look at what's burning (one big incident? a flaky dependency? risky deploys?), slow down risky launches, and expect a freeze around day 17 if nothing changes.

Things to try: switch the policy to Ship no matter what: more deploys, but the budget goes deeply negative and you miss the SLO. Switch back: freezes appear, and during them the team fixes its process (failure rate and rollback time improve). Now try 99.99%: with this process you're frozen most of the quarter. Either the SLO is stricter than users need, or you must invest: turn on canary deploys and watch velocity come back while the budget stays healthy. That's the real lesson: safer deploys buy speed.

Toil: the work that eats SRE teams

Google defines toil as operational work that's manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, has no enduring value, and grows linearly with the service. Restarting a stuck job by hand every morning is toil. Writing the fix so it never gets stuck is engineering.

Google's SRE organisation has an advertised goal of keeping toil below 50% of each SRE's time, because toil left unchecked expands to fill everyone's week. The practical habit: track toil like a bug (“restarted the importer: 20 min, 4th time this month”) and automate the top item every sprint.

Chaos engineering: break it on purpose

Your system will lose a server, a zone, a dependency. The only question is whether you find out how it reacts during a calm Tuesday afternoon or at 3 a.m. during Black Friday. The Principles of Chaos Engineering describe the method:

1 · Steady stateDefine a measurable output that means “normal”: e.g. 99.9% of requests succeed.
2 · HypothesisPredict that steady state will hold in both a control group and an experiment group.
3 · Inject real failuresServers crashing, disks failing, network links cut, latency added.
4 · Try to disprove itCompare the groups. A difference is a weakness to fix before it finds you.

Advanced principles: run in production when you can, automate experiments to run continuously, and minimise the blast radius: start with a tiny slice of traffic and a big red abort button.

Game days and disaster recovery

A game day (AWS's term) simulates a failure or event to test systems, processes and people: the team actually performs the actions it would take in a real emergency. Run one before every big launch.

Disaster recovery has two numbers. RTO (recovery time objective) is the longest acceptable time between the interruption and service being restored. RPO (recovery point objective) is the longest acceptable time since the last recovery point: how much recent data you can afford to lose. AWS groups strategies from cheapest to most expensive:

StrategyWhat's running in the backup regionRecoveryCost
Backup & restoreOnly backupsslowest: rebuild everythinglowest
Pilot lightData replicated; core pieces ready but switched offfaster: turn things on, scale uplow
Warm standbyA smaller full copy, already runningfast: scale it upmedium
Multi-site active/activeFull copies serving users everywherenear zero for most disastershighest, most complex

One catch: replication copies mistakes. A bad DELETE or corrupting bug reaches every replica in seconds, so even active/active still needs point-in-time backups, and backups only count once you've restored one.

🔥 Real outage · GitLab, 31 January 2017

While fighting a database replication problem, an engineer ran a deletion on the primary instead of the secondary. About 300 GB of data was gone before they stopped it. Then they went looking for backups:

  • The nightly pg_dump backups weren't there. The backup job used PostgreSQL 9.2 tools against a 9.6 database, so it failed every time.
  • The failure emails existed, but were rejected by the receiving mail server, so nobody knew.
  • Disk snapshots weren't enabled for the database servers, and the secondary had been wiped during the repair attempt.

They restored from a copy an engineer had happened to make about 6 hours earlier. GitLab estimated that roughly 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments and 700 new user accounts were affected by the data loss. Their public postmortem is one of the most honest ever written, and its lesson is short: test your restores, and alert on backup failures through a channel you know works.

🧰 Templates & tools you'll actually use

SLOs: Prometheus + Grafana (Lesson 6.3) with Sloth or the OpenSLO spec to generate burn-rate alerts. Chaos: Chaos Monkey (Netflix, open source), LitmusChaos and Chaos Mesh (open source, for Kubernetes), AWS Fault Injection Service, and commercial tools like Gremlin. An error-budget policy fits on a page:

# error-budget-policy.yaml: agreed by product, engineering and SRE, before you need it
service: checkout-api
slo: 99.9% of requests succeed (non-5xx) over a rolling 28 days
budget: 0.1% of requests  # ≈ 40 minutes of full outage
when_budget_remaining:
  above_0:  release normally
  at_or_below_0: freeze all releases except P0 bug fixes and security fixes
                 until back within SLO; team works on reliability items
single_incident_over_20pct: postmortem with at least one P0 action item
exceptions: decided by the VP of engineering, in writing
# Prometheus alert: page on a fast burn (2% of a 30-day budget in 1 hour, SLO 99.9%)
- alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
  expr: |
    sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h]))
      / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))
    > (14.4 * 0.001)
  labels: { severity: page }

Try it locally: run a tiny game day on your laptop. Start your app with docker compose up (Lesson 8.3), then docker compose stop redis while you click around. Does the app degrade gracefully, or does every page error? That's your first chaos experiment.

Choice✅ Benefit❌ Cost

Also: a user on a 99%-reliable phone network can't tell 99.99% from 99.999%, a point the Google SRE book makes. Set SLOs from what users notice, not from pride.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Founding engineer (0 → 1)

No SRE team, but the habits are free: one SLO on your most important user journey, an uptime check, a burn-rate alert, and a monthly restore drill. Your first “chaos experiment” is stopping Redis on staging. Pick backup & restore for DR, and practise it.

Big tech (1 → 100)

Dedicated SRE teams, written error-budget policies signed by leadership, automated chaos in production, regular game days, regional failover exercises, and the 50% toil cap. Senior engineers are judged on whether their systems keep their SLOs without heroics.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you make this reliable?” is not answered with “add redundancy”. A senior answer:

  • Defines the SLI and SLO for the key user journey, and the error budget it implies.
  • Walks through failure modes: lose an instance, a zone, the database primary, a slow dependency, and how the design degrades for each.
  • Explains how you'd prove it: chaos experiments with a small blast radius, game days, restore drills, stated RPO and RTO.
  • Ties reliability to velocity: canaries and fast rollback shrink the cost of each bad deploy.

Follow-ups: “Your SLO is 99.99% and you deploy 50 times a day. How?” “What's the difference between a replica and a backup?” “What would you alert on?”

🏭 In the real world
Error budgets 🏢 Google SRE book: Embracing Risk

How: product and SRE teams jointly define a quarterly error budget from the SLO; releases continue while there's budget left and pause when it's spent.

Why this method: it replaces an endless argument between “ship faster” and “be careful” with an objective metric both sides agreed on.

A written error-budget policy 🏢 Google SRE Workbook: Example Error Budget Policy

How: halt changes except P0 and security fixes when the 4-week budget is exceeded; a postmortem with a P0 action item for any single incident using more than 20% of the budget.

Why this method: agreeing the rules in advance makes the freeze a shared decision, not a punishment.

The Simian Army 🏢 Netflix · 2011 The Netflix Simian Army

How: Chaos Monkey randomly disables production instances, run during the business day with engineers standing by; Chaos Gorilla simulates the loss of an entire availability zone.

Why this method: failures happen anyway; practising them on purpose forces teams to build automatic recovery.

The method, written down 🧰 Open community Principles of Chaos Engineering

How: define steady state, hypothesise it holds, inject real-world failures, try to disprove the hypothesis; prefer production, automate, minimise blast radius.

Why this method: it turns “break things” into a controlled experiment with a measurable result.

Backups that weren't 🏢 GitLab · 2017 Postmortem of database outage of January 31

How: a public, detailed postmortem: an accidental deletion, backup jobs that had been failing silently, and a restore from a 6-hour-old copy.

Why this method: publishing it openly turned a painful failure into one of the most-read lessons on restore testing.

🤯 Fun fact · the gorilla was named before it was born

In Netflix's post about the April 2011 AWS outage, the team wrote that it hadn't yet engineered for losing a whole zone, and that people were “already starting to call this service Chaos Gorilla”. Three months later, the Simian Army post described Chaos Gorilla doing exactly that. An outage wrote the roadmap.

Check yourself

1. Your SLO is 99.9% over 30 days. An incident fails 10% of requests for 30 minutes. Roughly how much of the error budget did it use?
The budget is 0.1% of 43,200 minutes = 43.2 minutes. The incident cost 0.1 × 30 = 3 full-outage minutes, and 3 ÷ 43.2 ≈ 7%. Under the example policy that's below the 20% postmortem threshold, but worth a look.
2. Halfway through the window you've already spent the whole error budget. Product wants to launch a big feature tomorrow. Under an error-budget policy, what happens?
That's what the policy was agreed for, in advance. Exceptions are possible but explicit and escalated. Quietly lowering the SLO to fit a launch defeats the point; if the SLO is genuinely too strict for users' needs, change it deliberately, not tomorrow.
3. You want to test whether your service survives losing a zone. What's the best first experiment?
Chaos engineering is an experiment, not a stunt: a measurable steady state, a hypothesis, a minimal blast radius and an abort button. Grow the radius as confidence grows.
📝 TL;DR
  • Error budget = (1 − SLO) × window: 99.9% over 30 days ≈ 43 minutes. Budget left → ship; budget spent → freeze and fix.
  • Write the policy in advance (4-week window, P0/security exceptions, postmortem for any incident over 20%). Alert on burn rate, e.g. 14.4× for 1 hour.
  • Keep toil under ~50%; automate the biggest item every sprint.
  • Chaos engineering: steady state → hypothesis → inject failure → compare, with a tiny blast radius. Game days practise the people side.
  • Know your RPO and RTO; replicas copy mistakes; a backup counts only once you've restored it.
