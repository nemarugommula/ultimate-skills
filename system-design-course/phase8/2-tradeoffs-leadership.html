Phase 8 · Lesson 2

Trade-offs, tech debt & build vs buy

Seniority is mostly judgment: which decisions to slow down for, which technology to bet on, which debt to take, and what not to build. At scale, these choices cost more than any single bug.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Design docs and ADRs: where alternatives and trade-offs get written down (Lesson 8.1).
  • Monolith vs microservices: splitting early adds operational cost before it adds value (Lesson 4.1).
  • Cloud cost: managed services trade money for engineering time (Lessons 5.5 and 6.5).
🎯 Hook

In February 2023, David Heinemeier Hansson of 37signals (the company behind Basecamp and HEY) published their cloud bill: $3.2 million in 2022. Their plan was to buy their own servers instead, and their estimate was about $7 million saved over five years, “without changing the size of our ops team”.

Renting cloud servers was the right call for years: it let a small team move fast. With a big, stable workload, the math flipped. Build vs buy isn't a belief; it's a calculation that changes as you grow. This lesson teaches you to run it.

Numbers first: the real price of “we'll just build it”

Your 5-person startup needs login with single sign-on (SSO) for enterprise customers. Two options, with typical numbers. A “fully loaded” engineer (salary + benefits + equipment) costs about $200,000 a year:

Buying is about 3× cheaper here, and the bigger win is the 6 engineer-months that go into the product instead. Engineers almost always forget the maintenance row. That row never ends.

TCO = upfront + Σyears (running cost + engineer time × engineer cost) “Total cost of ownership is what you pay to get it, plus, every year you keep it, what it costs to run and the engineering time it eats.” Compare options on TCO over 3 years, never on the sticker price.
💡 Big idea Spend your scarce resources (engineering time, attention, risk) only where they make your product different. Everything else, buy, borrow or choose boring. And match the care you take to how hard a decision is to undo.

One-way doors and two-way doors

In Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter, Jeff Bezos split decisions into two types. Type 1 decisions are “one-way doors”: nearly irreversible, so they deserve slow, careful deliberation. Type 2 decisions are two-way doors: if you're wrong, you walk back through. Most decisions are Type 2, and he warned that large organisations tend to use the heavy Type 1 process for everything, which makes them slow.

🚪 One-way door (go slow, write a doc)🔄 Two-way door (decide fast, today)
Your core data model and the shard key of a big table (Lesson 2.4)Which HTTP library or logging library to use
A public API that customers integrate with (Lesson 4.5)An internal API between two of your own services
Where user data is stored, legally (region, residency)The instance size of your app servers
Deleting data, or a migration with no way backAnything behind a feature flag (Lesson 6.2)

The senior move is not just to classify doors, but to turn one-way doors into two-way doors: put the vendor behind an interface, dual-write during a database migration so you can switch back, ship behind a flag, version your API.

🧠 Analogy: trying on vs. tattoos

Buying a shirt you can return is a two-way door: just pick one. A tattoo is a one-way door: you look at it for a long time first. The mistake juniors make is treating tattoos like shirts. The mistake big companies make is treating every shirt like a tattoo.

Choose boring technology

In 2015, Dan McKinley (a former Etsy engineer) wrote a famous essay with a simple model: every company gets about three innovation tokens. Using a brand-new database spends one. Writing your own database spends more than you have. New technology carries far more unknown unknowns: problems you haven't even imagined, like “writing stats causes garbage-collection pauses”.

“Boring” doesn't mean bad. It means its failure modes are well understood: Postgres at 100% CPU has a thousand blog posts; your new database at 100% CPU has you, at 3 a.m. Spend tokens on the thing that makes your product special, and nowhere else.

    Technical debt: borrowing time

    In 1992, Ward Cunningham compared shipping first-time code to going into debt: a little debt speeds you up, as long as you pay it back. The interest is the extra time every future change costs because of the shortcut.

    Martin Fowler later split debt into four kinds along two questions: was it prudent or reckless, and deliberate or inadvertent?

    Reckless + deliberate
    “No time for design, go quick and dirty.” Usually a bad bet: people underestimate how soon it slows them down.
    Prudent + deliberate
    “We'll ship now with a known shortcut, because the earlier release is worth more than the cleanup.” A real loan, with a repayment plan.
    Reckless + inadvertent
    A team that doesn't know good design practices, piling up debt without noticing.
    Prudent + inadvertent
    “Now that we've built it, we know how it should have been designed.” Inevitable, because you learn while you build.

    Not all debt needs paying. Debt in code nobody touches charges almost no interest. Debt in the code everyone changes every week is a payday loan.

    ✏️ Worked example: should we pay this debt down?

    Your checkout module has a tangled pricing function. Every change to it costs an extra 2 hours of untangling. Cleaning it up properly would take 60 hours.

    • Checkout changes about 40 times a quarter. Interest = 40 × 2 h = 80 hours per quarter. Payback = 60 ÷ 80 = 0.75 of a quarter, about 10 weeks. Pay it down now.
    • The same mess in a yearly-report script changed twice a year: interest = 2 × 2 h = 4 hours a year. Payback = 60 ÷ 4 = 15 years. Leave it alone and spend the 60 hours on something else.

    This is how to argue for refactoring with a manager: not “the code is ugly”, but “this costs us 80 hours a quarter and pays back in 10 weeks”.

    Build vs buy, for real

    Rules of thumb that hold up well:

    • Build what makes customers choose you (your core). Buy commodities: auth, payments, email delivery, error tracking, monitoring, search for small teams.
    • Watch usage-based pricing: a vendor that's cheap at 1,000 users can be your biggest bill at 10 million. That's when building (or self-hosting open source) wins.
    • Count opportunity cost: the months spent building plumbing are months your competitors spend on features.
    • Prefer vendors you can leave: standard protocols (SQL, S3-compatible storage, OpenTelemetry for telemetry, Lesson 6.3), exportable data, a thin interface in your code.

    Saying no (without being “that engineer”)

    A lead engineer gets more requests than any team can handle. Saying no well is a skill:

    • “Yes, and here's what it costs.” “We can add SSO this quarter if the reporting work moves to next quarter.” Make the trade-off visible and let the person who owns priorities choose.
    • “Not now, and here's when.” Tie it to a trigger: “We'll add a cache when the database's CPU passes 70% at peak” (Lesson 8.3).
    • “No, and here's a cheaper way.” “We don't need a new service for this; a background job does it in a day.”
    • Write it down (Lesson 8.1). A one-paragraph decision note stops the same debate coming back every month.
    🧰 Templates you'll actually use

    A TCO comparison (spreadsheet or a few lines of Python), a tech-debt register (issues with a label and an interest estimate), a door check in every design doc, and a tech radar (ThoughtWorks publishes one and an open-source tool to build your own) to record which technologies are “adopt”, “trial”, “assess” or “hold”.

    # tco.py: compare options over N years (all inputs are your estimates)
def tco(upfront_eng_years, eng_per_year, run_per_month, eng_cost=200_000, years=3, growth=0.0):
    run = sum(run_per_month * 12 * (1 + growth) ** y for y in range(years))
    return upfront_eng_years * eng_cost + eng_per_year * eng_cost * years + run

print(tco(0.5, 0.25, 0))         # build auth: 250,000
print(tco(0.04, 0, 2000))       # buy auth:    80,000

    ⚖️ Trade-offs
    ✅ Build (or self-host) when…✅ Buy (managed or SaaS) when…
    It's your core differentiator, or a key competitive advantageIt's a commodity every company needs (auth, payments, email, monitoring)
    Vendor cost grows with usage and you're big and growingYou're small, and engineering time is your scarcest resource
    You have stable, predictable load and the team to run itLoad is spiky or unknown; you'd overprovision if you owned it
    Compliance, latency or data control truly need itA specialist vendor does it better and more securely than you could

    Boring tech has a cost too: sometimes the new thing really is 10× better for your core problem. That's exactly what innovation tokens are for: spend them on purpose.

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Founding engineer (0 → 1)

    Buy almost everything that isn't your product. Spend at most one or two innovation tokens, on your core. Take prudent, deliberate debt to learn faster, and keep a list of it. Most of your decisions are two-way doors: make them quickly.

    Big tech (1 → 100)

    At huge scale, usage-priced vendors get expensive and in-house platforms pay off, so “build” wins more often. The danger flips: dozens of teams each building their own version of the same thing. Senior engineers push for shared platforms, write the door analysis, and fight to keep Type 2 decisions fast.

    🎤 Interview angle

    Every “why X?” question is a trade-off question. Strong answers name the alternative, the criteria and the reversibility:

    • “I'd use Postgres over Cassandra here: our data is relational, we need transactions for payments, and 5,000 writes a second fits one primary. If writes grow 20×, sharding is the next step, and that's why I'd pick the shard key carefully now.”
    • “For search I'd start with a managed service and keep our own index-building code, so moving to self-hosted later is a two-way door.”

    Follow-ups to expect: “What would make you change your mind?”, “What's the cost at 10× scale?”, “What's hard to undo here?” Behavioural rounds ask: “Tell me about tech debt you chose to take on” and “Tell me about a time you said no to a stakeholder.”

    🏭 In the real world
    One-way and two-way doors 🏢 Amazon · 2015 2015 Letter to Shareholders

    How: Type 1 decisions (irreversible) get methodical, careful deliberation; Type 2 decisions (reversible) “can and should be made quickly” by high-judgment individuals or small groups.

    Why this method: using the heavy process for every decision leads, in the letter's words, to slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion and too little experimentation.

    Innovation tokens 🏢 Etsy (Dan McKinley) · 2015 Choose Boring Technology

    How: treat each company as having about three innovation tokens; spend them deliberately, and choose well-understood technology everywhere else. The essay describes Etsy's early years, when a pointless middle layer, built largely to give new Python hires something to do, took years to remove.

    Why this method: new technology has far more unknown unknowns, and every extra technology adds operational burden for the whole company.

    The debt quadrant 📜 Martin Fowler · 2009 Technical Debt Quadrant

    How: classifies debt as reckless or prudent, deliberate or inadvertent, and notes that prudent debt in rarely touched code may never be worth paying down.

    Why this method: “debt or not” is the wrong question; what matters is whether the interest is worth the speed you bought.

    The original debt metaphor 📜 Ward Cunningham · 1992 The WyCash Portfolio Management System (OOPSLA '92)

    How: an experience report that compares shipping first-time code to taking on debt, which must be repaid by rewriting as understanding improves.

    Why this method: the metaphor gave engineers a way to explain refactoring to business people in terms they already use.

    Leaving the cloud 🏢 37signals · 2023 We stand to save $7m over five years from our cloud exit

    How: after a $3.2m cloud bill in 2022, they bought their own servers for app, cache, database and search workloads, estimating about $1.5m a year in savings, and planned to move 8 PB of files out of S3 later.

    Why this method: for a mid-sized business with stable workloads, the post argues, renting costs far more than owning. The same analysis would have said “rent” when they were small and growing fast.

    🤯 Fun fact · the debt metaphor is older than most of its users

    Ward Cunningham, who also invented the wiki, introduced “technical debt” in a 1992 conference experience report about a financial product, WyCash. His key line: “Shipping first time code is like going into debt.” It's fitting that the metaphor came from someone building portfolio management software.

    🌉 How it connects

    Door analysis decides which decisions need a design doc (Lesson 8.1). Innovation tokens explain why the founding stack in Lesson 8.3 is so plain. Managed services (Lesson 5.5) are “buy”; Kubernetes (Lesson 5.2) is often an expensive token for a small team. Monolith-first (Lesson 4.1) is the boring default. In AI products the same logic applies: calling a hosted model API is “buy”, training your own model is a token you spend only if the model is your differentiator.

    Check yourself

    1. Building a feature takes 4 engineer-months, then 0.2 of an engineer per year. Engineers cost $180,000 a year. A vendor charges $1,500 a month. Roughly, what are the 3-year costs?
    Build: 4/12 × 180k = $60k upfront, plus 0.2 × 180k × 3 years = $108k of maintenance, so $168k. Buy: 1,500 × 36 months = $54k. The first option forgot maintenance, the most commonly forgotten row in any TCO.
    2. Which decision is the clearest one-way door, deserving a written design and careful review?
    Changing a shard key on billions of rows means moving all the data (Lesson 2.4). A library swap is hidden behind your code, and a feature flag is reversible by design: decide those fast.
    3. Your team ships a demo with a known shortcut in billing, writes a ticket to fix it in two weeks, and agrees the earlier launch is worth it. In Fowler's quadrant, this is…
    They know they're borrowing (deliberate) and weighed the payoff against the cost (prudent). It's still debt: if the ticket is ignored, the interest keeps growing. Reckless-inadvertent is a team that doesn't realise it's creating a mess.
    📝 TL;DR
    • Match care to reversibility: one-way doors get docs and review; two-way doors get decided today. Turn one-way doors into two-way ones when you can.
    • About three innovation tokens: spend them on your differentiator, choose boring everywhere else.
    • Tech debt: interest = extra cost per change × how often you change it. Pay down high-interest debt; ignore debt in code nobody touches.
    • Build vs buy on 3–5 year TCO including maintenance and opportunity cost; re-run it as you grow.
