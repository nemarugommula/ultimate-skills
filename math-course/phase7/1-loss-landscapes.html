Phase 7 · Lesson 1

Loss functions & landscapes

One number that says “how wrong is the model?”, and the hilly landscape it makes over the knobs. Choosing that number well is the first decision in training any regressor, classifier, LLM or game-playing agent.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Gradient descent: compute the gradient ∇L (read “nabla L”, the uphill direction) and step the other way: w ← w − η∇L (Lesson 3.4).
  • η (the Greek letter “eta”) is the learning rate: how big each step is (Lesson 3.4).
  • Cross-entropy: the average of −log(probability the model gave the right answer) (Lesson 6.3).
🎯 Hook

Your spam filter is right 80% of the time. You nudge one of its weights a tiny bit. Accuracy: still exactly 80%. Nudge it the other way: still 80%. Its slope is zero, so gradient descent has no idea which way to go.

That's why almost nobody trains on “percent correct”, even though it's what we care about. We train on a smooth stand-in instead. This lesson is about picking that stand-in, and about the landscape it creates.

Numbers first: four mistakes

A model predicts four house prices (in $100k). Its errors (prediction − truth) are [1, −2, 0.5, 10]. The last one is a disaster: maybe a typo in the data. How bad is the model overall? Three popular answers:

MSE: (1² + 2² + 0.5² + 10²) / 4 = (1 + 4 + 0.25 + 100) / 4 = 26.31
MAE: (1 + 2 + 0.5 + 10) / 4 = 3.375

Same mistakes, very different verdicts. In MSE (mean squared error) the one outlier contributes 100 of the 105.25 total: 95% of the loss. In MAE (mean absolute error) it contributes 10 of 13.5: still big (74%), but it no longer drowns out everything else.

MSE = (1/n) Σ (ŷ − y)²   ·   MAE = (1/n) Σ |ŷ − y| Read: for each example, take the error (prediction ŷ, “y-hat”, minus the truth y). MSE squares it, MAE takes its size without the sign. Σ adds over all n examples, and 1/n turns the total into an average.

The pull of each error

What really matters for training is the gradient: how hard each error pulls on the weights. The slope of e² is 2e, so in MSE an error of 10 pulls 20 times harder than an error of 1. The slope of |e| is always ±1: every error pulls equally hard, just in its own direction.

The Huber loss is the compromise. Small errors (size up to δ, “delta”, usually 1) are squared, like MSE. Big ones only grow in a straight line, like MAE:

Huberδ(e) = ½e²  if |e| ≤ δ,  otherwise  δ·(|e| − ½δ) Read: “if the error is small, half its square; if it's big, a straight line”. Its pull (slope) is e for small errors and capped at ±δ for big ones.
✏️ Worked example: Huber with δ = 1 on the same four errors
  • e = 1: small, so ½ · 1² = 0.5. e = 0.5: ½ · 0.25 = 0.125.
  • e = −2: big, so 1 · (2 − 0.5) = 1.5. e = 10: 1 · (10 − 0.5) = 9.5.
  • Average: (0.5 + 1.5 + 0.125 + 9.5) / 4 = 2.91. The outlier's pull is capped at 1, the same as the error of −2.

With the outlier far away, the MSE line tilts toward it to shrink one giant squared error, and fits the other eight points worse. The MAE and Huber lines barely care. With no outlier, all three agree. MSE is the right choice when big errors are real and costly; MAE or Huber when big errors are often junk.

💡 Big idea A loss function turns “how wrong is the model?” into one number, and its slope decides how hard each mistake pulls the weights. Squaring makes big mistakes shout; absolute value makes every mistake speak at the same volume.

Why we train on smooth losses

Back to the hook. A classifier decides “spam” when its score crosses a threshold. Below, one knob t slides that threshold. Compare two ways of scoring the model: percent wrong and log-loss (cross-entropy, Lesson 6.3: the average of −log of the probability given to the right answer).

🎮 Mini playground · Staircase vs smooth hill

Percent wrong is a staircase: flat almost everywhere, with sudden jumps. Its slope is 0 or undefined, so the gradient never tells you where to go. Log-loss is a smooth hill whose slope always points toward better thresholds. And when log-loss goes down, accuracy usually follows.

🧠 Analogy: hiking in fog

Training is hiking down a mountain in thick fog. You can't see the valley; you can only feel the slope under your boots. A smooth loss is a grassy slope: you always feel which way is down. Percent wrong is a set of flat terraces with cliffs: on a terrace your boots feel nothing at all.

The shape of the whole mountain, every height over every possible position, is the loss landscape.

Loss landscapes: bowls, valleys, saddles, plateaus

Give the model just two weights, w₁ and w₂. For each pair, compute the loss. Color the map by height: that's the landscape. A few shapes matter:

  • Convex bowl: one lowest point, and every downhill path leads there. MSE for linear regression is a bowl.
  • Local minimum: a dip that isn't the deepest one. Roll in, and the slope is 0 everywhere around you.
  • Saddle point: downhill in one direction, uphill in another, like a horse saddle or a mountain pass. The slope is exactly 0 in the middle.
  • Plateau: nearly flat for a long way. The slope is tiny, so steps are tiny.
🎮 Playground · Roll a ball on four landscapes

Stronger teal = lower loss; each colour band is one step of height, like contour lines on a hiking map. Drag the ball to choose a start, pick a landscape, then press Step or Roll. Each step is w ← w − η∇L.

Things to try. Two valleys: start on the right and the ball settles in the shallow valley, a local minimum, even though a deeper one exists. Saddle: starting exactly on the ridge (y = 0), the ball rolls to the middle and stops, because the slope there is 0. Drag it a hair off the ridge and it escapes. Plateau: the ball crawls for dozens of steps before the slope finally picks up.

Real networks have millions of weights, not two. There, true local minima turn out to be less of a problem than you'd fear, while saddles and flat regions are everywhere (the Deep Learning Book's chapter 8 discusses both). Lessons 7.2 and 7.3 give the ball tricks for exactly these places.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn
e = np.array([1, -2, 0.5, 10])               # errors: prediction - truth
(e**2).mean(), np.abs(e).mean()              # MSE 26.31, MAE 3.375
np.where(np.abs(e) <= 1, 0.5*e**2, np.abs(e) - 0.5).mean()   # Huber 2.91

pred, y = torch.tensor(e), torch.zeros(4, dtype=torch.float64)
nn.MSELoss()(pred, y), nn.L1Loss()(pred, y), nn.HuberLoss(delta=1.0)(pred, y)
nn.CrossEntropyLoss()(torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.5]]), torch.tensor([0]))  # classifier: logits, right class

🌍 Where it's used
MSE = least squares 📜 History · 1801 Least squares (Gauss and the asteroid Ceres)

How: fit a model by making the sum of squared errors as small as possible. It's still the default loss for regression: prices, demand forecasts, sensor readings.

Why this method: the bowl is perfectly smooth, so for a straight-line model there's even a direct formula for the bottom. And if the noise is bell-shaped, minimizing MSE is the same as maximum likelihood (Lesson 5.3).

Huber-style loss in DQN 🎮 RL · 2015 Mnih et al., Human-level control through deep RL (Nature)

How: DQN learns game-move values from a squared error. The paper clips that error's contribution to the update to between −1 and 1, and notes this equals using an absolute-value loss outside (−1, 1): Huber's shape with δ = 1. PyTorch's official DQN tutorial uses the Huber loss directly.

Why this method: the paper reports the clipping further improved stability. In RL the targets come from the network's own shaky guesses, so occasional huge errors are common, and capping their pull stops one bad target from yanking all the weights.

Cross-entropy instead of accuracy 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners

How: an LLM is trained to minimize cross-entropy on the next token, and the GPT-3 paper measures progress with cross-entropy validation loss, which falls smoothly as training compute grows.

Why this method: “percent of next tokens guessed exactly” is a staircase with zero slope. Cross-entropy rewards giving the right token a bit more probability, even before it's the top guess, so every step has a useful gradient.

Pictures of real loss landscapes 🖼️ Deep learning · 2018 Li et al., Visualizing the Loss Landscape of Neural Nets

How: pick two random directions in the space of millions of weights, step along them around the trained weights, and plot the loss on that 2D grid: exactly the heat map in the playground above, with a “filter normalization” trick so plots are comparable.

Why this method: you can't draw a million dimensions. These 2D slices showed that very deep networks without skip connections get chaotic landscapes, while skip connections keep them smooth, which helps explain why skip connections make deep networks trainable.

🤯 Fun fact · the valleys are secretly connected

Train the same network twice from different random starts and you land in two different valleys. Walk in a straight line from one to the other and the loss shoots up over a ridge. But in 2018, Garipov et al. found a simple curved path between them along which the loss stays low the whole way. So the low ground looks less like separate pits and more like valleys joined by low passes.

🌉 AI bridge · picking the loss

RL: DQN's loss is the squared gap between the network's guess and its target. The RL course's Lesson 4.3: DQN shows that loss, and why its labs swap the square for the Huber loss (smooth_l1_loss).

Check yourself

1. A model's errors are [2, −2, 6]. What is its MSE?
(4 + 4 + 36) / 3 = 44 / 3 ≈ 14.67. The MAE would be (2 + 2 + 6) / 3 ≈ 3.33. Notice the 6 makes up 36 of the 44 in MSE: squaring makes the biggest error dominate.
2. Why do we train classifiers on log-loss (cross-entropy) instead of directly on percent correct?
Tiny weight changes rarely flip a prediction, so accuracy doesn't change and its gradient is 0. Log-loss changes smoothly with every probability, so it always has a slope pointing somewhere useful.
3. DQN clipped its error term to between −1 and 1. Which loss does that match, and why did it help?
Clipping the pull to ±1 is exactly the slope of the Huber loss with δ = 1: squared for small errors, a straight line for big ones. In RL, targets are noisy guesses, so capping outliers' influence keeps the weights from being yanked around.
📝 TL;DR
  • A loss turns “how wrong?” into one number; its slope decides how hard each error pulls.
  • MSE squares errors (outliers dominate), MAE uses their size (every error pulls equally), Huber is squared for small errors and linear for big ones.
  • Train on smooth losses like log-loss: accuracy is a staircase with no useful gradient.
  • The loss over all weights is a landscape: convex bowls are easy; local minima, saddles and plateaus slow or trap plain gradient descent.
