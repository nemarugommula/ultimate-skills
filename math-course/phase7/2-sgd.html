Phase 7 · Lesson 2

Stochastic gradient descent & mini-batches

Don't read all the data before every step: estimate the slope from a small random handful. This shortcut is how every LLM, image model and DQN agent is trained.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − η∇L, a step downhill on the loss, with learning rate η (“eta”) (Lesson 3.4).
  • The training loss is an average over all examples, like the MSE from last lesson (Lesson 7.1).
  • Averages of random samples get less noisy as the sample grows, roughly like 1/√n (Lesson 4.7).
🎯 Hook

GPT-3 was trained on 300 billion tokens. Plain gradient descent would read all 300 billion before taking a single step downhill. Then do it again for the next step.

Instead, each step looked at one batch of 3.2 million tokens: about 1/94,000 of the data. That's a rough, noisy guess of the slope. It worked anyway, and this lesson shows why.

Numbers first: four examples, one weight

A tiny model predicts ŷ = w · x, with squared error per example. The data: (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 7), (4, 8). Right now w = 1. The slope of one example's loss (wx − y)² is 2 · (error) · x:

example 12·(1−2)·1 = −2
example 22·(2−4)·2 = −8
example 32·(3−7)·3 = −24
example 42·(4−8)·4 = −32

The true gradient is their average: (−2 − 8 − 24 − 32) / 4 = −16.5. Negative slope means “increase w”.

Now cheat: look at only two random examples. Examples 1 and 3 give (−2 − 24) / 2 = −13. Examples 2 and 4 give −20. Neither is exactly right, but both say “increase w”, at half the cost. And if you average the guesses from all six possible pairs, you get exactly −16.5: the shortcut is right on average.

g = (1/B) Σi in batch ∇ℓi   then   w ← w − η g Read: pick B random examples (the mini-batch), average their gradients (ℓᵢ, “ell i”, is the loss on example i) to get the estimate g, and step downhill with it.
  • B = all N examples: full-batch gradient descent. Exact, smooth, expensive.
  • B = 1: the original stochastic gradient descent (stochastic = “random”). Cheap, very noisy.
  • B = 32, 256, 3.2 million tokens…: a mini-batch. What everyone actually uses, and usually still called “SGD”.

An epoch is one full pass through the data. With 50,000 images and B = 100, one epoch is 50,000 / 100 = 500 steps.

💡 Big idea A mini-batch gradient is a noisy but unbiased estimate of the true gradient: right on average, wrong in each draw. Making B 4× bigger costs 4× more but only halves the noise (1/√4 = 1/2). So it usually pays to take many cheap, wobbly steps instead of a few perfect ones.
🧠 Analogy: asking for directions

You're lost in a city and want the station. Option A: survey all 10,000 residents, average their pointing directions, take one step. Option B: ask the 5 people nearest you, step that way, ask 5 more. Each small poll is a bit off, but the errors point randomly in different directions and cancel out over many steps. You arrive long before survey A is finished.

With B = 1 the path staggers like it's had too much coffee, but every step reads just 1 example. With B = 200 it glides straight in, but each step reads all 200. Near the bottom, small batches keep jittering around: the noise never fully goes away unless you also shrink η (Lesson 7.3).

✏️ Worked example: how much noise does a batch remove?

Suppose single-example gradients scatter around the truth with a typical error (standard deviation) of 10. Averaging B independent ones divides that by √B:

  • B = 1: error ≈ 10 / 1 = 10
  • B = 4: error ≈ 10 / 2 = 5 (4× the cost, half the noise)
  • B = 100: error ≈ 10 / 10 = 1 (100× the cost, a tenth of the noise)

Each 4× bigger batch only halves the noise. That's why “more steps with smaller batches” usually wins for a fixed budget of examples read.

Same budget, different batch sizes

Give four runs the same budget: 2,000 examples read. B = 1 gets 2,000 steps, B = 200 gets only 10. Who ends lowest? Then tick the box to try the linear scaling rule (Goyal et al. 2017): when the batch is k times bigger, make η k times bigger too.

🎮 Playground · The compute race

With one η for everyone, B = 200 is still far from the bottom after its 10 steps, while the small batches got there long ago (B = 1 then keeps jittering just above the best loss: its noise never settles). With the scaling rule, the big batch's 10 giant steps catch up. That matters because a big batch can be split across many GPUs that work at the same time, so 10 big steps can finish far sooner in wall-clock time. (The cap is there because, as Goyal et al. note, the rule breaks down when steps get too big, especially early in training.)

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
X, y = np.random.randn(10_000), np.random.randn(10_000)   # stand-in data
w, b, eta, B = 0.0, 0.0, 0.1, 32
for epoch in range(3):
    order = np.random.permutation(len(X))          # shuffle once per epoch
    for s in range(0, len(X), B):
        i = order[s:s+B]; err = w*X[i] + b - y[i]   # one mini-batch
        w -= eta * (2*err*X[i]).mean(); b -= eta * (2*err).mean()

🌍 Where it's used
Noisy steps still converge 📜 History · 1951 Robbins & Monro, A Stochastic Approximation Method

How: they studied updating a guess using only noisy measurements, with step sizes that shrink over time, and showed the guess homes in on the right answer. SGD is this idea applied to gradients.

Why this method: when exact measurements are impossible or too expensive, you can still get there by averaging noise across many small steps, as long as the steps shrink.

Mini-batches of millions of tokens 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3 paper (Table 2.1)

How: the smallest GPT-3 used batches of 0.5 million tokens, the 175B model 3.2 million (about 1,560 sequences of 2,048 tokens). The batch size also started small (32k tokens) and grew during early training.

Why this method: the paper notes larger models can typically use larger batches, and the authors measured the gradient's noise during training and used it to guide their choice of batch size.

Data-parallel training + the linear scaling rule 🏭 In production · 2017 Goyal et al., ImageNet in 1 Hour

How: 256 GPUs each compute the gradient on their own 32 images; the gradients are combined (an “allreduce”) into the gradient of one mini-batch of 256 × 32 = 8,192, and every GPU takes the same step. The learning rate is scaled up with the batch, plus a gentle warmup at the start.

Why this method: averaging gradients across GPUs is mathematically the same as one big mini-batch, so 256 GPUs train one model together. ResNet-50 on ImageNet took one hour while matching small-batch accuracy.

Random mini-batches from a replay buffer 🎮 RL · 2015 DQN (Nature)

How: DQN stores past game moments and trains on random mini-batches of 32 of them at a time (with the RMSProp optimizer, a cousin of Adam from Lesson 7.3).

Why this method: consecutive game frames look almost identical; a random mini-batch mixes moments from many games, so its gradient is closer to an honest average.

Linear models on huge data 🤖 Machine learning · 2010s scikit-learn SGDClassifier / SGDRegressor

How: logistic regression or linear SVMs trained one example (or chunk) at a time with SGD; partial_fit lets you stream data that doesn't fit in memory.

Why this method: the docs note SGD has been applied successfully to large-scale, sparse problems such as text classification, where exact solvers would be too slow or too memory-hungry.

🤯 Fun fact · the noise is a feature

Keskar et al. (2016) found that very large batches tend to land in sharp minima that generalize worse, while small batches end up in flat ones, and their experiments support the common view that this is due to the noise in small-batch gradients. The jitter you saw in the playground can shake the model out of narrow, fragile valleys. (paper)

🌉 AI bridge · mini-batches everywhere

RL: DQN grabs random mini-batches from its replay buffer so look-alike frames aren't trained on together, see RL Lesson 4.3: DQN. PPO reuses each batch of collected experience for several epochs of mini-batch updates, see RL Lesson 5.6: TRPO → PPO.

LLMs: one “training step” of an LLM is one mini-batch gradient, averaged across many GPUs. Next lesson gives the noisy ball momentum and a per-weight step size (Adam), and shrinks η over time with a schedule (Lesson 7.3).

Check yourself

1. You have 50,000 training images and a batch size of 100. How many steps are 3 epochs?
One epoch = 50,000 / 100 = 500 steps (one look at every image). Three epochs = 3 × 500 = 1,500 steps.
2. Why do we train with mini-batch gradients instead of the exact full-data gradient?
The mini-batch average is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient. Its noise only shrinks like 1/√B, so a huge batch costs a lot for little extra accuracy. Many cheap steps usually win, and the noise can even help find flatter minima.
3. A team trained with batch 256 and η = 0.1. They move to 4× more GPUs, so the batch becomes 1,024. What does the linear scaling rule suggest?
Batch × 4 → learning rate × 4: 0.1 × 4 = 0.4. With 4× fewer steps per epoch, each step must go about 4× further. Goyal et al. add a gradual warmup because the rule breaks down in the first, fast-changing part of training.
📝 TL;DR
  • SGD estimates the gradient from a random mini-batch of B examples: noisy, unbiased, cheap.
  • Noise shrinks like 1/√B: 4× the batch halves the noise. Many cheap steps usually beat a few exact ones.
  • An epoch = one pass over the data = N / B steps.
  • Used for every LLM (GPT-3: 3.2M tokens per batch), in data-parallel GPU training (gradients averaged), DQN replay and PPO epochs.
  • Linear scaling rule: batch × k → learning rate × k, with warmup.
