Constraints & Lagrange multipliers

How to find the best knobs when you're not allowed to set them freely: a budget, a safety limit, a “don't drift too far” rule. It's the math behind support vector machines, TRPO, PPO and the KL leash in RLHF.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The gradient ∇f points in the direction where f grows fastest, and it's perpendicular to f's contour lines (Lesson 3.3).
  • A penalty term λ × (something) in the loss pushes the model to keep that something small (Lesson 7.4).
  • KL divergence: a number for how differently two models behave; 0 means identical (Lesson 6.4).
🎯 Hook

When OpenAI fine-tuned GPT-3 into InstructGPT with RLHF, the model was told: maximize the reward model's score, but pay a price β for every unit you drift from the original model. In 2015 the TRPO algorithm said almost the same thing as a hard rule: improve, but never let the drift exceed δ.

A price or a limit? This lesson shows they're two sides of one coin, and that coin is called a Lagrange multiplier.

Numbers first: a corner garden

You're fencing a rectangular garden in the corner of two walls, so you only need fence on two sides: x metres one way and y the other. You have 10 metres of fence. Which rectangle has the most area?

The square wins: 5 × 5 = 25 m². Without the fence limit there'd be no answer at all (bigger is always better). The constraint x + y = 10 is what makes the problem interesting.

The picture: a contour that just touches

Draw the contour lines of the area: all the (x, y) with area 9, all with area 21, all with area 25… Then draw the fence line x + y = 10. Walk along the fence line. Wherever a contour crosses the line, you can keep walking toward bigger contours. You stop exactly where a contour just touches the line without crossing it.

At a touching point the two curves share a direction, so their gradients (each perpendicular to its own curve) point the same way. That's the whole method:

∇f = λ ∇g   and   g(x, y) = c Read: at the best point, the gradient of the goal f is a multiple of the gradient of the constraint g, and the constraint holds. The multiple, λ (“lambda”), is the Lagrange multiplier.

✏️ Worked example: the garden by Lagrange
  • Goal f = x·y, so ∇f = (y, x): how fast area grows if you lengthen x or y.
  • Constraint g = x + y = 10, so ∇g = (1, 1).
  • ∇f = λ∇g says (y, x) = (λ, λ), so x = y = λ.
  • Plug into the constraint: λ + λ = 10, so λ = 5, x = y = 5, area 25. ✓ Same as the table.

λ is the price of the constraint

The multiplier isn't just a helper number. It tells you how much the best result improves per extra unit of budget. With λ = 5, one more metre of fence should buy about 5 m². Check: 5.5 × 5.5 = 30.25, which is 5.25 more than 25. (It's “about” because λ is the rate at this exact budget; the rate creeps up as the budget grows.) Economists call λ the shadow price.

The usual way to package everything is one function, the Lagrangian:

ℒ(x, y, λ) = f(x, y) − λ · (g(x, y) − c) Read “script L”: the goal, minus λ times how much you're over budget. Setting all of ℒ's slopes to zero gives back both rules: the gradients line up, and the budget is met exactly.

Look at the shape: goal − price × (resource used). That's exactly a penalty, like λΣw² in Lesson 7.4. A penalty is a constraint written as a price.

🧠 Analogy: spending a calorie budget

You're picking snacks with a 600-calorie limit, trying to maximize enjoyment. At the best menu, the last calorie spent on chips brings as much joy as the last calorie spent on chocolate; otherwise you'd swap a bit of one for the other. “Equal joy per calorie at the margin” is ∇f = λ∇g, and λ is the joy one extra calorie would buy you.

💡 Big idea At a constrained best point, the goal's gradient lines up with the constraint's gradient: ∇f = λ∇g. The multiplier λ is the price of the constraint. So “maximize f with a hard limit on g” and “maximize f − λg” lead to the same answer when λ is chosen right.

Penalty or hard limit?

A toy version of the RLHF dilemma. Let θ be how far we move a model from where it started. The reward model always says “more!”: reward = 3θ. Drifting is risky, and we measure the drift as θ² (a stand-in for KL). Two ways to rein it in:

  • Penalty (RLHF style): maximize 3θ − βθ². The best θ = 3 / (2β).
  • Hard limit (TRPO style): maximize 3θ with drift θ² ≤ δ. The best θ = √δ, and its multiplier is λ = 3 / (2√δ).
🎮 Playground · Price vs limit

The teal curve is the penalized objective 3θ − βθ²; its peak is the penalty's answer. The red band is outside the hard limit θ² ≤ δ. Press “Match” to set β equal to the limit's multiplier λ, and see the two answers coincide.

Each price β produces some amount of drift, and each limit δ has a price λ that produces exactly that drift. The catch in practice: you usually know the drift you're comfortable with (δ), not the right price. That's why TRPO used the hard limit, and why PPO's “adaptive KL” variant keeps adjusting β during training: doubling it when the drift overshoots a target, halving it when the drift undershoots. It's learning the Lagrange multiplier on the fly.

For “at most” limits (≤): a limit that doesn't pinch has price λ = 0 (one of the “KKT conditions”).

🐍 In code 
from scipy.optimize import minimize
# maximize x*y subject to x + y = 10  (scipy minimizes, so minimize -x*y)
res = minimize(lambda v: -v[0]*v[1], x0=[1, 1],
               constraints=[{"type": "eq", "fun": lambda v: v[0] + v[1] - 10}])
res.x, -res.fun                                  # [5, 5], 25

# RLHF-style penalty instead of a hard limit (PyTorch sketch)
# loss = -(reward - beta * kl).mean()           # beta = the price of drifting
# PPO adaptive-KL: if kl > 1.5*target: beta *= 2;  if kl < target/1.5: beta /= 2

🌍 Where it's used
Maximum-margin classifiers (SVMs) 🤖 Machine learning · 1995 Support vector machine (Cortes & Vapnik, Support-vector networks)

How: make the gap (margin) between two classes as wide as possible, subject to one constraint per training point: “be on your correct side, at least a margin away”. Solving it with Lagrange multipliers gives one multiplier per point.

Why this method: most multipliers come out 0: those points' constraints don't pinch. Only the points touching the margin, the support vectors, get a price, so the boundary depends on just a handful of examples.

A hard limit on policy change (TRPO) 🎮 RL · 2015 Schulman et al., Trust Region Policy Optimization

How: maximize the estimated improvement of the policy subject to the average KL between old and new policy being at most δ: a “trust region” around the current policy.

Why this method: the PPO paper explains that TRPO uses a hard constraint rather than a penalty because it's hard to pick one β that works across different problems, or even throughout one problem.

Clipping and adaptive β (PPO) 🎮 RL · 2017 Schulman et al., Proximal Policy Optimization

How: PPO's main version clips the probability ratio so a single update can't push it far from 1, a cheap stand-in for TRPO's constraint. (The ratio itself, new probability ÷ old, is importance sampling: Lesson 4.6b.) Its alternative version uses a KL penalty β and doubles or halves β to hit a target KL.

Why this method: both avoid TRPO's expensive constrained solve and use ordinary SGD/Adam steps; the paper found the clipped version worked best.

🤯 Fun fact · most of the data doesn't matter (to an SVM)

Because only the support vectors have non-zero multipliers, you could delete every other training point, retrain, and get exactly the same SVM boundary. The decision is made entirely by the few examples sitting on the edge of the margin.

Check yourself

1. Maximize x·y with x + y = 8. What's the best area, and what's λ?
∇f = (y, x) = λ(1, 1) gives x = y = λ; then 2λ = 8, so λ = 4 and the area is 4 × 4 = 16. An extra unit of budget would add about 4 (exactly 4.5² − 16 = 4.25).
2. At the best garden for a 10 m budget, λ = 5. What does that 5 tell you?
λ is the price, or value, of the constraint: how fast the best achievable result grows as the budget grows. (Here it happens to equal the side length too, but that's a coincidence of this problem.)
3. In RLHF the reward is score − β · KL(new ‖ original). What happens if you make β much larger?
β is the price per unit of drift. A higher price means less drift, just like a tighter limit δ in TRPO. In the price-vs-limit playground, a bigger β moves the peak to a smaller θ.
📝 TL;DR
  • Constrained best point: a contour of the goal just touches the constraint, so ∇f = λ∇g.
  • λ, the Lagrange multiplier, is the price of the constraint: the gain per extra unit of budget.
  • Lagrangian ℒ = f − λ(g − c): a penalty is a constraint written as a price; the right price reproduces the hard limit.
  • Used in SVMs (support vectors = non-zero multipliers), TRPO's KL limit, PPO's clip and adaptive β, and RLHF's β.
