Phase 7 · Lesson 3

Momentum, Adam & learning-rate schedules

Three upgrades to plain gradient descent: keep rolling (momentum), give every weight its own step size (Adam), and change the step size over time (schedules). Together they're the default recipe for training LLMs.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • One training step: w ← w − ηg, where g is the mini-batch gradient (Lessons 3.4 and 7.2).
  • A loss landscape can have long narrow valleys and flat plateaus that slow plain gradient descent (Lesson 7.1).
  • The mean is the balancing point of a list of numbers (Lesson 5.1). Here we'll use a running average: keep most of the old value, mix in a bit of the new one.
🎯 Hook

Here's the training recipe Meta reported for the original LLaMA models in 2023: AdamW, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, a cosine learning-rate schedule down to 10% of the peak, 2,000 warmup steps, weight decay 0.1. GPT-3 used almost the same list.

That sentence looks like a magic spell. By the end of this lesson every word in it will make sense, except weight decay, which is next lesson.

The problem: a long, narrow valley

Picture a loss that's very steep in one direction and nearly flat in another, like a ravine. Plain gradient descent bounces off the steep walls and creeps along the gentle floor. Make η bigger to go faster along the floor, and the bouncing turns into an explosion. That one trade-off is what the tricks below fix.

Momentum: a heavy ball keeps rolling

Numbers first. Keep a running velocity m: each step, keep 90% of the old velocity and add the new gradient. If the gradient is 1 every time:

m: 1 → 0.9·1 + 1 = 1.9 → 2.71 → 3.44 → 4.10 → 4.69 → … → 10

Consistent pushes build up speed, up to 10× a single push. Now the gradient flips sign every step (+1, −1, +1, …), like bouncing between the walls of a ravine:

m: 1 → −0.1 → 0.91 → −0.18 → 0.84 → −0.25 → … settles near ±0.53

The back-and-forth pushes cancel out. So momentum speeds up along the valley floor (where gradients agree) and calms the bouncing across it (where they flip).

m ← β m + g   then   w ← w − η m Read: the new velocity m is β (“beta”, usually 0.9) times the old velocity plus today's gradient g. Then step along the velocity instead of the raw gradient.
🧠 Analogy: a bowling ball vs a ping-pong ball

Drop a ping-pong ball into a curved gutter: it rattles from wall to wall and barely moves forward. A bowling ball rolls down the middle: its weight carries it through the small bumps, and its side-to-side wobbles cancel out. Momentum makes your weights a bowling ball.

Adam: every weight gets its own step size

Momentum still uses one η for every weight. But in a network some weights get huge gradients (say 100) and others tiny ones (say 0.001). One η is too big for the first and too small for the second.

Adam (“adaptive moment estimation”) keeps two running averages for each weight:

  • m: the average gradient, which way it usually points (like momentum).
  • v: the average squared gradient, how big it usually is.

Then it steps by m / √v: “usual direction ÷ usual size”. A weight whose gradients are always 100 gets 100 / √(100²) = 1. A weight whose gradients are always 0.001 gets 0.001 / √(0.001²) = 1. Both move about η per step.

m ← β₁m + (1 − β₁)g  ·  v ← β₂v + (1 − β₂)g²  ·  w ← w − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) Read: m is a running average of the gradient (β₁, “beta one”, usually 0.9) and v a running average of its square (β₂, “beta two”, usually 0.999). m̂ and v̂ (“m-hat”, “v-hat”) are the same averages with a start-up correction, explained below. ε (“epsilon”, like 10⁻⁸) just prevents dividing by zero.

One difference from the momentum above: Adam's m has the (1 − β₁) factor, so it's a true average. Feed it a gradient of 1 every step and it settles at 1, not at 10 like the heavy-ball velocity. Same direction, smaller scale.

✏️ Worked example: Adam's very first step

Both averages start at 0. The first gradient is g = 0.5; β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999.

  • m = 0.9·0 + 0.1·0.5 = 0.05.  v = 0.999·0 + 0.001·0.25 = 0.00025.
  • These are far too small, because they started at 0. The correction divides by (1 − βt) at step t = 1: m̂ = 0.05 / (1 − 0.9) = 0.5,  v̂ = 0.00025 / (1 − 0.999) = 0.25.
  • Step = η · 0.5 / √0.25 = η · 0.5 / 0.5 = exactly η.

Try g = 50 or g = 0.0005 instead: the first step is still exactly η. Adam's step size is set by η, not by how loud the gradient is.

🎮 Playground · Race: gradient descent vs momentum vs Adam

The loss is L = 0.02·w₁² + 2·w₂²: a ravine whose walls curve 100× more sharply across (up–down) than along (left–right): 2 vs 0.02. (Its oval contour lines are 10× narrower than they are long.) All three start at the same spot with the same η. Drag the start, then Step or Run. Push η past 0.5 and watch plain gradient descent explode.

At η = 0.45, plain gradient descent rattles across the ravine, then creeps along the floor: it needs about 100 steps to settle. Momentum (β = 0.8) swings across the valley a few times, since a heavy ball overshoots, but races along the floor and settles in under 30. Adam heads almost straight for the centre, overshoots a little and settles in about 50. Things to try: set β = 0 and momentum becomes plain gradient descent again; set β = 0.9 and it arrives sooner but rattles around longer; push η to 0.6 and only gradient descent blows up, because Adam's steps stay about η in size however steep the slope.

💡 Big idea Momentum averages gradients over time: agreeing pushes add up, disagreeing ones cancel. Adam also divides by each weight's typical gradient size, so every weight takes steps of about η. Both make training less sensitive to the landscape's awkward shapes.

Learning-rate schedules: warm up, then cool down

A single fixed η is a compromise. At the very start, the weights are random and Adam's averages are based on only a few steps, so big steps are risky. Near the end, you want small steps to settle into the bottom instead of jittering around it (remember the mini-batch noise from Lesson 7.2). So LLMs change η over time:

  • Warmup: grow η in a straight line from 0 to the peak over the first few thousand steps.
  • Cosine decay: then glide down along half a cosine wave to a small final value (LLaMA: 10% of the peak).
ηt = ηmin + ½ (ηpeak − ηmin) (1 + cos(π · f)) Read: f is the “fraction done” of the decay, from 0 (start) to 1 (end). cos(π · f) slides from 1 down to −1, so η glides from the peak down to the minimum.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, torch
# Adam by hand, for any array of weights w and gradient g
m, v, t = np.zeros_like(w), np.zeros_like(w), 0
def adam_step(w, g, lr=1e-3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    global m, v, t; t += 1
    m = b1*m + (1-b1)*g;  v = b2*v + (1-b2)*g**2
    return w - lr * (m/(1-b1**t)) / (np.sqrt(v/(1-b2**t)) + eps)

# PyTorch: the LLM recipe = AdamW + warmup + cosine
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95), weight_decay=0.1)
sgd = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)      # heavy ball
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100_000, eta_min=3e-5)
# each step: loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step(); opt.zero_grad()

🌍 Where it's used
Momentum: the heavy ball 📜 History · 1964 Gabriel Goh, Why Momentum Really Works (Distill)

How: Polyak's 1964 “heavy ball” method adds the velocity term you saw above. Today it's the familiar momentum=0.9 in SGD training of image models; the ImageNet-in-an-hour run from Lesson 7.2 used momentum 0.9.

Why this method: it costs one extra number per weight and fixes the ravine problem; the Distill article shows interactively how it speeds up the slow directions.

Adam: per-weight step sizes 🖼️ Deep learning · 2014 Kingma & Ba, Adam

How: the two running averages and bias correction above, with suggested defaults η = 0.001, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸.

Why this method: the paper notes it's invariant to rescaling the gradients, that its steps are roughly bounded by η, and that its settings typically require little tuning. One recipe works across very different layers and models.

AdamW + warmup + cosine for LLMs 🧠 LLMs · 2023 LLaMA (Touvron et al.)

How: AdamW with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, a peak learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴ for the 7B model, 2,000 warmup steps, cosine decay to 10% of the peak, weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0 (Lesson 1.4: a gradient longer than 1 is shrunk to length 1), batches of 4M tokens. GPT-3 also used β₂ = 0.95 with cosine decay to 10%.

Why this method: AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter) fixes how Adam handles weight decay (Lesson 7.4). The schedule spends the big steps where they help and the small steps where precision matters.

Warmup in the original Transformer 🧠 LLMs · 2017 Attention Is All You Need

How: Adam with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.98, and a learning rate that rises in a straight line for 4,000 steps, then slowly shrinks for the rest of training.

Why this method: early on the model and Adam's averages are unreliable, so tiny steps first; once training is stable, large steps, shrinking as the model gets close.

Cosine annealing 🖼️ Deep learning · 2016 SGDR: SGD with Warm Restarts

How: within each run, decay the learning rate along a cosine curve, optionally restarting at the top. The cosine shape is what LLM schedules use today, usually without the restarts. PyTorch ships it as CosineAnnealingLR.

Why this method: one smooth curve with no step dates to pick. It stays high for a while, then spends a long, gentle tail at small values.

🤯 Fun fact · without the hats, Adam's first step is 3× too big

Drop the bias correction and the first step is η · 0.1g / √(0.001g²) = η · 0.1 / 0.0316 ≈ 3.16 η, for any gradient g. The little hats on m̂ and v̂ are there to stop that early lurch. (Check: 0.1 / √0.001 = 3.162.)

🌉 AI bridge · optimizers in RL and LLMs

RL: PPO's paper trains with Adam (step size 3 × 10⁻⁴ in its MuJoCo experiments), and the RL course's Lesson 5.6: TRPO → PPO explains why each update must stay small. DQN used RMSProp, which is Adam's “divide by the typical size” half without the momentum half (RL Lesson 4.3).

LLMs: Adam stores two extra numbers (m and v) for every weight, so a 7-billion-weight model carries 14 billion extra optimizer numbers during training. Next: the “W” in AdamW, weight decay (Lesson 7.4).

Check yourself

1. Momentum with β = 0.9. The velocity is m = 2 and the new gradient is g = 1. What's the new velocity?
m ← β·m + g = 0.9 × 2 + 1 = 1.8 + 1 = 2.8. The ball keeps 90% of its speed and gets one more push.
2. Weight A always gets gradients near 10; weight B always gets gradients near 0.01. With Adam (same η), how do their steps compare?
m̂/√v̂ ≈ 10/√(10²) = 1 for A and 0.01/√(0.01²) = 1 for B. Plain SGD would move A 1,000× more than B.
3. Why do LLM training runs start with a few thousand warmup steps?
The Transformer paper rose linearly for 4,000 steps; LLaMA used 2,000. Goyal et al. (Lesson 7.2) also needed warmup because big steps fail while the network is changing fast early in training.
📝 TL;DR
  • Momentum: m ← βm + g, step along m. Agreeing gradients add up, flip-flopping ones cancel.
  • Adam: running averages of g (m) and g² (v) per weight; step η·m̂/√v̂ ≈ η for every weight. Defaults 0.001, 0.9, 0.999, 10⁻⁸.
  • Schedules: warmup (linear rise), then cosine decay to a small final value.
  • The LLM recipe: AdamW, β₂ = 0.95, warmup + cosine to 10%, weight decay 0.1 (LLaMA, GPT-3).
