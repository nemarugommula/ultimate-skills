Integrals: area & adding up tiny pieces

Chop a shape into thin strips and add them up: that's an integral. It turns probability densities into chances, gives classifiers their AUC score, and is the reverse of the derivative.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Σ (“sigma”) means “add these up”, like a for-loop with a running total (Lesson 0.5).
  • The derivative is a slope: how fast something changes (Lesson 3.1).
🎯 Hook

A spam filter's report card says “AUC = 0.93”. A car's trip computer turns a stream of speed readings into kilometres driven. A statistics book says about 68% of adult heights fall within one “standard deviation” of the average.

All three numbers are areas under a curve. Computing an area under a curve is what an integral does, and the method is so simple you could do it with a ruler: slice it into thin rectangles and add them up.

Numbers first: distance from speed

You walk at 2 m/s for 3 seconds, then jog at 4 m/s for 2 seconds. How far did you go?

2 × 3  +  4 × 2  =  6 + 8  =  14 m

Draw speed on a graph against time: two flat steps. Each “speed × time” is the area of a rectangle under the graph. So distance = area under the speed curve. When the speed changes smoothly, slice time into thin strips, treat the speed as flat on each one, and add up the rectangles. Thinner strips, better answer.

Area under x², strip by strip

What's the area under y = x² from 0 to 1? Use 4 strips of width 0.25, each as tall as the curve at its right edge:

0.25 × (0.25² + 0.5² + 0.75² + 1²) = 0.25 × (0.0625 + 0.25 + 0.5625 + 1) = 0.46875

The sums close in on exactly 1/3. That limit is the integral.

∫ab f(x) dx  ≈  Σi f(xi) · Δx Read “the integral from a to b of f of x, d x”. The ∫ is a stretched S for “sum”: add up height f(x) times width dx over all the strips from a to b. Δx (“delta x”) is the strip width; dx is the same idea with the strips made infinitely thin.
💡 Big idea An integral is a sum of very many tiny pieces: height × width, added up. It measures the total amount: total distance from speed, total probability from a density, total area under a ROC curve.
🧠 Analogy: the tiled floor

To find the area of an oddly shaped room, cover it with square tiles and count them. Big tiles leave gaps at the curved wall; smaller tiles fit better. An integral is what you'd get with infinitely small tiles. And like a running tally on a cash register, the total at any moment is everything added so far.

🎮 Playground · Rectangles → area

Slide n up and watch the rectangles hug the curve and the sum settle. Switch where each rectangle measures its height (left, middle, right edge): the middle is far more accurate. Then pick the bell curve and the ±1 preset.

Area = probability

The bell curve is a probability density: a curve whose total area is exactly 1 (100%). The chance that a value lands between a and b is the area under the curve between a and b. With the playground's ±1 preset you get about 0.683: 68% of values fall within one standard deviation (a typical distance from the average) of the average (Lesson 4.3 unpacks this).

Big theorem, small box integrals undo derivatives

Keep a running total of the area under x², starting at 0. Up to t, the total is t³ / 3 (at t = 1: 1/3, matching the table).

Now take the slope of that running total with Lesson 3.2's power rule: d(t³/3)/dt = 3t²/3 = t². The curve you started with! The running total grows fastest exactly where the curve is tallest.

Same with travel: the slope of distance is speed, and the area under speed is distance. This pairing is the fundamental theorem of calculus: integrating and differentiating undo each other.

Area as a score: the ROC curve

A spam filter gives each email a score and flags everything above a threshold. Slide the threshold and two numbers change: how much spam is caught, and how much normal mail (“ham”) is wrongly flagged.

A ROC curve plots spam caught (up) against ham wrongly flagged (across), one point for every threshold. AUC (“area under the curve”) is its area: 1.0 for a perfect filter, 0.5 for a coin flip. When positives are rare, the precision–recall curve of Lesson 5.6b tells a more honest story.

🎮 Playground · Build a ROC curve and measure its area

A classifier gives each email a score. Ham scores (blue) and spam scores (pink) are two bell curves. Move the threshold: everything to its right is flagged as spam. Shaded areas = the fraction of spam caught (pink) and of ham wrongly flagged (blue). Then change how well the classifier separates them and watch the AUC.

Two ways to get the same number: add up thin strips under the ROC curve (an integral), or count how often a random spam email outscores a random ham one (a probability). The random-pairs button is a Monte Carlo estimate: approximating an integral by sampling instead of slicing (Lesson 4.6).

✏️ Worked example: AUC by hand

A classifier gives 4 emails spam scores. Two are ham (scores 0.1 and 0.4) and two are spam (0.35 and 0.8). Use the second meaning of AUC, the chance that a random spam email scores higher than a random ham email, and count the 2 × 2 = 4 pairs:

  • 0.35 vs 0.1 ✓ · 0.35 vs 0.4 ✗ · 0.8 vs 0.1 ✓ · 0.8 vs 0.4 ✓
  • 3 wins out of 4: AUC = 0.75. (scikit-learn's roc_auc_score gives 0.75 on these numbers too.)

1.0 is a perfect ranking; 0.5 is a coin flip.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
n = 1000; dx = 1 / n
x = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * dx                # midpoints of 1000 strips on [0, 1]
np.sum(x**2 * dx)                         # ≈ 0.3333333 (exact: 1/3)

z = np.linspace(-1, 1, 2001)
bell = np.exp(-z**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)
np.trapezoid(bell, z)                       # ≈ 0.6827: 68% within ±1 std

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
roc_auc_score([0, 0, 1, 1], [0.1, 0.4, 0.35, 0.8])   # 0.75

🌍 Where it's used
Probability = area under a density 📊 Data science · 1809 The 68–95–99.7 rule

How: for a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, the integral of its density from −1 to +1 standard deviation is 0.683. Gauss used this curve in 1809 to justify the method of least squares. Data scientists use it to flag outliers and set error bars.

Why this method: a density gives the chance of every range at once; one integral answers “how likely is anything between a and b?”.

ROC AUC: one number for a classifier 🤖 Machine learning · 1941 scikit-learn roc_auc_score · ROC history

How: sweep the threshold, plot the catch rate against the false-alarm rate, and integrate. ROC curves began with WWII radar operators (from 1941); today the area under them is a standard score for fraud, spam and medical-test models.

Why this method: accuracy depends on one chosen threshold. AUC averages over all thresholds and equals the chance a random positive is ranked above a random negative.

Integrated gradients: explaining a prediction 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 Sundararajan, Taly & Yan

How: fade an input in from a blank baseline (say a black image) and integrate the gradient (Lesson 3.3) along the way. In practice it's a rectangle sum: the paper finds 20 to 300 steps approximate the integral to within 5%.

Why this method: a single gradient only looks at one point and can be ~0 for a feature that clearly matters; adding up gradients along the whole path doesn't miss it.

🤯 Fun fact · ∫ is an S

Leibniz introduced the ∫ symbol in his private notes in 1675 (a manuscript dated 29 October). It's a stretched ſ, the old “long s”, because he thought of an integral as a sum of infinitely many tiny pieces. Same idea as Σ, just with the pieces made infinitely thin.

Check yourself

1. A robot moves at 3 m/s for 2 seconds, then 1 m/s for 4 seconds. How far did it go (the area under its speed graph)?
Two rectangles: 3 × 2 = 6 and 1 × 4 = 4, total 10 m. Area under speed = distance.
2. A fraud model has ROC AUC = 0.5. What does that mean?
AUC = the chance a random positive outscores a random negative. 0.5 is pure guessing (the diagonal ROC line), 1.0 is a perfect ranking. Try separation d = 0 in the playground.
3. Integrated gradients approximates an integral with m rectangles. What happens as m grows from 20 to 300?
Thinner strips hug the curve better, exactly like the x² table (0.47 → 0.385 → 0.338 → 1/3). Each extra strip costs one more gradient, so the paper suggests checking until the answer stops changing much.
📝 TL;DR
  • An integral ∫ab f(x) dx = area under f from a to b = sum of thin rectangles, height × width.
  • More (thinner) strips → closer to the exact answer. Middle-height strips converge fastest.
  • Integrals undo derivatives: the slope of the running total is the original curve.
  • Used for: probability = area under a density (68% within ±1 std), ROC AUC, integrated gradients, Monte Carlo estimates.
