Phase 3 · Recap

Calculus: how things change

Six ideas, one story: measure how the loss reacts to each knob, multiply slopes along the chain, and walk downhill. Plus integrals, for adding things up.

🧾 Cheat sheet

3.1 Derivative

f′(x) ≈ (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h

Slope = sensitivity: change in output ≈ f′ × change in input.

Gradient checking, ReLU's 0-or-1 slope, finance's delta.

3.2 Chain rule

dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx

Along a chain, local slopes multiply, like gear ratios. σ′ = σ(1 − σ) ≤ 0.25.

Vanishing gradients, ReLU (AlexNet), skip connections (ResNet).

3.3 Gradient

∇f = [∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, …]

One partial slope per input; points steepest uphill. −∇f points downhill.

Training (∇ over all weights), saliency maps, FGSM attacks.

3.4 Gradient descent

w ← w − η ∇L

Step against the slope. η (eta) too big → zig-zag or blow-up.

Every neural network and LLM; RL policy gradients go uphill (+).

3.4b Taylor & curvature

f(x+h) ≈ f + f′h + ½f″h² · η < 2/c

Up close a loss is a line (what gradient descent trusts) plus a bend; the curvature c caps the safe step. Newton steps by f′/f″.

Learning-rate limits and warmup, L-BFGS, Shampoo/K-FAC, SAM, TRPO.

3.5 Backprop

grad in = grad from the right × local slope

Forward pass computes values; backward pass gives every slope at once.

loss.backward(); training ≈ 3× a forward pass (6N vs 2N).

3.6 Integral

∫ab f(x) dx ≈ Σ f(xi) Δx

Area = sum of thin rectangles. Undoes the derivative.

Probability from a density (68% within ±1 std), ROC AUC, Monte Carlo.

🧩 Use it yourself

🧩 Problem: train a café's bill predictor by hand

A café predicts a bill from the number of items: ŷ = w × items. A customer bought 2 items and paid $6. The model starts at w = 1 and uses the squared-error loss L = (w × 2 − 6)².

  1. What's the loss right now?
  2. Use backprop to find ∂L/∂w, then confirm it with a nudge.
  3. Take two gradient-descent steps with η = 0.05. Does the loss drop?
  4. What's the largest learning rate that doesn't blow up? Test η = 0.3.
Reveal the worked solution

1. ŷ = 1 × 2 = 2, error e = 2 − 6 = −4, loss = (−4)² = 16.

2. Backward: the square box gives ∂L/∂e = 2e = −8. The multiply box hands w the gradient × its input: ∂L/∂w = −8 × 2 = −16. Nudge: (L(1.001) − L(0.999)) / 0.002 = −16.000 ✓. Negative: raising w lowers the loss.

3. Step 1: w = 1 − 0.05 × (−16) = 1.8, loss (3.6 − 6)² = 5.76. Step 2: e = −2.4, ∂L/∂w = 2 × (−2.4) × 2 = −9.6, w = 1.8 + 0.48 = 2.28, loss (4.56 − 6)² ≈ 2.07. The gap to the answer w = 3 goes 2 → 1.2 → 0.72: each step closes 40%.

4. The slope is 2 × (2w − 6) × 2 = 8(w − 3), so each step multiplies the gap by (1 − 8η). That stays between −1 and 1 only while η < 0.25. With η = 0.3 the factor is −1.4: w = 5.8 → −0.92 → 8.49, flying apart. Largest safe η: just under 0.25 (and η = 0.125 lands on w = 3 in one step).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. (Chain rule) What is the slope of y = σ(3x) at x = 0? (σ′(0) = 0.25)
Inner slope 3, outer slope σ′(0) = 0.25. Chain rule: 0.25 × 3 = 0.75.
2. (Gradient descent) Weights w = [1, 1], gradient ∇L = [2, −4], η = 0.5. What are the new weights?
w − η∇L = [1 − 0.5 × 2, 1 − 0.5 × (−4)] = [1 − 1, 1 + 2] = [0, 3]. Each weight moves against its own slope.
3. (Backprop) A multiply box a · b has a = 4, b = 0.5 and receives the gradient −2. What does a get?
The multiply box swaps: a gets the gradient × the other input, −2 × 0.5 = −1. (b gets −2 × 4 = −8.)
4. (Integrals) For a bell-shaped (normal) distribution, what is the area under the density between −2 and +2 standard deviations?
±1 std holds about 68.3%, ±2 about 95.4%, ±3 about 99.7%. The whole curve holds 100%, but only from −∞ to +∞.
5. (Where it's used) Why could AlexNet and ResNet train deeper networks than earlier sigmoid networks?
That's the chain rule (3.2) meeting backprop (3.5). Ten sigmoid layers can shrink the signal to below a millionth; slope-1 paths don't shrink it at all.
📝 TL;DR
  • Derivative = slope = sensitivity. Chain rule: slopes multiply. Gradient = all the slopes in one vector.
  • Gradient descent w ← w − η∇L trains everything; backprop computes ∇L cheaply.
  • Integrals add up tiny pieces: areas, probabilities, AUC.
