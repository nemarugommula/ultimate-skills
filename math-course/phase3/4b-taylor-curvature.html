Taylor approximation & curvature: why descent works

Zoom in on any smooth curve and it looks like a line; add its bend and it looks like a parabola. Those two pictures explain why gradient descent works, how big a learning rate is safe, and what “second-order” optimizers like Newton, L-BFGS and Shampoo do.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The derivative f′(x) is the slope: change in output ≈ slope × change in input (Lesson 3.1).
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − η · slope, where η (“eta”) is the learning rate (Lesson 3.4).
  • On the bowl (w − 3)², η = 1.0 bounced forever and η = 1.1 exploded (Lesson 3.4).
🎯 Hook

In Lesson 3.4 the bowl (w − 3)² had a sharp cliff edge: η = 1.0 bounced forever and 1.1 blew up. Why exactly 1? Make the bowl ten times steeper, 10(w − 3)², and the edge moves to 0.1.

It isn't luck. The limit is 2 divided by how sharply the bowl bends. By the end of this lesson you'll predict the biggest safe learning rate from that one number. You'll also see why a 2021 study found that neural-network training tends to sit right at that edge.

Numbers first: zoom in until it's a line

Take f(x) = x² at x = 3. You know two things there: the height, f(3) = 9, and the slope, f′(3) = 2 × 3 = 6. Pretend the curve were a straight line with that slope and predict the height a small step h away: 9 + 6h.

f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x) · h “The value a small step h away is about the value here, plus the slope times the step.” ≈ means “approximately equal”. This is the first-order Taylor approximation: “first-order” because it uses the first derivative.

Gradient descent trusts this line

A gradient-descent step is h = −η · slope. Plug that into the line: the loss changes by slope × h = −η × slope², which is always negative. That's Lesson 3.4's “why it works” box, and now you know its name: it's the first-order Taylor approximation.

But the line is only honest for small steps. On (w − 3)² at w = 0 with η = 0.1, the line promised a drop of 3.6 and the real drop was 3.24. The missing 0.36 is the bend the line ignores. The bend is what decides when a step is too big.

Add the bend: the second derivative

Look at how the slope of x² changes: at x = 1 it's 2, at x = 2 it's 4, at x = 3 it's 6. It grows by 2 for every step of 1. The slope of the slope is 2. That's the second derivative, f″(x) (“f double prime”), and it measures curvature: how fast the slope turns.

  • f″ > 0: the curve smiles, a bowl (a valley floor). (w − 3)² has f″ = 2; 10(w − 3)² has 20.
  • f″ < 0: it frowns, a dome (a hilltop).
  • f″ = 0: no bend, a straight line.

Add a curvature term to the line and you get a parabola that hugs the curve much better:

f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x) · h + ½ · f″(x) · h² “Value nearby ≈ value here, plus slope times step, plus half the curvature times the step squared.” This is the second-order Taylor approximation.

Try it on eˣ at x = 0, where the height, slope and curvature are all 1 (eˣ is its own slope, Lesson 3.2). Step h = 0.5: the line says 1 + 0.5 = 1.5, the parabola says 1.5 + ½ × 0.25 = 1.625, and the truth is e0.5 = 1.6487. The line is off by 0.149, the parabola by only 0.024. For x² the parabola is exact: 9 + 6h + h² is (3 + h)².

💡 Big idea Up close, every smooth loss looks like a line (slope only) and, a bit less close, like a parabola (slope + curvature). Gradient descent uses the line to pick a direction. The curvature decides how far the line can be trusted, so it sets the biggest safe step.
🧠 Analogy: the physics formula you already know

Where will a car be in t seconds? Position now, plus speed × t, plus ½ × acceleration × t². That's the second-order Taylor formula. The speedometer is the slope. The accelerator pedal is the curvature. For one second ahead, “position + speed × time” is nearly right. For a minute ahead, you'd better know whether the driver is braking.

Curvature sets the speed limit

Now the payoff. Take the simplest bowl, f(w) = ½ · c · w², bottom at w = 0. Its slope is c · w and its curvature is exactly c everywhere. One gradient-descent step:

w ← w − η · c · w  =  (1 − ηc) · w

Every step multiplies w by the same number, 1 − ηc. With c = 4, starting at w = 1:

The size of w shrinks only while the factor stays between −1 and 1. That happens exactly when η · c is between 0 and 2:

gradient descent settles only if  η < 2 / c “The learning rate must be less than 2 divided by the curvature.” A bowl that bends twice as sharply allows only half the step size. The single-jump rate is η = 1 / c.

Check against Lesson 3.4: (w − 3)² is ½ · 2 · (w − 3)², so c = 2 and the limit is 2 / 2 = 1. That's why 1.0 bounced and 1.1 exploded. For 10(w − 3)², c = 20 and the limit is 0.1. A real loss isn't a perfect bowl, but near its bottom it looks like one: that's the second-order Taylor picture, and its c sets the limit.

✏️ Worked example: same bowl, two learning rates

Loss ½ · 4 · w², start w = 1 (loss 2). The limit is 2 / 4 = 0.5.

  • η = 0.4: factor 1 − 0.4 × 4 = −0.6. w: 1 → −0.6 → 0.36 → −0.216. Loss: 2 → 0.72 → 0.2592 → 0.0933. It flips sides every step but gets closer.
  • η = 0.6: factor −1.4. w: 1 → −1.4 → 1.96 → −2.744. Loss: 2 → 3.92 → 7.68 → 15.06. Each step almost doubles the loss (1.4² = 1.96). That's a training run heading for NaN.
  • Fun twist: η = 0.1 (factor +0.6) and η = 0.4 (factor −0.6) shrink w equally fast. One slides, the other zig-zags.

Narrow valleys: one learning rate, two curvatures

A real network has many directions, and they bend by very different amounts. Lesson 3.4's narrow valley, x² + 10y², has curvature 2 along x and 20 along y. One η serves both, and the steep direction sets the limit: η < 2 / 20 = 0.1.

At η = 0.09, the steep direction's factor is 1 − 0.09 × 20 = −0.8 (zig-zag) while the flat direction's is 1 − 0.09 × 2 = 0.82 (a slow crawl). That's the zig-zag you saw. Make the gap 100× (curvatures 1 and 100) and the best you can do is η just under 0.02: the flat direction then shrinks by only 2% a step and needs about 230 steps to get 100× closer. Momentum and Adam (Lesson 7.3) were invented for exactly this.

Many knobs: the Hessian, a table of curvatures

With two weights, “how sharply does it bend?” has an answer for each pair of directions. Put them in a 2 × 2 table and you have the Hessian, written H:

f = x² + 10y²  →  H = [ 2   0 ; 0   20 ]   ·   f = x² + xy + 3y²  →  H = [ 2   1 ; 1   6 ]

Read the table like this: top-left is the curvature along x (the second derivative ∂²f/∂x², “partial squared f by x twice”, Lesson 3.3's ∂ used twice), bottom-right is the curvature along y, and the off-diagonal entries say how the slope in x changes when you move in y. The xy term gives 1 there: it tilts the valley.

The number that matters for the learning rate is the curvature of the steepest direction. People call it the sharpness. For the first table it's simply 20. For the tilted one it's about 6.24 (a bit more than 6, because of the tilt). Those are the table's eigenvalues, and the valley's axes are its eigenvectors (Lesson 2.5). The rule becomes η < 2 / sharpness.

For a model with N weights, the Hessian has N × N entries. For GPT-3's 175 billion weights that's about 3 × 10²² numbers. Nobody stores it. Everything in “Where it's used” below is a trick to get some of its benefit without building it.

Newton's method: jump to the bottom of the parabola

If the parabola f + f′h + ½f″h² is a good picture of the loss, why crawl? Jump straight to its lowest point. A parabola is lowest where its own slope, f′ + f″h, is zero: at h = −f′ / f″.

w ← w − f′(w) / f″(w) “Step by the slope divided by the curvature.” It's gradient descent with the learning rate picked for you: η = 1 / curvature. Steep bend, small step; gentle bend, big step.

On a perfect bowl it lands at the bottom in one jump (the η = 0.25 row above). On f(w) = eʷ − 2w, whose bottom is at w = ln 2 = 0.693147…, starting at 0, the distance to the bottom goes 0.31 → 0.043 → 0.0009 → 0.0000004. The number of correct digits roughly doubles every step. Plain gradient descent with η = 0.1 needs 63 steps to get as close as Newton's 4.

With many weights, Newton divides each direction by its own curvature (by the Hessian's “inverse”, Lesson 2.4). On x² + 10y² that means x moves by −2x / 2 = −x and y by −20y / 20 = −y: from any start it lands at (0, 0) in one step, with no zig-zag. Two catches. On a hilltop (f″ < 0) the parabola frowns, so Newton jumps to its top; try it in the first playground. And the Hessian is far too big for deep networks.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, torch
from scipy.optimize import minimize
x, h = 0.0, 0.5                                 # e^x: height, slope, curvature all = e^x
np.exp(x) + np.exp(x)*h                        # 1.5    line (1st order)
np.exp(x) + np.exp(x)*h + 0.5*np.exp(x)*h**2   # 1.625  parabola (2nd order); truth 1.6487

c = 4.0                                        # loss ½·c·w², limit 2/c = 0.5
for eta in [0.1, 0.4, 0.6]:
    print(eta, [(1 - eta*c)**k for k in range(4)])   # slide, zig-zag, explode

w = 0.0                                        # Newton on e^w − 2w
for _ in range(4): w -= (np.exp(w) - 2) / np.exp(w)   # slope ÷ curvature → 0.693148
minimize(lambda v: np.exp(v[0]) - 2*v[0], [0.0], method="L-BFGS-B").x   # quasi-Newton: [0.693]
torch.autograd.functional.hessian(lambda p: p[0]**2 + 10*p[1]**2, torch.ones(2))  # [[2,0],[0,20]]

🌍 Where it's used
η < 2 / sharpness: why learning-rate warmup helps 🖼️ Deep learning · 2021 Gilmer et al.: A Loss Curvature Perspective on Training Instability

How: they tracked the sharpness (the Hessian's biggest curvature) during training of many models, transformers included. Runs went unstable when it exceeded 2 / η. Warmup starts with a tiny η and raises it over the first steps; they found it gradually lowers the sharpness, so the model reaches flatter ground before the full η arrives. GPT-3 warmed up over its first 375 million tokens (Lesson 3.4).

Why this method: a tiny η forever is safe but slow. Warmup pays for safety only early on, when the curvature is highest. The paper found warmup improved stability as much as several fancier initialization and normalization fixes.

Quasi-Newton: L-BFGS 🤖 Machine learning · 1989 scikit-learn LogisticRegression · SciPy L-BFGS-B

How: L-BFGS (Nocedal 1980; Liu & Nocedal 1989) never builds the Hessian. It watches how the gradient changed over the last few steps (a change in slope per change in position is a curvature) and uses that to take Newton-like steps. It's scikit-learn's default solver for logistic regression, which also offers true Newton solvers (“newton-cg”, “newton-cholesky”).

Why this method: for classic models fit on the whole dataset at once, curvature-aware steps need far fewer iterations than plain gradient descent, and there's no learning rate to tune.

Second-order-inspired optimizers: Shampoo, K-FAC 🖼️ Deep learning · 2018 Gupta, Koren & Singer: Shampoo · Martens & Grosse: K-FAC

How: instead of one giant table, Shampoo keeps a small matrix for each side of every weight matrix (one for its rows, one for its columns) and uses them to reshape the gradient step, like a cheap Newton. K-FAC (2015) approximates each layer's curvature block as a combination of two much smaller matrices.

Why this method: the full Hessian is impossible (3 × 10²² entries for GPT-3), but ignoring curvature means zig-zagging in narrow valleys. Shampoo's paper reports converging considerably faster than common optimizers, at a per-step cost comparable to SGD, AdaGrad and Adam.

Sharpness & flat minima: SAM 🖼️ Deep learning · 2020 Foret et al.: Sharpness-Aware Minimization

How: each step first nudges the weights a small distance uphill along the gradient (roughly the worst nearby point), computes the gradient there, and applies that gradient back at the original weights. That's two backward passes per update. It looks for valleys whose whole neighbourhood is low: in the paper's words, “both low loss and low curvature”.

Why this method: two models with the same training loss can behave differently on new data. Minimizing the loss alone ignores the bend; SAM improved results on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet and fine-tuning tasks.

Linearizing a policy: TRPO 🎮 RL · 2015 Schulman et al.: Trust Region Policy Optimization

How: TRPO replaces its objective with a linear approximation (first-order Taylor) and its “don't change the policy too much” limit, measured by KL divergence (Lesson 6.4), with a quadratic one (second-order Taylor; that curvature table is called the Fisher information matrix). It solves that simple problem, then a line search checks the real one.

Why this method: the true objective is too complicated to optimize directly. The Taylor versions can be solved almost exactly, and the limit keeps each step small enough for them to stay honest. PPO later swapped all that math for a simple clip (RL course 5.6).

🤯 Fun fact · training lives on the edge

Cohen et al. (ICLR 2021) trained neural networks with full-batch gradient descent and tracked the sharpness. It kept rising until it reached 2 / η, exactly the limit from this lesson, and then hovered just above it. The loss bounced up and down over short stretches yet kept falling over the long run. They named it the “Edge of Stability”. It's as if training drifts toward the sharpest ground its learning rate can tolerate.

🌉 AI bridge · lines, parabolas and trust

Deep learning & LLMs: every optimizer is a bet on a Taylor picture. SGD trusts the line. Adam (Lesson 7.3) rescales each weight's step; its paper notes that its running average of squared gradients approximates the diagonal of the Fisher matrix, a curvature-like table. Shampoo goes further. Saddles and loss landscapes return in Lesson 7.1.

RL: TRPO's “linear objective, quadratic limit” is the same story, and its expensive second-order math is why PPO was invented. See RL Lesson 5.6: Trust regions, TRPO → PPO. Next here: computing the slope itself fast, backpropagation (Lesson 3.5).

Check yourself

1. Near the bottom of a loss valley the curvature is c = 50. Above which learning rate will plain gradient descent blow up?
The limit is 2 / c = 2 / 50 = 0.04. 0.02 = 1 / c is the one-jump rate; between 0.02 and 0.04 it still settles, but zig-zags. Above 0.04 each step multiplies the distance by more than 1 and it explodes.
2. A valley curves 100× more sharply in one direction than in another. Why is plain gradient descent slow there?
With curvatures 1 and 100, η must be under 0.02. The flat direction then shrinks by only about 2% a step, roughly 230 steps for a 100× improvement. Newton (divide each direction by its own curvature) or Adam-style per-weight steps help.
3. Newton's method converges in far fewer steps. Why don't LLMs train with it directly?
Curvature helps, which is why people build cheap stand-ins: L-BFGS (a few remembered gradients), Shampoo and K-FAC (small per-layer matrices), and Adam's per-weight scaling. On hilltops and saddles plain Newton can also jump the wrong way.
📝 TL;DR
  • 1st-order Taylor: f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′h, the tangent line. Gradient descent trusts it: loss change ≈ −η·slope².
  • 2nd-order: add ½f″h². The second derivative is curvature: + bowl, − hilltop.
  • On a bowl with curvature c, gradient descent settles only if η < 2/c (1/c = one jump). The sharpest direction sets the limit, so narrow valleys zig-zag and crawl.
  • Hessian = the table of curvatures; its biggest is the sharpness. Newton steps by slope ÷ curvature.
  • Used in: warmup and LR limits, L-BFGS, Shampoo/K-FAC, SAM (flat minima), TRPO's linearization.
