Partial derivatives & the gradient

When a function has many inputs, nudge them one at a time. The list of all those slopes is the gradient: an arrow pointing steepest uphill. It's how a network knows how to fix billions of weights at once.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Derivative = slope = “nudge the input, how much does the output move?” (Lesson 3.1).
  • A vector is a list of numbers and also an arrow, like [2, 6] (Lesson 1.1). Its length is ‖v‖ (Lesson 1.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2014, researchers took a photo of a panda that Google's image network classified as “panda” with 57.7% confidence. They added a faint layer of noise, invisible to a human: each pixel value changed by at most 0.007. The network now said “gibbon”, 99.3% confident.

The noise wasn't random. It was built from the gradient: for every pixel, which way to nudge it to make the network most wrong. Today you'll compute gradients and build a (tiny) attack yourself.

Numbers first: two knobs

Take a function of two inputs, f(x, y) = x² + 3y². Think of it as a loss with two weights. At the point (1, 1) it equals 1 + 3 = 4. Now nudge one input at a time, keeping the other frozen:

  • Nudge x to 1.01 (y stays 1): f = 1.0201 + 3 = 4.0201. Slope ≈ 0.0201 / 0.01 = 2.01 ≈ 2.
  • Nudge y to 1.01 (x stays 1): f = 1 + 3.0603 = 4.0603. Slope ≈ 0.0603 / 0.01 = 6.03 ≈ 6.

Each of those is a partial derivative: the slope in one direction while everything else holds still. Pack them into a list and you have the gradient: [2, 6].

∇f = [ ∂f/∂x , ∂f/∂y ] = [ 2x , 6y ] “The gradient of f is the list of its partial derivatives.” ∂ is a curly d, read “partial”: ∂f/∂x is “partial f, partial x”, the slope in the x direction with y frozen. ∇ is read “nabla” or just “grad”.

How do you get 2x and 6y with the Lesson 3.2 rules? Treat the frozen input as a plain number. For ∂f/∂x, 3y² is a constant (slope 0) and x² gives 2x. For ∂f/∂y, x² is the constant and 3y² gives 6y.

💡 Big idea The gradient is a vector with one slope per input. As an arrow, it points in the direction of steepest uphill, and its length says how steep. So −∇f, the opposite arrow, is the fastest way downhill: the direction that shrinks the loss fastest.
🧠 Analogy: hiking in fog

You're on a hillside in thick fog and can only feel the ground under your feet. Take one small step east: the ground rises 2 cm. One small step north: it rises 6 cm. You now know the slope in every direction. The steepest way up is mostly north, a bit east: the arrow [2, 6]. To get down to the valley fast, walk the opposite way.

✏️ Worked example: gradient at (2, −1)

Same f(x, y) = x² + 3y². At (2, −1):

  • ∂f/∂x = 2x = 2 × 2 = 4. Moving right goes up.
  • ∂f/∂y = 6y = 6 × (−1) = −6. Moving up (+y) goes down in loss.
  • ∇f = [4, −6]. Steepness: √(16 + 36) = √52 ≈ 7.2.
  • Downhill direction: −∇f = [−4, 6]: decrease x, increase y. Drag the dot to (2, −1) in the playground and check.

Gradients of a model's input: saliency and attacks

So far the inputs were weights. But you can take the gradient with respect to anything, including the pixels of an image. That answers two questions: “which pixels matter most to the prediction?” (a saliency map) and “which tiny change fools the model?” (an adversarial example).

🎮 Playground · Fool a tiny plus-sign detector

A 25-pixel classifier scores an image with z = w · x + b (one weight per pixel) and says “✚ plus” when σ(z) > 0.5. Click pixels to draw. Show the saliency map (pink = big gradient). Then attack: every pixel moves by ε in the direction its gradient says will hurt the score most.

Here's why it works. The gradient of z with respect to pixel i is just its weight wi. FGSM keeps only its sign: sign(g) is +1 when g is positive and −1 when it's negative. Every pixel moves by ε the way that lowers z.

Three pixels with weights [2, −1, 0.5] and ε = 0.1: the pixels move by [−0.1, +0.1, −0.1], and z changes by 2 × (−0.1) + (−1) × 0.1 + 0.5 × (−0.1) = −0.35. That's 0.1 × (2 + 1 + 0.5): every pixel helps, by ε × |wi|, where |w| (“absolute value”) is w without its minus sign.

So the total drop is ε × (|w₁| + |w₂| + … ). With 25 pixels that needs a big ε. A real photo has about 150,000 pixel values, so a change of 0.007 each adds up to a huge push. That is the FGSM paper's own explanation.

🐍 In code 
import torch
p = torch.tensor([1.0, 1.0], requires_grad=True)   # the point (x, y) = (1, 1)
f = p[0]**2 + 3 * p[1]**2
f.backward()
p.grad                                       # tensor([2., 6.])  the gradient

# FGSM on a 3-pixel linear detector: gradient with respect to the INPUT
w = torch.tensor([2.0, -1.0, 0.5])               # one weight per pixel
x = torch.tensor([0.5, 0.5, 0.5], requires_grad=True)  # the image
z = w @ x; z.backward()                      # x.grad = w = [2, -1, 0.5]
x_adv = x - 0.1 * x.grad.sign()              # z drops by 0.1 × (2 + 1 + 0.5) = 0.35
🌍 Where it's used
One gradient, 175 billion entries 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3 (Brown et al.)

How: the loss is one number; the weights are 175 billion inputs. Each training step computes ∇L, a vector with one partial derivative per weight, and nudges every weight against its own entry.

Why this method: the gradient tells every weight what to do in one go. Trying knobs one at a time would need billions of extra runs per step.

Saliency maps: which pixels mattered? 🖼️ Deep learning · 2013 Simonyan, Vedaldi & Zisserman

How: take the gradient of the class score (say “dog”) with respect to the input pixels. Big entries = pixels where a small change would move the score a lot. Plot their sizes as an image and the dog lights up.

Why this method: it needs only a single backward pass (Lesson 3.5) through an already-trained network: no retraining, no extra labels.

Adversarial examples (FGSM) 🖼️ Deep learning · 2014 Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy

How: x_adv = x + ε · sign(∇xL): take the gradient of the loss with respect to the image and push every pixel by ε in the direction that increases the loss. With ε = 0.007, the panda became a 99.3%-confident gibbon.

Why this method: one gradient gives the best direction for all pixels at once, so the attack is fast (“fast gradient sign method”). Training on such examples (adversarial training) makes models sturdier.

Least squares: set every partial to zero 📊 Data science · 1805 Legendre's method of least squares

How: to fit a line, the minimum of the sum of squared errors is where the gradient is zero: ∂/∂slope = 0 and ∂/∂intercept = 0. Each fitted coefficient also reads as a partial derivative: “how much does the prediction change per unit of this feature, the others held fixed?”.

Why this method: for a straight-line fit those equations can be solved exactly, no step-by-step search needed. Neural networks have no such shortcut, so they walk downhill instead (Lesson 3.4).

🤯 Fun fact · the curly d

The ∂ symbol was created by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1786. He later abandoned it, and it only caught on after Carl Jacobi reintroduced it in 1841. Legendre is the same person who published least squares in 1805: the grandfather of every “fit a line” you'll ever do.

🌉 AI bridge · many knobs, one arrow

Deep learning: “the gradient” in any paper means ∇ of the loss with respect to all the weights. Lesson 3.4 walks against it (gradient descent) and Lesson 3.5 computes it fast (backprop). Loss landscapes and saddles return in Lesson 7.1.

RL: a policy gradient is ∇ of the expected reward with respect to the policy's weights, and you walk with it to get more reward. See RL Lesson 5.2: REINFORCE.

Check yourself

1. f(x, y) = x² + 3y². What is the gradient ∇f at (2, 1)?
∂f/∂x = 2x = 4 and ∂f/∂y = 6y = 6, so ∇f = [4, 6]. A gradient is a vector (one slope per input), not a single number. [2, 6] is the gradient at (1, 1).
2. The gradient of the loss with respect to the weights is [3, −1]. Which way should the weights move to lower the loss fastest?
The gradient points steepest uphill. To go down, walk the opposite way: −∇L = [−3, 1]. Decrease weight 1, increase weight 2.
3. A saliency map is the gradient of the class score with respect to what?
Training uses ∇ with respect to the weights. Saliency maps and adversarial attacks use ∇ with respect to the input, with the weights frozen. Same math, different knobs.
📝 TL;DR
  • Partial derivative ∂f/∂x: the slope along one input with all the others frozen.
  • Gradient ∇f = the vector of all partials. It points steepest uphill; its length is the steepness; −∇f points downhill.
  • Training: ∇ of the loss with respect to every weight (175 billion for GPT-3).
  • ∇ with respect to the input: saliency maps (which pixels matter) and FGSM attacks (x + ε·sign(∇xL)).
