Phase 3 · Lesson 4

Gradient descent: rolling downhill

One line of arithmetic, repeated millions of times: step every weight a little bit against its slope. This is how every neural network, every LLM and every policy-gradient RL agent learns.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The gradient ∇L lists the slope of the loss for each weight and points steepest uphill (Lesson 3.3).
  • Change in loss ≈ slope × change in weight, for small changes (Lesson 3.1).
🎯 Hook

GPT-3's 175 billion weights started out as random numbers. Nobody set them by hand. A computer repeated one tiny rule, “move each weight a little bit downhill”, over and over while reading 300 billion tokens of text.

And the most important number in that whole process was chosen by humans: how big each step is. Too small and training takes forever. Too big and it explodes. You're about to feel both.

Numbers first: find the bottom of (w − 3)²

Pretend you can't see the curve L(w) = (w − 3)². You only know your position and the slope under your feet, dL/dw = 2(w − 3). Start at w = 0 and use a step size of 0.1:

stepwslope 2(w − 3)new w = w − 0.1 × slopegap to 3
10−60 − 0.1 × (−6) = 0.62.4
20.6−4.80.6 + 0.48 = 1.081.92
31.08−3.841.08 + 0.384 = 1.4641.536

The slope is negative (downhill is to the right), so subtracting it moves w right. Each step closes 20% of the remaining gap: 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536. Keep going and w creeps to 3, where the slope is 0 and the steps stop by themselves.

w ← w − η · ∇L(w) “Replace the weights with: the weights, minus the learning rate times the gradient.” η is the Greek letter eta (“AY-tuh”), the learning rate or step size. The arrow ← means “becomes”, like = in code.
w: all the weightsη: the learning rate (step size)∇L: the gradient of the loss
💡 Big idea Gradient descent is a loop: measure the slope, take a small step downhill, repeat. Steep ground gives big steps and flat ground small ones, automatically, because the step is η × slope. It needs no map of the landscape, only the slope where you stand.
🧠 Analogy: a ball in a foggy valley

Put a ball on a hillside and it rolls downhill without “knowing” where the bottom is: it just responds to the slope under it. Gradient descent is a ball that moves in jumps. The learning rate is the length of the jumps. Small jumps: safe but slow. Huge jumps: the ball leaps clean over the valley and lands higher up the other side.

Press Step a few times, then Run. Try η = 0.01 (slow), 0.5 (one jump!), 1.0 (bounces forever) and 1.1 (explodes). On the two-valley curve, drag the start point and see which valley you end up in.

Why it works the 3-line version, with numbers

From Lesson 3.1: change in loss ≈ slope × change in w. Gradient descent picks change in w = −η × slope. Substitute: change in loss ≈ slope × (−η × slope) = −η × slope². A square is never negative, so for a small enough η the loss always goes down.

Check at w = 0 with η = 0.1: slope −6, predicted change −0.1 × 36 = −3.6. Real change: L(0) = 9, L(0.6) = 5.76, a drop of 3.24. Close, and the gap is because η × slope isn't “tiny”. With a big η the approximation breaks completely, which is why huge steps can go up. Lesson 3.4b shows exactly how big is too big: on a bowl with curvature c, η must stay below 2/c.

✏️ Worked example: three learning rates, same start (w = 0)
  • η = 0.5: w = 0 − 0.5 × (−6) = 3. Exactly at the bottom in one step (lucky: this only happens for this exact bowl).
  • η = 1.0: w = 0 + 6 = 6, slope +6, w = 6 − 6 = 0, … It bounces between 0 and 6 forever.
  • η = 1.1: w = 6.6 → −1.32 → 8.18 → … Each jump lands farther away: divergence. In a real training run this is the loss shooting up to “NaN”.

In two dimensions: bowls, narrow valleys and traps

With two weights, the same rule moves a point on a landscape (Lesson 3.3's map). Three famous shapes:

🎮 Playground · Gradient descent on a landscape

More teal = lower loss. Drag the orange start point, then press Run. On the narrow valley, raise η until the path zig-zags, then a bit more until it flies off. On two valleys, find two starting points that end in different valleys.

The narrow valley is the everyday reality of deep learning: some directions are steep, others nearly flat. A step size that is safe in the steep direction crawls in the flat one, and one that is fast in the flat direction zig-zags or explodes in the steep one. Momentum and Adam (Lesson 7.3) were invented to fix exactly this. The two valleys show the other worry: plain gradient descent stops in whichever valley it rolls into first, a local minimum.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1., 2, 3]); y = np.array([3., 5, 7])   # points on y = 2x + 1
w = b = 0.0; eta = 0.05
for step in range(2000):
    err = w * x + b - y                     # prediction − target
    w -= eta * np.mean(2 * err * x)          # ∂L/∂w of the mean squared error
    b -= eta * np.mean(2 * err)              # ∂L/∂b
print(w, b)                                 # ≈ 2.0, 1.0

# PyTorch: the same loop, with autograd computing the gradient
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.05)
loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()  # w ← w − η∇L, then reset

🌍 Where it's used
Training an LLM 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3 (Brown et al.)

How: every GPT-3 model was trained with Adam (gradient descent with momentum and per-weight step sizes, Lesson 7.3). The learning rate warms up linearly over the first 375 million tokens, then slowly decays.

Why this method: with 175 billion weights there's no formula for the best ones and no way to search. Following the gradient is the only approach that scales.

Gradient ascent in RL 🎮 RL · 1992 REINFORCE (Williams), in the RL course

How: in RL you want reward to go up, so policy-gradient methods step with the gradient: θ ← θ + α∇J (θ, “theta”, are the policy's weights, α, “alpha”, is the step size, J is the expected reward). In code it's the same optimizer, minimizing −J.

Why this method: the policy is a neural network, so the same gradient machinery that trains classifiers can push it toward actions that earned more reward.

The original idea 📜 History · 1847 Cauchy's gradient method

How: Augustin-Louis Cauchy suggested the method in 1847 as a general way to solve systems of equations: turn them into one quantity to minimize, then step against its slope.

Why this method: it needs only slopes, not a clever solution formula, so it works on problems nobody can solve by hand. That's still the reason, almost 180 years later.

🤯 Fun fact · bigger models take smaller steps

In the GPT-3 paper's table of models, the smallest (125 million weights) used a learning rate of 6.0 × 10⁻⁴, and the full 175-billion model used 0.6 × 10⁻⁴: ten times smaller. Big models are usually trained with smaller learning rates: with that many weights moving at once, a step size that's fine for a small model risks the “η too big” explosion you saw above.

🌉 AI bridge · the universal learning loop

Deep learning & LLMs: forward pass → loss → gradient → step. Phase 7 upgrades the step: mini-batches (SGD, Lesson 7.2), momentum and Adam, and learning-rate schedules (Lesson 7.3). Lesson 3.5 shows how the gradient itself is computed.

RL: RL Lesson 4.2 trains value networks by walking downhill, and RL Lesson 5.2: REINFORCE walks uphill on reward. The RL course writes the step size as α instead of η.

Check yourself

1. A weight is w = 2, its gradient is 4, and η = 0.1. What is w after one gradient-descent step?
w ← w − η × gradient = 2 − 0.1 × 4 = 2 − 0.4 = 1.6. A positive gradient means “uphill to the right”, so the weight moves left. 2.4 adds instead of subtracting: that's gradient ascent.
2. Your training loss goes 2.1, 3.5, 9.8, 41, NaN. What's the most likely fix?
Growing loss means each step overshoots the valley and lands higher up the other side, like η = 1.1 on the bowl. A smaller η makes the steps small enough for “−η × slope²” to hold.
3. Policy-gradient RL updates the weights with θ ← θ + α∇J. Why a plus sign?
Minimizing a loss: step against the gradient. Maximizing a reward: step with it. In code, people usually minimize −J, which is the same thing.
📝 TL;DR
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − η ∇L. Measure the slope, step downhill, repeat.
  • The learning rate η (eta): too small = slow, just right = smooth, too big = zig-zag or divergence (NaN).
  • Narrow valleys cause zig-zags (fixed by momentum/Adam, Phase 7); bumpy landscapes have local minima.
  • It trains every neural network and LLM; RL's policy gradients use the uphill version (ascent).
