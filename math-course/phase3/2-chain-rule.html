Derivative rules & the chain rule

A handful of shortcuts gives you any derivative, and one of them, the chain rule, is how every neural network learns. It also explains why deep networks once refused to train.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The derivative f′(x) = slope = “nudge the input, how much does the output move?” (Lesson 3.1).
  • eˣ (“e to the x”, e ≈ 2.718) and ln x (the natural log, its undo button) (Lessons 0.3–0.4).
  • The sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ) squashes any number into (0, 1). σ is read “sigma”. It turns a score into a probability.
🎯 Hook

For years, deep networks with many sigmoid layers barely learned: the last layers improved, the first layers sat frozen. Nothing was broken in the code. The culprit was a multiplication.

Multiply 0.25 by itself 10 times and you get 0.00000095. By the end of this lesson you'll see where those 0.25s come from, and why switching to ReLU (2012) and adding shortcuts (ResNet, 2015) fixed it.

The shortcuts: no more nudging

Nudging works but is slow. Mathematicians worked out the slopes of the basic building blocks once and for all. Each row below was checked with a nudge like Lesson 3.1's:

Look at the sigmoid row. At z = 2, σ = 0.88 and the slope is 0.88 × 0.12 ≈ 0.105. At z = 5 it's only 0.0066. The sigmoid's slope is never more than 0.25, and far from 0 it's nearly flat. Keep that in mind.

The chain rule: machines inside machines

Neural networks are functions inside functions inside functions: multiply, add, squash, multiply, add, squash… How do you get the slope of a chain?

Numbers first. Take y = (3x + 1)². Call the inside part u = 3x + 1, so y = u². At x = 1:

  • Nudge x by a little: u moves 3× as much (slope of 3x + 1 is 3).
  • Nudge u by a little: y moves 2u = 2 × 4 = 8× as much (power rule, at u = 4).
  • So a nudge to x moves y by 3 × 8 = 24× as much. Check by nudging: ((3·1.001 + 1)² − 16) / 0.001 = 24.009 ✓
dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx “The slope of y with respect to x equals the slope of y with respect to u, times the slope of u with respect to x.” Along a chain, the slopes multiply.
🧠 Analogy: gears

Gear A drives gear B, which spins 3× as fast. Gear B drives gear C, which spins 2× as fast as B. How fast does C spin compared with A? 3 × 2 = 6×. You don't need to know anything else about the machine: speed ratios multiply.

A derivative is a speed ratio (“output moves this many times as much as input”). The chain rule is the gear rule.

💡 Big idea To get the slope of a long chain, find each link's local slope and multiply them. A network with 100 layers is a chain of 100 links, and its gradient is a product of 100 local slopes. That's backpropagation in one sentence (Lesson 3.5).
🎮 Playground · The chain-rule gearbox

Pick the inner machine u = f(x) and the outer machine y = g(u), and set x. Press “Turn x” and watch each gear turn by its speed ratio. Compare the chain-rule answer with the slope measured by nudging: they always match.

✏️ Worked example: a tiny neuron

A neuron computes y = σ(w · x). Take w = 2 and x = 1. How sensitive is the output to the weight?

  • Inner: u = w · x = 2. Its slope with respect to w is the input: du/dw = x = 1. (If x were 0, the weight couldn't matter at all.)
  • Outer: y = σ(2) = 0.88. dy/du = σ(1 − σ) = 0.88 × 0.12 ≈ 0.105.
  • Chain: dy/dw = 0.105 × 1 = 0.105. Turn w up by 0.1 and the output rises by about 0.01.

Notice the two lessons hiding here: the slope with respect to a weight is multiplied by its input, and by the squash's slope, which is small when the neuron is saturated (near 0 or 1).

Why deep sigmoid networks got stuck

Stack 10 sigmoid layers. The learning signal for layer 1 is a product of 10 sigmoid slopes, each at most 0.25. Even in the best case: 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.00000095. Layer 1 gets about a millionth of the signal layer 10 gets. This is the vanishing gradient problem.

🎮 Playground · Vanishing gradients

Each bar is the size of the learning signal that reaches a layer (the output is on the right, the first layer on the left). Each layer multiplies it by its local slope. Change the depth, the switch type and the input z each layer sees.

Sigmoid: the bars collapse toward the left, fastest when z is far from 0 (saturated neurons). ReLU: the slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so the signal arrives undamaged (but a negative input gives 0, a “dead” path). Skip connections: each layer computes x + F(x), whose slope is 1 + F′(x), so there's always a “+1 highway” the signal can ride through untouched.

🐍 In code 
import torch
x = torch.tensor(1.0, requires_grad=True)
y = (3 * x + 1) ** 2
y.backward(); x.grad                         # tensor(24.)  = 8 × 3, the chain rule

x = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
h = x
for _ in range(10):                          # 10 stacked sigmoids
    h = torch.sigmoid(h)
h.backward(); x.grad                         # ≈ 3.9e-07: the gradient has vanished

🌍 Where it's used
Why sigmoid networks were hard to train 🖼️ Deep learning · 2010 Glorot & Bengio: Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward networks

How: they measured activations and gradients layer by layer and found the sigmoid unsuited to deep networks with random starting weights: the top hidden layer was driven into saturation, where the slope is nearly 0.

Why this method: the chain rule says the gradient is a product of local slopes, so watching those slopes per layer shows exactly where the signal dies.

ReLU: a slope of exactly 1 🖼️ Deep learning · 2012 AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton)

How: AlexNet used ReLU everywhere. The paper reports deep networks with ReLUs “train several times faster” than with tanh (another squashing switch).

Why this method: multiplying by 1 doesn't shrink anything, so active paths keep their full gradient no matter how deep.

Skip connections: the +1 highway 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 He et al.: Deep Residual Learning (ResNet)

How: each block outputs x + F(x) instead of F(x). Its slope is 1 + F′(x), so the gradient can flow straight down the shortcut. ResNets were trained with up to 152 layers on ImageNet, 8× deeper than the VGG nets before them. Every transformer in an LLM uses the same trick.

Why this method: adding an identity path is free (one addition) and guarantees a slope-1 route through every layer.

The log rule in RL: slope of ln p = (slope of p) ÷ p 🎮 RL · 1992 REINFORCE (Williams), in the RL course

How: to make good actions more likely, policy-gradient methods take the slope of log probability. Chain rule + the log rule: the slope of ln p is (slope of p) ÷ p. RL uses it where p is the probability of an action (Lesson 8.6).

Why this method: that one identity turns an average over all possible actions into something you can estimate from the actions you actually tried. It's under PPO and GRPO, used to fine-tune LLMs.

🤯 Fun fact · a sign error from 1676

The chain rule seems to have first been used by Leibniz, who mentioned it in a 1676 memoir, with a sign error in the calculation. Three centuries later the same rule runs trillions of times a second in GPU data centres, hopefully with the signs right.

🌉 AI bridge · chains everywhere

Deep learning & LLMs: a transformer is a chain of dozens of blocks, each one a chain of multiplies, adds and squashes. The chain rule, applied link by link from the loss backwards, is backpropagation (Lesson 3.5). The sigmoid slope σ(1 − σ) reappears in logistic regression (Lesson 8.1).

Check yourself

1. y = (5x − 1)². What is dy/dx at x = 1?
Inside: u = 5x − 1 = 4, with slope 5. Outside: y = u², slope 2u = 8. Chain: 8 × 5 = 40. Answering 8 forgets to multiply by the inner slope.
2. A sigmoid neuron outputs σ = 0.9. What is its local slope σ(1 − σ)?
0.9 × (1 − 0.9) = 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09. A neuron near 1 (or 0) is saturated: its slope is small, so it passes back very little learning signal.
3. Why did ReLU and skip connections make very deep networks trainable?
Sigmoid slopes are at most 0.25, so a product of many of them vanishes. Multiplying by 1 keeps the signal intact through any depth.
📝 TL;DR
  • Shortcuts: xⁿ → nxⁿ⁻¹, eˣ → eˣ, ln x → 1/x, σ → σ(1 − σ) (at most 0.25), ReLU → 0 or 1. Sums and constants pass through.
  • Chain rule: dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx. Along a chain the local slopes multiply, like gear ratios.
  • Many small slopes multiplied = a vanishing gradient (stacked sigmoids). Fixes: ReLU (slope 1) and skip connections (1 + F′).
  • The chain rule, applied backwards through a network, is backpropagation (Lesson 3.5).
