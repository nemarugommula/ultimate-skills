Derivatives: slope & sensitivity

One number that answers “if I nudge this input a tiny bit, how much does the output move?”. Training a neural network asks that question about every single weight.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A function is a machine: number in, number out. f(x) = x² turns 3 into 9 (Lesson 0.2).
  • A loss is one number that says how wrong a model is: smaller is better. Training means making it small.
🎯 Hook

GPT-3 has 175 billion weights: knobs that set what it says. Training asks the same tiny question about every knob, over and over: “if I turn this knob up a hair, does the loss go up or down, and by how much?”

The answer to that question has a name: the derivative. It's the most-used idea in all of deep learning, and it's just a slope.

Numbers first: how fast are you driving?

At 2 pm you're at kilometre 100. At 3 pm you're at kilometre 160. Your average speed is the change in position divided by the change in time:

(160 − 100) km  /  (3 − 2) h  =  60 / 1  =  60 km/h

That's a slope: rise over run, how much the output (position) changes per unit of input (time). On a straight line the slope is the same everywhere. The interesting question is curves.

Slope of a curve: zoom in until it's straight

Take f(x) = x². How steep is it at x = 3? A curve has no single “rise over run”… unless you look at a tiny piece. Step from 3 to 3 + h and measure the slope of that little piece:

The answers settle on 6. Try it at x = 5 and they settle on 10. At any x they settle on 2x. That limiting slope is the derivative.

f′(x) = df/dx ≈ ( f(x + h) − f(x) ) / h   for a tiny h Read “f prime of x” or “d f d x”: the tiny change in f divided by the tiny change in x that caused it. The d means “a tiny bit of”. It is one fraction-like symbol, not d times f.
💡 Big idea The derivative is a sensitivity: change in output ≈ derivative × change in input. If f′(3) = 6, nudging x from 3 to 3.01 moves f by about 6 × 0.01 = 0.06 (really: 9 → 9.0601). Its sign says which way the output moves, its size says how much.
🧠 Analogy: the speedometer and the flat Earth

Your speedometer doesn't wait an hour to tell you “60 km/h”. It reports how fast you're going right now: the slope of your position at this instant. That's a derivative.

And why can we measure a curve's slope at all? Same reason the Earth looks flat from your garden: zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. The derivative is the slope of that line, called the tangent line.

🎮 Playground · Drag a point along the curve

Drag the pink dot. The dashed pink line is the tangent: the straight line the curve looks like up close. Then push the zoom slider and watch the curve straighten out. Try ReLU and find the corner.

Three things to notice. Positive slope: going right goes uphill. Negative: downhill. Zero: a flat spot, the bottom of a valley or the top of a hill. Finding flat spots at the bottom of the loss is what training is all about (Lesson 3.4).

Math refresher ReLU and its derivative (0 or 1)

ReLU (“rectified linear unit”) is the most common switch inside neural networks: ReLU(x) = max(0, x). Negative inputs become 0, positive ones pass through unchanged.

Its slope is 0 on the left (flat) and 1 on the right (a 45° line). At exactly x = 0 there's a corner with no single slope; libraries just pick one. PyTorch uses 0 there.

Why it matters: a neuron whose input is negative has slope 0, so nudging its weights doesn't change anything. It gets no learning signal for that example.

✏️ Worked example: one knob, one loss

A one-weight model predicts ŷ = w × 2 (input 2) and the right answer is 3. Its loss is the squared error L(w) = (2w − 3)². Where are we at w = 2?

  • Loss: (2·2 − 3)² = 1² = 1.
  • Nudge to w = 2.1: (4.2 − 3)² = 1.44. Slope ≈ (1.44 − 1) / 0.1 = 4.4.
  • Nudge both ways, to 2.1 and 1.9: (1.44 − 0.64) / 0.2 = 4.0. The exact derivative here is 4 (Lesson 3.2 shows how to get it with a formula).

Reading it: dL/dw = +4 means “turning w up makes the loss go up, about 4× as fast”. So to improve, turn w down. That one sentence is gradient descent.

Checking a derivative with nudges

Real libraries compute derivatives with exact formulas (next two lessons), because nudging every knob one by one would be far too slow. But nudges are perfect for one job: catching bugs. If the formula and the nudge disagree, the formula is wrong. This is called gradient checking.

As in the worked example, nudging both ways, (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h, is more accurate than nudging only forward. The playground shows both.

🎮 Playground · Gradient checking, like a deep-learning engineer

The curve is L(w) = (2w − 3)². Drag w and change the step h: the grey secant line becomes the pink tangent as h shrinks. Then press “Use the buggy formula” and see the check catch it. Finally try the absurdly tiny h.

With the buggy formula (someone forgot a ×2), the check fails. One surprise: an absurdly tiny h makes the nudge worse, because the computer runs out of decimal places (Lesson 8.5). Engineers use something like h = 10⁻⁵.

🐍 In code 
import torch
L = lambda w: (2 * w - 3) ** 2

h = 1e-5                                      # gradient check by nudging (centered)
(L(2 + h) - L(2 - h)) / (2 * h)                   # ≈ 4.0

w = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)    # autograd: the exact derivative
L(w).backward()
w.grad                                       # tensor(4.)  -> dL/dw = 4

x = torch.tensor([-1.0, 0.0, 2.0], requires_grad=True)
torch.relu(x).sum().backward(); x.grad       # tensor([0., 0., 1.])  ReLU slope: 0 or 1

🌍 Where it's used
dLoss/dWeight: “which way do I turn this knob?” 🏭 In production · 2006 Simon Funk's Netflix Prize write-up

How: each user and each movie gets a few numbers, and the predicted rating comes from them. Funk's recipe: take the derivative of the squared rating error with respect to each number, then move each number a little in the direction that shrinks the error.

Why this method: with about 100 million ratings, trying random tweaks would take forever. The derivative tells you the right direction for every number at once.

Gradient checking with finite differences 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 Stanford CS231n notes · torch.autograd.gradcheck

How: compare the formula's derivative with the nudge (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h and look at the relative error. CS231n's rule of thumb: above 10⁻² the formula is probably wrong. PyTorch ships the same test as gradcheck.

Why this method: nudges are slow (one or two extra runs per knob) but almost impossible to get wrong, so they make the perfect referee for fast hand-written formulas.

The ReLU derivative: 0 or 1 🖼️ Deep learning · 2012 The Greeks in finance

How: traders call the derivative of an option's value with respect to the stock price its delta, one of the “Greeks” that come out of the 1973 Black–Scholes pricing model. A delta of 0.6 means: the stock goes up $1, the option goes up about $0.60.

Why this method: it turns “how risky is this position?” into one number per input, the same sensitivity idea a neural network uses per weight.

🤯 Fun fact · whose notation?

Newton and Leibniz both invented calculus in the late 1600s, and fought bitterly over who was first. The dx and dy/dx notation you just learned is Leibniz's: he first used dx in print in his 1684 paper “Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis” (“a new method for maxima and minima”). Finding minima is still exactly what we use it for.

🌉 AI bridge · one slope per knob

Deep learning & LLMs: after each batch of text, training computes one derivative per weight (175 billion of them for GPT-3), then nudges every weight against its slope. Check yourself

1. The derivative of the loss with respect to a weight is −2. You increase that weight by 0.01. Roughly what happens to the loss?
Change in output ≈ derivative × change in input = −2 × 0.01 = −0.02. The minus sign means “up in the weight, down in the loss”, so increasing this weight is a good move.
2. A ReLU neuron receives the input −3. What is ReLU's slope there, and what does that mean for learning?
ReLU is flat (output 0) for every negative input, so its slope is 0. Slope 0 means the output doesn't react to small changes, so the derivative passed back to its weights is 0 too.
3. Why do engineers still compute derivatives by nudging, when libraries have exact formulas?
That's gradient checking. Nudging needs extra runs for every single weight, far too slow for training, but as a referee on a few weights it catches bugs like a missing ×2.
📝 TL;DR
  • Slope = rise / run. A curve's slope at a point = the slope of the straight line you see when you zoom in (the tangent).
  • f′(x) = df/dx ≈ (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h for tiny h. Example: x² has slope 2x.
  • It's a sensitivity: change in output ≈ derivative × change in input. Sign = direction, size = how much.
  • Used for: which way to turn each weight, gradient checking, ReLU (slope 0 or 1), finance's delta.
