Phase 6 · Lesson 5

Mutual information & contrastive learning

How much does knowing one thing tell you about another? That question picks features, grows decision trees, and trained CLIP to connect pictures with words.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Entropy H(Y) = how uncertain Y is, in bits (Lesson 6.2). KL divergence = how different two distributions are (Lesson 6.4).
  • P(Y | X) (“probability of Y given X”) = the chances of Y once you know X (Lesson 4.2).
  • Cosine similarity scores how alike two embedding vectors are, from −1 to 1 (Lesson 1.3); softmax turns scores into probabilities (Lesson 4.6).
🎯 Hook

OpenAI's CLIP (2021) learned to recognize what's in a picture without anyone labelling images as “dog” or “car”. It read 400 million images with their internet captions and played one game, over and over: “in this batch, which caption belongs to which picture?”

It then matched the ImageNet accuracy of a classic ResNet-50 without using any of the 1.28 million images that ResNet was trained on. Why does that guessing game work so well? Because it squeezes up the mutual information between pictures and words.

Numbers first: does “FREE” tell you it's spam?

Half of your emails are spam. Before you look at an email, its label Y (spam or not) is a coin flip: H(Y) = 1 bit of uncertainty.

Now look for the word “FREE” (call that X). Half the emails contain it. Of those, 90% are spam; of the rest, only 10% are. Once you've checked for the word, you're only as uncertain as a 90/10 coin:

H(Y | X) = ½ · H(0.9, 0.1) + ½ · H(0.1, 0.9) = ½ · 0.469 + ½ · 0.469 = 0.469 bits

Looking at X removed 1 − 0.469 = 0.531 bits of uncertainty about Y. That's the mutual information.

I(X ; Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) Read “the mutual information between X and Y”. In words: how uncertain Y was, minus how uncertain it still is once you know X. H(Y | X), “entropy of Y given X”, is the average entropy left in Y after seeing X.
💡 Big idea Mutual information measures how much knowing X tells you about Y, in bits. It's 0 when they're independent (X is useless), and at most H(Y) (X tells you Y exactly). It's also symmetric: X tells you as much about Y as Y tells you about X.

The “perfect but rare” preset is worth a look: when “FREE” appears it guarantees spam, but it almost never appears, so on average it tells you little. Mutual information scores a feature by how much it helps on average, which is what you want when choosing features.

🧠 Analogy: a weather app's tip

Your app says “dark clouds this morning”. If dark clouds always mean rain, that tip tells you everything about the afternoon. If clouds and rain are unrelated where you live, the tip is noise. Mutual information is the value of the tip: how much of your uncertainty about rain it removes, on an average day.

Math refresher mutual information is a KL divergence

If X and Y were independent, the joint table would just be P(X) × P(Y). Mutual information measures how far the real table is from that “nothing to do with each other” table, using KL (Lesson 6.4):

I(X ; Y) = DKL( P(X, Y) ‖ P(X) · P(Y) ) “How much extra surprise you'd suffer by pretending X and Y are unrelated.” The playground computes it both ways; the numbers always agree.

In the spam example: the cell (FREE, spam) really has probability 0.45, but independence would predict 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. Summing p · log₂(p / predicted) over all four cells gives 2 × 0.45 · log₂(1.8) + 2 × 0.05 · log₂(0.2) ≈ 0.763 − 0.232 = 0.531 bits ✔.

✏️ Worked example: information gain in a decision tree is mutual information

In Lesson 6.2, a decision tree split 10 emails (5 spam) on “contains FREE”: 4 spam + 1 normal on one side, 1 spam + 4 normal on the other.

  • H(Y) = 1 bit (5 of 10 are spam).
  • Each side has entropy H(0.8, 0.2) ≈ 0.722 bits, and each side holds half the emails, so H(Y | X) = ½ · 0.722 + ½ · 0.722 = 0.722 bits.
  • I(X ; Y) = 1 − 0.722 = 0.278 bits: exactly the “information gain” the tree computed.

So a decision tree greedily asks the question with the most mutual information with the label.

Contrastive learning: the matching game

We'd love to train an image encoder and a text encoder so that a picture's embedding carries as much information as possible about its caption: high I(image ; caption). Computing mutual information directly is hopeless for photos. So we play a matching game instead, and its score tells us something about it.

Take a batch of N image–caption pairs. Compute the cosine similarity of every image with every caption: an N × N grid. For each image (each row), softmax over the N captions and ask for the right one. The loss is the cross-entropy from Lesson 6.3: −ln(probability of the matching caption). This loss is called InfoNCE.

Numbers first, with N = 4 pairs. A model that guesses blindly gives each caption 1/4, so its loss is ln 4 ≈ 1.39 nats. That proves nothing: the embeddings might share no information at all. A perfect matcher gives the right caption probability ≈ 1, so its loss is ≈ 0. It has shown the embeddings share at least 1.39 nats (= ln 4) of information, or it couldn't pick right every time. In between, the proof is ln 4 minus the loss:

I(image ; caption) ≥ ln N − LInfoNCE “The mutual information is at least the log of the batch size minus the matching-game loss.” Winning the game (loss near 0) proves the embeddings share at least ln N nats of information.
🎮 Playground · A tiny CLIP

Four images (blue arrows) and four captions (green dashed arrows) live as directions in 2D. The grid shows, for each image, the softmax over captions of cosine / τ (“tau”, the temperature). Train and watch matching pairs line up and the diagonal light up.

Two things to try. First, set τ = 1 and train: cosines only go from −1 to 1, so without a low temperature the softmax can't become confident and the loss stalls. CLIP learns its temperature for exactly this reason. Second, notice the bound can never exceed ln 4 ≈ 1.39 nats with 4 pairs. A bigger batch raises that ceiling and makes the game harder, which is why contrastive methods like big batches.

🐍 In code 
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
# img, txt: (N, d) embeddings of N matching image–caption pairs (CLIP-style loss)
img = F.normalize(img, dim=1); txt = F.normalize(txt, dim=1)  # length 1, so dot = cosine
logits = img @ txt.T / tau                    # N × N similarity grid
labels = torch.arange(N)                      # the match for row i is column i
loss = (F.cross_entropy(logits, labels) + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)) / 2
mi_lower_bound = torch.log(torch.tensor(N)) - loss   # InfoNCE bound, in nats

🌍 Where it's used
Matching pictures and captions 🖼️ Deep learning · 2021 CLIP (Radford et al.)

How: an image encoder and a text encoder are trained on 400 million pairs with a symmetric cross-entropy over the N × N grid of cosine similarities (rows and columns), with a learned temperature and a minibatch of 32,768 pairs.

Why this method: predicting the caption's words is a harder task than needed. In the paper's comparison, swapping a predict-the-words objective for this contrastive one made learning about 4× more efficient (measured by zero-shot ImageNet accuracy). And new classes need no retraining: compare an image with “a photo of a …”.

Learning from unlabelled photos 🖼️ Deep learning · 2020 SimCLR (Chen et al.)

How: two random augmentations (crops, colour changes) of the same photo form a positive pair; every other photo in the batch is a negative. The loss, NT-Xent, is a temperature-scaled cross-entropy of the same InfoNCE shape. With a 4×-wide ResNet-50, a linear classifier on top reached 76.5% ImageNet top-1, matching a supervised (standard-width) ResNet-50.

Why this method: no labels needed, just “these two views are the same thing”. The paper found contrastive learning benefits from larger batches and longer training than supervised learning.

Ranking features by how informative they are 📊 Data science · 2016 scikit-learn: mutual_info_classif

How: estimate I(feature ; label) for every column of a table and keep the top-scoring ones, the playground's “is this clue worth anything?” for hundreds of columns at once.

Why this method: correlation only sees straight-line relationships. Mutual information catches any dependence (see the fun fact), and it's in bits, so features of different types compare fairly.

🤯 Fun fact · correlation can be 0 while MI is big

Let X be −2, −1, 0, 1 or 2 (equally likely) and Y = X². The correlation between X and Y is exactly 0 (Lesson 5.2): no straight-line trend. But Y is completely determined by X, so I(X ; Y) = H(Y) ≈ 1.52 bits. Mutual information notices any kind of relationship, not just lines.

🌉 AI bridge · shared information as a training signal

Generative AI: CLIP's shared image–text space became a building block: DALL·E 2 generates a CLIP image embedding from a caption, then decodes it into a picture (Ramesh et al., 2022).

Classic ML: information gain in decision trees (Lesson 6.2) is mutual information between a question and the label.

RL: exploration bonuses reward an agent for what it couldn't predict, a form of surprise from Lesson 6.1: RL course 6.5: curiosity & RND.

Check yourself

1. A label has entropy H(Y) = 1 bit. After seeing a feature X, the remaining entropy is H(Y | X) = 0.3 bits. What is I(X ; Y)?
I(X ; Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7 bits: knowing X removed 70% of the uncertainty about Y.
2. X and Y are independent. What is their mutual information?
Independent means P(Y | X) = P(Y), so H(Y | X) = H(Y) and the difference is 0. Equivalently, the joint table equals P(X)·P(Y), and a KL between identical distributions is 0.
3. Why do CLIP-style contrastive methods like very large batches (CLIP used 32,768 pairs)?
With N pairs, the bound on mutual information can never exceed ln N. Bigger batches mean more negatives per example, so the model must learn finer distinctions, and the ceiling rises.
📝 TL;DR
  • I(X ; Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X): how much knowing X reduces uncertainty about Y. 0 if independent, symmetric, in bits or nats.
  • It's also DKL(P(X, Y) ‖ P(X)·P(Y)): the distance from “unrelated”.
  • Decision-tree information gain is mutual information; feature selection ranks columns by it.
  • Contrastive learning (InfoNCE, CLIP, SimCLR) plays “which one matches?” with cross-entropy, and ln N − loss is a lower bound on the mutual information.
