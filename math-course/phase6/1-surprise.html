Phase 6 · Lesson 1

Information = surprise (bits and −log p)

How to measure “how much did I just learn?” with one number. It's the per-token loss of every LLM, and the reason ZIP files are small.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • log₂ x (“log base 2 of x”) asks “2 to what power gives x?”. log₂ 8 = 3 because 2³ = 8 (Lesson 0.3).
  • ln x (“natural log of x”) is the same question with base e ≈ 2.718 (Lesson 0.4).
  • A probability is a number from 0 (never) to 1 (certain) (Lesson 4.1).
🎯 Hook

I'm thinking of a number from 1 to 100. You may only ask yes/no questions. With a smart strategy you'll always find it in 7 questions or fewer. Why 7, and not 10 or 50?

And here's the strange part: the answer to that party game is the same math that sits in every LLM training log, where the number printed as “loss” is literally how surprised the model was by each word it read.

Numbers first: how surprised are you?

Your friend flips a coin and says “heads”. Mildly interesting. You roll a die and get a 6. A bit more interesting. You win a 1-in-a-million raffle. Very interesting. The rarer the event, the more it tells you.

Shannon's idea (1948) was to measure that surprise in coin flips. One fair coin flip, a 1-in-2 event, is worth 1 bit. Something with chance 1 in 4 is like guessing two coin flips right in a row: 2 bits. 1 in 8 is three coin flips: 3 bits.

p = 1/2 → 1 bit  ·  p = 1/4 → 2 bits  ·  p = 1/8 → 3 bits  ·  p = 1/1024 → 10 bits

See the pattern? Count how many times you halve 1 to reach p. That count is exactly what log₂ does.

surprise(p) = −log₂ p = log₂(1 / p)  bits Read “minus log base 2 of p”. In plain words: “how many fair coin flips would I have to call correctly in a row to be this lucky?”
p: the probability of what happened−log p: its surprise (later: the loss)
💡 Big idea Information is surprise. A certain event (p = 1) tells you nothing: 0 bits. A rare event tells you a lot. And because it's a log, surprises of independent events add up: two coin flips = 1 + 1 = 2 bits.
🎮 Playground · The surprise meter

Slide the probability of an event. The purple dot rides the surprise curve. Try p = 0.5, then make the event rarer and rarer and watch the surprise climb (slowly, because it's a log).

Two things to notice. Near p = 1 the curve sits on 0: no surprise, no news. And the curve never stops rising as p shrinks: an event with probability 0 would be infinitely surprising. Remember that: it's why an LLM that gives the true word probability 0 gets an infinite loss.

🧠 Analogy: the newspaper test

“Sun rises in the east” would never make the front page: everyone expected it. “Snow in the Sahara” makes headlines because it's rare. A newspaper editor is a surprise meter: column inches ≈ −log p. Bits are just a precise way to count those column inches.

Why 7 questions? Twenty questions in bits

Back to the hook. A good yes/no question splits the remaining possibilities in half, so its answer is a 50/50 event: worth exactly 1 bit. To pin down 1 out of 100 equally likely numbers you need log₂ 100 ≈ 6.64 bits. Questions come in whole numbers, so 7 always do it.

A bad question, like “is it bigger than 95?”, usually gets “no”. A “no” you expected (p = 0.95) is worth only −log₂ 0.95 ≈ 0.07 bits. A lucky “yes” (p = 0.05) would be worth 4.32 bits, but you rarely get it. Try both kinds below.

🎮 Playground · Guess my number (1–100)

I've picked a secret number. Ask “is it bigger than N?”. Each answer's value in bits is its surprise: −log₂(the fraction of the still-possible numbers that agreed with it). Or let the 🤖 halve for you.

The bits always add up to log₂(100 / numbers left). Halving questions collect about 1 bit each, so they finish in 7. Lopsided questions mostly collect crumbs. The best question is the one whose answer you can't predict. Hold that thought: that's entropy, next lesson.

Bits vs nats: same surprise, different ruler

Using log₂ measures surprise in bits (coin flips). Using ln measures it in nats (“natural units”). It's like inches and centimetres: one is a fixed multiple of the other, 1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits, and 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 nats.

Information theorists like bits. Deep learning code uses ln, because that's what torch.log computes, so LLM losses are reported in nats. When a training log says the loss on one token was 1.386, that's −ln 0.25: the model gave the true next token a 25% chance. In bits that's −log₂ 0.25 = 2.

Math refresher why surprises add up

Two independent events with probabilities p and q both happen with probability p × q (Lesson 4.1). Logs turn multiplying into adding (Lesson 0.3):

−log₂(p · q) = −log₂ p + (−log₂ q) “The surprise of both together is the surprise of the first plus the surprise of the second.”

Check: a coin flip (1/2) and a die roll (1/6) together have probability 1/12. −log₂(1/12) ≈ 3.585 bits, and 1 + 2.585 = 3.585. ✔ This is why an LLM can add up per-token losses for a whole document instead of multiplying thousands of tiny probabilities.

✏️ Worked example: an LLM reads “the cat sat on the …”

The model's next-token guesses: “mat” 0.25, “floor” 0.25, “sofa” 0.125, and the rest spread over thousands of other tokens.

  • The real text says “mat”: surprise = −log₂ 0.25 = 2 bits (= −ln 0.25 ≈ 1.386 nats).
  • If the real text had said “sofa”: −log₂ 0.125 = 3 bits. A bit more surprised.
  • If it had said “moon” with probability 0.001: −log₂ 0.001 ≈ 9.97 bits. Very surprised, so a big loss and a big learning signal.

Training nudges the model so that, on average, real text surprises it less. That average surprise is the cross-entropy loss (Lesson 6.3).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
p = np.array([0.5, 0.25, 1/1024, 0.001])
-np.log2(p)             # [1, 2, 10, 9.97]  surprise in bits
-np.log(p)              # same surprise in nats (what PyTorch losses use)
np.log2(100)            # 6.64 bits to find 1 number out of 100

# Per-token surprise of a sentence: just add the logs
probs_of_true_tokens = np.array([0.6, 0.3, 0.05, 0.9])
(-np.log(probs_of_true_tokens)).sum()   # 4.82 nats for the 4 tokens together

🌍 Where it's used
Spotting machine-written text by its low surprise 🧠 LLMs · 2019 GLTR (Gehrmann, Strobelt & Rush)

How: run a text through a language model and colour each word by how probable (unsurprising) the model found it. Generated text is mostly the model's top picks; human text has more surprising words. In the paper's study, people spotted fake text 54% of the time unaided and over 72% with the tool.

Why this method: it needs no extra training, and a heat map of per-word surprise is something a non-expert can read.

Short codes for frequent symbols 🏭 In production · 1952 Huffman coding inside DEFLATE (ZIP, gzip, PNG)

How: a symbol with probability p gets a code about −log₂ p bits long, so common letters get short codes and rare ones long codes. DEFLATE, the format inside ZIP, gzip and PNG files, uses Huffman codes as one of its two stages.

Why this method: a fixed 8 bits per character wastes space on letters you could have predicted. Lesson 6.2 builds a Huffman code live.

Information measured in bits 📜 History · 1948 Shannon: A Mathematical Theory of Communication

How: Claude Shannon, at Bell Labs, defined information by probability alone, ignoring meaning, and showed it sets hard limits on how far messages can be compressed and how fast they can be sent.

Why this method: ignoring meaning sounds like a weakness, but it made the theory work for any message: phone calls, images, DNA, and much later, tokens.

🤯 Fun fact · who named the bit?

Shannon's 1948 paper was the first published use of the word “bit” (short for binary digit), and Shannon credited the word to his Bell Labs colleague, the statistician John Tukey.

🌉 AI bridge · surprise everywhere

LLMs: pretraining is “be less surprised by real text”. Every token's loss is its surprise, −ln q(true token). Lesson 6.3 turns this into cross-entropy and perplexity.

RL: −log π(a|s), the surprise of the action the policy just took, shows up as a bonus in max-entropy RL: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) rewards an agent both for scoring well and for keeping its choices surprising, so it keeps exploring instead of locking in too early. See RL course 6.2: Max-entropy RL (SAC).

Next: average the surprise over all outcomes and you get entropy (Lesson 6.2).

Check yourself

1. An event has probability 1/8. How much information (surprise) does it carry?
−log₂(1/8) = log₂ 8 = 3, because 2³ = 8. It's as surprising as calling three fair coin flips correctly in a row.
2. Which message carries more information?
Information depends on probability, not length. An almost-certain event has surprise near −log₂ 1 = 0 bits. A rare event has a large −log₂ p.
3. Why do LLMs use −log p(true token) as the per-token loss instead of something like 1 − p?
The probability of a whole text is a product of token probabilities; the log turns that into a sum of per-token surprises. And at p = 0.001, −log₂ p ≈ 10 bits while 1 − p barely moves (0.999), so the log gives a much stronger signal to fix big mistakes.
📝 TL;DR
  • Surprise of an event = −log₂ p bits: 1 bit for a coin flip, 0 for a certainty, ∞ for “impossible”.
  • Surprises of independent events add up, because logs turn × into +.
  • A yes/no question is worth at most 1 bit, so finding 1 of 100 takes log₂ 100 ≈ 6.64 → 7 questions.
  • Bits use log₂, nats use ln (1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits). LLM losses are in nats.
  • An LLM's per-token loss is its surprise at the real next token.
