e, exp() and growth: the most useful number in AI

The number e ≈ 2.718 falls out of compound interest. Raising e to a power turns any score into a positive number, which is how every LLM turns scores into probabilities.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Powers: 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2, 2⁰ = 1, and a negative exponent means divide: 2⁻¹ = 1/2 (Lesson 0.3).
  • bm × bn = bm+n, and logs undo powers (Lesson 0.3).
  • A probability is between 0 and 1, and the chances of all options add up to 1 (100%).
🎯 Hook

Every time GPT-2 picks its next token, it gives a score to each of the 50,257 tokens it knows. Some scores are negative. To turn them into probabilities, it raises a strange number, e = 2.71828…, to the power of every score, then divides by the total.

Why that number, of all numbers? It was introduced in 1683 by Jacob Bernoulli, who was thinking about bank interest, and it turns out to be the natural number for anything that grows or shrinks by a percentage. Let's find it ourselves.

Numbers first: the greedy bank

You put $1 in a bank that pays 100% interest per year. After a year you have $2. Now you ask: “Could you pay me 50% every half year instead?” After 6 months you have $1.50, and the next 50% is paid on $1.50: you end with 1.5 × 1.5 = $2.25. More often is better!

Paying more often helps less and less. The total never passes a limit, and that limit is a number like π: it goes on forever without repeating. It's called e.

(1 + 1/n)n → e ≈ 2.71828   as n gets huge “One plus one over n, to the power n, gets closer and closer to e as n grows.” e is what $1 becomes after a year of 100% growth paid continuously, every instant.

exp(x): e to the power x

Raising e to a power is so common it has its own name: exp.

exp(x) = ex “exp of x” or “e to the x”. Feed in any number, get e multiplied by itself that many times (with the same rules as Lesson 0.3 for negative and fractional powers).

Look at that table and notice three superpowers:

  • Always positive. Even e−5 is a small positive number, 0.0067. Never zero, never negative.
  • Keeps the order. A bigger input always gives a bigger output. And it exaggerates differences: 2 vs 1 becomes 7.39 vs 2.72.
  • Turns + into ×. ea + b = ea × eb, the power rule from Lesson 0.3.

The undo button for exp is the natural logarithm, written ln (“log base e”): ln(ex) = x. So ln(1) = 0, ln(e) = 1 and ln(2) ≈ 0.693. When AI papers write “log”, they almost always mean ln.

💡 Big idea exp turns any score, positive or negative, into a positive amount, while keeping the order. That's exactly what you need to turn scores into probabilities. And growing or shrinking by the same percentage every step is always an exp curve.

Scores → probabilities: softmax (a preview)

A model's scores can be anything: 3, −1, 0.2. Probabilities must be positive and add up to 1. Two steps fix that: exp every score (now all positive), then divide by the total (now they add to 1). This recipe is called softmax.

pi = ezi / Σj ezj “The probability of option i is e to its score, divided by the total of e to every option's score.” zi (“z sub i”) is the score of option number i. Σ (“sigma”) is just shorthand for that total: “add up over all options j” (Σ, Lesson 0.5).
🧠 Analogy: slices of a pie

Every option makes a bid. exp turns the bid into a pile of coins: always some coins, and a bit higher bid means a lot more coins. Then the pie is shared in proportion to coins. Even the worst option gets a sliver, and the slices always make one whole pie.

Adding the same number to every score changes nothing: ez + 1 = ez × e, so every pile of coins is multiplied by the same 2.718 and the shares stay the same. Only the differences between scores matter. Negative scores are fine too: exp makes them positive.

With just two options, softmax has a famous special form. Give one option score z and the other 0: its probability is ez / (ez + 1) = 1 / (1 + e−z). That's the sigmoid, σ(z) (“sigma of z”), which squashes any number into a probability: σ(0) = 0.5, σ(2) ≈ 0.88, σ(−2) ≈ 0.12. It's the yes/no version used in classifiers (Lesson 8.1).

✏️ Worked example: softmax by hand

Scores for three words: 2, 1, 0.

  • exp each: e² ≈ 7.389, e¹ ≈ 2.718, e⁰ = 1.
  • Total: 7.389 + 2.718 + 1 = 11.107.
  • Divide: 7.389 / 11.107 ≈ 0.665, 2.718 / 11.107 ≈ 0.245, 1 / 11.107 ≈ 0.090. They add to 1.

Scores 1 apart became probabilities about 2.7× apart: each extra point of score multiplies the odds by e.

Shrinking by a percentage: exponential decay

Exp also runs backwards. Keep 90% of something each step and after t steps you have 0.9t of it: 0.9, 0.81, 0.73, … after 10 steps 0.35. That's the same curve as e−0.105 t, because 0.9 = eln 0.9 ≈ e−0.105. AI uses this “keep a fixed fraction each step” pattern all over the place.

🐍 In code 
import math, numpy as np
(1 + 1/365) ** 365, math.e                # (2.71457..., 2.71828...)
np.exp([-5, 0, 2]), math.log(2)          # [0.0067, 1., 7.389], ln 2 = 0.693

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - np.max(z))               # subtracting the max changes nothing (see playground)
    return e / e.sum()                      # ...but keeps exp from overflowing
softmax(np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.0]))           # [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]
0.99 ** 100                                # 0.366: a reward 100 steps away, discounted

🌍 Where it's used
Softmax for next-token probabilities 🧠 LLMs · 2017 Attention Is All You Need

How: the paper says it uses “the usual learned linear transformation and softmax function to convert the decoder output to predicted next-token probabilities”. One score per token, exp, divide by the total.

Why this method: dividing raw scores by their sum breaks when scores are negative or add to 0. exp makes every score positive while keeping the order, and it plays perfectly with logs: ln(softmax) is just score − ln(total), which makes the loss simple (Lesson 8.2).

Softmax with a temperature 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 Hinton, Vinyals & Dean: Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network

How: the paper divides every score by a temperature T before the exp: exp(zi/T) / Σ exp(zj/T). T = 1 is normal; a higher T gives softer, more spread-out probabilities, which a small “student” network learns from.

Why this method: the soft probabilities carry more information than a single right answer (which wrong answers were “almost right”). The same temperature knob controls how adventurous a chatbot's sampling is (Lesson 4.6).

Discounting future rewards: γk 🎮 RL · 2018 Sutton & Barto: Reinforcement Learning (2nd ed.)

How: a reward k steps in the future is multiplied by γk (γ is the Greek letter “gamma”, a number just below 1). With γ = 0.99, a reward 100 steps away counts 0.99100 ≈ 0.37 of its value.

Why this method: one number sets how far ahead the agent cares, about 1 / (1 − γ) steps, and it keeps the total of an endless stream of rewards finite.

Fading memories of past gradients 🤖 Machine learning · 2014 Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba)

How: training nudges every weight a little each step (the gradient, Lesson 3.3). Adam keeps a fading average of recent nudges: with β = 0.9 (“beta”), a nudge from 10 steps ago counts 0.910 ≈ 35% as much as today's. It keeps two such averages, a short memory β₁ = 0.9 and a long one β₂ = 0.999.

Why this method: an exponentially fading average needs just one number of memory and adapts to recent changes; β = 0.9 remembers roughly the last 10 steps, 0.999 roughly the last 1,000.

🤯 Fun fact · the rule of 70

Anything growing at a steady r% per year doubles in about 70 / r years. At 7% a year, money doubles in about 10 years (the exact answer is 10.24). The 70 comes from ln 2 ≈ 0.693. The same trick tells you that with γ = 0.99 a reward's weight halves every ln 2 / 0.01 ≈ 69 steps.

🌉 AI bridge · exp everywhere

LLMs: softmax is the last step of every language model and the heart of attention (Lessons 8.2 and 8.4). Temperature reshapes it when sampling (Lesson 4.6), and its partner, the log, gives the cross-entropy loss (Lesson 6.3). Learning-rate schedules and Adam are in Lesson 7.3.

RL: the discount γk from the third playground is the core of an agent's goal. The RL course builds it up with a “discount dial” in Lesson 0.3: rewards, returns & discounting.

Check yourself

1. $1 at 100% yearly interest, paid twice a year (50% each time). How much after one year?
(1 + 1/2)² = 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25. The second payment earns interest on the first. Paying infinitely often would give e ≈ $2.72, the upper limit.
2. Softmax turns the scores [3, 1] into about [0.88, 0.12]. What happens if you add 10 to both scores, giving [13, 11]?
ez + 10 = ez × e10: both piles are multiplied by the same factor, so the shares don't change. Only the difference between scores (here 2) matters.
3. Why does softmax use escore instead of simply dividing each score by the sum of scores?
Scores like [2, −1, −1] sum to 0, so plain division would explode, and a negative score would give a negative “probability”. exp fixes both and also makes the math with logs clean.
📝 TL;DR
  • e ≈ 2.71828 is the limit of (1 + 1/n)n: 100% growth compounded continuously.
  • exp(x) = ex is always positive, keeps order, and turns + into ×. ln undoes it.
  • Softmax: pi = ezi / Σ ezj. Adding a constant to all scores changes nothing; sigmoid is the 2-option case.
  • Keeping a fixed fraction each step (γt) is exponential decay: RL discounting, Adam's averages, learning-rate decay.
