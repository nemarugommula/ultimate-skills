Phase 0 · Lesson 1

Why math for AI? A map of the journey

A tour of what happens inside a chatbot, step by step, and which piece of math does each step. This is the map for the whole course.

🎯 Hook

ChatGPT has never read a single word. Not one. Every word you type is turned into numbers before the model sees it, and its answer comes back as numbers that get turned into words at the very end.

In between, GPT-3 pushes those numbers through 175 billion adjustable numbers called parameters. Nobody set them by hand. They were found by one simple idea repeated billions of times: guess, measure how wrong you were, nudge every knob a tiny bit to be less wrong. That's the whole secret, and every part of it is math you'll learn here.

You don't need to be “a math person”

This course has no proofs. You'll meet each idea with small numbers first, then a picture, then a toy you can play with, and only then the symbols. Every symbol will be read out loud in plain English.

And you don't need all of mathematics. Modern AI leans on a surprisingly short list of ideas: functions, vectors, matrices, slopes, probability, logs and averages. That's what the next eight phases cover.

💡 Big idea Almost every AI system is three things: (1) a function with lots of knobs that turns inputs into outputs, (2) a number that scores how wrong the outputs are (the loss), and (3) a rule for turning the knobs to make that number smaller. Learning = turning knobs to reduce error.
🧠 Analogy: the hotel shower

You step into a hotel shower with an unfamiliar knob. Too cold. You turn it a bit left. Too hot! You turn it back a little. Within a few tries it's just right.

That's training. The knob is a parameter, “too hot / too cold” is the error, and “which way and how much to turn” is the gradient. An AI model is a shower with billions of knobs, and calculus (Phase 3) tells it which way to turn all of them at once.

✏️ Worked example: scoring a guess by hand

A model guesses that output = 1 × input. The true data is three points: input 1 → 2, input 2 → 4, input 3 → 6.

  • Guesses: 1, 2, 3. Misses (guess − truth): −1, −2, −3.
  • Square each miss so negatives don't cancel positives: 1, 4, 9.
  • Average them: (1 + 4 + 9) / 3 = 14 / 3 ≈ 4.67. That's the error.

Now turn the knob from 1 to 2: guesses 2, 4, 6, misses 0, 0, 0, error 0. Perfect. This “average of squared misses” is called the mean squared error, and you're about to minimize it yourself.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6])            # hours of practice
y = np.array([3, 5.5, 6.5, 9, 10.5, 13])   # scores
w, b, lr = 0.5, 1.0, 0.03                   # two knobs and a step size
for step in range(400):
    miss = (w * x + b) - y                     # guess minus truth
    w -= lr * 2 * np.mean(miss * x)          # nudge each knob downhill
    b -= lr * 2 * np.mean(miss)              # (Phase 3 explains these lines)
print(w, b, np.mean(miss ** 2))                # ≈ 1.93, 1.17, error ≈ 0.10

🌍 Where it's used
A model = a function with billions of knobs 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3 (Language Models are Few-Shot Learners)

How: GPT-3 is one giant function: text goes in as numbers, next-word probabilities come out. Its 175 billion parameters are the knobs, and the paper reports it was trained on 300 billion tokens.

Why this method: instead of writing grammar rules by hand, you let the knobs be found from data. The paper's headline finding is that making this function bigger made it much better at new tasks from just a few examples.

Turning knobs downhill: backpropagation 📜 History · 1986 Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, Nature

How: this paper showed how to compute, for every weight in a multi-layer network, which way to turn it to reduce the error. It's the “Train one step” button in the first playground, done for every knob at once (Lesson 3.5).

Why this method: trying each knob one by one would need one test run per knob. Backpropagation gets all the directions in roughly the cost of one extra pass through the network.

Fitting a line by minimizing squared error 📊 Data science · 1805 Least squares (Legendre)

How: the error you minimized in the second playground, the average of squared misses, was published by Legendre in 1805, who used it on measurements of the shape of the Earth. It's still the default loss for predicting numbers like prices or temperatures.

Why this method: squaring makes every miss positive and punishes big misses more than small ones, and it gives a smooth bowl-shaped error that's easy to roll down.

Rewarding good answers: RLHF 🎮 RL · 2022 InstructGPT (OpenAI)

How: people ranked model answers, a second model learned to predict those rankings as a score, and reinforcement learning nudged GPT-3's knobs toward higher-scoring answers.

Why this method: “guess the next word” makes a model fluent, not helpful. In the paper, people preferred answers from a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT over the 175B GPT-3, despite 100× fewer parameters.

🤯 Fun fact · how big is “big”?

In the largest GPT-3, every single token becomes a list of 12,288 numbers, and that list passes through 96 layers before the model picks the next word (from the size table in the GPT-3 paper). Each of those layers is “just” the matrix math of Phase 2.

Check yourself

1. A model predicts output = 2 × input. The data is input 1 → 3 and input 2 → 5. What is its mean squared error?
Guesses 2 and 4. Misses −1 and −1. Squared: 1 and 1. Average: (1 + 1) / 2 = 1. Moving the intercept up by 1 (output = 2 × input + 1) would make it 0.
2. In the chatbot pipeline, which step turns the model's raw scores for each possible next word into chances that add up to 100%?
The tokenizer turns text into numbers at the very start, and the optimizer turns knobs during training. Softmax (Lesson 0.4 previews it, Lesson 8.2 does it fully) turns any list of scores into positive numbers that add to 1.
3. Why does training a neural network need calculus (gradients)?
With billions of knobs you can't try each one by hand. The gradient (Phase 3) gives every knob its own “turn this way, about this much” instruction in one go. That's what the 🤖 button used.
📝 TL;DR
  • AI models only see numbers: text → tokens → vectors → layers → scores → probabilities.
  • Training = guess, measure the loss, nudge every knob downhill using the gradient, repeat.
  • Each step uses one phase of this course: vectors (1), matrices (2), calculus (3), probability (4), statistics (5), information theory (6), optimization (7).
