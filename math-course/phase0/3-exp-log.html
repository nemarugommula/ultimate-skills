Exponents & logarithms: the multiply ↔ add trick

Exponents are repeated multiplication. Logs undo them and turn multiplying into adding. That one trick keeps every LLM's probabilities from vanishing, and turns AI's scaling laws into straight lines.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A function is a machine, f(x) = “the output when you feed in x” (Lesson 0.2).
  • x² means x × x, so 3² = 9 (Lesson 0.2).
  • A probability is a number between 0 and 1: 0.1 means a 10% chance.
🎯 Hook

How likely is a 1,000-token answer? A language model finds out by multiplying the probability of each token. Even if every token had a 50% chance, the result is 0.5 multiplied by itself 1,000 times: a decimal point followed by 301 zeros before the first digit.

Computers can't hold numbers that small. With 10% per token, standard 32-bit numbers give exactly 0 after just 46 tokens. Every LLM dodges this with a trick invented in 1614: logarithms, which turn multiplying into adding.

Powers: multiplying again and again

2 × 2 × 2 is “three 2s multiplied together”. Writing that out gets old fast, so we write it as 2³:

23 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 “2 to the power 3 is 8.” The small raised number (the exponent) counts how many copies of the big number (the base) to multiply.

Powers grow ridiculously fast. 2¹⁰ = 1,024, about a thousand. 2²⁰ is about a million. 10⁶ is exactly a million: a 1 followed by 6 zeros.

Now multiply two powers of 2: 2³ × 2² = (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2). That's five 2s, so it's 2⁵ = 32. You just added the exponents:

bm × bn = bm + n “b to the m, times b to the n, equals b to the m plus n.” Multiplying powers of the same base = adding their exponents. Hold on to this: it's the whole lesson in one line.

Zero, negative and fractional exponents

Walk down the powers of 2 from right to left. Each step left divides by 2. Keep going past 1 and the pattern tells you what the strange exponents must mean:

  • Power 0 is always 1: you multiplied no copies, so you're left with the starting value 1.
  • Negative = divide. 2⁻³ = 1 / 2³ = 1/8. And 10⁻³ = 0.001.
  • Scientific notation uses powers of 10 to write tiny or huge numbers compactly: 0.000123 = 1.23 × 10⁻⁴ (“move the dot 4 places left”), and 175,000,000,000 = 1.75 × 10¹¹.
  • Fraction = root. 20.5 is “half a doubling”: the number that, multiplied by itself, gives 2. That's √2 (“the square root of 2”) ≈ 1.414. Check: 1.414 × 1.414 ≈ 2.

Logarithms: “how many times do I multiply?”

A logarithm asks the question backwards. How many 2s do I multiply to get 8? Three. So the log base 2 of 8 is 3.

log2(8) = 3   because   23 = 8 “Log base 2 of 8 is 3.” In general, logb(y) = x means bx = y: the log tells you which exponent you need.

Base 10 is the friendliest: log₁₀(1,000) = 3, log₁₀(1,000,000) = 6. For numbers of 1 or more, log₁₀ is roughly “how many digits, minus 1”. 4,500 has 4 digits, and log₁₀(4,500) ≈ 3.65: between 3 and 4. Numbers below 1 have negative logs: log₁₀(0.1) = −1, log₁₀(0.001) = −3.

🧠 Analogy: a zero-counter

Think of log₁₀ as a machine that counts zeros: 1,000 → 3, 1,000,000 → 6. Multiply those two numbers and you get 1,000,000,000, which has 3 + 6 = 9 zeros. Multiplying the numbers = adding their zero counts. Logs work this way for every number, not just round ones.

log(a × b) = log(a) + log(b) “The log of a product is the sum of the logs.” It's the same fact as adding exponents, seen from the other side. Example: log₁₀(100 × 1,000) = 2 + 3 = 5, and 100 × 1,000 = 100,000 indeed has 5 zeros.
💡 Big idea Logs turn × into +, tiny products into ordinary-sized sums, and “times 10” into “plus 1”. Anything in AI that multiplies many numbers together (probabilities, growth factors) is usually computed in log-land instead.

✏️ Worked example: a product by adding

Three tokens had probabilities 0.2, 0.5 and 0.1. How likely is the whole sequence?

  • Directly: 0.2 × 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.01.
  • In log-land: log₁₀(0.2) + log₁₀(0.5) + log₁₀(0.1) ≈ −0.699 + (−0.301) + (−1) = −2.
  • Back out of log-land: 10⁻² = 0.01. Same answer, and we only ever added.

With 3 tokens it makes no difference. With 1,000 tokens the direct product is too small for the computer, but the sum of logs is a perfectly normal number like −301.

Why LLMs live in log-land

Let's watch the problem from the hook happen. Computers store numbers in a fixed number of bits. Below a certain size, the number becomes exactly 0 (this is called underflow). For the 32-bit numbers common in AI that floor is around 10⁻⁴⁵; for 64-bit numbers about 10⁻³²⁴.

🎮 Playground · Multiply probabilities vs add logs

Each token has the same probability p. The red line multiplies them the direct way, the green line adds their logs. The chart shows log₁₀ of the result (−40 means 10⁻⁴⁰). Watch the red line fall off the cliff.

The sum of logs never breaks: it just gets more negative, one step per token. That's why LLM tools report log-probabilities (“logprobs”) instead of probabilities, and why the training loss is built from −log(p) (Lesson 6.1 calls that number “surprise”).

Log scales: making curves straight

Logs also help you see. In 2020, Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI measured how a language model's loss falls as the model gets bigger, over more than seven orders of magnitude (factors of 10). On ordinary axes, the curve is a useless hockey stick. On axes where every gridline means “×10” (a log scale), it's a straight line.

🎮 Playground · A scaling law on two kinds of axes

The curve is the paper's fitted formula: loss = (8.8 × 10¹³ / N)0.076, where N is the number of parameters (not counting the embedding table). Switch the axes, then slide the model size.

Why straight? A power law y = a · Nk becomes log y = log a + k · log N after taking logs: a straight line (Lesson 0.2) whose slope is the exponent k, here −0.076. Each 10× bigger model multiplies the loss by 10−0.076 ≈ 0.84, about 16% lower, at every size. A straight line is easy to extend, so labs use such laws to forecast bigger models before paying to train them. The GPT-4 report says some aspects of GPT-4's performance were predicted from models trained with no more than 1/1,000th of its compute.

Which base? log₂, log₁₀ and the “natural log” ln (base e, Lesson 0.4) all have the same shape; they differ only by a constant factor. AI code almost always uses ln.

🐍 In code 
import math, numpy as np
math.log2(8), math.log10(1000)        # (3.0, 3.0)
2 ** -3, 2 ** 0.5                       # (0.125, 1.414...)

p = np.full(60, 0.1, dtype=np.float32)     # 60 tokens, 10% each
np.prod(p)                                # 0.0  -- underflow!
np.sum(np.log10(p))                       # -60.0 -- fine: the answer is 10^-60
np.sum(np.log(p))                         # -138.16, natural log: what AI code uses
🌍 Where it's used
Log-probabilities for every token 🏭 In production · 2023 OpenAI Cookbook: Using logprobs

How: with logprobs switched on, the API returns the log probability of each generated token. Adding them gives the log probability of the whole answer; the guide shows using them as a confidence score, for example in classification.

Why this method: the direct product of hundreds of probabilities underflows to 0, as in the playground. The sum of logs stays an ordinary number, and comparing two answers is just comparing two sums.

Power laws as straight lines on log-log plots 🧠 LLMs · 2020 Kaplan et al.: Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

How: the paper found that the cross-entropy loss falls as a power law in model size, dataset size and compute, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude. On log-log axes each trend is a straight line.

Why this method: a curve over a range of 10 million is impossible to read on ordinary axes. Logs squash it so every factor of 10 gets equal space, and the straight line can be extended to predict bigger models.

Negative and fractional exponents in the Transformer 🧠 LLMs · 2017 Attention Is All You Need

How: each attention head compares vectors of 64 numbers, and their scores are divided by √64 = 8, which is 640.5. Later in training, the step size shrinks like 1/√step = step−0.5: 4× more steps → half-size steps.

Why this method: the paper explains that dot products of long vectors grow large and push the softmax into regions with tiny gradients; dividing by √64 tames them. step−0.5 shrinks the step size gently rather than all at once.

Log-transforming skewed data 📊 Data science · 2018 scikit-learn TransformedTargetRegressor

How: to predict something like income or price, fit the model to log(value) instead, then undo with exp (Lesson 0.4). scikit-learn (since version 0.20, 2018) wraps this in one class whose docs use np.log and np.exp as the example pair; transforming incomes by the log is a textbook case.

Why this method: a few huge values (billionaires, mansions) dominate a squared-error fit. On a log scale, a 10% error counts the same for a cheap flat and a mansion.

🤯 Fun fact · a 400-year-old AI trick

John Napier published logarithms in 1614 for exactly the reason LLMs use them: to replace hard multiplications with easy additions. Henry Briggs printed the first base-10 log table in 1617, and navigators, scientists and engineers adopted them quickly. Today's LLMs add log-probabilities for the same reason, billions of times a second.

🌉 AI bridge · logs everywhere

LLMs: the training loss is the average of −log(probability of the right token) (Lesson 6.3), and the safe way to compute softmax in log-land is the “log-sum-exp” trick (Lesson 8.5). Training as “make the data probable” uses log-likelihoods (Lesson 5.3).

RL: the most important formula in policy-gradient RL nudges log π (the log-probability of the chosen action). See it in the RL course's Lesson 5.2: REINFORCE & the log-derivative trick.

Check yourself

1. What is 2⁻³?
A negative exponent means divide: 2⁻³ = 1 / 2³ = 1/8 = 0.125. It's never negative: a power of a positive number is always positive.
2. Using logs, what is log₁₀(1,000 × 0.01)?
log₁₀(1,000) = 3 and log₁₀(0.01) = −2. The log of a product is the sum: 3 + (−2) = 1. Check: 1,000 × 0.01 = 10, and log₁₀(10) = 1.
3. Why do language models add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?
0.1 multiplied 46 times is already 0 in 32-bit numbers. log(a × b) = log a + log b gives the same information as a sum, like −60 instead of 10⁻⁶⁰, and sums of normal-sized numbers never underflow.
📝 TL;DR
  • bn = n copies of b multiplied. Negative exponent = divide, fractional = root, power 0 = 1.
  • logb(y) = “which power of b gives y?”. log₁₀ ≈ number of digits − 1; logs of numbers below 1 are negative.
  • log(a × b) = log a + log b: logs turn multiplying into adding.
  • Used for: log-probabilities in LLMs (no underflow), the log-loss, scaling laws on log-log plots, log-transformed data.
