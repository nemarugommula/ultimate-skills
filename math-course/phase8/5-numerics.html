Numbers on computers: floats, log-sum-exp, bf16

Computers store numbers in a fixed number of bits, so they round, overflow and underflow. Knowing how is what lets LLMs train in 16 bits, run in 4, and never compute e1000.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Powers of 2 and scientific notation: 6.5 = 1.625 × 2², just like 6,500 = 6.5 × 10³ (Lesson 0.3).
  • ex and log undo each other, and log turns products into sums: log(a·b) = log a + log b (Lessons 0.3–0.4).
  • Softmax doesn't change if you subtract the same number from every score (Lesson 8.2).
🎯 Hook

Type 0.1 + 0.2 into Python or a browser console. You get 0.30000000000000004. Type math.exp(1000) in Python: OverflowError. In NumPy: inf, with a warning.

Meanwhile, LLMs with hundreds of billions of weights are trained with 16-bit numbers and often served with 8 or even 4 bits per weight. This lesson shows how numbers are really stored, and the handful of tricks that keep all that from blowing up.

Numbers first: scientific notation in binary

A computer stores a “floating-point” number the way you'd write 6,500 as 6.5 × 10³, but in base 2. Take 6.5. It's 1.625 × 2². So the computer keeps three things:

  • a sign bit: 0 for +, 1 for −;
  • an exponent: here 2 (stored with a fixed exponent offset added, so negative exponents fit too; the official name is the “bias”, which has nothing to do with the b of Lessons 8.1–8.3);
  • a mantissa: the digits after “1.”, here .625 = .101 in binary.
value = (−1)s × 1.m × 2e − offset “Minus one to the sign, times one-point-mantissa, times two to the (stored exponent minus the offset).” For fp32 the offset is 127, so 6.5's exponent 2 is stored as 129. The exponent sets the range (how big or small). The mantissa sets the precision (how many digits).

The catch: each part gets a fixed number of bits. 0.1 in binary is 0.000110011001100… forever, like 1/3 = 0.333… in decimal. It gets chopped, so the computer's 0.1 is really 0.100000001490116… (in 32 bits). Add two chopped numbers and the error shows.

💡 Big idea A float has a huge range but only a few significant digits, and the gap between neighbouring numbers grows with their size. ML numerics is about three dangers: overflow (too big → ∞), underflow (too small → 0) and rounding (small changes vanish).
🧠 Analogy: a calculator with only 3 digits

Imagine a calculator that shows 3 significant digits and an exponent. It can show 1.23 × 10−30 and 9.99 × 1030, a huge range. But 1,000 + 1 = 1.00 × 10³ again: the +1 falls below the third digit and vanishes. That's fp16 at 2,048: 2,048 + 1 = 2,048.

“Smallest at full precision” (officially the smallest normal number): below it, numbers can still be stored but keep fewer and fewer digits (“subnormal”) until they round to 0.

Read the table like a trade-off. fp16 keeps more digits but tops out at 65,504. bf16 keeps fp32's 8 exponent bits, so it has the same range as fp32, but only 7 mantissa bits. For training, range turns out to matter more than digits.

Overflow, underflow and the log-sum-exp trick

Softmax needs ez. In fp32, e89 is already too big (∞). In fp16, e12 ≈ 162,755 is too big. And LLM logits can easily be in the tens. So naive softmax of [1000, 999] computes ∞ / ∞ = NaN (“not a number”), and one NaN spreads through the whole network.

The fix uses Lesson 8.2's fact: subtracting the same number from every score changes nothing. Subtract the max. Now the biggest exponent is e⁰ = 1 and nothing can overflow:

softmax([1000, 999]) = softmax([0, −1]) = [1, 0.368] / 1.368 = [0.731, 0.269]

Cross-entropy needs the log of a probability, and log pk = zk − log Σj ezj. That last piece is called log-sum-exp, and it gets the same trick:

log Σj ezj = m + log Σj ezj − m,   m = maxj zj “The log of the sum of e to each score equals the biggest score, plus the log of the sum of e to each (score minus the biggest).” Example: log(e1000 + e999) = 1000 + log(1 + e−1) = 1000.313, with no ∞ anywhere.

This is why PyTorch has log_softmax and why cross_entropy takes raw logits. Its documentation warns that computing log(softmax(x)) in two steps is “slower and numerically unstable”.

Quantization: storing weights in 8 or 4 bits

A 7-billion-weight model takes 28 GB in fp32, 14 GB in bf16, 7 GB in int8 and 3.5 GB in 4 bits. To squeeze weights into a few bits, pick a scale so the largest weight maps to the largest integer, round, and multiply back when you need the number:

s = max|w| / (2k−1 − 1)    q = round(w / s)    ŵ = q × s “The scale is the biggest absolute weight divided by the biggest k-bit integer. Store the rounded integer q. To use it, multiply back: w-hat ≈ w.” For k = 8 the biggest integer is 127, for k = 4 it's 7.
✏️ Worked example: 4 weights to int8 and int4
  • Weights [0.5, −1.27, 0.03, 0.9]. Largest |w| = 1.27.
  • int8: s = 1.27 / 127 = 0.01. q = round(w / 0.01) = [50, −127, 3, 90]. Multiply back: exactly [0.5, −1.27, 0.03, 0.9]. (Lucky numbers; usually the error is up to s/2 = 0.005.)
  • int4: s = 1.27 / 7 = 0.181. w / s = [2.76, −7, 0.17, 4.96] → q = [3, −7, 0, 5] → ŵ = [0.544, −1.27, 0, 0.907]. Errors [0.044, 0, 0.03, 0.007]: the small weight 0.03 became 0.

Now imagine one weight is 8.0, an outlier. The scale grows to 8/7 ≈ 1.14 and every normal weight rounds to −1, 0 or 1. That's the problem LLM.int8() solved (below).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
0.1 + 0.2                                # 0.30000000000000004
np.float32(16777216) + np.float32(1)        # 16777216.0: the +1 is below the gap
np.finfo(np.float16).max                 # 65504.0
z = np.array([1000., 999.])
np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()              # [nan, nan] with an overflow warning
m = z.max(); m + np.log(np.exp(z - m).sum())  # log-sum-exp = 1000.313, safe

import torch
torch.log_softmax(torch.tensor(z), dim=0)  # tensor([-0.3133, -1.3133]): does the trick for you
torch.finfo(torch.bfloat16).max             # about 3.39e38, same range as float32

🌍 Where it's used
Mixed-precision training 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 Micikevicius et al., Mixed Precision Training

How: weights, activations and gradients are stored in fp16, while a master copy of the weights stays in fp32 to accumulate the small updates. The loss is multiplied by a scale factor before the backward pass, so tiny gradients (like 10⁻⁸, which fp16 rounds to 0) are pushed up into fp16's range, then divided back.

Why this method: 16-bit numbers halve memory (the paper reports nearly 2× savings) and run faster on hardware built for them. The fp32 copy and loss scaling fix exactly the rounding and underflow you saw in the explorer.

bfloat16: fp32's range in 16 bits 🏭 In production · 2017 bfloat16 (Google Brain, TPU v2)

How: keep fp32's 8 exponent bits, cut the mantissa to 7. Google Brain designed it for its TPU v2 chips (2017), and it's now widely used for training on GPUs too.

Why this method: an Intel/Facebook study (arXiv 1905.12322) found bf16 training matches fp32 results with no hyper-parameter changes, because it has the same range; fp16 needs tuning such as loss scaling.

8-bit LLMs without the outlier problem 🧠 LLMs · 2022 Dettmers et al., LLM.int8()

How: quantize with a separate scale for each row and column of the matrix multiply (like the per-group toggle), and keep the few “emergent outlier” feature dimensions in 16 bits. More than 99.9% of the values are still multiplied in 8 bits.

Why this method: one scale for everything lets a few huge values wreck the precision of all the others (the outlier toggle). With this split, a 175B-parameter model runs in int8 “without performance degradation”, using half the memory.

4-bit fine-tuning 🧠 LLMs · 2023 Dettmers et al., QLoRA

How: the frozen model is stored in 4 bits with a new data type, 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), whose 16 levels are placed for bell-curve-shaped weights. Small LoRA adapters (Lesson 2.4) are trained on top in higher precision.

Why this method: it cut memory enough to fine-tune a 65B-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU while matching 16-bit fine-tuning performance, according to the paper.

🤯 Fun fact · a $370-million overflow

On 4 June 1996, the first Ariane 5 rocket veered off course 37 seconds after launch and self-destructed. The cause: code reused from Ariane 4 converted a 64-bit floating-point number (a horizontal velocity value) into a 16-bit signed integer. Ariane 5 flew faster, the number didn't fit, and the conversion overflowed. The loss was more than US$370 million.

🌉 AI bridge · logs everywhere

LLMs: the probability of a 1,000-token answer is a product of 1,000 probabilities. If each is 0.5, that's 0.51000 ≈ 10−301: fp32 rounds it to 0. So models only ever work with log-probabilities and sums (Lessons 4.5, 6.3), computed with log-sum-exp.

RL: policy gradients and PPO are written in terms of log π, for exactly this reason. The RL course makes the same point in Lesson 5.2's log refresher. Next lesson: that math, start to finish.

Check yourself

1. The largest fp16 number is 65,504 and e11 ≈ 59,874. What happens when you compute e12 in fp16?
e12 ≈ 162,755, far above 65,504, so it rounds to ∞. That's why naive softmax in fp16 dies with logits above about 11, and why everyone subtracts the max first.
2. Compute log(e100 + e100) safely with the log-sum-exp trick.
m = 100. Then 100 + log(e⁰ + e⁰) = 100 + log 2 ≈ 100.693. You never had to store e100 (about 2.7 × 10⁴³, which would overflow fp16).
3. Why do most LLMs train in bf16 rather than fp16, even though fp16 has more digits?
Both use 16 bits. bf16 spends 8 of them on the exponent (fp16: 5), trading digits for range. Training cares more about not hitting ∞ or 0 than about the 3rd digit.
📝 TL;DR
  • Float = sign × 1.mantissa × 2exponent: exponent bits give range, mantissa bits give precision.
  • fp32 (8/23), fp16 (5/10, max 65,504), bf16 (8/7, fp32's range). Gaps between numbers grow with size.
  • Never compute ebig: subtract the max; use log-sum-exp, log_softmax, and log-probabilities.
  • Quantization: q = round(w / s), ŵ = q·s. Outliers hurt; per-group scales and outlier handling fix it.
  • Used in mixed-precision training, bf16 TPUs/GPUs, LLM.int8() and QLoRA's 4-bit NF4.
