Phase 8 · Lesson 2

Softmax classifiers & the cross-entropy gradient

Turn a list of scores into probabilities that add up to 1, then learn from mistakes with the simplest gradient in deep learning: p − y. It's the last layer of image classifiers, of every LLM and of many RL policies.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • ex is always positive and grows fast: e⁰ = 1, e¹ ≈ 2.72, e² ≈ 7.39 (Lesson 0.4).
  • Logistic regression: p = σ(w · x + b), and its gradient is (p − y) × x (Lesson 8.1).
  • Cross-entropy: the loss is −log of the probability you gave the right answer (Lesson 6.3).
🎯 Hook

GPT-2 knows 50,257 tokens. To pick the next one, its very last step takes a list of 768 numbers (its “thought” about the text so far) and computes 50,257 dot products, one per token. That gives 50,257 scores like 3.1, −0.4, 7.9… Then one formula turns them all into probabilities that add up to exactly 1.

During training, the correction for every one of those 50,257 scores is a single subtraction. This lesson is that formula and that subtraction.

Numbers first: from scores to probabilities

A photo classifier gives three scores (called logits): 🐱 cat 2, 🐶 dog 1, 🐦 bird 0. Scores can be negative and don't add up to anything. Two moves fix that:

  1. Exponentiate each score, so all of them become positive: e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1.
  2. Divide by the total (7.39 + 2.72 + 1 = 11.11), so they add up to 1.
p = [7.39, 2.72, 1] / 11.11 = [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]  →  66.5% cat, 24.5% dog, 9.0% bird
pk = softmax(z)k = ezk / Σj ezj “The probability of class k is e to the power of its score, divided by the sum of e to the power of every score.” z is the list of logits, Σj adds over all classes j.
💡 Big idea Softmax = exponentiate, then share out. Bigger score, bigger share, but nobody ever gets exactly 0. And when you train it with cross-entropy, the gradient for each score is just its probability minus its target: p − y.
🧠 Analogy: splitting the prize money

Three contestants get scores from the judges. The prize pot is split in proportion to escore. One extra point multiplies your share by about 2.7, so a small lead becomes a big slice. Everyone adds 10 points? The slices stay exactly the same: only differences between scores matter.

Three handy facts

  • Only differences matter. Scores [102, 101, 100] give the same [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]. Computers use this to stay safe from overflow (Lesson 8.5).
  • Two classes = the sigmoid. softmax([z, 0]) gives ez/(ez + 1) = σ(z) for the first class: Lesson 8.1's logistic regression is the 2-class case.
  • Temperature. Divide all scores by T first: T < 1 sharpens, T > 1 flattens. That's the “temperature” knob on chatbots (Lesson 4.6).

Learning: the gradient is p − y

Say the photo really was a cat. Write the truth as a one-hot list y = [1, 0, 0]: a 1 for the right class, 0 elsewhere. The loss is cross-entropy, −log of the cat's probability: −log 0.665 = 0.408. How should each score move? Nudge each one by 0.01 and watch the loss:

cat score 2 → 2.010.4043−0.00330.01 × (0.665 − 1) = −0.0033

bird score 0 → 0.010.4085+0.00090.01 × (0.090 − 0) = +0.0009

Every change is 0.01 × (probability − target). Why p − y? Since pcat = ezcat / total, the loss splits in two: −log pcat = −zcat + log(total) (log of a ratio = difference of logs, Lesson 0.3). Raising any score k grows log(total) at a rate equal to that class's share of the total, pk. Raising the right class's score also lowers the −zcat part at rate 1, which is yk. Together: pk − yk.

Same gradient as Lesson 6.3's q − p, new letters: in Phase 6 the target was p and the model q; from here on the model is p and the target y.

∂L/∂zk = pk − yk “The slope of the loss with respect to score k is the probability of class k minus its target.” For the right class that's p − 1 (negative, so gradient descent pushes it up). For every wrong class it's just p (positive, so it gets pushed down by exactly how much it was believed).
✏️ Worked example: one gradient step on the logits
  • p = [0.665, 0.245, 0.090], y = [1, 0, 0]. Gradient p − y = [−0.335, +0.245, +0.090]. Notice it adds up to 0: what the cat gains, the others lose.
  • Step with η = 0.5: z = [2 + 0.167, 1 − 0.122, 0 − 0.045] = [2.167, 0.878, −0.045].
  • New softmax: [0.722, 0.199, 0.079]. Cat went from 66.5% to 72.2%, and the loss fell from 0.408 to 0.326.
  • In a real model the logits come from weights, z = Wx + b, and the chain rule (Lesson 3.2) passes p − y back: row k of W gets gradient (pk − yk) × x. Exactly 8.1's rule, once per class.

A softmax classifier: one weight vector per class

Give each class its own weight vector. The score for class k is a dot product, zk = wk · x + bk. Stack the vectors as rows of a matrix and all the scores come out of one matrix-vector product, z = Wx + b (Lesson 2.2). The predicted class is the one whose weight vector points most toward the input. Each pair of classes is split by a straight line, so the plane gets cut into straight-edged regions.

The same layer inside every LLM

Swap “3 classes” for “50,257 tokens” and x for the model's final hidden vector h (768 numbers in GPT-2 small). The output layer is a 50,257 × 768 matrix: 38.6 million weights, one row per token. Logits = Wh, probabilities = softmax, loss = −log p(the real next token), gradient = p − y. Many models even reuse the input embedding matrix as this output matrix (“weight tying”), so a token's row is both how it's read in and how it's scored on the way out (Lesson 1.5).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
z = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.0]); y = np.array([1, 0, 0])
p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()        # softmax (minus the max: Lesson 8.5)
loss = -np.log(p[y.argmax()])                # 0.408
grad = p - y                                 # [-0.335, 0.245, 0.090]

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
logits = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.0]], requires_grad=True)
F.cross_entropy(logits, torch.tensor([0])).backward()  # takes raw logits, not probabilities
logits.grad                                  # tensor([[-0.335, 0.245, 0.090]])  = p - y

🌍 Where it's used
Softmax over 1,000 image classes 🖼️ Deep learning · 2012 AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton)

How: the network ends in “a final 1000-way softmax”: 1,000 logits, one per ImageNet category, turned into probabilities and trained with cross-entropy.

Why this method: it gives a full ranked list (the famous “top-5 accuracy” counts a hit if the right label is among the 5 most probable), and p − y gives every class a useful gradient on every image.

The LLM output layer over the vocabulary 🧠 LLMs · 2019 GPT-2 config (vocab_size 50257, n_embd 768)

How: final hidden vector (768 numbers) → one dot product per token → 50,257 logits → softmax → sample the next token. Training minimizes −log p(actual next token), whose gradient on the logits is p − y.

Why this method: next-token prediction is a 50,257-way classification problem, and softmax is the standard way to get a proper probability distribution that you can sample from with a temperature.

Weight tying: one matrix in, the same matrix out 🧠 LLMs · 2016 Press & Wolf, Using the Output Embedding

How: the softmax layer's weight matrix has one row per word, just like the embedding table. The paper shows this output matrix is itself a valid word embedding and recommends tying the two. The original Transformer paper shares the same matrix between its embedding layers and the pre-softmax layer.

Why this method: the paper reports lower perplexity, and tying can shrink translation models to less than half their size without hurting performance.

Softmax policies in RL 🎮 RL · 1992 RL course 5.1: Why learn the policy directly?

How: a policy network outputs one score per action and softmax turns them into π(a|s), the probability of each action. Training the policy uses ∇ log π, and for softmax that's one-hot(action) − π: the same p − y, with the sign flipped and scaled by how good the outcome was (Lesson 8.6, and Williams' 1992 REINFORCE in RL 5.2).

Why this method: argmax over scores always picks the same action. Softmax keeps every action possible, so the agent keeps exploring, and small weight changes shift behavior smoothly.

🤯 Fun fact · softmax is 150 years old

Long before neural networks, physicists used exactly this formula. In statistical mechanics it's the Boltzmann distribution: the probability of a state with energy E is proportional to e−E/(kT), where T is the temperature. It goes back to Boltzmann's 1868 paper and was made famous by Gibbs' 1902 textbook. That's softmax with scores −E/k, and it's why the knob that flattens a chatbot's choices is called temperature.

🌉 AI bridge · softmax everywhere

LLMs: softmax appears twice in every transformer: once at the output (this lesson) and once inside every attention head, where it turns “how relevant is each word?” scores into mixing weights (Lesson 8.4).

RL: the softmax gradient one-hot − π is the heart of the policy gradient (Lesson 8.6, and the RL course's REINFORCE worked example, which uses exactly this update).

Numerics: e1000 overflows a computer. Lesson 8.5 shows the one-line fix (subtract the max) that every library uses.

Check yourself

1. Logits [0, 0, ln 2] (ln 2 ≈ 0.69). What does softmax give?
e⁰ = 1, e⁰ = 1, eln 2 = 2. Total 4. So [1/4, 1/4, 2/4]. Scores of 0 don't mean probability 0: e⁰ = 1.
2. A model outputs p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1] but the right answer is class 3. What is the gradient on the logits?
p − y = [0.7 − 0, 0.2 − 0, 0.1 − 1]. Gradient descent subtracts it: class 3's logit goes up by the most, the wrongly favoured class 1 goes down the most. The entries add up to 0.
3. Why do LLMs end with a softmax over the vocabulary instead of just outputting the highest-scoring token?
Argmax has no useful gradient and always gives the same answer. Softmax gives a full distribution: cross-entropy can train it, and sampling (Lesson 4.6) can make answers varied.
📝 TL;DR
  • softmax(z)k = ezk / Σ ezj: exponentiate, then share out. Only score differences matter.
  • With cross-entropy, the gradient on the logits is p − y: right class pushed up, wrong classes pushed down by their probability.
  • A softmax classifier: z = Wx + b, one weight vector per class, straight-edged regions.
  • It's the output layer of image classifiers and LLMs (one row per token), the mixer inside attention, and the policy in RL.
