Phase 8 · Lesson 4b

Multivariate Gaussians & diffusion models

A bell curve in many dimensions is a tilted cloud of points. Add a little of that noise to a picture again and again, teach a network to guess the noise, and you have the engine behind Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2 and robot policies.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The Gaussian N(μ, σ²): a bell curve with centre μ (“mu”) and width σ (“sigma”). N(0, 1) is the standard normal (Lesson 4.3).
  • The covariance matrix: each feature's variance on the diagonal, “how much two features move together” off the diagonal. Its eigenvectors (directions a matrix only stretches) are the directions of most spread, as in PCA (Lessons 5.2, 2.5, 2.6).
  • MSE = the average of squared errors, the standard loss for “guess a number” (Lesson 7.1). A network with knobs θ can learn to output any guess we train it for (Lesson 8.3).
🎯 Hook

Ask Stable Diffusion for a 512 × 512 picture of “a corgi astronaut”. It doesn't start with a sketch. It starts with pure static: 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 random numbers drawn from a bell curve. Then it asks one neural network the same question over and over: “what noise is hiding in here?”, and subtracts a bit of the answer each time. A corgi appears.

The network was trained with nothing fancier than mean squared error. This lesson explains every piece: what “bell curve noise” means in thousands of dimensions, how to destroy a picture in one jump, and how guessing the noise lets you run that movie backwards.

Numbers first: a bell curve in 2D is a cloud

Measure many adults. Height averages 170 cm with a spread σ of 10 cm. Weight averages 70 kg with σ = 12 kg. Tall people tend to be heavier: the correlation (Lesson 5.2) is 0.5. Plot each person as a dot and you get a tilted, oval cloud.

Two numbers describe the centre, and a 2 × 2 grid describes the shape. The grid's diagonal holds the variances (10² = 100, 12² = 144). The off-diagonal holds the covariance: correlation × σ × σ = 0.5 × 10 × 12 = 60.

μ = [170, 70]     Σ = [[100, 60], [60, 144]]
x ~ N(μ, Σ) “x is drawn from a multivariate normal with mean vector mu and covariance matrix Sigma.” The ~ means “is drawn from”. μ says where the cloud sits; Σ says how wide it is in each direction and how it's tilted.

Careful: Σ (capital sigma) has a third job here. It is not “add these up” (Lesson 0.5), and not the stretch grid in the SVD A = UΣVᵀ (Lesson 2.6). In this lesson Σ is the covariance matrix. It got the letter because it's the many-dimensional version of σ².

x: a data point (a person, a picture)μ, Σ, ε: the distribution and its noiseL: a matrix

How a computer draws one person

Start with two independent standard-normal draws, say z = [1, −0.5]. Then: height = 170 + 10 × 1 = 180. Weight borrows half of the height's noise (the correlation 0.5) and adds its own: 70 + 12 × (0.5 × 1 + 0.866 × (−0.5)) = 70 + 6 − 5.20 = 70.80. In matrix form, that's one matrix times the noise, plus the mean:

x = μ + L z,   z ~ N(0, I),   LLᵀ = Σ “x equals mu plus L times z.” z is a list of independent standard-normal numbers; N(0, I) means centre 0 and covariance I (the identity: 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere), a perfectly round cloud. L is a “square root” of Σ: L times L-transposed gives back Σ. Here L = [[10, 0], [6, 10.39]].

Check: LLᵀ = [[10·10, 10·6], [6·10, 6·6 + 10.39·10.39]] = [[100, 60], [60, 144]] ✓. The round cloud gets stretched and tilted by L, then slid into place by μ.

💡 Big idea A multivariate Gaussian is a round cloud of standard noise, reshaped by a matrix and shifted by a mean: x = μ + Lz. Σ's diagonal sets the width along each axis, its off-diagonal sets the tilt, and the cloud's long and short axes are Σ's eigenvectors. Every diffusion model is built from this one recipe.

The shape is an ellipse, and its axes are eigenvectors. Take Σ = [[1, 0.8], [0.8, 1]]. Its eigenvectors are [1, 1] (eigenvalue 1.8) and [1, −1] (eigenvalue 0.2), like Lesson 2.6's worked example. So the cloud is stretched along the diagonal with spread √1.8 ≈ 1.34 and squeezed across it to √0.2 ≈ 0.45. That's PCA in reverse: PCA reads the axes off a cloud; here we build the cloud from them.

Push ρ to 0.9 and the cloud becomes a thin tilted cigar: one eigenvalue grows, the other shrinks toward 0. Notice also the “inside” numbers. In 1D, 68% of draws land within 1 σ (Lesson 4.3). In 2D only about 39% land inside the 1-σ ellipse, and about 86% inside 2 σ. With more dimensions there are more ways to be a little bit off, so fewer points stay near the centre. Hold that thought for the fun fact.

🧠 Analogy: a can of spray paint

Spray straight at a wall and you get a round speckle: that's N(0, I). Tilt the nozzle and the speckle stretches into a slanted oval: that's L at work, and Σ describes the oval. Where you aim is μ. Every speck is random, but the shape of the spray is completely described by an aim and a 2 × 2 grid.

Diffusion part 1: destroy a picture, one sprinkle at a time

A picture is just a long list of numbers (pixels scaled to between −1 and 1), so it's one point in a huge space. Take one pixel with value 0.8. One step of the forward process shrinks it a hair and sprinkles in a little fresh noise:

xt = √(1 − βt) · xt−1 + √βt · ε,   ε ~ N(0, I) “The picture at step t is a slightly shrunk copy of the picture at step t − 1, plus a little noise.” β (“beta”, the noise amount per step; not Adam's or RLHF's β) is tiny. ε (“epsilon”) is a fresh standard-normal draw for every pixel.

The original DDPM paper uses T = 1,000 steps, with β growing in a straight line from 0.0001 to 0.02. The first step multiplies the pixel by 0.99995 and adds 0.01 × noise: you can't see the change. But after all 1,000 steps the pixel keeps only 0.0064 of its value (0.8 → 0.005). What's left is pure N(0, I) static.

The shortcut: jump to any step at once

Here's the neat fact. Gaussian noise added to Gaussian noise is still Gaussian (variances add, Lesson 4.4). So 250 small sprinkles are the same as one bigger sprinkle, and you can skip straight to step t:

xt = √ᾱt · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε,   ᾱt = (1 − β₁)(1 − β₂)⋯(1 − βt) “Keep root-alpha-bar of the picture, add root-(one minus alpha-bar) of noise.” ᾱ (“alpha-bar”) is all the per-step keep factors multiplied together (a Π, “multiply these up”, Lesson 0.5). x₀ is the clean picture.
step t01002505007501000
keep √ᾱt of the picture10.9470.7240.2800.0580.006

The two numbers in each column always satisfy keep² + noise² = 1 (at t = 250: 0.724² + 0.690² = 0.524 + 0.476 = 1). That keeps the overall size of the numbers steady while the picture fades into noise.

✏️ Worked example: noise a 2-pixel picture, then grade a guess
  • Picture x₀ = [0.8, −0.4], noise draw ε = [0.5, −1.2], step t = 250 (keep 0.724, noise 0.690).
  • x₂₅₀ = 0.724 × [0.8, −0.4] + 0.690 × [0.5, −1.2] = [0.579, −0.290] + [0.345, −0.828] = [0.924, −1.117].
  • A network sees only [0.924, −1.117] and t = 250. It guesses the noise: ε̂ = [0.4, −1.0].
  • MSE loss: ((0.5 − 0.4)² + (−1.2 + 1.0)²) / 2 = (0.01 + 0.04) / 2 = 0.025.
  • Undo the jump with the guess: (x₂₅₀ − 0.690 × ε̂) / 0.724 = [0.895, −0.591], a decent estimate of the real [0.8, −0.4].

Diffusion part 2: train a network to guess the noise

Training repeats that worked example millions of times: pick a real picture, pick a random step t from 1 to 1,000, draw noise, jump with the shortcut, and ask the network εθ what noise was added. Grade it with plain MSE:

loss = ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱt x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, t ) ‖² “The loss is the squared length of the gap between the real noise and the network's guess.” ε_θ (“epsilon-theta”) is the network with knobs θ; it gets the noisy picture and the step number t. ‖ ‖² means square every entry's error and add them up (average them, and it's Lesson 7.1's MSE).

That's the whole training loop of DDPM (Algorithm 1 in the paper). No adversary, no labels: the “right answer” is the noise we added ourselves.

Diffusion part 3: generate by removing noise, step by step

To make a brand-new picture, start from pure noise x₁₀₀₀ ~ N(0, I). At each step, ask the network for its noise guess, subtract the right amount of it, and (except on the very last step) add back a small fresh sprinkle z:

xt−1 = ( xt − βt / √(1 − ᾱt) · εθ(xt, t) ) / √(1 − βt) + √βt · z “One step back: remove a small slice of the guessed noise, undo the shrink, then add a little fresh randomness.” That last sprinkle keeps the samples varied. This is DDPM's Algorithm 2, with its σ_t² = β_t option.

Another way to see the guess: −εθ is an arrow pointing from the noisy point toward where real data is more likely. Divided by √(1 − ᾱ), it's called the score. Song et al. (2020) showed that running the noise process backwards needs only this score, which ties DDPM and “score-based” models into one picture.

Things to notice. By t ≈ 500 the smiley is unrecognisable, though √ᾱ = 0.28 of it is still in there. During generation the green cloud just shrinks slowly for hundreds of steps; the shape only snaps into focus in roughly the last hundred. And the denoiser's error (the MSE readout) is largest at small t: a tiny wobble on a picture is hard to tell from detail, while at t = 1,000 the noise is almost all there is, so it's easy to guess.

Honest note: no network is trained in your browser. The arrows come from the exact best noise guess for a Gaussian mixture: a tiny bell curve (σ = 0.04) around every toy point. For that the formula fits in a few lines. Real images have no such formula, so a big network (a U-Net or a transformer) learns an approximation of the same arrows from millions of examples with the MSE loss above. The step-by-step loop is the same.

Making it practical: latents and guidance

Latent diffusion. A 512 × 512 colour image has 786,432 numbers, and the network runs 1,000 times per picture. Stable Diffusion first squeezes the image with an autoencoder into a 64 × 64 × 4 “latent” grid, 48 times fewer numbers, runs the whole diffusion there, and decodes the result back to pixels at the end. The starting static of the hook lives in that latent space.

Classifier-free guidance. To follow a prompt, the network also gets the text c. During training the text is randomly blanked (often 10–20% of the time), so the same network learns two guesses: with the prompt and without. At generation time, exaggerate the difference: ε̃ = (1 + w) · εθ(x, c) − w · εθ(x). A bigger guidance weight w gives pictures that match the prompt more closely but vary less.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
mu = np.array([170, 70]); Sigma = np.array([[100, 60], [60, 144]])
L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)          # [[10, 0], [6, 10.39]]: L @ L.T == Sigma
x = mu + L @ np.random.randn(2)        # one person: mu + L z
X = np.random.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=10_000); np.cov(X.T)   # ≈ Sigma

beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000); alpha_bar = np.cumprod(1 - beta)  # DDPM schedule
alpha_bar[249]                         # 0.524 at t = 250 (Python counts from 0)

# One DDPM training step in PyTorch (model = any network taking (x_t, t))
t   = torch.randint(0, 1000, (B,))               # a random step for each image in the batch
eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                         # the noise we hide
ab  = alpha_bar[t].view(B, 1, 1, 1)                # alpha_bar as a torch tensor
xt  = ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps        # jump straight to step t
loss = F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)               # guess the noise, plain MSE
loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

🌍 Where it's used
Predict-the-noise with MSE 🖼️ Deep learning · 2020 Ho, Jain & Abbeel: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)

How: exactly this lesson: T = 1,000, β from 0.0001 to 0.02, the shortcut xt = √ᾱ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ) ε, a U-Net trained on ‖ε − εθ‖², and the step-by-step sampler. It reached an FID of 3.17 on CIFAR-10, state of the art at the time.

Why this method: the full probabilistic loss has a different weight for each step. The authors dropped those weights and kept plain MSE, and found that this simpler loss gave better samples, because it focuses training on the harder, noisier steps.

Diffusion in a compressed latent space 🏭 In production · 2022 Rombach et al.: Latent Diffusion Models · Stable Diffusion v1-4 model card

How: an autoencoder maps an H × W × 3 image to an H/8 × W/8 × 4 latent; a U-Net does diffusion there, reading the prompt through cross-attention (Lesson 8.4) from a CLIP text encoder. The model card describes the loss as reconstructing the noise that was added to the latent.

Why this method: the paper notes that training powerful pixel-space diffusion models often takes hundreds of GPU days. Working with 48× fewer numbers (for 512 × 512) makes training and generation far cheaper; the paper found compression factors of 4 to 16 struck a good balance between speed and quality.

Classifier-free guidance for text-to-image 🖼️ Deep learning · 2022 Ramesh et al.: DALL·E 2 (unCLIP) · Ho & Salimans: Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance

How: DALL·E 2's decoder is a 3.5-billion-parameter diffusion model that paints an image from a CLIP image embedding. It enables classifier-free guidance by setting that embedding to zero 10% of the time during training, and uses the guided mix (1 + w) ε(x, c) − w ε(x) when sampling.

Why this method: the older “classifier guidance” needed a separate image classifier trained on noisy images. Ho & Salimans showed one network, trained with the condition sometimes dropped (they found 10–20% best), gives the same quality-versus-variety dial for free.

Diffusion Policy for robot arms 🎮 RL · 2023 Chi et al.: Diffusion Policy

How: instead of pixels, the “picture” is a short sequence of future robot actions. Conditioned on camera images, the policy starts from noise and denoises it into a plan, learned from human demonstrations. With 10 denoising steps it ran in 0.1 s on one GPU. The paper reports an average improvement of 46.9% over earlier methods across its benchmarks.

Why this method: demos are often multimodal: in their Push-T task the arm can go around the block on the left or the right. A policy trained with plain MSE predicts the average of its examples (the MSE-best single guess is the mean), and the average of left and right is straight into the block. A diffusion model, like the “Two blobs” dataset above, keeps both options.

μ + σ·ε: the reparameterization trick 🤖 Machine learning · 2013 Kingma & Welling: Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (VAE)

How: a VAE's encoder outputs a mean μ and a width σ for each hidden number, and the code is drawn as z = μ + σ · ε with ε ~ N(0, 1): this lesson's x = μ + Lz with a diagonal L. A KL term pulls those bell curves toward N(0, I) (Lesson 6.4).

Why this method: you can't backpropagate through “roll a die”. Writing the randomness as an outside ε makes z a plain formula of μ and σ, so ordinary gradient descent can train them; the paper shows the resulting loss can be optimized directly with standard stochastic gradient methods.

🤯 Fun fact · pure noise lives on a thin shell

Stable Diffusion's starting static has 16,384 standard-normal numbers. You might picture it as a fuzzy ball around 0. It isn't. Its length ‖ε‖ is almost always very close to √16,384 = 128: we drew 100 such noise grids and every length fell between 126.2 and 129.5. In high dimensions a bell curve is a thin hollow shell, which is the 39% from the playground taken to the extreme.

🌉 AI bridge · Gaussians and denoising everywhere

Deep learning: the same Gaussian recipe sets initial weights (Lesson 4.3), samples a VAE's code (Lesson 6.4), and drives every diffusion model. Song et al.'s score-based SDE paper (2020) shows the 1,000 small steps as one continuous noising process whose reverse needs only the score, and uses it to build new samplers, including one the paper says samples more efficiently.

LLMs: an image generator reads your prompt with a transformer text encoder and listens to it through cross-attention, the same softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V you met in Lesson 8.4.

RL: Diffusion Policy is a form of behavior cloning, learning a policy by copying expert demonstrations. Check yourself

1. Two features have σ₁ = 2, σ₂ = 3 and correlation ρ = 0.5. What number goes in the off-diagonal of their covariance matrix Σ?
Covariance = ρ × σ₁ × σ₂ = 0.5 × 2 × 3 = 3. So Σ = [[4, 3], [3, 9]]: variances 2² and 3² on the diagonal, covariance off it. 0.5 is the correlation (the unit-free version), and 6 forgets the ρ.
2. At some step t, √ᾱt = 0.6. A pixel has x₀ = 1 and its noise draw is ε = 0.5. What is xt?
The noise share is √(1 − 0.6²) = √0.64 = 0.8. So xt = 0.6 × 1 + 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.6 + 0.4 = 1.0. Keep 0.6 of the picture, add 0.8 of the noise, and 0.6² + 0.8² = 1.
3. Why did Diffusion Policy beat robot policies that output one action trained with plain MSE?
The single number that minimizes squared error is the mean of the examples. Averaging “left” and “right” gives “straight into the obstacle”. Diffusion models learn the whole distribution, bumps and all, like the “Two blobs” dataset in the playground. (They're actually slower: many denoising steps per action plan.)
📝 TL;DR
  • x ~ N(μ, Σ): a cloud with centre μ and covariance matrix Σ (variances on the diagonal, covariances off it). Σ here is not “add up” or the SVD's Σ.
  • Sample it as x = μ + Lz with z ~ N(0, I) and LLᵀ = Σ. The cloud's axes are Σ's eigenvectors.
  • Diffusion's forward process adds small Gaussian noise 1,000 times; the shortcut xt = √ᾱt x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) ε jumps to any step: keep √ᾱ of the picture, add √(1 − ᾱ) of noise.
  • Training: a network guesses ε from xt and t, with plain MSE. Generation: start from noise and subtract the guesses step by step.
  • Used in DDPM, Stable Diffusion (in a 48× smaller latent space), DALL·E 2 with classifier-free guidance, Diffusion Policy for robots, and VAEs' μ + σε.
