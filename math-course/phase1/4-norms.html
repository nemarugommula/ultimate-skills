Length, distance & norms (L1, L2)

There's more than one way to measure how big a vector is, or how far apart two are. The choice changes which neighbours a model finds, which weights it keeps, and how LLM training stays stable.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The length of a vector by Pythagoras: ‖[3, 4]‖ = √(3² + 4²) = 5, read “the length of x” (Lesson 1.1).
  • a − b is the arrow from b's tip to a's tip (Lesson 1.2).
  • Cosine similarity compares directions and ignores length (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook

Your map app says the café is 5 blocks away as the crow flies. The taxi meter says you drove 7 blocks. Both are right: they measure distance differently.

AI has the same choice, and it matters. Before every one of its training steps, GPT-3 measured the length of one gigantic vector (the direction to change all 175 billion weights) and, if that length was more than 1.0, shrank the vector to exactly 1.0. Measuring vector length is called taking a norm, and this lesson covers the three you'll meet everywhere.

Numbers first: three ways to measure [3, −4]

🐦 crow (L2): √(3² + (−4)²) = √25 = 5  ·  🚕 taxi (L1): |3| + |−4| = 3 + 4 = 7  ·  👑 chess king (L∞): max(|3|, |−4|) = 4

The bars |−4| mean absolute value: “drop the minus sign”, so |−4| = 4. Distances can't be negative.

  • L2 (Euclidean norm): the straight-line length from Lesson 1.1. When people just say “the norm”, they mean this one.
  • L1 (Manhattan or taxicab norm): add up the sizes of all the steps. A taxi on a street grid can't cut diagonally.
  • L∞ (max norm, read “L-infinity”): only the biggest step counts. A chess king moves diagonally for free, so going 3 across and 4 up takes 4 moves.
‖x‖₁ = Σi |xi|    ‖x‖₂ = √( Σi xi² )    ‖x‖∞ = maxi |xi| “The L1 norm of x is the sum of the absolute values. The L2 norm is the square root of the sum of squares. The L-infinity norm is the largest absolute value.” Σ means “add up over every slot i” (Lesson 0.5); the little 1, 2 or ∞ says which norm.

They're all members of one family, the p-norm, and the slider's p blends between them: p = 1 is the taxi, p = 2 is the crow, and as p grows the biggest slot takes over until you get L∞, the chess king.

💡 Big idea A norm turns a vector into one number: its size. And the distance between two points is just the size of the arrow between them: d(a, b) = ‖a − b‖. Pick a norm and you've picked what “close” means.

What does a “circle” look like?

A circle is “every point at length 1 from the center”. That depends on the norm! In L2 it's the round circle you know. In L1 it's a diamond (the corners are where all the length goes into one slot). In L∞ it's a square.

🎮 Playground · Norm shapes

Drag the blue point x. The bold shape is every point with the same p-norm as x; the small faint one is the “unit circle” (norm 1). Change p and watch the shape morph from diamond to circle to square.

Notice the diamond's corners sit on the axes, where one slot is exactly 0. Keep that picture: it's the reason L1 makes models sparse (many weights exactly 0), coming up below.

🧠 Analogy: how far is the café?

A bird flies straight there (L2). A taxi follows the grid (L1). A chess king, who can step diagonally, only cares about the longer of the two directions (L∞). Same two points, three honest answers. Which is “right” depends on how you're allowed to move, and in AI, on what you want “similar” to mean.

✏️ Worked example: distance and normalizing

Distance from a = [4, 6] to b = [1, 2]: first the arrow between them, a − b = [3, 4]. Then measure it: L2 = 5, L1 = 7, L∞ = 4.

Normalizing = dividing a vector by its own length, so it keeps its direction but has length exactly 1. Written x / ‖x‖ and read “x divided by its length”:

[3, 4] / 5 = [0.6, 0.8]   check: √(0.6² + 0.8²) = √(0.36 + 0.64) = √1 = 1 ✓

This is how embeddings get ready for cosine similarity (Lesson 1.3): after normalizing, a plain dot product is the cosine.

Squared L2 is the most common error in ML

A model predicts house prices ŷ = [3, 5, 2] (in $100k; the hat, “y-hat”, means “predicted”). The true prices are y = [2, 5, 4]. The error vector is y − ŷ = [−1, 0, 2]. Square its slots and average them:

MSE = (1/3) · ((−1)² + 0² + 2²) = (1 + 0 + 4) / 3 = 5 / 3 ≈ 1.67

That's the mean squared error: the squared L2 distance between predictions and truth, divided by n. The same move shows up as L2 regularization (also called weight decay): add λ‖w‖² (lambda times the squared length of the weights) to the loss, so the model prefers small weights. GPT-3 trained with weight decay 0.1 (Lesson 7.4).

Which neighbours are nearest? It depends on the norm

The k-nearest-neighbours classifier (k-NN) is the simplest ML model there is: to label a new point, find the k closest labelled points and take a vote. No training at all. But “closest” needs a norm.

Presets:

At [2.5, 1] the nearest apple is 0.2 across and 2.6 up; the nearest lemon is 2.1 across and 1.2 up. The taxi adds the steps: 2.8 vs 3.3, the apple wins. The crow squares them first, which punishes one long step: 2.61 vs 2.42, the lemon wins. L2 dislikes big differences in any single slot; L1 treats every unit of difference the same.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
x = np.array([3, -4])
np.linalg.norm(x, 1), np.linalg.norm(x), np.linalg.norm(x, np.inf)   # 7.0 5.0 4.0
a, b = np.array([4, 6]), np.array([1, 2])
np.linalg.norm(a - b)                # 5.0  distance = norm of the difference
x / np.linalg.norm(x)                # [ 0.6 -0.8]  normalized: length 1

# PyTorch, inside a training loop: shrink the gradient if its total L2 norm is above 1.0
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
🌍 Where it's used
Distance decides the neighbours (k-NN) 🤖 Machine learning · 1951 scikit-learn KNeighborsClassifier

How: k-NN (first described by Fix and Hodges in 1951) labels a point by a vote of its k nearest training points. scikit-learn's default metric is Minkowski with p = 2, the L2 distance; set p = 1 for L1.

Why this method: it needs no training and works with any norm. L1 is often tried when one huge difference in a single feature shouldn't dominate, since L2 squares that difference first.

The L1 norm makes weights exactly zero (Lasso) 📊 Data science · 1996 Tibshirani's lasso

How: fit a regression while adding λ‖w‖₁ to the loss. Many weights end up exactly 0, so the model also selects which features matter.

Why this method: an L2 penalty (λ‖w‖²) shrinks every weight a bit but rarely to exactly 0. The L1 diamond has corners on the axes, and the best fit tends to land on a corner, where some weights are 0. Fewer features = a model you can read.

Gradient clipping by global norm 🧠 LLMs · 2020 GPT-3 paper, Appendix B

How: “we clip the global norm of the gradient at 1.0”. Stack every weight's gradient into one huge vector g, take its L2 norm, and if ‖g‖ > 1, replace g with g / ‖g‖: same direction, length 1.

Why this method: one bad batch can produce a gigantic gradient that throws the weights far off. Rescaling keeps the direction (which way to improve) and only caps the step size.

RMSNorm keeps every layer's vector a steady size 🧠 LLMs · 2023 LLaMA · RMSNorm

How: before each sub-layer, LLaMA divides the vector by its root mean square: √(mean of the squared slots), which equals ‖x‖ / √n. For [2, −2, 2, −2]: √((4+4+4+4)/4) = 2, so it becomes [1, −1, 1, −1]. A learned scale is applied after.

Why this method: vectors that grow or shrink layer after layer make training unstable. The RMSNorm paper reports results comparable to the older LayerNorm while cutting running time by 7% to 64%, because it skips subtracting the mean.

🤯 Fun fact · the taxi is older than the crow

Fitting data by minimizing the L1 (sum of absolute) errors goes back to Roger Boscovich in 1757, about half a century before squared-error least squares (Lesson 0.1). Today L2 is the default almost everywhere in ML, largely because squares are smooth and easy to do calculus on (Phase 3). The taxi was never wrong, just harder to differentiate.

🌉 AI bridge · norms in RL and LLMs

RL: the RND curiosity bonus pays an agent the squared L2 distance between a network's guess and a target vector, which is big on screens it hasn't seen much (RL Lesson 6.5: exploration). And when an RL run goes wrong, one of the first numbers to log is the gradient norm (RL Lesson 9.2).

Check yourself

1. What is the L1 norm ‖x‖₁ of x = [−6, 8]?
|−6| + |8| = 6 + 8 = 14. 10 is the L2 norm (√(36 + 64)), and 2 forgot to drop the minus sign.
2. During training the gradient is g = [6, 8] and the rule is “clip the global norm at 1.0”. What gradient is actually used?
‖g‖ = √(36 + 64) = 10 > 1, so divide by 10: [0.6, 0.8]. Same direction, length exactly 1. [1, 1] would change the direction (and its length is 1.41).
3. You want a regression model that uses only a handful of your 500 features. Which penalty should you add to the loss?
L1's diamond-shaped “circle” has corners on the axes, where some weights are exactly 0, and solutions tend to land there. L2 shrinks all weights a little but keeps almost all of them non-zero.
📝 TL;DR
  • A norm = a vector's size. L2 = √(sum of squares) (crow), L1 = sum of |slots| (taxi), L∞ = biggest |slot| (chess king).
  • Distance = norm of the difference: d(a, b) = ‖a − b‖. Normalizing: x / ‖x‖ has length 1.
  • Squared L2 = MSE loss and weight decay. L1 = sparsity (lasso).
  • LLMs use norms for gradient clipping (GPT-3: global norm 1.0) and RMSNorm (LLaMA). k-NN's answer depends on the norm you pick.
