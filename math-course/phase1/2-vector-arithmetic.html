Phase 1 · Lesson 2

Adding & scaling vectors (king − man + woman)

Add vectors slot by slot, stretch them by multiplying, average them. These three moves power word analogies, sentence embeddings, LLM steering and every step of training.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector is a list of numbers and an arrow from the origin: [3, 4] means 3 right, 4 up (Lesson 1.1).
  • Its length ‖x‖ (“the length of x”) comes from Pythagoras: ‖[3, 4]‖ = √(9 + 16) = 5 (Lesson 1.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2013, researchers at Google trained word vectors on news text and then did something odd: they subtracted words from each other. vector(“Paris”) − vector(“France”) + vector(“Italy”) landed right next to vector(“Rome”). In 2023, other researchers took a language model's inner vector, added vector(“Love”) − vector(“Hate”), and the model started writing kinder text.

No new training, just plus and minus on lists of numbers. This lesson teaches those moves, and you'll build king − man + woman yourself.

Numbers first: adding is slot by slot

Monday you walk [3, 1] (3 blocks east, 1 north). Then you walk [1, 2]. Where are you? Add the matching slots:

[3, 1] + [1, 2] = [3 + 1, 1 + 2] = [4, 3]

As arrows, this is tip to tail: put the start of the second arrow at the tip of the first. The sum is the arrow from the very start to the final tip. The order doesn't matter: [1, 2] + [3, 1] lands on the same spot.

a + b = [a₁ + b₁, a₂ + b₂, …, an + bn] “a plus b”: add slot 1 to slot 1, slot 2 to slot 2, and so on. Both vectors must have the same number of slots. The answer is a vector, not a single number.

Subtracting: the arrow from one point to another

Subtraction is also slot by slot: [4, 3] − [1, 2] = [3, 1]. The picture is the useful part: a − b is the arrow that starts at b's tip and ends at a's tip. It answers “how do I get from b to a?”.

That's why subtraction captures relationships. If “king” and “man” are points, king − man is the trip from “man” to “king”: roughly “add royalty”. Its length (Lesson 1.1) is also the distance between the two points, which Lesson 1.4 builds on.

Scaling: stretch, shrink, flip

Multiply every slot by the same number: 2 · [1, 2] = [2, 4]. The arrow keeps its direction and gets twice as long. 0.5 halves it. A negative number flips it around: −1 · [1, 2] = [−1, −2]. A plain number like 2 or 0.5 is called a scalar, because it scales.

c · a = [c · a₁, c · a₂, …, c · an] “c times a”: multiply every slot by the same number c. The length gets multiplied by |c| (c without its minus sign); a negative c also reverses the direction.
💡 Big idea Mix the two moves and you get a linear combination: c₁ · a + c₂ · b, “some amount of a plus some amount of b”. An average is a linear combination. A training step is one. An attention layer's output is one. Most of deep learning is linear combinations plus a few bends.

🧠 Analogy: recipes

A smoothie recipe is a vector: [bananas, strawberries, cups of milk] = [2, 1, 1]. Making a double batch is scaling: 2 · [2, 1, 1] = [4, 2, 2]. Adding a friend's recipe to yours is addition. “Your recipe minus mine” tells you exactly what to add to mine to turn it into yours. And averaging the whole family's favorite recipes gives a recipe that's a bit of everyone: the centroid.

✏️ Worked example: averaging words into a sentence

Pretend each word has a 2-number vector: “the” = [0, 4], “cat” = [2, 0], “sat” = [4, 2]. One simple way to get one vector for the whole sentence “the cat sat” is the average (the mean, also called the centroid):

sum = [0 + 2 + 4, 4 + 0 + 2] = [6, 6]  →  mean = (1/3) · [6, 6] = [2, 2]

Adding, then scaling by 1/3. That's “mean pooling”: a sentence of any length becomes one vector of a fixed size. Real models do it with hundreds of numbers per word instead of 2.

Word arithmetic: build king − man + woman

Below is a tiny hand-made word space with only 2 directions: left–right is “how royal”, up–down is “she-words vs he-words”. Pick A − B + C. Following the 2013 recipe, the answer is the nearest word to the result, not counting A, B or C. “Nearest” here means the shortest arrow between the two points: the length of their difference.

Training is vector arithmetic too

A model's knobs (its weights) form one long vector w. Training computes another vector, the gradient g, which points in the direction that makes the error grow fastest (Lesson 3.3). So you step the other way, scaled by a small learning rate η:

w ← w − η · g “w becomes w minus eta times g”. η is the Greek letter eta, a small number like 0.1. The arrow ← means “replace with”. Scale the gradient down, then subtract it: one scaling and one subtraction.
w = [2, −1], g = [0.5, −1], η = 0.1:   [2, −1] − 0.1 · [0.5, −1] = [2 − 0.05, −1 + 0.1] = [1.95, −0.9]

GPT-style models repeat this with billions of slots, thousands of times. Lesson 3.4 is all about it.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
a = np.array([3, 1]); b = np.array([1, 2])
a + b, a - b, 2 * a            # [4 3], [2 -1], [6 2]: slot by slot

words = np.array([[0, 4], [2, 0], [4, 2]])  # "the", "cat", "sat"
words.mean(axis=0)                 # [2. 2.]  mean pooling

w = np.array([2.0, -1.0]); g = np.array([0.5, -1.0])
w = w - 0.1 * g                     # [1.95 -0.9]  one training step

🌍 Where it's used
Subtraction = a relationship (word analogies) 🤖 Machine learning · 2013 Mikolov et al.: word2vec

How: to answer “big is to biggest as small is to ?”, compute vector(biggest) − vector(big) + vector(small) and return the closest word, skipping the question words. The paper built a test of 8,869 meaning questions and 10,675 grammar questions this way.

Why this method: the authors wanted to check that the vectors captured kinds of similarity, not just “these words are related”. Plain arithmetic gave a quick, automatic test with no extra training.

Averaging = one vector per sentence 🏭 In production · 2019 all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (sentence-transformers)

How: a transformer outputs one vector per word piece. This popular search model averages them (mean pooling) into one 384-number vector per sentence, then rescales it to length 1. The Sentence-BERT paper made mean pooling its default.

Why this method: sentences have different lengths but a search index needs one fixed-size vector each. The average is cheap and uses every word, not just the first one.

Adding a direction = steering an LLM 🧠 LLMs · 2023 Turner et al.: Activation Addition

How: run the model on “Love” and on “Hate”, take the difference of its inner vectors at one layer, multiply by a coefficient c, and add it into that layer while the model writes. Output shifts toward the “Love” side (GPT-2-XL, OPT and LLaMA-3 in the paper).

Why this method: fine-tuning needs data and training runs. A steering vector needs one pair of prompts, one subtraction and one addition, and can be switched off at any time.

Scale and subtract = a training step 📜 History · 1847 Gradient descent (Cauchy)

How: w ← w − η · g, the same scale-and-subtract you just did, applied to every weight of a network at once. PyTorch's optimizer.step() does this for you.

Why this method: the gradient tells every weight which way to move in one shot. Nudging the weights one at a time and checking would take billions of trials for a big model.

🤯 Fun fact · even the paper gets one wrong

The word2vec paper's showcase table (300-number vectors trained on 783 million words) includes Paris − France + Italy = Rome. It also honestly lists, for big → bigger, the answer small → larger. The authors note that if you demand exact matches, the examples in that table would score only about 60%. Vector arithmetic finds meaning, but approximately.

🌉 AI bridge · scale and add, everywhere

RL: the RL course's linear value estimate learns with exactly this move: weights ← weights + (step size × surprise) × features. Scale the feature vector, add it to the weights. See RL Lesson 4.1: function approximation and the gradient-descent refresher in RL Lesson 4.2.

LLMs: the output of an attention head is a weighted average of “value” vectors: a linear combination where the weights come from dot products (Lesson 1.3) and softmax (Lesson 8.4).

Check yourself

1. Compute [2, 5] − [1, 3] + [4, 0].
Slot 1: 2 − 1 + 4 = 5. Slot 2: 5 − 3 + 0 = 2. Adding and subtracting vectors gives back a vector, not a single number. [7, 8] is what you get if you add all three.
2. Activation Addition makes GPT-2 more positive without any training. What does it actually do?
A difference of two vectors is a direction (“from Hate toward Love”). Adding a scaled copy of it nudges the model's inner state that way, just like king − man + woman nudges “king” in the “she-words” direction.
3. Weights w = [1, 2], gradient g = [4, −2], learning rate η = 0.1. What is w − η · g?
First scale: 0.1 · [4, −2] = [0.4, −0.2]. Then subtract: [1 − 0.4, 2 − (−0.2)] = [0.6, 2.2]. [1.4, 1.8] adds instead of subtracting (walking uphill), and [−3, 4] forgot the learning rate.
📝 TL;DR
  • Add and subtract slot by slot. a + b = tip to tail; a − b = the arrow from b's tip to a's tip.
  • c · a stretches (|c| > 1), shrinks (|c| < 1) or flips (c < 0) the arrow.
  • Linear combinations (some of a + some of b) include averages: mean pooling turns many word vectors into one.
  • Used in word analogies (king − man + woman ≈ queen), LLM steering vectors and every training step w ← w − η·g.
