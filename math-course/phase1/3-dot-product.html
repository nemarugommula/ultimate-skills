The dot product & cosine similarity

One multiply-and-add that answers “how much do these two point the same way?”. It's inside every neuron, every attention head and every search engine.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector is a list of numbers, like [3, 4], and also an arrow from the origin to that point (Lesson 1.1).
  • The length of [3, 4] is √(3² + 4²) = 5, by Pythagoras. We write it ‖x‖, read “the length of x” (Lesson 1.1).
  • Σ means “add these up”, like a for-loop with a running total (Lesson 0.5).
🎯 Hook

You ask a chatbot a question about your company's docs. In a few milliseconds it finds the 5 most relevant paragraphs out of a million. It doesn't read them. It turns every paragraph into a list of numbers, turns your question into one too, and then runs one tiny calculation a million times: multiply matching numbers, add them up.

That calculation is the dot product. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to do it by hand and know why it measures “meaning”.

Numbers first: a shopping receipt

You buy 2 coffees, 1 bagel and 3 cookies. A coffee costs $3, a bagel $2, a cookie $1. What's the total?

2 × 3  +  1 × 2  +  3 × 1  =  6 + 2 + 3  =  $11

You just computed a dot product. You had two lists of the same size, quantities = [2, 1, 3] and prices = [3, 2, 1]. You multiplied the matching entries and added up the results. That's all a dot product is.

x · w = x₁w₁ + x₂w₂ + … + xnwn = Σi xi wi Read “x dot w”: multiply entry 1 with entry 1, entry 2 with entry 2, and so on, then add everything up. The answer is a single number.
x: the input (quantities, a word, a question)w: the weights (prices, another word, a document)
💡 Big idea The dot product is a weighted sum: “how much of each thing, times how much each thing counts”. When both lists describe directions, the same number also measures how much they point the same way.

A neuron is a dot product

Swap “quantities” for inputs and “prices” for weights and you have the basic unit of every neural network. A spam filter might look at x = [number of links, number of “FREE!!!”, is the sender in your contacts]. It learns w = [0.5, 2.0, −3.0]: links are a bit suspicious, “FREE!!!” is very suspicious, a known sender is reassuring. The spam score is x · w.

An email with 4 links, 2 “FREE!!!” and an unknown sender: 4 × 0.5 + 2 × 2.0 + 0 × (−3.0) = 6. Very spammy. The same email from your boss (contacts = 1): 6 − 3 = 3. Less spammy. Training a neural network is mostly finding good weights for millions of dot products like this one.

What the number means: “same direction” meter

Now draw both lists as arrows. Something nice happens: the dot product is big and positive when the arrows point the same way, zero when they're at a right angle, and negative when they point in opposite directions.

Notice two things. First, the sign tells you the direction story: + agree, 0 unrelated, − disagree. Second, press “Same direction, longer x”: the dot product doubles even though the direction didn't change. The dot product mixes up “which way” with “how long”. We'll fix that in a minute.

Math refresher cos θ without trigonometry class

θ (the Greek letter “theta”) is the angle between the two arrows. cos θ (“cosine of theta”) is a number between −1 and +1 that you can treat as a sameness meter:

Angle θ0°60°90°120°180°
cos θ10.50−0.5−1
In wordssame wayroughly sameunrelatedroughly oppositeexactly opposite

Where it comes from: shine a light straight down onto arrow w and look at the shadow of an arrow of length 1. The shadow's length is cos θ. Pointing the same way: the full length (1). At a right angle: no shadow (0). Pointing backwards: a shadow on the wrong side (negative).

That gives the second formula for the dot product: length × length × sameness.

x · w = ‖x‖ · ‖w‖ · cos θ “x dot w equals the length of x, times the length of w, times the cosine of the angle between them.” Both formulas always give the same number.
✏️ Worked example: three pairs by hand
  • x = [3, 4], w = [4, 3]: 3·4 + 4·3 = 24. Lengths: √(9+16) = 5 and 5. So cos θ = 24 / (5 × 5) = 0.96: almost the same direction (about 16°).
  • x = [2, 1], w = [−1, 2]: 2·(−1) + 1·2 = 0. A right angle: these two have nothing in common.
  • x = [1, 2], w = [−2, −4]: −2 − 8 = −10. Lengths √5 and √20, which multiply to √100 = 10. cos θ = −10 / 10 = −1: exactly opposite.

Check any of them in the playground above: drag the tips to those points.

Cosine similarity: keep the direction, drop the length

In AI, a vector's direction usually carries the meaning (what is this about?) and its length often carries something boring (how long was the document, how loud was the recording). To compare meaning only, divide out both lengths:

cos_sim(x, w) = x · w  /  ( ‖x‖ · ‖w‖ ) The dot product divided by both lengths. What's left is just cos θ: a score from −1 to +1 that ignores how long the arrows are.

A common shortcut: rescale every vector to length 1 first (“normalize” it). Then the dot product is the cosine similarity, and you can skip the division. Most vector databases store normalized embeddings for exactly this reason.

🧠 Analogy: the taste-match score

You and a friend rate five music genres from −2 (hate) to +2 (love). Multiply your ratings genre by genre and add them up. You both love jazz: +4. You love metal, they hate it: −4. Neither of you cares about country: 0. The total is your dot product: high means similar taste.

But a friend who rates everything ±2 gets bigger numbers than a friend who only uses ±1, even with identical taste. Cosine similarity is the fair version: it asks “do we like the same things?”, not “how strongly do we say it?”.

Presets:

With raw dot products the encyclopedia [1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5] wins almost every query, because it's a long vector: it mentions everything a lot. Cosine similarity asks only “is it pointing toward my query?”, so the kitten wins “a pet”. Real search systems hit this exact problem with long documents.

Real embeddings aren't hand-labelled like these. A neural network learns them, and each one has hundreds or thousands of numbers that no human named. The math is the same (Lesson 1.5 shows how they're learned).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
x = np.array([3, 4]); w = np.array([4, 3])
x @ w                                        # 24   (same as np.dot(x, w) or sum(x * w))
x @ w / (np.linalg.norm(x) * np.linalg.norm(w))   # 0.96 cosine similarity

# Search 100,000 documents at once: one matrix times one vector (Phase 2)
docs = np.random.randn(100_000, 384).astype(np.float32)  # 100k embeddings, 384 numbers each (~150 MB)
docs /= np.linalg.norm(docs, axis=1, keepdims=True)  # normalize: length 1
q = docs[42]                                  # pretend this is the question
top5 = np.argsort(-(docs @ q))[:5]            # 100,000 dot products, the 5 best (42 comes first)

A strange fact about many dimensions

On paper, two random arrows often point roughly the same way. Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions, and there something odd happens. Try it:

🎮 Playground · Random arrows in many dimensions

Each bar counts how many random pairs had that cosine similarity, from −1 (left) to +1 (right).

In 2D the cosines are spread from −1 to +1. In 1,000 dimensions they pile up tightly around 0: almost any two random vectors are nearly perpendicular. That's good news for AI. It means there's room for a huge number of distinct “directions of meaning”.

One caution: these were random arrows. Real embedding models have their own baseline, and even unrelated texts often score 0.3 to 0.7. So compare scores with each other (which document scores highest?), not with 0.

🌍 Where it's used
Dot product = a neuron's weighted sum 📜 History · 1958 Rosenblatt's perceptron

How: inputs · weights + a bias gives a score, and a threshold turns it into yes/no. Every neuron in every modern network still starts with this dot product.

Why this method: it's the simplest way to let each input count more or less, and it's cheap: one multiply and one add per input. Millions of them fit on a GPU at once.

Dot product as “how relevant is that word to this one?” 🧠 LLMs · 2017 Attention Is All You Need (the Transformer)

How: each word makes a “query” vector (what am I looking for?) and a “key” vector (what do I contain?). The attention score between two words is query · key, divided by √d to keep the numbers tame. Big score = look at that word more (Lesson 8.4).

Why this method: the paper chose dot products over a small neural network per pair because all the scores come out of one matrix multiplication, which the authors note is much faster and uses less memory in practice.

Cosine similarity for semantic search & RAG 🏭 In production · 2019 Sentence-BERT

How: every sentence becomes one embedding vector, stored once. A question becomes a vector too, and the answer candidates are the stored vectors with the highest cosine similarity to it. This is the “retrieval” step in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

Why this method: the paper's own example: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences took about 65 hours by feeding every pair through BERT, and about 5 seconds by comparing precomputed embeddings with cosine similarity.

Predicted rating = user · item 🏭 In production · 2009 Matrix factorization (Netflix Prize)

How: each user gets a short taste vector and each movie a short “what it offers” vector, both learned from past ratings. Your predicted rating for a movie is the dot product of the two: the taste-match score from the analogy.

Why this method: one small vector per user and per movie can predict a rating for every pair. You don't need anyone to have watched both movies.

Cosine between an image and a sentence 🖼️ Deep learning · 2021 CLIP (OpenAI)

How: one network turns images into vectors, another turns captions into vectors, and training pushes the cosine similarity of matching image–caption pairs up and mismatched pairs down. To classify a photo, compare it with “a photo of a dog”, “a photo of a cat”… and pick the highest cosine.

Why this method: once pictures and words live in the same space, a single dot product compares them, so new labels need no retraining.

🤯 Fun fact · two million near-misses

In 1,000 dimensions you can fit only 1,000 arrows that are exactly perpendicular to each other. But “nearly perpendicular” is a different story. We drew 2,000 random arrows in 1,000 dimensions and checked all 1,999,000 pairs: not one pair had a cosine above 0.17 or below −0.17. The typical cosine was about ±0.03, which is 1/√1000. That's the room embeddings use to keep thousands of meanings apart.

🌉 AI bridge · the dot product everywhere

LLMs: the very last step of an LLM is a dot product too. The model's final hidden vector is dotted with one learned vector per word in its vocabulary (often the same vectors it uses as word embeddings), and each result becomes that word's score before softmax picks the next token (Lesson 8.2).

RL: the simplest way to estimate “how good is this situation?” is weights · features, a single dot product. The RL course builds on exactly that in Lesson 4.1: function approximation.

Check yourself

1. A neuron has weights w = [2, −1] and sees input x = [3, 4]. What is x · w?
3 × 2 + 4 × (−1) = 6 − 4 = 2. A dot product always gives back one number, not a list. [6, −4] is what you have before the “add them up” step.
2. Two embeddings have a dot product of exactly 0. What does that tell you?
Zero means perpendicular (cos 90° = 0). It can also happen if one vector is all zeros, but that's rare. [2, 1] and [−1, 2] are both non-zero and still have a dot product of 0.
3. Why do search systems usually rank by cosine similarity instead of the raw dot product?
The dot product is length × length × cos θ. Dividing out both lengths leaves just the direction. Remember the 📚 encyclopedia in the search playground.
📝 TL;DR
  • x · w = multiply matching entries, add them up. The result is one number (a weighted sum).
  • It's also ‖x‖ ‖w‖ cos θ: positive = same direction, 0 = right angle, negative = opposite.
  • Cosine similarity = dot product ÷ both lengths: compares direction (meaning) and ignores length.
  • Used in every neuron, in attention (query · key), in semantic search/RAG, in recommenders and in CLIP.
