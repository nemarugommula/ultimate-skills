Embeddings: meaning as geometry

An embedding is a learned vector for a word, a video or a picture, placed so that similar things land close together. It's the first layer of every LLM and the heart of search and recommendations.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Subtracting and scaling: p + 0.1 · (q − p) moves point p one tenth of the way toward q (Lesson 1.2).
  • Cosine similarity: dot product ÷ both lengths, from −1 to +1; it compares directions (Lesson 1.3).
  • Distance = length of the difference, ‖a − b‖ (Lesson 1.4).
🎯 Hook

Nobody ever tells a language model that “kitten” is like “cat” or that “pizza” is food. It only sees which words show up near which other words, billions of times. Yet afterwards, the vector for “kitten” sits right next to “cat”, and far from “bus”.

How can “which words appear together” turn into a map of meaning? You'll watch it happen in this lesson, on 12 words and 13 sentences.

Numbers first: the naive way, one-hot

A computer needs a vector for each word. The simplest idea: give every word its own slot. With the vocabulary [cat, kitten, dog, pizza, pasta, car]:

cat = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]   kitten = [0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0]   car = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1]

This is called one-hot encoding: a single 1 (the “hot” slot) and zeros everywhere else. It has two big problems:

  • It knows nothing. cat · kitten = 0 and cat · car = 0. Every pair of different words is exactly √2 ≈ 1.41 apart. To one-hot vectors, a kitten is as unlike a cat as a car is.
  • It's huge. GPT-2 knows 50,257 tokens, so each one-hot vector would have 50,257 slots, all but one of them 0.

The fix is a dense vector: a short list (the smallest GPT-2 uses 768 numbers) where every slot carries a bit of meaning, and similar words get similar lists. That's an embedding.

💡 Big idea An embedding turns “similar meaning” into “nearby in space”. Once that's true, all of Phase 1 becomes a toolbox for meaning: distance and cosine find similar things (1.3, 1.4), averages summarize (1.2), and differences capture relationships like king → queen (1.2).

Where do the numbers come from? Training

The idea is old: a word is known by the company it keeps. A linguist, J. R. Firth, popularized it in the 1950s with the line “a word is characterized by the company it keeps”. word2vec turned it into a recipe:

  1. Start every word at a random vector.
  2. For words that appear together in a sentence, nudge their vectors closer.
  3. For random pairs that don't appear together (“negative samples”), nudge them apart.
  4. Repeat millions of times.

Each nudge is Lesson 1.2 arithmetic. To pull cat toward dog by 10%:

cat ← cat + 0.1 · (dog − cat) “cat becomes cat plus one tenth of the arrow from cat to dog”. The difference dog − cat points from cat to dog; scaling it by 0.1 makes it a small step.

Two things to notice. First, cat and puppy never appear in the same sentence, yet they end up neighbours: they keep the same company (dog, kitten). That's how embeddings generalize. Second, “the dog sniffed the pizza” pulls dog a little toward the food, but one sentence can't beat many. Real training does the same with billions of sentences and hundreds of dimensions.

Simplified: real word2vec nudges dot products up or down rather than moving points by distance, but the result is the same: words that keep the same company end up close.

🧠 Analogy: seating a wedding

You're seating 200 guests but only know who arrived together and who chatted. Each time two people talk, you move their chairs a little closer. Each time two strangers ignore each other, you nudge them apart. After a while, families, old school friends and work colleagues form tables, though nobody told you who's who. The tables are clusters; a guest's chair is their embedding.

✏️ Worked example: one training nudge, and an embedding lookup

The nudge. cat is at [1, 3], dog at [3, −1]. They share a sentence, so pull cat 10% toward dog: dog − cat = [2, −4]; 0.1 · [2, −4] = [0.2, −0.4]; new cat = [1.2, 2.6]. The distance dropped from √(4 + 16) ≈ 4.47 to √(1.8² + 3.6²) ≈ 4.02.

The lookup. A model stores all embeddings in one table: one row per word. With a vocabulary of 10,000 words and 300 numbers each, that's 10,000 × 300 = 3,000,000 numbers to learn. Getting a word's embedding just means reading its row: word number 42 → row 42. The smallest GPT-2's table has 50,257 × 768 = 38,597,376 numbers.

🐍 In code 
import torch
table = torch.nn.Embedding(50257, 768)        # GPT-2-sized table: random at first, learned in training
ids = torch.tensor([464, 3797])               # token IDs from the tokenizer
vecs = table(ids)                              # shape (2, 768): one row per token

# nearest neighbour by cosine (Lesson 1.3): normalize rows, then one big dot product
E = torch.nn.functional.normalize(table.weight, dim=1)
top5 = (E @ E[3797]).topk(5).indices          # the 5 most similar tokens (itself first)

🌍 Where it's used
The token embedding table: an LLM's first layer 🧠 LLMs · 2019 GPT-2 config

How: the tokenizer turns text into IDs; the table turns each ID into a 768-number vector (vocab_size 50,257, n_embd 768 in the config). Every layer after that works on these vectors. GPT-3's largest model uses 12,288 numbers per token.

Why this method: a lookup is free compared with a matrix multiply on a 50,257-slot one-hot vector, and it gives the same answer (Lesson 2.2). The table's numbers are learned along with the rest of the model.

Pull together, push apart: word2vec 🤖 Machine learning · 2013 Mikolov et al.: negative sampling

How: the skip-gram model learns vectors that predict the surrounding words. Negative sampling makes each step cheap: raise the score of the real (word, neighbour) pair, lower the score of k random words. The paper found k = 5–20 useful for small datasets and 2–5 for large ones.

Why this method: the exact version would compare each word against the entire vocabulary at every step. A handful of random negatives gives nearly the same push for a tiny fraction of the work.

A vector database for RAG 🧠 LLMs · 2020 Lewis et al.: Retrieval-Augmented Generation

How: Wikipedia was cut into 21 million chunks of 100 words, each embedded once and stored in a FAISS index. A question is embedded too, and the chunks whose vectors have the highest dot product with it are handed to the generator as context.

Why this method: keyword search misses paraphrases (“car” vs “automobile”). Embeddings match meaning, and a nearest-neighbour index can search millions of vectors quickly.

Embeddings for videos, not just words 🏭 In production · 2016 Covington et al.: YouTube recommendations

How: in the paper's experiments, each of 1 million videos gets 256 learned numbers. Your recent watches (up to 50) are averaged into one vector (Lesson 1.2's mean pooling), fed through the network, and candidates are found by a nearest-neighbour search in dot-product space.

Why this method: the authors tried sum and component-wise max too; simple averaging worked best. And nearest-neighbour search lets them score millions of videos within tens of milliseconds.

Seeing embeddings: 2D maps 📊 Data science · 2008 van der Maaten & Hinton: t-SNE

How: t-SNE gives each high-dimensional point a location in a 2D or 3D map, trying to keep neighbours as neighbours. It's the standard way to eyeball whether embeddings formed sensible clusters, like the playground above but from 768 dimensions.

Why this method: you can't look at 768 numbers. Simply keeping 2 of the slots would throw away almost everything; t-SNE (and its faster cousin UMAP) focuses on who is near whom.

🤯 Fun fact · a 256-number taste profile

In the YouTube paper's setup, your viewing history is boiled down to the average of the 256-number vectors of your last 50 videos. The same paper also embeds your recent searches the same way. Your “taste” is literally a point in a 256-dimensional space, and recommendations are its nearest neighbours.

🌉 AI bridge · embeddings everywhere

RL: when an LLM is trained with RL, its “state” is the token sequence so far, and the first thing the policy does with it is look up these embeddings: see RL Lesson 7.1: an LLM is a policy. In classic RL, an embedding is just a learned version of the hand-made feature vectors in RL Lesson 4.1.

Check yourself

1. With one-hot encoding over 50,257 tokens, what is the dot product of the vectors for “cat” and “kitten”?
Their 1s sit in different slots, so every product is 0 × something. One-hot vectors of different words are always perpendicular: they carry no similarity at all. That's why we learn dense embeddings.
2. A model has a vocabulary of 32,000 tokens and 4,096 numbers per embedding. How many numbers are in its embedding table?
One row per token, one column per slot: 32,000 × 4,096 = 131,072,000. 36,096 adds instead of multiplying.
3. Why does a RAG system embed all its documents ahead of time and store the vectors?
Documents are embedded once; each question costs one embedding plus a fast nearest-neighbour search. Because similar meanings sit close together, “automobile” can find a chunk that only says “car”.
📝 TL;DR
  • One-hot: one slot per word, all pairs equally far apart, no meaning. Dense embedding: a short learned vector where similar = close.
  • Embeddings are learned by pulling together things that appear together and pushing apart random pairs (word2vec, negative sampling).
  • An embedding table has one row per token (GPT-2: 50,257 × 768); looking up a token = reading its row.
  • Used for LLM inputs, RAG vector search, recommendations (YouTube) and 2D maps (t-SNE).
