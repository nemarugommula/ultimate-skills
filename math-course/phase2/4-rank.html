Phase 2 · Lesson 4

Inverse, rank & independence (why LoRA works)

Can a matrix's move be undone? How many directions survive it? The answers solve equations, power linear regression, and explain why you can fine-tune a giant LLM by training a sliver of it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A matrix's columns are where [1, 0] and [0, 1] land; “Squash flat” collapsed the plane onto a line (Lesson 2.1).
  • Matrix × matrix = do one move, then another; (m×n)(n×p) → (m×p) (Lesson 2.2).
🎯 Hook

GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters. Fine-tuning all of them for your task means storing a full new copy of the model. In 2021, a method called LoRA reported cutting the number of trainable parameters by 10,000 times and GPU memory by 3 times, while matching full fine-tuning on the tasks it tested.

The trick is one idea from this lesson: a huge matrix that is secretly the product of two thin ones. That idea is called low rank.

Numbers first: a price puzzle

2 apples and 1 banana cost $5. 1 apple and 3 bananas cost $10. What does each cost?

2·a + 1·b = 5     1·a + 3·b = 10    →   A = [[2, 1], [1, 3]],   x = [a, b],   target = [5, 10]

That's one matrix equation, Ax = t: “which point x does A move onto the target t?” To answer it, we need to undo A.

The inverse: the undo button

The inverse A⁻¹ (“A inverse”) is the matrix that moves everything back. Do A, then A⁻¹, and every point is home again. For a 2×2 matrix there's a recipe: swap the two diagonal numbers, flip the signs of the other two, divide by one special number, the determinant:

A = abcd   →   A⁻¹ = 1 / (ad − bc) · d−b−ca “A inverse is one over (a d minus b c), times the matrix d, minus b, minus c, a.” The number ad − bc is the determinant, written det A.
✏️ Worked example: solve the price puzzle
  • det A = 2·3 − 1·1 = 5.
  • A⁻¹ = (1/5) · [[3, −1], [−1, 2]].
  • x = A⁻¹ · [5, 10] = (1/5) · [3·5 − 1·10, −1·5 + 2·10] = (1/5) · [5, 15] = [1, 3].
  • Check: 2·1 + 1·3 = 5 ✓ and 1·1 + 3·3 = 10 ✓. Apples $1, bananas $3.

The determinant = how much area gets scaled

Here's the picture behind that special number. Take the 1×1 unit square. After A moves it, it's a slanted parallelogram. Its area is exactly |det A| (the size of det A, ignoring the sign). A minus sign means the square got flipped like a mirror image.

And if det A = 0? The square has no area: the whole plane got squashed onto a line (or a point). Many different points land on the same spot, so nothing can tell them apart again. No inverse. Look at the recipe: it would divide by zero.

Notice “Almost squashed”: det is small (0.4) but not zero, so an inverse exists, but it has big numbers (it divides by a tiny det). Nudge the target t one notch down, from [6, 3.5] to [6, 3], and the answer jumps from [−2, 2.5] to [3, 0]. In machine learning that's a warning sign: nearly dependent inputs make solutions unstable.

💡 Big idea The determinant says how much a matrix scales area (or volume). det = 0 means space got squashed into fewer dimensions: information is lost and there's no inverse. The number of dimensions that survive is the matrix's rank.

Rank: how many independent directions survive

Columns [1, 2] and [2, 4]: the second is just 2 × the first. They point along the same line, so they're called dependent. A matrix with those columns squashes the plane onto that line: rank 1. Columns [2, 1] and [1, 3] point in genuinely different directions (independent): rank 2, nothing lost.

In the price puzzle, dependent columns would mean the second shopping trip tells you nothing new (“1 apple + 2 bananas = $5” and “2 apples + 4 bananas = $10” is the same fact twice), so there are infinitely many answers.

Big matrices work the same way: rank = the number of truly different directions in its columns, anywhere from 1 up to the matrix's size.

Low rank = two thin matrices

The simplest rank-1 matrix is a column times a row (each entry = row number × column number):

[1, 2, 3]ᵀ × [1, 0, 2] = [[1, 0, 2], [2, 0, 4], [3, 0, 6]]    9 entries built from 3 + 3 = 6 numbers, rank 1

Add r of those layers and you get a rank-r matrix: a (d × r) matrix times an (r × d) matrix. The saving is huge when r is small:

full: d × d numbers   vs   low rank: (d × r)·(r × d) → 2 · d · r numbers With d = 4,096 and r = 8: 4,096² = 16,777,216 numbers for the full matrix, but 2 × 4,096 × 8 = 65,536 for the two thin ones. 256 times fewer.
🧠 Analogy: a plaid tablecloth

A plaid pattern looks complicated, but it's made of just one list of row stripes and one list of column stripes, woven together. That's rank 1: one column × one row. A few plaids layered on top of each other can draw surprisingly rich pictures: low rank. A thin diagonal line, though, can't be made from a few plaids at all: full rank.

LoRA: train the thin matrices, freeze the big one

Fine-tuning changes a weight matrix W (size d × d) into W + ΔW (“delta W”, the change). LoRA's bet: the useful change is low rank. So it writes ΔW = B·A with B of size d × r and A of size r × d, freezes W, and trains only B and A. (This A is a new matrix, not the price-puzzle A from earlier; the letters follow the LoRA paper.)

Wnew = W + B·A “The new weights are the old frozen weights plus B times A”, where B is tall and thin (d × r) and A is short and wide (r × d). Only 2·d·r numbers are trained.
🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]]); t = np.array([5, 10])
np.linalg.det(A)                       # 5.0: the unit square's area becomes 5
np.linalg.solve(A, t)                  # array([1., 3.])  (better than inv(A) @ t)
np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array([[1, 2], [2, 4]]))   # 1: dependent columns

d, r = 4096, 8                            # LoRA-style update
B = np.zeros((d, r)); A_ = np.random.randn(r, d) * 0.01
delta_W = B @ A_                       # (4096×8)·(8×4096) → 4096×4096, rank ≤ 8
print(B.size + A_.size, d * d)         # 65536 vs 16777216 trained numbers

🌍 Where it's used
Low-rank fine-tuning 🧠 LLMs · 2021 LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation (Hu et al.)

How: for chosen weight matrices, LoRA freezes W and learns W + BA, with B (d × r) and A (r × d). For GPT-3 175B it reports 10,000× fewer trainable parameters and 3× less GPU memory. Libraries like Hugging Face PEFT make it a few lines of code.

Why this method: one frozen base model can carry many tiny task-specific adapters, and unlike some earlier adapter methods, BA can be merged into W after training, so there's no extra delay when the model runs.

Solving for the best line: the normal equations 🤖 Machine learning · 2011 scikit-learn: Ordinary Least Squares

How: linear regression's best weights solve (XᵀX) w = Xᵀy, a matrix equation like the price puzzle, where X holds one data row per example. It needs XᵀX to be invertible, which fails when two feature columns are dependent (say, price in dollars and price in cents). scikit-learn's docs warn that nearly dependent columns make the answer “highly sensitive” to small errors: the “Almost squashed” effect.

Why this method: one exact formula instead of a search, which is why it's still the textbook first model. Lesson 8.1 derives it.

Rank-1 adapters for RL fine-tuning 🎮 RL · 2025 Thinking Machines: LoRA Without Regret

How: when an LLM is trained with policy-gradient RL, they found LoRA fully matched full fine-tuning even with ranks as low as 1.

Why this method: their explanation: RL feedback carries very little information per episode, so even a rank-1 update has more than enough capacity to absorb it.

🤯 Fun fact · rank 1 out of 12,288

GPT-3's hidden vectors have 12,288 numbers, so its big weight matrices can have rank up to 12,288. The LoRA paper reports that an update of rank just 1 or 2 was enough for its GPT-3 experiments. The changes that adapt a giant model to a new job seem to live in a tiny number of directions.

🌉 AI bridge · rank in modern AI

LLMs & RL: the RL course's Lesson 8.11: RL infrastructure has a sidebar on LoRA for RL training of LLMs. You now know what “rank” means there: the number of column × row layers in the update.

Data science: nearly dependent features (det close to 0) make regression unstable. Regularization (Lesson 7.4) is one standard fix.

Check yourself

1. A = [[3, 1], [2, 2]] (rows). What area does the 1×1 unit square have after A moves it?
det A = a·d − b·c = 3·2 − 1·2 = 4, so the area is 4. 8 adds instead of subtracting. −4 subtracts in the wrong order, and an area can't be negative anyway (a negative det means flipped, with area |det|).
2. A matrix has columns [1, 2] and [3, 6]. What's true?
Dependent columns point along the same line. det = 1·6 − 3·2 = 0, which confirms it: zero area, information lost, nothing to undo.
3. A layer has a 1000 × 1000 weight matrix. LoRA with rank r = 4 trains how many numbers for it?
B is 1000 × 4 and A is 4 × 1000: 2 · d · r = 2 × 1000 × 4 = 8,000. That's 125 times fewer than the full million, and the frozen W isn't trained at all.
📝 TL;DR
  • Ax = t is a system of equations; the inverse A⁻¹ undoes A, so x = A⁻¹t.
  • det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc = how much area is scaled (negative = flipped). det = 0 → squashed, no inverse.
  • Rank = number of independent directions that survive. Dependent columns lower it.
  • Low rank = (d×r)(r×d): 2dr numbers instead of d². That's LoRA and image compression.
