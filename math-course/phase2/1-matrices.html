Matrices: machines that move space

A small grid of numbers that rotates, stretches, flips or squashes every point at once. It's how images get augmented, how neural layers reshape data, and how LLMs tell words where they sit in a sentence.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector like [3, 2] is a list of numbers and an arrow from the origin to that point (Lesson 1.1).
  • Scaling and adding arrows: 3 · [1, 0] + 2 · [0, 1] = [3, 2] (Lesson 1.2).
  • The dot product: multiply matching entries, add them up. [2, −1] · [3, 2] = 6 − 2 = 4 (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook

Rotate a photo by 30°. Every one of its million pixels moves to a new spot. Yet the whole move is described by just four numbers.

Stranger still: LLMs such as LLaMA tell each word where it is in the sentence by rotating its vectors, a little more for each position. Same four-number trick. By the end of this lesson you'll build both.

Numbers first: a recipe for moving points

Here's a rule for moving any point [x, y]:

new x = 2·x − 1·y     new y = 1·x + 1·y

Feed it the point [3, 2]: new x = 6 − 2 = 4, new y = 3 + 2 = 5. The point lands on [4, 5].

The rule only needs its four numbers. Write them in a grid and you have a matrix (plural: matrices):

A = 2−111    Ax = 2−111 32 = 45 Read “A times x”. Each row of A is a recipe for one output number: row 1 · [3, 2] = 2·3 − 1·2 = 4, row 2 · [3, 2] = 1·3 + 1·2 = 5. That's one dot product per row.

We say A is a 2×2 matrix (“two by two”: 2 rows, 2 columns). Matrices are written in orange in this course, because in AI they're usually the weights a model learns.

The column trick: watch where the two arrows land

There's a second way to read the same grid, and it's the one that makes pictures easy. Feed A the two simplest arrows, î = [1, 0] (“i-hat”, one step right) and ĵ = [0, 1] (“j-hat”, one step up):

A·[1, 0] = [2, 1] = column 1     A·[0, 1] = [−1, 1] = column 2

Now any point is “some î plus some ĵ”: [3, 2] = 3 î + 2 ĵ. A matrix keeps that recipe, so [3, 2] must land on 3 × (where î went) + 2 × (where ĵ went):

Ax = x₁ · (column 1) + x₂ · (column 2) = 3 · [2, 1] + 2 · [−1, 1] = [4, 5] “A times x is x-one copies of the first column plus x-two copies of the second column.” x₁ and x₂ (“x-one, x-two”) are the entries of x. Same answer as the row recipe, every time.
💡 Big idea A matrix is a machine that moves every point of space at once. Its columns tell you where the basis arrows î and ĵ land; everything else follows. Straight lines stay straight, the origin stays put, and grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced. That's what “linear” means.

A small naming wrinkle: a neural layer computes Wx + b (Lessons 0.2 and 2.2). The + b slides everything over, so the origin moves. Strictly that's “linear plus a shift” (called affine), but ML still calls the layer “linear”, as in PyTorch's nn.Linear.

Rotation: the most famous matrix

To rotate by an angle θ (“theta”) counterclockwise, ask: where should î and ĵ land? î = [1, 0] should end up at angle θ on the circle of radius 1, which is the point [cos θ, sin θ]. ĵ is 90° further along, at [−sin θ, cos θ]. Those are the two columns:

R(θ) = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ “R of theta”: column 1 is where î lands (cos θ, sin θ), column 2 is where ĵ lands (−sin θ, cos θ). Every rotation in 2D is this one matrix with a different θ.
Math refresher cos θ and sin θ in one picture

Walk around a circle of radius 1, starting at [1, 0]. After turning by θ, your position is [cos θ, sin θ]: cos (“cosine”) is how far right you are, sin (“sine”) is how far up.

cos θ10.8660−1
sin θ00.510

So rotating by 90° uses the columns [0, 1] and [−1, 0].

🧠 Analogy: a rubber sheet with two pins

Draw a grid and a picture on a rubber sheet nailed down at the center. Stick two pins in it, one at [1, 0] and one at [0, 1]. Move the pins and the whole sheet stretches evenly to follow, like the grid in the playground. A matrix is just a note saying “put pin 1 here, pin 2 there”.

✏️ Worked example: three moves by hand
  • Stretch-and-tilt A = [[2, −1], [1, 1]] on [3, 2]: rows give 2·3 − 1·2 = 4 and 1·3 + 1·2 = 5. Columns give 3·[2, 1] + 2·[−1, 1] = [6 − 2, 3 + 2] = [4, 5]. Same.
  • Rotate 90°, columns [0, 1] and [−1, 0], on [3, 1]: 3·[0, 1] + 1·[−1, 0] = [−1, 3]. Check the length: √(3² + 1²) = √10 before, √(1² + 3²) = √10 after. Rotations never change lengths.
  • Rotate 30° on [2, 0]: 2 × column 1 = 2·[0.866, 0.5] = [1.73, 1]. Length still 2.

Check them in the playground: press a preset, then drag the blue dot.

Rotations inside an LLM

A transformer compares words with a dot product: a query vector from one word · a key vector from another (the dot product measures “how much do they point the same way”, Lesson 1.3). But a plain dot product doesn't know where the two words are in the sentence.

Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) fix that with rotation matrices: a word at position m gets its vectors rotated by m × θ. The neat consequence: the score only depends on how far apart the words are, not where they are. Test it.

🎮 Playground · RoPE: rotate by position, compare by dot product

The query word sits at position m, the key word at position n. Each vector is rotated by its position × 20°. Faint dashed arrows are the unrotated vectors. Change m and n and watch the log: rows with the same gap m − n get the same score.

Why it works, in one line: rotating both arrows by the same extra angle doesn't change the angle between them, and rotations don't change lengths, so the dot product (length × length × cos of the angle between) can't change. Only the gap m − n survives. Real models rotate many pairs of numbers at once, each pair at its own speed. Lesson 8.4 explains why attention needs position information in the first place.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[2, -1],
              [1,  1]])          # columns = where [1,0] and [0,1] land
x = np.array([3, 2])
A @ x                              # array([4, 5])  (@ means matrix multiply)
3 * A[:, 0] + 2 * A[:, 1]         # same: 3·column1 + 2·column2

th = np.radians(30)
R = np.array([[np.cos(th), -np.sin(th)],
              [np.sin(th),  np.cos(th)]])   # rotate 30° counterclockwise
house = np.array([[0,0], [2,0], [2,1.5], [1,2.5], [0,1.5]])  # one corner per row
house @ R.T                        # every corner rotated in one line
# R.T = transpose (rows ↔ columns); for a rotation it's also the undo: R.T @ R = identity

🌍 Where it's used
Flips & rotations for data augmentation 🖼️ Deep learning · 2012 AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al.)

How: a horizontal flip is the matrix [[−1, 0], [0, 1]] applied to every pixel's coordinates. AlexNet trained on random crops and their mirror images. Today libraries such as torchvision's RandomAffine pick a random rotation, scale and shear for each photo.

Why this method: a flipped cat is still a cat, so you get extra training images for free. One small matrix moves every pixel with the same formula.

A neural layer moves space 🖼️ Deep learning · 2014 Chris Olah: Neural Networks, Manifolds, and Topology

How: Olah describes a layer as three steps: a linear transformation by the weight matrix W, a shift by a vector b, then a bend (tanh: an S-curve squashing numbers to between −1 and +1, like sigmoid). His animations show the grid warping layer by layer until the two classes can be split by a straight line.

Why this method: the matrix step is cheap and has a learnable number per input–output pair, so training can find whatever stretch-and-turn untangles the data.

Networks that learn their own transformation 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 Spatial Transformer Networks

How: a small sub-network looks at the image and outputs 6 numbers: a 2×2 matrix plus a shift. Those warp the image (un-rotate, zoom in) before the main network classifies it.

Why this method: the network learns to straighten tilted or off-center inputs during training, with no extra labels. Six numbers are enough to describe any rotate/scale/shear/shift.

Rotation matrices encode word position 🧠 LLMs · 2021 RoFormer: Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)

How: each pair of numbers in a query or key vector is rotated by (position × a fixed angle), as in the playground. Meta's LLaMA (2023) removed the classic “absolute” position embeddings and added RoPE at every layer instead.

Why this method: the attention score then depends on the relative distance between words, and, as the RoPE paper notes, rotation keeps the vectors' lengths unchanged.

🤯 Fun fact · 2048 times more pictures

The AlexNet paper says its random crops plus mirror images increased the size of its training set by a factor of 2048 (the copies are, of course, very similar). Without it, the authors wrote, the network overfit badly. Mirroring is the simplest matrix there is: one minus sign.

🌉 AI bridge · matrices everywhere

LLMs & deep learning: every layer of a network multiplies its input vector by a weight matrix, which moves it to a new spot in space. Lesson 2.2 shows that “matrix times vector” is exactly a layer of neurons, and Lesson 8.4 explains why attention needs position information.

RL: a game-playing agent's network starts with the same move: the observation vector times a matrix of weights. The RL course's Lesson 4.2: neural nets refresher shows how those weights get trained.

Check yourself

1. A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] (rows). What is A · [1, 1]?
One dot product per row: [1, 2]·[1, 1] = 3 and [3, 4]·[1, 1] = 7. Column view: 1·[1, 3] + 1·[2, 4] = [3, 7]. [4, 6] adds the numbers in each column instead, which is a different operation.
2. A matrix has columns [0, 1] and [−1, 0]. What does it do to the plane?
î = [1, 0] lands on [0, 1] (straight up) and ĵ = [0, 1] lands on [−1, 0] (left). Both turned a quarter turn counterclockwise, so everything does. It's R(90°) with cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1.
3. Why does RoPE encode position with rotations?
Turning both arrows by the same extra amount leaves the angle between them, and their lengths, unchanged. So the score sees the gap m − n, which is what language cares about (“the word two places back”). You saw it in the RoPE log.
📝 TL;DR
  • A matrix is a grid of numbers that moves every point: Ax = one dot product per row.
  • Column view: column 1 is where [1, 0] lands, column 2 is where [0, 1] lands, and Ax = x₁·col 1 + x₂·col 2.
  • Rotation R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]] turns everything and keeps lengths.
  • Used in image augmentation, every neural layer, spatial transformers and RoPE in LLMs.
