SVD & PCA: finding what matters in data

Every matrix is a rotate (or flip), a stretch and another rotate (or flip). Keep only the biggest stretches and you can compress images, draw 768-number embeddings on a flat screen, and find the hidden topics in a pile of documents.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches; λ is the stretch (Lesson 2.5).
  • A rank-r matrix is a sum of r column × row layers; it needs r × (rows + columns) numbers (Lesson 2.4).
  • The shadow of a point on a direction u of length 1 is just the dot product point · u (Lesson 1.3): [3, 1] on [1, 0] casts a shadow of 3.
🎯 Hook

In 2013, the word2vec paper showed a picture of country and capital-city word vectors. The vectors had 1,000 numbers each, yet the figure was a flat 2D plot, and in it the country → capital pairs lined up in a strikingly regular pattern, even though nobody had told the model what a capital is.

How do you squash 1,000 dimensions onto a page and keep what matters? The caption says it: “Two-dimensional PCA projection”. That's this lesson.

Numbers first: rotate, stretch, rotate (flips allowed)

Take A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]] and feed it every arrow of length 1 (a circle). The result is an ellipse, a stretched circle. Its longest half-axis has length 6.71 and its shortest 2.24. Those two numbers are A's singular values, σ₁ and σ₂ (“sigma one, sigma two”).

Here's the surprise: you can always get from the circle to that ellipse in three simple moves. Rotate, then stretch along the axes, then rotate again. Either turn may also be a mirror flip, so strictly it's “rotate or flip, stretch, rotate or flip”. Every matrix, any size. That's the singular value decomposition (SVD):

A = U Σ Vᵀ Read right to left, like Lesson 2.2: first Vᵀ (“V transpose”) rotates (or flips), then Σ (capital sigma) stretches along the axes by σ₁, σ₂, …, then U rotates (or flips) again. The σ's are sorted from biggest to smallest and are never negative.

Careful: this Σ isn't “add up” (Lesson 0.5). Here it's a grid with the stretch amounts σ₁, σ₂ on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere: for our A, Σ = [[6.71, 0], [0, 2.24]]. And this little σ isn't the sigmoid (Lesson 3.2) or the standard deviation (Lesson 4.3); it's just a stretch amount.

Check: the ellipse's area is σ₁ × σ₂ times the circle's, and 6.71 × 2.24 = 15 = det A = 3·5 − 0·4 (Lesson 2.4's area factor). Rotations don't change area; only the stretch does.

SVD as layers: the important part first

SVD also writes A as a sum of rank-1 layers (column × row, Lesson 2.4), already sorted by importance:

A = σ₁ u₁ v₁ᵀ + σ₂ u₂ v₂ᵀ + σ₃ u₃ v₃ᵀ + … “A is sigma-one times the first column-times-row layer, plus sigma-two times the second, and so on.” u's are columns of U, v's are columns of V. Big σ = an important layer.

Keep only the first k layers and you get the best possible rank-k approximation of A (a 1936 result by Eckart and Young). That's how the picture playground in Lesson 2.4 worked, and how this one does:

PCA: the directions of most spread

Now point the same tool at data. You have a cloud of points (each point = one example, each axis = one feature). Principal component analysis (PCA) asks: along which direction do the points spread out the most? Project (cast a shadow of) every point onto a line, and measure how spread out the shadows are. 💡 Big idea PCA finds the directions where data varies most. The first principal component (PC1) is the direction of most spread; PC2 is the most spread at a right angle to it; and so on. They are the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix, a grid that records how the features spread and move together (its eigenvalues are the spreads). They are also the same arrows that the SVD of the centered data finds. Keep the top few and you've summarized many features with a few numbers.
✏️ Worked example: PCA on four points

Points [3, 1], [1, 3], [−1, −3], [−3, −1]. Their average is [0, 0], so they're already centered. “Spread” = the average of the squared shadows.

  • Onto the x-axis [1, 0]: shadows 3, 1, −1, −3. Spread = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9) / 4 = 5.
  • Onto the diagonal [1, 1]/√2 (length 1): shadows 4/√2 ≈ 2.83 for the first two, −2.83 for the others. Spread = 4 × 8 / 4 = 8. Best.
  • Onto [1, −1]/√2: shadows ±2/√2 ≈ ±1.41. Spread = 4 × 2 / 4 = 2.

The covariance matrix (average of x·xᵀ over the points) is [[5, 3], [3, 5]], whose eigenvalues are 8 and 2 with eigenvectors along [1, 1] and [1, −1]. PC1 = the diagonal, and it holds 8 / (8 + 2) = 80% of the total spread.

🧠 Analogy: photographing a baguette

You can only take a flat photo of a 3D baguette. Shoot it end-on and it's a boring circle; shoot it from the side and you see its whole length. PCA picks the camera angle that shows the most. For 1,000-dimensional embeddings, it picks the two directions that show the most, and that's your 2D plot.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[3, 0], [4, 5]])
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)            # S = [6.708, 2.236]; U and Vt are rotations or flips (here both flip)
np.allclose(U @ np.diag(S) @ Vt, A)    # True: (rotate or flip) · stretch · (rotate or flip)

X = np.random.randn(500, 768)             # 500 embeddings, 768 numbers each
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)                 # 1. center (broadcasting, Lesson 2.3)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
xy = Xc @ Vt[:2].T                      # 2. shadows on PC1, PC2 → (500, 2), ready to plot
explained = S**2 / (S**2).sum()           # share of spread per component
# scikit-learn: from sklearn.decomposition import PCA; PCA(2).fit_transform(X)

🌍 Where it's used
PCA to look at embeddings 🤖 Machine learning · 2013 word2vec (Mikolov et al.), Figure 2

How: 1,000-number skip-gram vectors for countries and capitals were projected onto their top 2 principal components and plotted. Google's Embedding Projector (2016) does the same interactively: it computes the top 10 principal components of any embedding table.

Why this method: PCA is fast, has no settings to tune, and gives the same answer every run, so it's the standard first look before fancier maps.

Eigenfaces 📜 History · 1991 Turk & Pentland: Eigenfaces for Recognition

How: PCA on many face photos gives “eigenfaces”: ghostly face-like images that are the top directions of variation. Each face becomes a short list of weights (“23% of eigenface 1 + …”), and recognition compares those lists.

Why this method: comparing a few dozen weights is far cheaper than comparing every pixel. Turk and Pentland also showed how to get the eigenvectors from a matrix sized by the number of images rather than the number of pixels, which made it feasible on computers of the time.

Hidden topics in documents (LSA) 📜 History · 1990 Latent semantic analysis (Deerwester et al.)

How: build a words × documents table of counts, keep only its top few SVD layers, and compare documents (and queries) in that small space. Each kept layer acts like a hidden “topic”.

Why this method: plain keyword matching misses synonyms: a query for “car” won't find a page that only says “automobile”. Keeping few layers merges words that show up in similar documents, so related words land close together.

Looking inside LoRA updates 🧠 LLMs · 2021 LoRA, Section 7

How: to understand why a tiny rank works, the authors ran SVD on the learned adapter matrices for different ranks and compared their top singular directions.

Why this method: SVD sorts any matrix's directions by importance, so it's the standard microscope for asking “how many directions does this matrix really use?”.

🤯 Fun fact · discovered four times

The SVD kept being rediscovered. Eugenio Beltrami found it in 1873, Camille Jordan in 1874, James Joseph Sylvester in 1889 and Léon Autonne in 1915, each apparently independently. The name came from Émile Picard in 1910, who first called the σ's “singular values”. Today the same 150-year-old idea draws maps of LLM embeddings.

🌉 AI bridge · what SVD and PCA feed

LLMs: PCA is how most “map of the embedding space” pictures are made, and SVD is how researchers measure the rank of weight updates (LoRA) and compress big matrices into thin factor pairs (Lesson 2.4).

Data science: the covariance matrix PCA uses gets its full story in Lesson 5.2 (covariance & correlation). Centering the data first matters: PCA measures spread around the average.

RL: PCA isn't a core RL tool, but the linear features in the RL course's Lesson 4.1: function approximation are exactly the kind of “few good directions” that PCA can provide from raw observations.

Check yourself

1. You keep the top k = 5 SVD layers of a 100 × 80 image. How many numbers do you store?
Each layer needs a column u (100 numbers), a row v (80 numbers) and its σ (1 number): 181. Five layers: 905, about 11% of the original 8,000.
2. What is the first principal component of a data cloud?
PCA rotates the line until the shadows' spread (variance) is as big as possible, exactly what you did in the playground. In the worked example that was the diagonal, holding 80% of the spread.
3. Why do people run PCA on LLM embeddings before plotting them?
PCA doesn't change the model; it chooses the best “camera angle”. It's quick, deterministic and needs no tuning, which is why the word2vec paper and the Embedding Projector use it.
📝 TL;DR
  • SVD: every matrix = (rotate or flip) · stretch · (rotate or flip), A = U Σ Vᵀ. Singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … are the stretches.
  • Keeping the top k layers is the best rank-k approximation: compression, denoising, LoRA analysis.
  • PCA = the directions of most spread in centered data = eigenvectors of the covariance = SVD of the data.
  • Used to plot embeddings, in eigenfaces, and to find hidden topics in documents (LSA).
