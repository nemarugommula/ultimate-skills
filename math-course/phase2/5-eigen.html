Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix can't turn

Most arrows get knocked off course by a matrix. A few special ones only get stretched. Those directions ranked the early web, predict where a Markov chain settles, and explain exploding signals in neural networks.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A matrix moves vectors: Av = one dot product per row (Lessons 2.1–2.2).
  • Scaling a vector keeps its direction: 3 · [1, 1] = [3, 3] points the same way (Lesson 1.2).
  • A Markov chain hops between states using a fixed table of chances: if today is sunny, tomorrow is sunny with chance 0.8 and rainy with 0.2; if rainy, it's 0.5 and 0.5. Only today matters.
🎯 Hook

In 1998, Google's founders described how they ranked the web: imagine a “random surfer” clicking links forever, and rank each page by how often the surfer lands there. Their paper says this ranking corresponds to the principal eigenvector of the web's link matrix.

Let's find out what an eigenvector is, and compute a PageRank yourself.

Numbers first: who gets turned, who doesn't

Take A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] and feed it three arrows:

A·[1, 0] = [2, 1]   ← turned (it used to point along the x-axis)
A·[1, 1] = [3, 3] = 3 × [1, 1]   ← same direction, 3 times longer
A·[1, −1] = [1, −1] = 1 × [1, −1]   ← same direction, same length

[1, 1] and [1, −1] are special: A doesn't turn them at all, it only stretches them. They're the eigenvectors of A (“eigen” is German for “own”: the matrix's own directions). The stretch factor is the eigenvalue: 3 for [1, 1], and 1 for [1, −1].

Av = λv “A times v equals lambda times v.” λ (the Greek letter lambda) is just a number. (A different λ from Lesson 1.4's penalty strength: Greek letters get reused.) Moving v with the matrix gives the same answer as stretching v by λ. A negative λ flips v to point backwards along its own line.
💡 Big idea An eigenvector is a direction the matrix only stretches, never turns. Its eigenvalue λ is how much. Along those directions, a complicated matrix behaves like plain multiplication by a number, which makes repeated multiplication easy to predict.
🎮 Playground · Hunt for eigenvectors

Drag the blue arrow v. The green arrow is Av. When they line up on the same line, you've found an eigenvector and the banner turns green. Try every matrix; one of them has no eigenvectors at all.

What you should have seen: the symmetric matrix has two eigen-directions at a right angle. The stretch has the two axes. The shear has only one (the x-axis). The 90° rotation turns every arrow, so it has no real eigenvectors. (On a spinning globe, by contrast, the axis is the one direction that doesn't move: in 3D, a rotation's axis is an eigenvector with λ = 1.)

🧠 Analogy: the grain of the wood

Squeeze a block of wood. Most lines you drew on it bend sideways, but lines running exactly along the grain, or exactly across it, just get shorter or longer. Those are the block's “own” directions. Find them, and you can describe any squeeze as “this much along the grain, that much across”.

Multiply again and again: power iteration

Here's the superpower. Take any starting vector and multiply it by A over and over. Each time, the part along the biggest-λ eigenvector grows the most. After a few rounds, that part dominates, and the vector points along the top eigenvector. This is power iteration, and it needs nothing but matrix × vector.

✏️ Worked example: tomorrow's weather, forever

The RL course's weather machine: a sunny day stays sunny 80% of the time, a rainy day stays rainy 50% of the time. Write today's chances as [P(sunny), P(rainy)]. The transition matrix has one column per “from” state: P = [[0.8, 0.5], [0.2, 0.5]].

  • Start on a rainy day: [0, 1]. Day 1: P·[0, 1] = [0.5, 0.5].
  • Day 2: [0.8·0.5 + 0.5·0.5, 0.2·0.5 + 0.5·0.5] = [0.65, 0.35]. Day 3: [0.695, 0.305]. Day 6: [0.714, 0.286].
  • It settles on [5/7, 2/7] ≈ [0.714, 0.286]. Check: P·[5/7, 2/7] = [(4 + 1)/7, (1 + 1)/7] = [5/7, 2/7]. Unchanged, so it's an eigenvector with λ = 1.

The other eigenvalue is 0.3, so the gap to the final answer shrinks to 0.3× each day. Load “Weather” in the playground and drag v to [2.5, 1] (the same direction as [5/7, 2/7]) to see Av land right on top of it.

🎮 Playground · Compute a tiny PageRank by power iteration

Four web pages. Arrows are links. A random surfer follows a random link 85% of the time and jumps to a random page 15% of the time. Press Step to multiply the ranks by the link matrix once. Watch them stop changing: that's the top eigenvector.

Page C wins because three pages link to it. D has no incoming links, so it only gets the random-jump share, 0.15 / 4 = 0.0375. Real PageRank ran the same loop at web scale (the 1998 paper mentions computing it for 26 million pages): each step is one huge but simple matrix × vector. The paper also says the damping factor is usually set to 0.85, as here.

Above 1 explodes, below 1 vanishes

Power iteration has a dark side. A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sentence one word at a time, multiplying its memory by the same weight matrix at every step. Along the top eigenvector, 30 steps multiply the signal by λ30:

🎮 Playground · Repeat the same multiply 30 times

1.130 ≈ 17.4 but 0.930 ≈ 0.042. A tiny difference in λ becomes a 400-fold difference. That's the exploding / vanishing problem, and the same thing happens to the training signal flowing backwards (Lesson 3.2).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 2]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)        # vals = [3, 1]; columns of vecs ∝ [1, 1] and [-1, 1]

P = np.array([[0.8, 0.5], [0.2, 0.5]])  # weather chain, columns = "from"
v = np.array([0.0, 1.0])                  # start: rainy
for _ in range(50):                    # power iteration
    v = P @ v
v                                    # array([0.714, 0.286]): the λ = 1 eigenvector

🌍 Where it's used
PageRank = the top eigenvector of the link matrix 🏭 In production · 1998 Brin & Page: The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine

How: each page's rank is the long-run fraction of time a random surfer spends there. The paper says it can be computed with a simple iterative algorithm and corresponds to the principal eigenvector of the normalized link matrix.

Why this method: counting links alone is easy to fool. The eigenvector asks a smarter question, “are you linked to by pages that are themselves important?”, and power iteration answers it with only matrix × vector steps.

Steady state of a Markov chain 🎮 RL · 1906 RL course 2.1: the Markov property

How: Markov's first paper on chains (1906) showed their averages settle to a fixed vector. Today we'd say: the long-run share of time in each state is the eigenvector of the transition matrix with λ = 1, as in the weather example. RL uses the same object when it averages over “how often the agent visits each state”.

Why this method: one eigenvector gives the long-run behaviour directly, instead of simulating thousands of days and counting.

Exploding & vanishing gradients in RNNs 🖼️ Deep learning · 2013 Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio: On the difficulty of training RNNs

How: for a simplified (linear) RNN, the paper shows that a largest eigenvalue of the recurrent weight matrix below 1 is enough for long-term signals to vanish, and above 1 is needed for them to explode.

Why this method: the eigenvalue turns a vague “training is unstable” into a number you can check. The same paper proposes a fix for the exploding side: clip the gradient when it gets too large.

Principal components 📊 Data science · 1901 PCA (Karl Pearson)

How: the eigenvectors of a dataset's covariance matrix (a table of how features vary together) are the directions in which the data spreads most; the eigenvalues say how much. That's all of Lesson 2.6.

Why this method: a few top eigenvectors summarize hundreds of features with little loss, which is how people draw 768-number embeddings on a flat screen.

🤯 Fun fact · the $25,000,000,000 eigenvector

Kurt Bryan and Tanya Leise wrote an article for linear-algebra students titled “The $25,000,000,000 Eigenvector: The Linear Algebra Behind Google”. The footnote explains the number: it was roughly Google's market value when the company went public in 2004. It's a great next read once you finish this lesson.

🌉 AI bridge · eigenvectors in RL and deep learning

RL: the RL course's Lesson 2.1 has the weather machine, where the long-run fraction of sunny days settles to the same number however you start. That number is the λ = 1 eigenvector you just computed, and the same lesson notes that PageRank's random surfer is a Markov chain.

Deep learning: keeping the “gain” of each layer near 1 so signals neither explode nor vanish is a big reason for careful random weight initialization (Lesson 4.3) and for normalization layers like RMSNorm, which rescale every vector to a steady size (Lesson 1.4).

Check yourself

1. A = [[3, 0], [0, 2]] and v = [0, 1]. Is v an eigenvector? If so, what is λ?
A·[0, 1] = [3·0 + 0·1, 0·0 + 2·1] = [0, 2] = 2 × [0, 1]. Same direction, twice as long, so λ = 2. (The other eigenvector, [1, 0], has λ = 3.)
2. Which matrix has no real eigenvectors at all?
A rotation turns every arrow by 90°, so no direction stays on its own line. The shear keeps the x-axis (λ = 1), and the stretch keeps both axes.
3. Why was power iteration a good fit for ranking the whole web?
Each step just spreads every page's rank along its links. No fancy algebra on a giant matrix, only the same cheap step repeated until the numbers stop changing, like in the playground.
📝 TL;DR
  • Eigenvector: Av = λv. The matrix only stretches v (by λ) and never turns it.
  • Power iteration (multiply again and again) points any vector toward the top eigenvector.
  • A Markov chain's long-run distribution and PageRank are λ = 1 / top eigenvectors.
  • |λ| above 1 explodes, below 1 vanishes under repetition: the RNN gradient problem.
