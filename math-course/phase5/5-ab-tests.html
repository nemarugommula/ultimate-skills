Phase 5 · Lesson 5

Confidence intervals, p-values & A/B tests

How to tell a real improvement from luck: error bars, the p-value (and what it isn't), and the experiments that decide what ships at tech companies and which LLM really scores higher.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The standard deviation s measures spread, and about 95% of a bell curve lies within 2 standard deviations of its center (Lesson 5.1).
  • The central limit theorem: an average of many random values is approximately bell-shaped (Lesson 4.7).
  • Randomizing who gets which version removes confounders, so a difference can be credited to the change (Lesson 5.2).
🎯 Hook

In 2012 a Microsoft employee suggested a small change to how Bing displayed ad headlines. It was judged low priority and sat for more than six months. Then an engineer ran a quick A/B test. Within hours, revenue alarms fired. The change raised revenue by 12%, worth over $100 million a year in the US alone.

Bing's alarm said “too good to be true”, and usually such alerts mean a bug. How do you decide whether a difference is real or just noise? That's this lesson.

Numbers first: two buttons

You test two sign-up buttons. Version A: 1,000 visitors, 100 sign-ups (10%). Version B: 1,000 visitors, 120 sign-ups (12%). B is 2 points better. Ship it?

Imagine running the same test again tomorrow with new visitors. You wouldn't get exactly 100 and 120 again; maybe 93 and 126, maybe 108 and 104. Each measured rate wobbles. The question is whether a 2-point gap is bigger than the wobble.

The typical size of that wobble is the standard error from Lesson 4.7: SE = s / √n, the typical miss of an average of n. A rate p̂ (“p-hat”, the measured rate) is an average of 1s (signed up) and 0s, and its spread is s = √(p̂(1 − p̂)). Why: with 10% ones, variance = 0.1 × 0.9² + 0.9 × 0.1² = 0.09, and √0.09 = 0.3 = √(0.1 × 0.9). So for A, SE = 0.3 / √1000 ≈ 0.0095, about ±1 point.

The new part is the gap B − A, and both rates wobble. Variances add, so:

SEdiff = √( SEA² + SEB² ) “The standard error of the difference is the square root of the sum of the two squared standard errors.” The gap wobbles more than either rate alone, but less than their two wobbles added up.
💡 Big idea Every number measured from a sample comes with a wobble, its standard error, which shrinks like 1/√n. A 95% confidence interval is the estimate ± about 2 standard errors. If the interval for “B minus A” includes 0, the data can't rule out “no difference”.
95% CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE “The 95% confidence interval is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors.” The recipe catches the true value 95% of the time: run 100 experiments and about 95 of their intervals contain the truth.

The p-value: how surprising would this be if nothing were going on?

Start by assuming the boring explanation, the null hypothesis H₀ (“H-nought”): A and B are really the same, and any gap is luck. Then ask: if that were true, how often would luck alone produce a gap at least as big as the one I saw? That probability is the p-value.

z = (difference) / SEdiff     p = P( |gap| ≥ what we saw  |  H₀ true ) z counts how many standard errors the gap is from 0. The p-value is the chance of a gap at least that many standard errors away, in either direction, if there were truly no difference. By convention, p < 0.05 is called “statistically significant”.
✏️ Worked example: is B really better?
  • SEA = √(0.10 × 0.90 / 1000) = 0.0095 and SEB = √(0.12 × 0.88 / 1000) = 0.0103.
  • The difference's SE combines both (variances add): √(0.0095² + 0.0103²) = 0.0140.
  • 95% CI for B − A: 0.020 ± 1.96 × 0.0140 = 0.020 ± 0.027 → [−0.7, +4.7] points. It includes 0.
  • z = 0.020 ÷ 0.0140 = 1.43, so p ≈ 0.15. If A and B were identical, a gap this big would show up 15% of the time. Not convincing.
  • Same rates with 10,000 visitors each: SEdiff = 0.0044, CI = [+1.1, +2.9] points, z = 4.5, p ≈ 0.000006. Now it's real. Same 2-point gap, 10 times the data.
Careful What a p-value is NOT

In 2016 the American Statistical Association published a statement because these mistakes are so common. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean:

  • “there's a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true” (it's computed assuming the null is true);
  • “B is better by a lot” (a tiny, useless effect becomes significant with enough data);
  • “the result is important” (p doesn't measure the size of an effect: the confidence interval does).

It means: “if there were no real difference, data at least this extreme would turn up about 3% of the time”.

🧠 Analogy: a courtroom

The null hypothesis is “innocent until proven guilty”. The p-value asks: “if the defendant were innocent, how likely is evidence this damning?” Very unlikely (small p) → convict, i.e. reject H₀. But “not enough evidence” doesn't prove innocence: a non-significant A/B test doesn't prove A and B are equal, it may just be too small. The chance of convicting a guilty defendant is the test's power.

🎮 Playground · Run an A/B test

You set the truth (the base rate and how much better B really is); the simulator sends random visitors. Run one experiment and read its interval and p-value. Then run it 100 times: each line is one experiment's 95% interval for B − A. Set the lift to 0 (an A/A test) and count the false alarms.

With lift 0, about 5 of every 100 experiments come out “significant”: that's what p < 0.05 means, a 5% false-alarm rate by design. With a real 2-point lift and 1,000 visitors, most experiments miss it: low power. Slide the visitors up and nearly every interval clears 0. Planning the sample size before you start is how you avoid running a test that can't succeed.

The peeking problem

A tempting shortcut: check the dashboard every day and stop as soon as p < 0.05. The trouble is that p-values wander. Even with no real difference, if you look 50 times, the chance that the p-value dips below 0.05 at least once is far bigger than 5%.

🎮 Playground · Peeking at an A/A test

A and B are identical (both 10%). We check the p-value after every 100 visitors per version, up to 5,000. Run one experiment to watch its p-value wander, then run 200 and compare “stop at the first p < 0.05” with “look once, at the end”.

Peeking after every 100 visitors turns a 5% false-alarm rate into roughly 30%. Fixes: decide the sample size in advance and look once, or use methods designed for continuous monitoring (“sequential” or “always-valid” tests, which large A/B platforms offer).

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
nA, kA, nB, kB = 1000, 100, 1000, 120
pA, pB = kA / nA, kB / nB
se = np.sqrt(pA*(1-pA)/nA + pB*(1-pB)/nB)     # 0.0140  standard error of the difference
ci = (pB - pA) + np.array([-1.96, 1.96]) * se    # [-0.007, 0.047]  95% interval
z = (pB - pA) / se                             # 1.43
p = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))                    # 0.153  two-sided p-value

# Eval error bars: 0/1 scores on 800 questions -> accuracy ± 1.96 * SE
scores = np.random.binomial(1, 0.6, 800)
scores.mean(), 1.96 * scores.std() / np.sqrt(len(scores))   # about 0.60 ± 0.034

🌍 Where it's used
A/B tests decide what ships 🏭 In production · 2017 Kohavi & Thomke: The Surprising Power of Online Experiments (HBR)

How: users are randomly split between the current site (A) and the change (B), metrics are compared with confidence intervals, and the change ships only if the improvement is clearly bigger than the noise. The Bing headline in the hook is the article's opening story.

Why this method: experts are poor at predicting which ideas will work. Randomization handles confounders, and the statistics handle luck, so the test answers “did this change cause the gain?”.

Error bars for LLM evals 🧠 LLMs · 2024 Miller: Adding Error Bars to Evals

How: treat a benchmark's questions as a sample from all possible questions. The paper recommends reporting standard errors of the mean, clustered standard errors when questions come in related groups, and paired comparisons when two models answer the same questions.

Why this method: “model B scored 1 point higher” means nothing without the wobble. Even on a 2,000-question benchmark, an 85% score has a standard error of about 0.8 points.

Peeking-proof A/B tests 🏭 In production · 2015 Johari, Pekelis & Walsh: Always Valid Inference

How: “always valid” p-values and intervals stay correct no matter when you look, so users can watch results live and stop whenever they like.

Why this method: the paper notes that ordinary p-values are “wholly unreliable” under continuous monitoring, which is how people actually use dashboards. Its method runs on a large commercial A/B testing platform.

Seeds and significance in deep RL 🎮 RL · 2017 Henderson et al.: Deep RL that Matters

How: the paper recommends running each algorithm with several random seeds and backing claims with significance metrics (confidence intervals, significance tests) before saying one method beats another.

Why this method: the paper showed how much RL results vary from run to run; without error bars, a “new state of the art” can be a lucky seed.

What p-values can't tell you 📜 History · 2016 ASA Statement on p-values

How: six principles, including “P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true” and that decisions shouldn't rest only on passing a threshold.

Why this method: a single cutoff (p < 0.05) invites gaming and misreading. Reporting the effect size with its interval says both how big and how certain.

🤯 Fun fact · the first p-value (1710)

John Arbuthnot looked at London's birth records for the 82 years from 1629 to 1710. In every single year, more boys were born than girls. If boys and girls were equally likely to “win” a year, that would happen with probability (1/2)⁸² ≈ 2 × 10⁻²⁵. He concluded it wasn't chance. The 0.05 cutoff came much later: Ronald Fisher suggested “one in twenty” as a convenient threshold in 1925.

Check yourself

1. A metric has standard deviation 20 across users. You measure its average on 400 users. What is the standard error of that average?
SE = s / √n = 20 / √400 = 20 / 20 = 1. So a 95% interval is roughly the average ± 2.
2. An A/B test reports p = 0.03. Which statement is correct?
The p-value is computed assuming the null hypothesis is true, so it can't be the probability that the null is true. And it says nothing about how big the effect is: look at the confidence interval for that.
3. A team checks its A/B dashboard every morning and stops the test the first day p < 0.05. Why is that a problem?
The 5% guarantee holds for one look at a sample size chosen in advance. With many looks the chances add up (around 30% with 50 looks in the playground). Use a fixed horizon or a sequential, always-valid method.
📝 TL;DR
  • Standard error = s / √n: the wobble of an estimate. 4× the data halves it.
  • 95% confidence interval ≈ estimate ± 1.96 SE. If the interval for B − A contains 0, you can't rule out “no difference”.
  • p-value = chance of a result at least this extreme if the null were true. Not the chance the null is true, not the effect size.
  • Plan for power (enough data), don't peek, randomize. Used in A/B tests, LLM eval error bars, RL seed comparisons.
