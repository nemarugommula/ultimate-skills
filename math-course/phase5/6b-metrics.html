Classification metrics: precision, recall, F1 & calibration

How to score a yes/no model honestly: count its four kinds of right and wrong, trade catching more for crying wolf less, and check whether its “80% sure” really means 80%. Used for spam, fraud, medical screening, object detectors and LLM safety classifiers.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A classifier gives each item a score; a threshold turns it into yes/no. The ROC curve plots “positives caught” against “false alarms” for every threshold (Lesson 3.6).
  • When the thing you look for is rare, most alarms are false, even with a good test: think in 1,000 people (Lesson 4.2).
  • Scores only count on test data the model never trained on (Lesson 5.6).
🎯 Hook

A famous public dataset of European credit-card transactions has 492 frauds out of 284,807 transactions. Here's a fraud detector you can build in one line: return "not fraud". Its accuracy? 284,315 ÷ 284,807 = 99.83%.

It catches zero fraud. The dataset's own page warns that accuracy is “not meaningful” here and asks for a different score. This lesson is about the numbers that don't lie.

Numbers first: 100 emails, four boxes

100 emails, 10 of them spam. A filter flags 12. Of those, 8 really are spam. Sort every email into one of four boxes:

flagged as spamlet through
really spam (10)✅ TP = 8
true positive		❌ FN = 2
false negative (missed)
really ham (90)❌ FP = 4
false positive (false alarm)		✅ TN = 86
true negative

That grid is the confusion matrix. Accuracy = (8 + 86) ÷ 100 = 94%. The lazy filter that flags nothing gets 90%. Only 4 points apart, yet one is useful and the other worthless. We need numbers that look at the spam itself:

precision = of the 12 I flagged, how many were spam? 8 ÷ 12 = 0.67  ·  recall = of the 10 spam, how many did I catch? 8 ÷ 10 = 0.80
precision = TP / (TP + FP)      recall = TP / (TP + FN) “Precision is true positives divided by everything I flagged. Recall is true positives divided by everything that was really positive.” Precision asks “can I trust an alarm?”; recall asks “how much did I miss?”. The lazy filter has recall 0 ÷ 10 = 0, and its precision is 0 ÷ 0: undefined, because it never raised an alarm.

Recall is the same number as the ROC curve's “positives caught” (also called the true-positive rate or sensitivity). The ROC's other axis is the false-alarm rate FP ÷ (FP + TN) = 4 ÷ 90 = 0.044.

💡 Big idea When positives are rare, accuracy is dominated by the easy “no” cases and tells you almost nothing. Precision and recall both put the rare class in the numerator: one checks your alarms, the other checks your misses. Moving the threshold trades one for the other.
🧠 Analogy: fishing in a lake full of boots

You cast a net. Precision: what fraction of your catch is fish rather than old boots? Recall: what fraction of all the lake's fish ended up in your net? A tiny net near one fish has great precision and terrible recall. Dragging a huge net along the bottom catches every fish (recall 1) and every boot too. The threshold is the size of your net.

The threshold trade-off

Raise the threshold and the model only flags what it's very sure about: fewer false alarms (precision up), more misses (recall down). Lower it and the reverse happens. Which side matters more depends on the cost of each mistake:

  • Spam filter: a real email lost in the spam folder hurts more than one spam getting through, so filters lean toward high precision.
  • Medical screening: a missed disease is far worse than an extra follow-up test, so screening leans toward high recall, and accepts that, with a rare disease, most positives will be false (Lesson 4.2's 9%).
  • Fraud: analysts can only review so many alerts a day, which fixes how many you can flag; then you want the best recall at that number.

One number: F1, the harmonic mean

To compare models with one number, people combine precision P and recall R. Why not just average them? Take a filter that flags every email: recall 1.0, precision 0.1 (only 10 of 100 are spam). Plain average: (0.1 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0.55, which sounds half-decent for a useless filter. The harmonic mean punishes the weak side:

F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R) “F-one is two times precision times recall, divided by precision plus recall.” For the flag-everything filter: 2 × 0.1 × 1 ÷ 1.1 = 0.18. For our 100-email filter: 2 × 0.67 × 0.8 ÷ 1.47 = 0.73. F1 is only high when both P and R are high.

If recall matters β times as much as precision, there's a weighted version, Fβ; F1 is the β = 1 case.

Try:

Precision–recall vs ROC when positives are rare

The ROC curve's two axes are each computed within one class: catch rate among positives, false-alarm rate among negatives. So it ignores how many of each there are. Precision mixes the two classes, so it feels the base rate. Same model, catch rate 0.9 and false-alarm rate 0.05:

base rate 50%: precision = 0.45 ÷ (0.45 + 0.025) = 0.95  ·  base rate 1%: precision = 0.009 ÷ (0.009 + 0.0495) = 0.15

Identical ROC point, but at 1% only 15% of alarms are real. That's Bayes' rule from Lesson 4.2 again. When positives are rare, the precision–recall (PR) curve shows the pain the ROC curve hides, and its area (average precision, AP, or AUPRC) is the usual one-number summary. A random guesser's precision equals the base rate, so that's the PR curve's “coin-flip” line.

✏️ Worked example: a useful model with lower accuracy

On the 284,807 card transactions (492 frauds), a model flags 1,000 transactions and 400 of them are fraud.

  • TP = 400, FP = 600, FN = 492 − 400 = 92, TN = 284,807 − 400 − 600 − 92 = 283,715.
  • Accuracy = (400 + 283,715) ÷ 284,807 = 99.76%: lower than the never-flag model's 99.83%!
  • Precision = 400 ÷ 1,000 = 0.40. Recall = 400 ÷ 492 = 0.81. F1 = 2 × 0.40 × 0.81 ÷ 1.21 ≈ 0.54. The lazy model: recall 0, F1 0.

The model catches 81% of fraud, with 4 in 10 alerts real. Accuracy ranks it below doing nothing. Precision, recall and F1 rank it correctly.

Calibration: does “80% sure” mean 80%?

Precision and recall only use the yes/no decisions. But models also output a probability, and people act on it: “the model is 95% sure this is a tumour”. A model is calibrated if, among all the times it says 80%, it's right about 80% of the time.

To check, sort predictions into bins by confidence (0–10%, 10–20%, …), and in each bin compare the average confidence with how often the event really happened. Plot one against the other: that's a reliability diagram. Perfect calibration is the diagonal.

A weather app says “90% rain” on 5 days; it rained on 3 (60%). It says “60%” on 5 other days; it rained on 3 (60%).
ECE = (5/10) · |0.6 − 0.9| + (5/10) · |0.6 − 0.6| = 0.15 + 0 = 0.15
ECE = Σm ( nm / n ) · | actualm − confm | “The expected calibration error is a weighted average, over the bins m, of the gap between how often it really happened and how confident the model was.” nm / n is the share of predictions in bin m, so busy bins count more. 0 = perfectly calibrated.

Our app is overconfident: its 90% days behave like 60% days. Overconfident means probabilities too close to 0 and 1; underconfident means too timid, stuck near 50%. (Guo et al. use the same recipe with a classifier's top-class confidence against its accuracy.)

The fix: temperature scaling

Many models turn a score z into a probability with the sigmoid σ(z) (or softmax for many classes, Lesson 0.4). Temperature scaling divides the score by one number T first:

q = σ( z / T ) “q equals sigma of z divided by T.” T > 1 pulls every probability toward 50%, T < 1 pushes it toward 0 or 1. Score z = 3: σ(3) = 0.95, but with T = 2, σ(1.5) = 0.82.

Dividing by a positive T never changes which side of 50% a probability is on, or the ranking of items, so accuracy, ROC and PR curves don't change; only the confidence does. T is fitted on a validation set by making the observed labels as probable as possible: maximum likelihood (Lesson 5.3).

🎮 Playground · Break the calibration, then fix it with T

Bars show how often the event really happened in each confidence bin; red boxes are the gaps from the diagonal. Make it overconfident, then press Fit T: it picks T on a separate validation set.

Notice the accuracy never moves while you slide T: temperature scaling only changes how sure the model sounds. And “Fit T” lands near the overconfidence you set, because the best fix for scores that were multiplied by s is to divide them by s.

🐍 In code 
from sklearn.metrics import (confusion_matrix, precision_score, recall_score, f1_score,
                             precision_recall_curve, average_precision_score)
from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve
y_true = [1]*8 + [1]*2 + [0]*4 + [0]*86      # the 100 emails: 8 TP, 2 FN, 4 FP, 86 TN
y_pred = [1]*8 + [0]*2 + [1]*4 + [0]*86
confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred)                 # [[86, 4], [2, 8]]  rows = truth (0, 1)
precision_score(y_true, y_pred), recall_score(y_true, y_pred), f1_score(y_true, y_pred)  # 0.667 0.8 0.727

P, R, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_true, scores) # one (P, R) point per threshold
average_precision_score(y_true, scores)          # area under the PR curve (AP)
frac_pos, mean_conf = calibration_curve(y_true, probs, n_bins=10)  # reliability diagram
# temperature scaling in PyTorch: probs = torch.softmax(logits / T, dim=-1), T fitted on validation NLL

🌍 Where it's used
Precision–recall for rare fraud 📊 Data science · 2015 Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset (ULB) · Saito & Rehmsmeier

How: frauds are 0.172% of the transactions, so the dataset's description recommends measuring the area under the precision–recall curve instead of confusion-matrix accuracy. Teams then read precision (are the alerts real?) and recall (how much fraud is caught?) off the curve to choose a threshold.

Why this method: accuracy rewards flagging nothing. A 2015 PLOS ONE study of imbalanced datasets found the PR plot more informative than the ROC plot, because it reacts to the base rate.

mAP: the score of every object detector 🖼️ Deep learning · 2014 COCO detection evaluation · COCO paper

How: a predicted box is a true positive if it overlaps a real object enough: IoU, the overlap area ÷ the combined area, above a threshold. Sort boxes by confidence, build a precision–recall curve per class, take its area (AP), and average over the 80 classes and over 10 IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95. The older PASCAL VOC metric is AP at IoU 0.50 alone.

Why this method: a detector outputs many scored boxes and no single threshold is right for everyone; AP scores the whole ranking. COCO notes that averaging over IoUs rewards detectors with better localization.

AUPRC for LLM safety classifiers 🧠 LLMs · 2023 Llama Guard (Meta)

How: an LLM is fine-tuned to label prompts and responses safe or unsafe; its probability for “unsafe” is the score. The paper reports AUPRC on its own test set and on OpenAI's moderation dataset, following OpenAI's moderation paper. For GPT-4 and Azure baselines, which return no score, it falls back to precision, recall and F1.

Why this method: the authors chose AUPRC because it focuses on the positive (“unsafe”) class and helps pick the threshold that balances precision and recall for each use case.

Temperature scaling fixes overconfident networks 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 Guo et al.: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

How: they found modern deep networks much more overconfident than older ones. Dividing the logits by one T, fitted on validation NLL, cut a 110-layer ResNet's ECE on CIFAR-100 from 16.53% to 1.26%.

Why this method: it beat fancier methods (histogram binning, isotonic regression, vector and matrix scaling) on most of their datasets, was the fastest, and can't change a single prediction, so accuracy stays exactly the same.

GPT-4's reliability diagram 🧠 LLMs · 2023 GPT-4 Technical Report, Figure 8

How: on a subset of the MMLU multiple-choice exam, the model's probability for each A/B/C/D answer is its confidence; bins of confidence are plotted against accuracy. The pre-trained model scored ECE 0.007; after RLHF post-training, 0.074.

Why this method: one plot shows whether a model's probabilities can be trusted. The report says post-training hurt calibration significantly, so a chat model's confidence needs checking before you use it as a probability.

🤯 Fun fact · weather forecasters got there first

Calibration was a weather problem long before it was an AI problem. In 1950 Glenn Brier, writing in Monthly Weather Review, proposed scoring probability forecasts by the squared gap between forecast and outcome. Say “80% rain” and it rains: (0.8 − 1)² = 0.04. Say 80% and it stays dry: (0.8 − 0)² = 0.64. The Brier score is still in scikit-learn as brier_score_loss.

🌉 AI bridge · scoring classifiers everywhere

RL & RLHF: a reward model is a classifier for “which answer is better?”, scored by accuracy on held-out comparisons, and humans only agree with each other about 73% of the time, which caps it: see RL Lesson 7.3: reward models.

LLMs: safety filters, LLM-as-a-judge evaluators and hallucination detectors are all yes/no classifiers, so they're reported with precision, recall, F1 or AUPRC, plus error bars (Lesson 5.5). Calibrated confidence is what lets a system say “I'm not sure” at the right moments.

Check yourself

1. A tumour detector has TP = 30, FP = 10, FN = 20. What are its precision and recall?
Precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP) = 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75: three in four alarms are real. Recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN) = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.60: it misses 2 in 5 tumours. For screening, that recall is the worrying number. (F1 = 2 × 0.75 × 0.6 ÷ 1.35 ≈ 0.67.)
2. Why do fraud and LLM-safety teams report the area under the precision–recall curve rather than accuracy or ROC AUC?
With 0.17% fraud, “never flag” is 99.83% accurate. ROC rates are computed within each class, so they look the same at any base rate; precision drops as positives get rarer (Bayes, Lesson 4.2). Llama Guard picked AUPRC for exactly this focus on the “unsafe” class.
3. You apply temperature scaling with T = 2 to an overconfident image classifier. What happens to its accuracy?
T rescales all scores by the same positive number, so the ranking and the top class are unchanged. Only the confidence moves toward 50% (σ(3) = 0.95 becomes σ(1.5) = 0.82). That's why Guo et al. could fix calibration “for free”.
📝 TL;DR
  • Confusion matrix: TP, FP, FN, TN. With rare positives, accuracy lies (never-flag fraud = 99.83%).
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): can I trust an alarm? Recall = TP / (TP + FN): how much did I miss? The threshold trades them.
  • F1 = 2PR / (P + R), a harmonic mean that's only high if both are. PR curves (AP / AUPRC) beat ROC when positives are rare.
  • Calibrated = “says 80%, right 80%”. Check with a reliability diagram and ECE; fix with temperature scaling σ(z / T), which leaves accuracy unchanged.
  • Used for spam, fraud and screening thresholds, COCO mAP, Llama Guard's AUPRC, and GPT-4's calibration plot.
