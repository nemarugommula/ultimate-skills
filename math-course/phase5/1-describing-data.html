Describing data: mean, median, spread

A handful of numbers that summarize thousands: the center, the spread and the tail. They are the first thing any data scientist computes, and they run inside every normalization layer of a neural network.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Σ means “add these up”, like a for-loop with a running total (Lesson 0.5).
  • The expected value is the long-run average of a random quantity, and the variance is the average squared distance from it (Lesson 4.4).
  • The bell curve (normal distribution) shows up whenever many small effects add up (Lesson 4.7).
🎯 Hook

Your chatbot API answers most requests in 120 milliseconds. The dashboard says the average is 130 ms. Everyone is happy. Yet users are complaining it “sometimes hangs for ages”.

Both facts are true at once. The average hides the slow tail. In this lesson you'll learn the three or four numbers that describe a pile of data honestly, and which one to look at when.

Numbers first: nine friends in a bar

Nine friends earn (in thousands of dollars a year): 40, 45, 50, 50, 55, 60, 60, 65, 70.

  • The mean (average): add them all up and divide by how many. 495 ÷ 9 = 55.
  • The median: sort them and take the middle one. The 5th of 9 is 55.
  • The mode: the most common value. Here there's a tie: 50 and 60 each appear twice.

Now a billionaire walks into the bar. Ten people, and one of them earns 1,000,000 thousand (one billion).

mean = (495 + 1,000,000) ÷ 10 = 100,049.5 thousand ≈ $100 million each  ·  median = (55 + 60) ÷ 2 = 57.5 thousand

On average, everybody in the bar is now a multi-millionaire. Nobody's bank account changed. The mean got dragged by one extreme value. The median barely moved. That's the whole story of “mean vs median” in one picture.

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xn) / n = (1/n) Σi xi Read “x-bar”: add up all n data values and divide by n. The bar on top is statisticians' shorthand for “the average of the x's”.
💡 Big idea The mean is the balance point: every value pulls on it, so one extreme value can drag it far. The median is the middle value: it only cares about order, so outliers barely move it. When the data has a long tail (incomes, latencies, file sizes), report both.

Spread: how far from the center?

Two classes both average 70 on a test. In one, everyone scored between 65 and 75. In the other, half scored 40 and half scored 100. Same mean, totally different stories. We need a number for spread.

The recipe: measure each value's distance from the mean, square it (so negatives don't cancel positives), average those squares. That's the variance. Take its square root to get back to the original units: the standard deviation.

s² = (1/n) Σi (xi − x̄)²     s = √s² “s squared” (the variance) is the average squared distance from the mean. “s” (the standard deviation) is its square root: roughly, the typical distance of a value from the mean, in the data's own units.

Reminder from Lesson 4.3: in a bell-shaped pile, about 68% of values sit within 1 standard deviation of the mean and 95% within 2. That's why “2 standard deviations away” is a common rule of thumb for “unusual”.

✏️ Worked example: five wait times by hand

Five customers wait 2, 3, 3, 4, 8 minutes for coffee.

  • Mean: (2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 8) ÷ 5 = 20 ÷ 5 = 4.  Median: sorted, the middle is 3.  Mode: 3.
  • Distances from the mean: −2, −1, −1, 0, +4. Squared: 4, 1, 1, 0, 16. Sum = 22.
  • Variance: 22 ÷ 5 = 4.4.  Standard deviation: √4.4 ≈ 2.10 minutes.
  • The 8-minute wait is (8 − 4) ÷ 2.10 ≈ 1.9 standard deviations above the mean. Hold on to that number: it's called a z-score.
Gotcha Divide by n or by n − 1?

You'll see the variance written with n − 1 instead of n. Dividing by n − 1 (“Bessel's correction”) gives a slightly bigger number. It's used when your data is a sample and you want to guess the spread of the whole population. For the wait times: 22 ÷ 4 = 5.5, so s ≈ 2.35 instead of 2.10.

The practical trap: np.std(x) divides by n by default, but pandas' .std() divides by n − 1. Same data, two answers. With thousands of data points the difference is tiny.

Percentiles, histograms and box plots

The median is the 50th percentile: half the data is below it. The 90th percentile (p90) has 90% of the data below it, and the 99th (p99) has 99% below it. Engineers live by p99: “99% of requests finish within this time”. It's the number that catches the slow tail the mean hides.

A histogram shows the whole shape: chop the number line into bins and draw a bar for how many values land in each. A box plot squeezes the shape into five numbers: the box runs from the 25th to the 75th percentile (the middle half of the data), the line inside is the median, the whiskers reach to the furthest values that aren't too far out, and anything beyond is drawn as a dot: a candidate outlier. John Tukey introduced the box plot in 1970.

🧠 Analogy: describing a crowd over the phone

A friend asks what the crowd at a concert was like. You don't list 5,000 ages. You say: “mostly mid-twenties (the center), give or take five years (the spread), plus a few grandparents at the back (the tail)”. Mean or median, standard deviation, and a high percentile do exactly that job for data.

Z-scores: putting features on the same scale

A house has 3 bedrooms and 150 square meters. To a learning algorithm, “150” looks 50 times more important than “3”, just because of the units. The fix is to standardize every feature: subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation.

z = (x − x̄) / s “z equals x minus the mean, divided by the standard deviation”: how many standard deviations above (+) or below (−) average this value is. After standardizing, every feature has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

A 190 cm person, when the mean is 170 and s = 8, has z = (190 − 170) ÷ 8 = 2.5: tall. A 5-bedroom house, when the mean is 3 and s = 1, has z = 2: also unusual, and now the two numbers are comparable.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
x = np.array([2, 3, 3, 4, 8])
x.mean(), np.median(x), x.std()          # 4.0, 3.0, 2.098  (np.std divides by n)
np.percentile(x, [50, 90, 99])            # 3.0, 6.4, 7.84
z = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()             # standardized: mean 0, std 1

df = pd.DataFrame({"wait": x})
df.describe()                            # count, mean, std (n-1!), min, 25%, 50%, 75%, max

🌍 Where it's used
Standardizing features before training 🤖 Machine learning · 2011 scikit-learn StandardScaler

How: for each feature column, learn the mean and standard deviation on the training data, then turn every value into a z-score. Square meters and bedroom counts end up on the same scale.

Why this method: the docs warn that many estimators “might behave badly” if features aren't roughly mean 0 and variance 1: penalties like L2 regularization and distance-based methods treat all features as if they had the same units.

Batch mean & variance inside a network 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 Batch Normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy)

How: inside the network, for every feature, compute the mean and variance over the current mini-batch of examples, standardize, then let the network learn its own scale and shift.

Why this method: keeping each layer's inputs on a steady scale made training much faster: the paper reports the same image-classification accuracy with 14 times fewer training steps.

Mean & std of one token's features 🧠 LLMs · 2016 Layer Normalization (Ba, Kiros & Hinton)

How: the same z-score recipe, but computed across the features of a single example (in a transformer: one token's vector) instead of across a batch. Transformers apply it in every block (Lesson 8.4).

Why this method: batch statistics depend on the batch size and are awkward for sequences. Layer norm uses only the example itself, so it does exactly the same computation at training and test time.

p99 latency, not the mean 🏭 In production · 2013 Dean & Barroso: The Tail at Scale

How: services are judged by high percentiles. The paper's example: a server that's usually fast but has a p99 of one second. If one user request must wait for 100 such servers, 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of requests will take over a second.

Why this method: a mean blends the slow tail into a comfortable-looking number. A percentile says directly how bad the slow cases are, which is what users feel.

Robust averages for RL results 🎮 RL · 2021 Agarwal et al.: Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice

How: instead of the mean score over runs, report the interquartile mean (IQM): drop the bottom 25% and top 25% of runs, average the middle half, and add an interval for the uncertainty.

Why this method: the mean is dragged by a few lucky or crashed runs, the median ignores most of the data. IQM sits in between: robust to outliers but still uses half the runs.

🤯 Fun fact · above-average legs

Almost everybody has more legs than average. Nobody has three legs, but some people have one or none, so the mean number of legs per person is a little under 2. Everyone with two legs is above the mean. The median, of course, is exactly 2.

🌉 AI bridge · summaries everywhere

Deep learning & LLMs: normalization layers (batch norm, layer norm) are this lesson's z-score, computed millions of times per second inside the network. Training curves are usually smoothed with a moving average before anyone reads them.

RL: RL results are famously noisy, so “mean ± spread over several seeds” is the minimum honest report. The RL course starts with exactly that in Lesson 0.5: one run lies.

Check yourself

1. Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100. What are the mean and the median?
Sum = 110, divided by 5 = 22. Sorted, the middle value is 3. The single 100 drags the mean far above every “typical” value, but not the median.
2. You run an LLM API. Which number best tells you whether users are suffering from slow responses?
Latency has a long tail. The mean blends the slow requests into a pleasant average; p99 tells you directly that 1 in 100 requests is slower than this. With many requests per user session, those 1-in-100 cases hit almost everyone.
3. Model inputs: a house is 200 m² where the mean is 150 m² and the standard deviation is 25 m². What is its z-score?
z = (200 − 150) ÷ 25 = 50 ÷ 25 = 2: two standard deviations above average. 50 is the raw difference before dividing by the spread.
📝 TL;DR
  • Mean x̄ = sum ÷ n (the balance point, pulled by outliers). Median = middle value (robust). Report both for long-tailed data.
  • Standard deviation s = √(average squared distance from the mean): the typical distance from the center.
  • Percentiles (p50, p90, p99), histograms and box plots show the tail; production systems watch p99.
  • z = (x − x̄) / s standardizes a feature to mean 0, std 1: used before training and inside batch norm and layer norm.
