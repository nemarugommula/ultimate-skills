Covariance, correlation & causation

Do two columns of data move together, and does that mean one causes the other? The question behind feature selection, PCA, and some of the most embarrassing failures of medical AI.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Mean x̄ (the average) and standard deviation s (the typical distance from the mean) (Lesson 5.1).
  • A z-score (x − x̄) / s says how many standard deviations a value is above average (Lesson 5.1).
  • The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up; cosine similarity divides out the lengths (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2018, a network learned to spot pneumonia on chest X-rays from three hospital systems. It did well on X-rays from those hospitals, and mostly worse on a hospital it had never seen. Why?

It could tell which hospital an X-ray came from (over 99.9% accuracy), and one hospital had far more pneumonia: 34% of patients versus about 1%. Hospital was correlated with pneumonia, so the network partly learned the hospital, not the disease. This lesson separates “moves together” from “causes”.

Numbers first: study hours and test scores

Five students: hours studied x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, test score y = 52, 55, 61, 64, 68. More hours, higher score. How do we put a number on “moves together”?

Step 1: subtract the means (x̄ = 3, ȳ = 60). Step 2: multiply each student's two deviations. Step 3: average.

studentx − x̄y − ȳproduct
1−2−8+16
2−1−5+5
30+10
4+1+4+4
5+2+8+16

Every product is positive or zero: when x is below its average, so is y, and when x is above, so is y. The average product, 41 ÷ 5 = 8.2, is the covariance.

cov(x, y) = (1/n) Σi (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) “The covariance of x and y”: for each data point, multiply how far x is from its mean by how far y is from its mean, then average. Positive = they rise together, negative = one rises as the other falls, near 0 = no straight-line pattern.

Covariance has a problem: its size depends on the units. Measure time in minutes instead of hours and it becomes 60 times bigger. The fix, like in Lesson 5.1: divide by both standard deviations. The result is the correlation r, always between −1 and +1.

r = cov(x, y) / ( sx · sy ) “r equals the covariance divided by the standard deviation of x times the standard deviation of y”. It's the covariance of the z-scores: units cancel, and only the pattern is left.
sx = √2 ≈ 1.414 · sy = √34 ≈ 5.831 · r = 8.2 ÷ (1.414 × 5.831) = 8.2 ÷ 8.246 ≈ 0.994

Almost a perfect +1: these five students lie nearly on a straight line.

💡 Big idea Correlation r measures how well a straight line describes two variables: +1 perfect rising line, −1 perfect falling line, 0 no straight-line pattern. It says nothing about curves, and nothing about why they move together.
Connection r is a cosine similarity in disguise

Write the deviations as two vectors: [−2, −1, 0, 1, 2] and [−8, −5, 1, 4, 8]. Their dot product is 41, the sum of the products column. Their lengths are √10 ≈ 3.162 and √170 ≈ 13.04. Cosine similarity: 41 ÷ (3.162 × 13.04) ≈ 0.994. Same number!

So the correlation is the cosine of the angle between the two centered columns (Lesson 1.3). r = 1 means the two arrows point the same way, r = 0 means they're at a right angle.

Drag any point. The dashed cross marks the means. A point is green when its product (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) is positive (top-right or bottom-left of the cross) and red when negative. The orange line is the best straight-line fit. Then load Anscombe's four datasets one by one.

Anscombe built these four datasets in 1973. All four have (to two decimals) the same means, the same standard deviations, the same best-fit line and the same r ≈ 0.816. One is a noisy line, one is a smooth curve, one is a perfect line with a single outlier, and one is a vertical stack plus one far-away point that creates the whole correlation. Always plot your data.

✏️ Worked example: −1 and a sneaky 0
  • x = [1, 2, 3], y = [6, 4, 2]. Means 2 and 4. Deviations: x → −1, 0, +1 and y → +2, 0, −2. Products: −2, 0, −2, so cov = −4 ÷ 3 ≈ −1.33. sx ≈ 0.816, sy ≈ 1.633, and 0.816 × 1.633 ≈ 1.33. So r = −1.33 ÷ 1.33 = −1: a perfect falling line.
  • x = [1, 2, 3], y = [5, 2, 5]. Means 2 and 4. Deviations: x → −1, 0, +1 and y → +1, −2, +1. Products: −1, 0, +1, which add to 0. So r = 0, even though y clearly depends on x (it dips in the middle).

Lesson: r only measures straight-line togetherness. r = 0 means “no straight-line pattern”, not “no relationship”.

Correlation is not causation

Across a year, ice-cream sales and swimming accidents are positively correlated. Ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Hot weather drives both. A hidden third variable that drives two others is called a confounder.

In the hook, the hospital was a confounder: it left detectable traces in the images (the network could read them almost perfectly) and it also changed how likely pneumonia was. The network found the shortcut.

🧠 Analogy: the rooster and the sunrise

Every morning the rooster crows, then the sun comes up. The correlation is perfect. A pure pattern-matcher concludes the rooster raises the sun. To test it, you'd have to intervene: keep the rooster quiet one morning and watch. Randomized experiments (Lesson 5.5) are that intervention, done carefully.

Simpson's paradox: when pooling flips the answer

A real 1986 study compared two kidney-stone treatments. A was open surgery, B a less invasive procedure. Here are the success rates:

A (open surgery)B (less invasive)
Small stones93% (81/87)87% (234/270)
Large stones73% (192/263)69% (55/80)
All patients78% (273/350)83% (289/350)

A wins for small stones. A wins for large stones. Yet B wins overall! The confounder is stone size: doctors gave the hard cases (large stones) mostly to A. The pooled numbers compare “A on mostly hard cases” with “B on mostly easy cases”.

🎮 Playground · Make (and break) Simpson's paradox

The success rates inside each group are fixed at the study's values. You choose how many of each treatment's patients had large (hard) stones. The overall rate is just a weighted average. Find the mix where the paradox appears, then press “Randomize”.

Randomizing who gets which treatment gives both groups the same mix of easy and hard cases, so the confounder can't tilt the comparison. That's why a randomized experiment can turn a correlation into a causal answer, and why tech companies run A/B tests instead of just mining logs (Lesson 5.5).

Many features at once: the covariance matrix

With 100 features you have 100 × 100 pairs. Put every pair's covariance in a grid and you get the covariance matrix (its diagonal holds each feature's own variance). Divide each entry by the two standard deviations and it becomes the correlation matrix, the colorful heatmap at the start of most data-science notebooks. PCA (Lesson 2.6) finds the directions in which this matrix says the data spreads the most. Lesson 8.4b uses the same matrix to build and sample a tilted 2D bell curve.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5]); y = np.array([52, 55, 61, 64, 68])
np.mean((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean()))   # 8.2  covariance (divide by n)
np.cov(x, y, bias=True)                    # 2x2 covariance matrix (bias=True: divide by n)
np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]                    # 0.994  correlation r

df = pd.DataFrame({"hours": x, "score": y})
df.corr()                                  # correlation matrix of every column pair

🌍 Where it's used
Spurious correlation = shortcut learning 🖼️ Deep learning · 2018 Zech et al.: pneumonia CNNs across hospitals

How: the network detected the hospital system for 99.95% of one hospital's X-rays, and pneumonia rates differed hugely between hospitals (34.2% vs 1.2%). Simply sorting X-rays by hospital already scored an AUC of 0.861 on the pooled data.

Why this method: testing on an external hospital, not just a held-out slice of the same data, is what exposed the confounder. Same-hospital test data shares the shortcut.

Length correlates with “preferred” 🧠 LLMs · 2023 Singhal et al.: Length Correlations in RLHF

How: in human preference data, longer answers tend to be chosen, so reward models learn “longer = better”. The paper found that a reward based purely on length reproduced most of the RLHF improvements.

Why this method: comparing against a length-only baseline is a cheap confounder check. If it explains the gain, the model learned a correlation, not quality.

Simpson's paradox in a clinical study 📜 History · 1986 Kidney stones (Charig et al., BMJ)

How: the numbers in the playground. Within each stone size, open surgery did better; pooled, the other treatment looked better, because the hard cases weren't spread evenly.

Why this method: splitting by the confounder (or randomizing it away) is the only way to compare like with like. The pooled average alone gives the wrong answer.

Covariance matrix → PCA 🤖 Machine learning · 1901 PCA (Pearson 1901; scikit-learn docs)

How: compute the covariance matrix of all features, then find its main directions (Lesson 2.6). Strongly correlated features collapse onto one direction.

Why this method: when features are highly correlated they carry the same information twice. PCA keeps the few directions that explain most of the variance, which compresses data and speeds up models.

🌉 AI bridge · correlation in learning systems

RL: consecutive frames of a game are highly correlated, and training on them in order keeps pushing a network the same way. DQN's replay buffer shuffles past experience to break those correlations: see RL Lesson 4.3: DQN. And the length bias above is exactly how reward models go wrong in RL Lesson 7.3: reward models.

LLMs & deep learning: a model learns whatever correlates with the label, including shortcuts. Fixing it takes better data, external test sets (Lesson 5.6) and experiments (Lesson 5.5).

Check yourself

1. Two features have covariance 6. Their standard deviations are 2 and 5. What is the correlation r?
r = 6 ÷ (2 × 5) = 0.6: a moderate positive straight-line relationship. r can never be bigger than 1, so 30 and 1.2 are impossible.
2. A dataset has r = 0 between x and y. What can you conclude?
Points on the curve y = x² for x from −3 to 3 have r = 0, yet y is perfectly determined by x. Correlation only measures straight-line patterns. Plot the data.
3. Why do tech companies randomly assign users to versions A and B, instead of comparing users who happened to choose each version?
Users who choose a new feature are often the most engaged ones: a confounder, just like stone size. Randomizing gives both groups the same mix, which is what the “Randomize” button in the Simpson playground did.
📝 TL;DR
  • Covariance = average of (x − x̄)(y − ȳ): positive if they rise together, negative if opposite.
  • Correlation r = cov / (sx sy), between −1 and +1: how well a straight line fits. Same r can hide very different shapes (Anscombe): plot it.
  • Correlation ≠ causation: a confounder can drive both. Pooling groups can even flip the answer (Simpson's paradox).
  • Randomized experiments break confounders. Models learn spurious correlations (shortcuts) unless you test them on truly new data.
