Bayesian thinking: priors & posteriors

Start with a belief, look at the evidence, end with a better belief. The recipe behind Thompson sampling, hyperparameter search, spam filters and the true meaning of L2 regularization.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bayes' rule: updated belief = old belief × how well it explains the evidence, then rescale to sum to 1 (Lesson 4.2).
  • The likelihood L(θ) = P(data | θ) and the maximum-likelihood estimate θ̂ = the knob setting that makes the data most probable (Lesson 5.3).
  • A density curve shows where a continuous quantity is likely to be; the area under it is 1 (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

Three online sellers. A: 10 reviews, 100% positive. B: 50 reviews, 96% positive. C: 200 reviews, 93% positive. Who do you buy from?

Maximum likelihood says A: 100% beats everything. Your gut says “10 reviews isn't much”. Bayesian thinking turns that gut feeling into arithmetic, and the answer (spoiler: B) falls out in one line.

Numbers first: a brand-new restaurant

A restaurant opened last week. It has 3 reviews, all positive. Maximum likelihood says its chance of a happy customer is 3/3 = 100%. Nobody believes that. Why not? Because before you saw any reviews you already knew something: most restaurants make most people happy, but none makes everyone happy.

That “before” knowledge is the prior. The simplest way to write it down: pretend you've already seen a few reviews. Say you start with 1 imaginary positive and 1 imaginary negative (“I have no idea, could be anything”). Add the real ones:

(1 + 3 positive) ÷ (2 imaginary + 3 real) = 4 ÷ 5 = 80%

If you know restaurants are usually around 50/50, use a stronger prior: 10 imaginary positive and 10 imaginary negative. Then (10 + 3) ÷ (20 + 3) = 13 ÷ 23 ≈ 57%. Three reviews barely move a strong belief. After 300 reviews, though, the data would swamp either prior.

P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ) × P(θ) “The posterior (belief about θ after the data) is proportional to the likelihood times the prior (belief before)”. ∝ is read “is proportional to”: equal up to a rescaling that makes the total probability 1.

For a success rate, the beliefs are curves over every possible rate from 0 to 1. The standard family is the Beta curve. Beta(a, b) is exactly the “a imaginary successes and b imaginary failures” prior, and the update is just counting:

Beta(a, b)  +  h successes, t failures  →  Beta(a + h, b + t)     mean = (a + h) / (a + b + h + t) “Beta a, b” is a bump over the rates 0 to 1, peaked near a / (a + b), and narrower when a + b is bigger. Seeing data adds the real counts to the imaginary ones. The posterior's average is the blended success rate.
💡 Big idea A Bayesian doesn't produce one number, they produce a belief curve: which values are plausible and how sure we are. Prior × likelihood → posterior. With little data the prior keeps you sensible; with lots of data the evidence wins. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
✏️ Worked example: the three sellers

Use the “no idea” prior Beta(1, 1): one imaginary positive and one imaginary negative.

  • A (10 of 10 positive): (1 + 10) ÷ (2 + 10) = 11 ÷ 12 ≈ 91.7%.
  • B (48 of 50): (1 + 48) ÷ (2 + 50) = 49 ÷ 52 ≈ 94.2%. ✅ The best bet.
  • C (186 of 200): (1 + 186) ÷ (2 + 200) = 187 ÷ 202 ≈ 92.6%.

“Add one success and one failure, then take the fraction” is Laplace's rule of succession, over 200 years old. A's perfect record is mostly luck from a small sample; B has nearly as good a record with five times the evidence.

🎮 Playground · Prior × data → posterior

Choose a prior (where you think the rate is, and how strongly), then flip a coin whose true heads rate you set. Watch the dashed prior, the blue likelihood (what the data alone says) and the purple posterior (the blend). Then try a strong wrong prior and flip a lot.

Three things to notice. With prior strength 2 (the flat “no idea” prior), the posterior peak sits exactly at the maximum-likelihood answer. A strong prior pulls the estimate toward its center: it shrinks it. And every flip makes the posterior narrower: the curve is your uncertainty.

MAP: the peak of the posterior, and where L2 regularization comes from

Sometimes you just want one number. The MAP estimate (maximum a posteriori) is the peak of the posterior: maximum likelihood with the prior's opinion added in. In log form, the product becomes a sum:

θMAP = argmaxθ [ log P(data | θ) + log P(θ) ] “Theta-MAP is the θ that maximizes the log-likelihood plus the log-prior.” With a flat prior, the second term is constant and MAP equals maximum likelihood.

Now give a neural network's weights a bell-curve prior centered at 0: “I believe weights are probably small”. Numbers first, with the prior N(0, 1): the bell's height at w = 3 is e−3²/2 = e−4.5 ≈ 0.011 times its height at w = 0, so w = 3 is about 90× less plausible. Its log is just −3²/2 = −4.5.

So the log-prior is a penalty, −w²/2, that grows with the square of the weight (plus a constant that doesn't depend on w). With a narrower or wider prior of spread τ (“tau”), it's −w²/(2τ²). Flip the signs to turn “maximize” into “minimize a loss”:

minimize   loss(w) + λ · w² “Loss plus lambda times w squared”: the usual negative log-likelihood plus a penalty on big weights. That's L2 regularization (weight decay). λ (lambda) = 1 / (2τ²): a narrower prior means a bigger penalty.

Tiny example: the data alone says w = 3, with loss (w − 3)². Add a penalty 0.5 · w². The slope of (w − 3)² + 0.5w² is 2(w − 3) + w, which is zero at w = 2. The prior pulled the weight from 3 toward 0. That's all weight decay is: a Gaussian prior, applied as MAP (Lesson 7.4 shows how it's used in training).

🧠 Analogy: a GPS that knows the map

Your phone's GPS says you're 20 meters into a lake. The phone also has a prior: you're in a car, cars are on roads. It combines the noisy reading (likelihood) with the map (prior) and puts you on the road by the lake (posterior). One bad reading doesn't throw you into the water, but if the GPS keeps insisting for a mile, the phone will believe you're on a ferry.

Using the uncertainty: Thompson sampling

A news site has three headlines and wants the one people click most. Showing each a fixed number of times wastes traffic on losers. Thompson sampling uses the posteriors directly: for every visitor, draw one random plausible click rate from each headline's posterior curve, and show the headline whose draw is highest. Uncertain headlines sometimes draw high (so they get explored); clearly bad ones almost never do.

🎮 Playground · Thompson sampling picks a headline

Three headlines with hidden true click rates. Each starts at Beta(1, 1). Step one visitor at a time to see the random draws, or run many. Watch the posteriors separate and the traffic flow to the winner.

Early on, all three curves are wide and traffic is spread out. As evidence piles up, the best headline's curve pulls ahead and gets most visitors, but the others still get the occasional look in case we were unlucky. No exploration rate to tune: the posterior's width decides.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
a, b = 1, 1                                  # prior Beta(1, 1): "no idea"
h, t = 48, 2                                 # seller B's reviews
post = stats.beta(a + h, b + t)              # posterior Beta(49, 3)
post.mean(), post.interval(0.95)            # 0.942, and a 95% range of plausible rates

# Thompson sampling: one draw per arm from its posterior, pick the biggest
wins, losses = np.array([3, 10, 1]), np.array([40, 90, 20])
arm = np.argmax(np.random.beta(1 + wins, 1 + losses))

🌍 Where it's used
Beta posteriors pick ads and news 🏭 In production · 2011 Chapelle & Li: An Empirical Evaluation of Thompson Sampling

How: each ad or article keeps a posterior over its click rate; each impression draws from every posterior and shows the highest draw. The paper tested it on display-ad selection and news-article recommendation data.

Why this method: it explores in proportion to uncertainty, and the authors found it competitive with or better than alternatives like UCB, while being a few lines to implement.

Bayesian optimization of hyperparameters 🤖 Machine learning · 2012 Snoek, Larochelle & Adams

How: treat “validation accuracy as a function of the learning rate, depth, …” as unknown, keep a posterior belief over that function (a Gaussian process), and try next the setting the posterior says is most promising.

Why this method: each training run is expensive. Grid search wastes runs on settings already known to be bad; the posterior uses every past run to choose the next. The paper matched or beat expert tuning on several models.

L2 penalty / weight decay = a Gaussian prior 🤖 Machine learning · 1970 Ridge regression (Hoerl & Kennard; Bayesian interpretation)

How: adding λ‖w‖² to the loss is the MAP estimate under a bell-curve prior centered at zero on every weight. In machine learning this penalty is known as weight decay, and deep-learning optimizers apply it to network weights (Lesson 7.4).

Why this method: with more weights than data can pin down, the prior “weights are small” picks a sensible solution among the many that fit, and tends to reduce overfitting (Lesson 5.6).

RLHF's KL penalty as a prior 🧠 LLMs · 2022 Korbak, Perez & Buckley: RL with KL penalties is better viewed as Bayesian inference

How: RLHF fine-tunes a model to get high reward while a KL penalty keeps it close to the original model (Lesson 6.4). The paper shows this is equivalent to Bayesian inference: the pretrained model is the prior, the reward is the evidence.

Why this method: pure reward maximization collapses the model onto a few outputs. Seeing the pretrained model as the prior explains what the KL “leash” is for: it keeps the fine-tuned model anchored to everything it learned in pretraining.

🤯 Fun fact · will the sun rise tomorrow?

Laplace applied his rule of succession to the sunrise: if the sun has risen on each of the k days of the last 5,000 years (k ≈ 5,000 × 365.25, about 1.8 million), the chance it rises tomorrow is (k + 1) ÷ (k + 2): odds of about 1,826,200 to 1. He immediately added that this badly underrates it, because we know far more about the sun than a list of sunrises. The prior matters!

Check yourself

1. Prior Beta(2, 2). You see 3 successes and 1 failure. What is the posterior mean?
Posterior Beta(2 + 3, 2 + 1) = Beta(5, 3). Mean = 5 ÷ (5 + 3) = 0.625. The maximum-likelihood answer would be 3/4 = 0.75; the prior pulled it toward 0.5.
2. You have a strong prior and then collect 100,000 data points. What happens to the posterior?
A prior of strength 20 is worth 20 imaginary observations. Against 100,000 real ones, it's a rounding error. Priors matter most when data is scarce.
3. In Thompson sampling, why draw a random sample from each posterior instead of always picking the highest posterior mean?
Picking the highest mean never explores: if the true best option had a bad start, it never gets another chance. Sampling explores exactly as much as the uncertainty justifies.
📝 TL;DR
  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Beliefs are curves; data narrows them.
  • Beta(a, b) = “a imaginary successes, b imaginary failures”. Update: Beta(a + h, b + t). Beta(1, 1) gives Laplace's rule: (h + 1)/(n + 2).
  • MAP = the posterior's peak. A Gaussian prior on weights turns MAP into L2 regularization / weight decay.
  • Used in Thompson sampling, Bayesian optimization, spam filters, and as a lens on RLHF's KL penalty.
