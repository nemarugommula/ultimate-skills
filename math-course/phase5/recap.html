Phase 5 · Recap

Recap: statistics & data science

Six tools for turning data into decisions: summarize it, relate it, fit it, update beliefs about it, test it, and make sure your model works on data it has never seen.

🧾 Cheat sheet

📏

5.1 Describing data

x̄ = Σxᵢ/n · s = √(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n) · z = (x − x̄)/s

Mean is pulled by outliers, the median isn't. Percentiles show the tail. z-scores put features on one scale.

Used in: feature scaling, batch/layer norm, p99 latency, IQM for RL results.

🔗

5.2 Correlation & causation

r = cov(x, y) / (sx sy), between −1 and +1

How well a straight line fits. Confounders can fake a relationship or flip it (Simpson's paradox). Randomize to get causation.

Used in: correlation matrices, PCA, catching shortcut learning and length bias.

🫧

5.2b k-means clustering

J = Σ ‖xᵢ − ca(i)‖²

Assign each point to its nearest centre, move each centre to its points' mean; the loss never rises. Use k-means++ starts, an elbow plot and standardized features.

Used in: FAISS product quantization, VQ-VAE codebooks, customer segments.

🎯

5.3 Maximum likelihood

θ̂ = argmaxθ Σ log P(xᵢ | θ)

Pick the knobs that make the observed data most probable. Negative log-likelihood is the loss. Least squares = MLE with bell-shaped noise.

Used in: LLM pretraining, logistic regression, Bradley–Terry reward models.

🔄

5.4 Bayesian thinking

posterior ∝ likelihood × prior · Beta(a, b) → Beta(a + h, b + t)

Beliefs are curves that data narrows. A prior acts like imaginary data. A Gaussian prior on weights = L2 regularization.

Used in: Thompson sampling, Bayesian optimization, spam filters, weight decay.

🧪

5.5 Intervals & A/B tests

SE = s/√n · 95% CI ≈ estimate ± 1.96 SE · p = P(this extreme | H₀)

Every estimate wobbles. Significance means the interval excludes 0. Plan for power, don't peek.

Used in: online experiments, LLM eval error bars, comparing RL seeds.

🪤

5.6 Overfitting

test error ≈ bias² + variance + noise

Low training error can hide high test error. Split train / validation / test, cross-validate, and watch for leakage.

Used in: every ML project, Kaggle private leaderboards, benchmark contamination checks.

🎚️

5.6b Precision, recall, F1 & calibration

P = TP/(TP+FP) · R = TP/(TP+FN) · F1 = 2PR/(P+R)

Rare positives → use precision–recall, not accuracy. Calibrated = “says 80%, right 80%”; fix with temperature scaling σ(z/T).

Used in: fraud and safety classifiers, COCO mAP, Llama Guard AUPRC, GPT-4's calibration plot.

🧩 Use it yourself: is the new model really better?

✏️ The problem

You evaluate two LLMs on the same 500-question benchmark. Model A gets 412 right, model B gets 431 right. Your manager wants to announce “B is better”. Using only this phase's math: what's each model's accuracy with a 95% interval, and is the difference real?

Extra info from the per-question results: both models were right on 400 questions, only A was right on 12, only B was right on 31, both were wrong on 57.

Solution Work it out, then click

1. Each accuracy with its error bar (Lesson 5.5). A: 412/500 = 82.4%, SE = √(0.824 × 0.176 / 500) = 1.70 points → 82.4 ± 3.3. B: 431/500 = 86.2%, SE = 1.54 points → 86.2 ± 3.0. The intervals overlap.

2. Treating the two scores as independent. Gap = 3.8 points. SE of the gap = √(1.70² + 1.54²) = 2.30 points. z = 3.8 / 2.30 = 1.65 → p ≈ 0.10. The 95% CI is [−0.7, +8.3] points. Not significant.

3. But they answered the same questions: use a paired comparison. For each question, score B − A as +1 (only B right: 31 times), −1 (only A right: 12 times) or 0 (457 times). Mean = (31 − 12)/500 = 0.038. Their standard deviation (Lesson 5.1): the distances from the mean are +0.962 (31 times), −1.038 (12 times) and −0.038 (457 times); squared, 0.9254, 1.0774 and 0.00144. Average square = (31 × 0.9254 + 12 × 1.0774 + 457 × 0.00144) / 500 ≈ 42.27 / 500 ≈ 0.0845, so s = √0.0845 ≈ 0.291 and SE = 0.291/√500 = 1.30 points. z = 3.8 / 1.30 = 2.92 → p ≈ 0.003. 95% CI: [+1.3, +6.3] points. Significant.

Why the difference? Most questions are easy (both right) or hard (both wrong) for both models. Pairing cancels that shared noise and looks only at the 43 questions where they disagree. This is exactly the advice in Miller's “Adding Error Bars to Evals” (Lesson 5.5).

Before announcing: check the benchmark isn't contaminated (Lesson 5.6), and report the interval, not just “B wins”.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. Five salaries (in $10k): 3, 4, 5, 6, 100. Which number best describes a “typical” salary here?
The single 100 drags the mean to 23.6, higher than four of the five people. The median (the middle value, 5) ignores how extreme the outlier is (Lesson 5.1).
2. Suppose that across many fires, the number of firefighters sent and the damage caused have r = 0.8. What's the best conclusion?
Bigger fires get more firefighters and cause more damage. Correlation measures moving together, not cause and effect (Lesson 5.2).
3. A coin lands heads twice in 2 flips. Maximum likelihood says p̂ = 1. With a Beta(1, 1) prior, what is the posterior mean?
Posterior Beta(1 + 2, 1 + 0) = Beta(3, 1), mean 3/4 = 0.75 (Laplace's rule of succession). The prior keeps two flips from making you certain (Lessons 5.3 and 5.4).
4. An A/B test reports the 95% confidence interval for B − A as [−0.5, +3.1] percentage points. What should you conclude?
Significance at 5% means the 95% interval excludes 0. This one doesn't. It does say the effect is unlikely to be very negative, which can still be useful (Lesson 5.5).
5. Your model has 99% training accuracy and 70% validation accuracy. Which change is most likely to help?
A big train/validation gap is the signature of overfitting (high variance). More epochs usually widen it. The fixes reduce variance (Lessons 5.6 and 5.4; Lesson 7.4 covers them in depth).
