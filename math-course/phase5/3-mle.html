Phase 5 · Lesson 3

Maximum likelihood: training = making data probable

Pick the knob settings that make the data you actually saw as probable as possible. That one idea is how LLMs are pretrained, how logistic regression is fit, and why “squared error” is the default loss.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • For independent events, probabilities multiply: two heads in a row with a fair coin is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 (Lesson 4.1).
  • Logs turn multiplying into adding: log(a × b) = log a + log b (Lesson 0.3).
  • The bell curve N(μ, σ²) is centered at μ (“mu”) with spread σ (“sigma”, the standard deviation) (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

The 2018 paper that introduced GPT describes its whole pretraining objective in one sentence: use “a standard language modeling objective to maximize the following likelihood”, followed by one line of math.

Today's LLMs train on trillions of tokens with thousands of GPUs, and the goal is still that one number: how probable the model finds the real text. By the end of this lesson you'll know what that number is, why it's a sum of logs, and why making it big is the same as making the loss small.

Numbers first: a mystery coin

A friend flips a coin 10 times and gets 7 heads and 3 tails. What's your best guess for p, the coin's chance of heads?

Try a few guesses. For each one, compute how probable the exact sequence you saw would be. With heads chance p, the chance of 7 particular heads and 3 particular tails is p × p × … (7 times) × (1 − p) × … (3 times):

guess p0.50.60.70.80.9
p⁷ (1 − p)³0.000980.001790.002220.001680.00048

The winner is p = 0.7: it makes what actually happened about 2.3 times more probable than a fair coin does. That's maximum likelihood: among all possible settings, pick the one under which your data is the least surprising.

L(θ) = P(data | θ)      θ̂ = argmaxθ L(θ) “L of theta”, the likelihood, is the probability of the data you saw if the knobs were set to θ (theta: the model's parameters, here just p). “Theta-hat equals the arg-max of L”: the value of θ that makes L as big as possible. The hat means “our estimate”.

argmax versus max: the max is the biggest value of L (0.00222); the argmax is the input that produces it (0.7). We want the knob setting, so argmax.

💡 Big idea Probability fixes the knobs and asks about data: “with a fair coin, how likely is 7 heads?”. Likelihood fixes the data and asks about the knobs: “given 7 heads, how plausible is each p?”. Same formula, read the other way. Training a model by maximum likelihood means turning the knobs until the real data looks as probable as possible.

Why everyone takes the log

Real datasets have millions of points, so the likelihood is a product of millions of numbers below 1. It gets unimaginably small: 1,000 fair-coin flips have probability 0.5¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 10⁻³⁰¹, and a computer's ordinary floating-point numbers simply round anything below about 10⁻³²⁴ to zero.

The fix: take the log. Logs turn the product into a sum, and since log only ever goes up when its input goes up, the peak stays at the same place.

ℓ(p) = log L(p) = 7 · log p + 3 · log(1 − p) “Little ell of p”, the log-likelihood: add up the log-probability of each observation. Seven heads each contribute log p, three tails each contribute log(1 − p). Biggest at the same p = 0.7.

In this lesson, log always means the natural log, ln (Lesson 0.3): log 0.7 = ln 0.7 ≈ −0.357.

At p = 0.7: 7 × (−0.357) + 3 × (−1.204) = −6.11. At p = 0.5: 10 × (−0.693) = −6.93. Less negative is better. Flip the sign and you get the negative log-likelihood (NLL), a number to minimize: a loss function.

🎮 Playground · The likelihood curve

Set the data (number of heads and tails), then slide your guess p. The curve shows how plausible each p is, scaled so the best one is 1. Compare 7-out-of-10 with 70-out-of-100: same peak, very different sharpness.

The peak is always at heads ÷ total: maximum likelihood says “use the fraction you observed”. More data doesn't move the peak, it makes it narrower: with 100 flips, p = 0.5 becomes wildly implausible, while with 10 flips it's merely less likely. (How narrow, exactly, is the topic of Lesson 5.5.)

🧠 Analogy: the detective

A detective finds muddy boot prints, size 44. Suspect A wears size 44 and was out in the rain; suspect B wears size 38 and stayed home. The detective doesn't ask “how probable is suspect A?”, they ask “under which story is this evidence least surprising?”. That's likelihood. Maximum likelihood picks the story that best explains the clues.

✏️ Worked example: a tiny language model by maximum likelihood

Training text: “the cat sat · the cat ran · the dog sat”. Our model has one knob: q = P(next word is “cat” | previous word is “the”), and P(“dog” | “the”) = 1 − q.

  • After “the” we saw: cat, cat, dog. Likelihood L(q) = q × q × (1 − q).
  • Log-likelihood ℓ(q) = 2 log q + log(1 − q). At q = 0.5: 3 × (−0.693) = −2.08. At q = 2/3: 2 × (−0.405) + (−1.099) = −1.91. Better!
  • The argmax is q̂ = 2/3: exactly the fraction of times “cat” followed “the”. For simple counting models, maximum likelihood = counting.
  • The loss per word is −1.91 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.64. An LLM does the same thing with billions of knobs and a neural network instead of a count table, and that per-token negative log-likelihood is its cross-entropy loss (Lesson 6.3).

Fitting a bell curve, and where squared error comes from

Now the data is continuous: heights in cm. The model is a bell curve with two knobs, its center μ and spread σ (the standard deviation, not the sigmoid of Lesson 3.2). Each data point contributes the height of the curve at that point (its density), and the log-likelihood adds up their logs.

How tall is the bell at a point? Numbers first, with σ = 1. At the centre it's at its peak, 1 / √(2π) ≈ 0.40. A point 1σ from the centre gets e−½ ≈ 0.61 of the peak height: 0.40 × 0.61 ≈ 0.24. A point 2σ away gets e−2 ≈ 0.14 of it: about 0.054. A point d standard deviations away gets e−d²/2 of the peak, and a wider curve has a lower peak, 1 / (σ√(2π)).

height at x = 1 / (σ√(2π)) × e−(x − μ)² / (2σ²) “The bell's height at x is the peak height, one over sigma root two pi, times e to the minus (distance from the centre squared, over two sigma squared).”

Take its log and it splits into two penalties. For the point 1σ away: ln 0.24 ≈ −1.42 = −0.92 (ln of the peak: the width penalty) − 0.5 (1² ÷ 2: the miss penalty). Add that up over all n points:

ℓ(μ, σ) = − n · ln(σ√(2π))  −  Σi (xi − μ)² / (2σ²) Width penalty: every one of the n points pays ln(σ√(2π)), more for a wider (flatter) curve. Miss penalty: the sum of squared errors between each point and the centre, divided by 2σ². Σ means “add up over every point i”.

Look at the second part: for any fixed σ, the best μ is the one with the smallest sum of squared errors, which is the mean x̄. That's the deep reason squared error (least squares) is the default loss in regression: minimizing squared error is maximum likelihood when the noise is bell-shaped. Gauss made exactly this connection in 1809.

🎮 Playground · Fit a bell curve by hand

The blue dots along the bottom are 12 heights. Drag the orange handle at the top of the curve: left/right moves the center μ, up makes it narrower (smaller σ), down makes it wider. The thin lines show each point's density; the log-likelihood adds up their logs. Try to beat the score, then press “Jump to the MLE”.

The winner is always μ̂ = the mean and σ̂ = the standard deviation (dividing by n). Maximum likelihood rediscovers the summaries of Lesson 5.1. A curve that's too narrow gets punished hard by any point in its tails; one that's too wide gets a low density everywhere.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
p = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 99)
loglik = 7 * np.log(p) + 3 * np.log(1 - p)   # log-likelihood of 7 heads, 3 tails
p[np.argmax(loglik)]                         # 0.7  (argmax = the best knob setting)

heights = np.array([168, 175, 181, 162, 170, 177])
mu, sigma = stats.norm.fit(heights)          # MLE: the mean and the std (divide by n)

🌍 Where it's used
LLM pretraining = maximum likelihood on next tokens 🧠 LLMs · 2018 Radford et al.: GPT (Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training)

How: the objective is the sum over every token of log P(token | the previous tokens; θ). The parameters θ are the network's weights. Gradient descent (Lesson 3.4) nudges θ to raise that sum.

Why this method: raw text is its own label: every next token is a free training example, so no human labeling is needed. And maximizing log-likelihood is the same as minimizing cross-entropy, a smooth loss that gradients handle well.

Reward models fit by Bradley–Terry likelihood 🎮 RL · 2022 InstructGPT (Ouyang et al.)

How: the model gives each answer a score r, and the chance a human prefers answer A over B is σ(rA − rB) (careful: this σ is the sigmoid from Lesson 3.2, which squashes any number into 0–1, not the bell curve's spread from above). The loss is −log σ(rchosen − rrejected): the negative log-likelihood of the human's actual choice.

Why this method: people agree more easily on “A is better than B” than on a 1–10 score. Maximum likelihood turns those comparisons into a score without anyone assigning numbers.

Logistic regression's “log loss” 🤖 Machine learning · 2011 scikit-learn LogisticRegression

How: the model predicts p̂ = P(y = 1 | x). The cost it minimizes is −y log p̂ − (1 − y) log(1 − p̂), summed over examples: the coin-flip negative log-likelihood, with a different p for every example (Lesson 8.1).

Why this method: squared error on probabilities gives weak gradients when the model is confidently wrong; the log loss punishes confident mistakes hard, and it comes straight from the probability model.

The method gets its modern form 📜 History · 1922 Aldrich: R.A. Fisher and the making of maximum likelihood 1912–1922

How: Gauss and Laplace used versions of the idea, but Ronald Fisher created the modern version of the method between 1912 and 1922, in a series of papers ending with his 1922 foundations paper.

Why this method: it gave one general recipe for estimating any model's parameters, which is still the default a century later, from biology to LLMs.

🤯 Fun fact · the vanishing probability

In standard 64-bit floating point, 0.5¹⁰⁷⁴ is the smallest positive number a computer can store, and 0.5¹⁰⁷⁵ rounds to exactly 0. So multiplying the probabilities of just 1,075 fair-coin flips gives a likelihood of zero for every setting, and the model can't tell good knobs from bad. A single sentence of text is a product over tokens, each often far below 0.5. Logs aren't optional.

🌉 AI bridge · maximum likelihood everywhere

LLMs: pretraining and supervised fine-tuning are both maximum likelihood on text. The per-token negative log-likelihood is called cross-entropy, and its exponential is perplexity (Lesson 6.3).

RL: reward models are maximum-likelihood fits to human comparisons: see RL Lesson 7.3: reward models & Bradley–Terry. DPO (RL Lesson 7.5) turns preference learning into a similar classification-style log-likelihood loss.

Next: maximum likelihood trusts the data completely: 3 heads in 3 flips gives p̂ = 1. Lesson 5.4 adds a prior to stay sensible with little data.

Check yourself

1. A coin lands heads 3 times out of 4. What is the maximum-likelihood estimate p̂?
p̂ = heads ÷ total = 3/4. Check: p³(1 − p) at 0.75 is about 0.105, higher than at 0.5 (0.0625) or at 1 (exactly 0, since a tail happened).
2. Why do we maximize the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood itself?
log is always increasing, so the argmax doesn't change. But a sum of logs stays a sensible number (like −6.11) where the product would be too small for the computer to store.
3. An LLM is pretrained to minimize cross-entropy on next tokens. In this lesson's language, what is it doing?
Cross-entropy on next tokens is the average negative log-likelihood of each true next token. Minimizing it = maximizing the likelihood of the training text. Squared error on token IDs would be meaningless: token 500 isn't “close to” token 501.
📝 TL;DR
  • Likelihood L(θ) = P(data | θ): the data is fixed, the knobs θ vary.
  • Maximum likelihood: θ̂ = argmax L(θ). 7 heads in 10 → p̂ = 0.7. For counts, MLE = the observed fraction.
  • Use the log-likelihood (a sum, same peak, no underflow). Negative log-likelihood = the loss you minimize.
  • LLM pretraining, logistic regression and reward models are all MLE. Least squares = MLE with bell-shaped noise.
