Clustering with k-means: finding groups

No labels, just points: k-means finds groups by repeating two tiny steps. It segments customers, picks image palettes, compresses the billions of vectors behind vector search, and builds the “codebooks” of VQ-VAE.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The distance between two points is the length of the arrow between them: d(a, b) = ‖a − b‖, e.g. from [1, 2] to [4, 6] it's 5 (Lesson 1.4).
  • The mean x̄ is the balance point: add up, divide by how many (Lesson 5.1).
  • A z-score (x − x̄) / s puts features measured in different units on the same scale (Lesson 5.1).
🎯 Hook

In 2017, Facebook's FAISS library searched 1 billion image-feature vectors on a single GPU, keeping each 128-number vector in just 8 bytes. Stored normally (4 bytes per number), each one would take 512 bytes: 64 times more.

The trick: chop each vector into 8 pieces and replace every piece by the ID of its nearest “typical piece” from a list of 256. That list of typical pieces was found by the algorithm in this lesson, k-means, which nobody had to label anything for.

Numbers first: six customers, two groups

A café knows the ages of six regulars: 18, 20, 22, 60, 64, 68. Nobody told us there are “students” and “retirees”. Can a computer find the two groups on its own? Guess two group centres, say c₁ = 18 and c₂ = 30, then repeat two steps:

stepwhat happensresult
1. Assigneach person joins the nearer centre. 22 is 4 from 18 and 8 from 30, so it joins c₁. 60 is far nearer 30 than 18.{18, 20, 22} and {60, 64, 68}
2. Updatemove each centre to the average of its groupc₁ = 60 ÷ 3 = 20, c₂ = 192 ÷ 3 = 64
1. Assignnobody switches groupsdone ✓

How good is the answer? Add up each person's squared distance to their own centre. With the first guess (18 and 30): 0 + 4 + 16 + 900 + 1156 + 1444 = 3520. After one update: (4 + 0 + 4) + (16 + 0 + 16) = 40. That total is the loss k-means is trying to make small.

J = Σi ‖ xi − ca(i) ‖² Read “J equals the sum over every point i of the squared distance from x-i to the centre of its own group”. a(i) (“a of i”) is the group point i is assigned to, so ca(i) is that group's centre. scikit-learn calls J the inertia.
x: the data pointsc: the k centres (the knobs k-means learns)a(i): which group point i is inJ: the loss

The two steps, written as formulas:

assign:  a(i) = argminj ‖ xi − cj ‖     update:  cj = mean of all xi with a(i) = j “argmin over j” means “the j that makes this smallest”: each point picks its nearest centre. Then each centre jumps to the average of the points that picked it. Repeat until nobody switches.
💡 Big idea k-means = repeat “assign each point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the average of its points”. It's unsupervised learning: there are no labels, no right answers to copy. The only goal is a small total squared distance, and each of the two steps can only make it smaller or keep it the same.
🧠 Analogy: ice-cream vans on a beach

Three ice-cream vans park at random on a long beach. Every sunbather walks to the nearest van (assign). At lunch, each driver looks at their own queue and re-parks in the middle of it, so their customers walk less (update). Next day the sunbathers pick the nearest van again… After a few days the vans stop moving, each sitting in the middle of its own crowd. That's k-means, with “walking distance squared” as the bill.

The 😈 bad start puts two centres in one corner and one in the middle. k-means dutifully splits the corner blob in two and lumps the other two blobs together, then stops: no single point wants to switch, yet the loss is several times worse than the best answer. That's a local optimum: a valley that isn't the deepest one, the same trap gradient descent can fall into (Lesson 3.4).

Why the loss can only go down

Assign step: a point moves to a different centre only if that centre is nearer, so its squared distance shrinks. Update step: the mean is the spot with the smallest total squared distance to a group. Check with the group {1, 2, 6} (mean 3):

at 2: 1 + 0 + 16 = 17  ·  at the mean 3: 4 + 1 + 9 = 14  ·  at 4: 9 + 4 + 4 = 17

So every half-step lowers J or leaves it alone, and since there are only finitely many ways to split the points, it must stop. But “stops” doesn't mean “best”.

Better starts: k-means++

In 2007 David Arthur and Sergei Vassilvitskii proposed a smarter way to pick the first centres. Choose the first one at random from the data. Then pick each next centre at random too, but with chance proportional to its squared distance to the nearest centre chosen so far. Far-away points are likely picks, so the centres start spread out. They proved that, on average, the resulting loss is at most a modest multiple of the best possible one (a multiple that grows only like log k), and it's the default in scikit-learn. The other cheap fix is to restart several times and keep the run with the lowest loss.

✏️ Worked example: a bad start in 2D, by hand

Points A(1,1), B(1,2), C(2,1) and D(8,8), E(8,9), F(9,8). Bad start: both centres in the first group, c₁ = (1,1), c₂ = (2,1).

  • Assign: A, B → c₁. C → c₂ (distance 0). D, E, F are nearer c₂ too. Loss = 0 + 1 + 0 + 85 + 100 + 98 = 284.
  • Update: c₁ = mean of A, B = (1, 1.5). c₂ = mean of C, D, E, F = (27/4, 26/4) = (6.75, 6.5). Loss = 72.25.
  • Assign: C is now 1.12 from c₁ but 7.27 from c₂, so it switches. Loss = 20.69.
  • Update: c₁ = (4/3, 4/3), c₂ = (25/3, 25/3). Loss = 2.67. Next assign: nobody moves. Done.

284 → 72.25 → 20.69 → 2.67: down every half-step. Here the bad start recovered, because the far group dragged c₂ away. With three blobs (the playground) it can stay stuck.

How many groups? The elbow

You have to choose k yourself. You can't just pick the k with the lowest loss: more centres always fit better, and with one centre per point the loss is 0 (that's overfitting, Lesson 5.6). Instead, plot the best loss for k = 1, 2, 3, … and look for the elbow: the k after which adding a centre barely helps.

🎮 Playground · Find the elbow

For each k from 1 to 8 we run k-means++ 5 times and keep the best loss. Slide k to see that clustering and the size of each drop. Compare 4 clear blobs with one big blob (no real groups) and the ring.

With 4 blobs the loss falls off a cliff until k = 4 and then flattens: a sharp elbow. One big blob gives a smooth curve with no bend, which is a hint that there are no natural groups at all; k-means will still happily cut it into k slices. Real data is often like that, so people also use other scores (such as the silhouette score in scikit-learn) and, above all, whether the groups make sense.

Where k-means goes wrong

Groups must be roughly round blobs of similar size. Every point simply goes to its nearest centre, so the border between two groups is always the straight line halfway between their centres. Load Ring + core in the first playground with k = 2: instead of “ring” and “core”, k-means slices the picture into two halves, each with a piece of ring and a piece of core. With Uneven sizes, the edge of the big spread-out group gets handed to the small tight one. For rings, moons and snakes, people use methods that follow chains of nearby points instead (for example DBSCAN or spectral clustering).

Standardize first. Distances add up all the features, so a feature with big numbers bullies the rest. Three customers, as [age, income in $]:

A = [25, 50,000] · B = [65, 52,000] · C = [27, 70,000]
raw: d(A, B) = √(40² + 2,000²) ≈ 2,000   d(A, C) = √(2² + 20,000²) ≈ 20,000 → A is “closer” to the 65-year-old
z-scores (age s = 15, income s = 20,000): d(A, B) = √(2.67² + 0.1²) ≈ 2.67   d(A, C) = √(0.13² + 1²) ≈ 1.01 → A groups with C

Raw, the age column hardly counts: a $2,000 difference outweighs 40 years. After turning every column into z-scores (Lesson 5.1), both features get a fair vote.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X = np.array([[18], [20], [22], [60], [64], [68]])   # the café ages
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10).fit(X)       # k-means++ starts, best of 10 runs
km.cluster_centers_, km.inertia_                     # centres 20 and 64, loss J = 40.0

C = np.array([[18.], [30.]])                           # our first guess

# the whole algorithm by hand (works for any number of columns)
for _ in range(20):
    a = ((X[:, None] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(1)     # assign: nearest centre
    C = np.array([X[a == j].mean(0) for j in range(len(C))])  # update: average of its points

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)                          # standardize first!
[KMeans(k, n_init=5).fit(Xs).inertia_ for k in range(1, 9)]    # numbers for an elbow plot

🌍 Where it's used
k-means codebooks compress vectors for search 🏭 In production · 2017 FAISS: Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs · Jégou et al. 2011: product quantization

How: two k-means steps. A coarse k-means sorts all vectors into lists by nearest centre, so a query only scans a few lists. Then product quantization splits each vector into b chunks, runs k-means with 256 centres on each chunk position, and stores each chunk as the 1-byte ID of its nearest centre: a b-byte code per vector.

Why this method: a billion full vectors don't fit in GPU memory; a billion 8-byte codes do. On 1 billion SIFT vectors, FAISS reports 17.7 µs per query at 8 bytes per vector, 8.5× faster than the previous best GPU method at similar accuracy.

A learned codebook turns images into tokens 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al.)

How: an encoder turns an image into a grid of vectors; each is snapped to its nearest entry in a codebook of K = 512 vectors (the assign step). Codebook entries are pulled toward the vectors assigned to them; the paper's moving-average version is the k-means update, done batch by batch. A 128×128 colour image becomes a 32×32 grid of code IDs, about 42.6 times fewer bits.

Why this method: a grid of a few hundred discrete symbols is far easier for a second model to learn and generate than raw pixels, much like text is easier to model as tokens than as raw characters.

Clusters become the states of an RL problem 🎮 RL · 2018 The AI Clinician's code (Komorowski et al., Nature Medicine)

How: each 4-hour snapshot of an ICU patient's measurements is a point; k-means with k-means++ starts ('Start','plus', 32 restarts) groups them into 750 clusters, and each cluster becomes one state of the decision problem the RL agent learns on.

Why this method: classic table-based RL needs a finite list of states, but patients are described by many continuous measurements. Clustering turns “similar patients” into “the same state”, at the price of throwing away detail (a point the critics later raised).

k-means++ seeding, the default everywhere 🤖 Machine learning · 2007 Arthur & Vassilvitskii: k-means++ · scikit-learn KMeans

How: spread the starting centres out by picking each new one with chance ∝ squared distance to the nearest existing centre. scikit-learn's KMeans uses init='k-means++' by default and keeps the best of n_init runs by inertia.

Why this method: plain random starts can land in bad local optima like the 😈 button. The authors report runs typically about 2× faster than with random starts, and much lower loss on some datasets, for a few extra distance computations.

Lloyd's algorithm: fewer levels for a phone signal 📜 History · 1957 k-means history (Lloyd, Bell Labs)

How: digital telephony (pulse-code modulation) rounds every sample of a sound wave to one of a few allowed levels. Lloyd chose the levels by alternating “send each sample to its nearest level” and “move each level to the average of its samples”: our two steps, in 1D.

Why this method: it directly minimizes the squared rounding error for the signals you actually send, instead of spacing the levels evenly. Run the same idea on pixels' RGB colours with k = 16 and you get a 16-colour palette: image colour quantization, where a 2011 study found a well-implemented k-means beats many specialised methods.

Customer and market segmentation is the textbook data-science use (Google's clustering course lists it first): standardize columns like visits, spend and age, cluster, then give each group a name and a marketing plan.

🤯 Fun fact · invented again and again

Hugo Steinhaus had the idea in 1956. Stuart Lloyd wrote it up at Bell Labs in 1957, but his note wasn't published in a journal until 1982, 25 years later. Edward Forgy published essentially the same method in 1965, and James MacQueen gave it the name “k-means” in 1967. Today it's still called “Lloyd's algorithm”.

🌉 AI bridge · groups without labels

LLMs & speech: audio has no ready-made “words”, so HuBERT (2021) ran k-means with 100 clusters on sound features and used the cluster IDs as the hidden labels a BERT-style model learns to predict. Turning continuous vectors into a few discrete IDs is how images and audio get “tokens” too.

RL: the AI Clinician above is an offline RL system: it learns only from old hospital records. See why that's hard in RL Lesson 6.4: offline RL, which uses its 750 patient-state clusters as an example.

In this course: clustering embeddings (Lesson 1.5) finds topics; PCA (Lesson 2.6) is often run first to shrink the dimensions; the loss-keeps-falling trap is the training-error lesson of 5.6.

Check yourself

1. A cluster contains the points (1, 2), (3, 4) and (5, 0). Where does the update step move its centre?
Average each coordinate: x = (1 + 3 + 5) ÷ 3 = 3, y = (2 + 4 + 0) ÷ 3 = 2. (9, 6) is the sum before dividing. The mean is the point with the smallest total squared distance to the group.
2. Why can't you choose k by picking the value with the lowest loss?
More centres can only fit better, like a more flexible model always lowers training error (Lesson 5.6). Look for the elbow where extra centres stop paying off, and check whether the groups make sense.
3. Product quantization in FAISS stores, for each chunk of a vector, the ID of its nearest k-means centre. What does that buy?
256 centres per chunk means an ID fits in one byte. Each vector is replaced by its nearest “typical pieces”, so distances are approximations, but 8 bytes instead of 512 per vector is what makes billion-scale search fit in memory.
📝 TL;DR
  • Clustering is unsupervised: find groups with no labels. k-means repeats assign (nearest centre) and update (centre = mean of its points).
  • Loss J = Σ ‖xi − ca(i)‖² never goes up, but can get stuck in a local optimum: use k-means++ starts and several restarts.
  • Choose k with an elbow plot (the loss always falls with k), and standardize features first.
  • k-means expects round, similar-sized blobs; it fails on rings and moons.
  • Used in vector search (FAISS product quantization), VQ-VAE codebooks, HuBERT's speech targets, RL state discretization and colour palettes.
