Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Probability: counting chances

A number from 0 to 1 that says how often something happens. Every LLM token, every spam score and every random exploration step is one.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Fractions and percentages: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.
  • A power is repeated multiplication: 0.9³ = 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 (Lesson 0.3).
  • Π (capital pi) means “multiply these all together”, like a for-loop with a running product (Lesson 0.5).
🎯 Hook

How many people do you need in a room before it's more likely than not that two of them share a birthday? Most people guess around 180 (half of 365). The real answer is 23.

The same surprise bites AI agents. A model that gets each step right 99% of the time sounds great. Ask it to do 100 steps in a row and it finishes without a single mistake only about 37% of the time. Both puzzles use one tiny rule you'll learn below.

Numbers first: count the ways

Roll a normal die. There are 6 outcomes, all equally likely. How likely is a 6? One outcome out of six:

P(roll a 6) = 1 / 6 ≈ 0.167  (about 17%)

How likely is an even number? Three outcomes work (2, 4, 6), so it's 3 / 6 = 0.5. When every outcome is equally likely, probability is just counting:

P(A) = (number of outcomes where A happens) / (total number of outcomes) Read “P of A”: the probability of event A. Count the ways A can happen and divide by all the ways anything can happen.
P: a probability, always between 0 and 1A: an event, an outcome we care about

Probabilities live between 0 (never happens) and 1 (always happens). An LLM's “I'm 97% sure this email is spam” is just P(spam) = 0.97.

💡 Big idea A probability is a long-run frequency. P(heads) = 0.5 doesn't predict the next flip. It says: flip a thousand times and very close to half will be heads. One flip is luck; many flips reveal the probability.
🎮 Playground · Flip it a thousand times

Choose a coin or a die, then flip. Watch the running fraction of hits (purple line) wobble wildly at first, then settle onto the true probability (dashed line).

After 10 flips the fraction can easily be 0.3 or 0.7. After 1,000 it's almost always within a few hundredths of the truth. That's how probabilities are measured in AI: a model's accuracy, a click-through rate, a win rate are all “count the hits, divide by the tries” (Lesson 4.7 explains exactly how fast the wobble shrinks).

Three rules that do almost everything

1. “Not”. Something either happens or it doesn't, so the two chances add up to 1:

P(not A) = 1 − P(A) The chance it doesn't happen is one minus the chance it does. P(not a 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.

2. “And”, for independent events. Two events are independent when one tells you nothing about the other, like two separate dice. Then you multiply:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) Both happen: multiply the two chances. Two sixes in a row = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36 ≈ 0.028. (Only when they're independent! Lesson 4.5 handles the other case.)

3. “Or”, for outcomes that can't both happen. Rolling a 1 or a 2: they can't happen on the same roll, so add: 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6. When they can overlap, the easiest trick is to flip the question: “at least one” = 1 − “none at all”.

P(at least one 6 in two rolls) = 1 − P(no 6, no 6) = 1 − (5/6 × 5/6) = 1 − 25/36 = 11/36 ≈ 0.306
🧠 Analogy: a row of doors

“And” is walking through a row of doors that each might be locked. Every extra door is another chance to get stuck, so the chances multiply and shrink: 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.73. “At least one” is the opposite question: “did any door open?” With many doors, that becomes almost certain, because getting stuck at every door is so unlikely.

✏️ Worked example: the 100-step agent

An AI agent must do 100 steps in a row (click, type, read, …). Each step succeeds with probability 0.99, independently. What's the chance the whole task succeeds?

  • “All 100 succeed” is an and: 0.99 × 0.99 × … (100 times) = 0.99100.
  • 0.99100 ≈ 0.366. Only about 37%.
  • “At least one mistake” = 1 − 0.366 = 0.634.
  • With 50 steps: 0.9950 ≈ 0.605. Shorter chains, or more reliable steps, matter a lot.

In Π notation: P(all succeed) = Πi pi, read “multiply the success chance of every step”.

The birthday problem

Same trick. Flip the question: what's the chance that nobody shares a birthday? Person 1 can have any birthday (365/365). Person 2 must avoid that day (364/365). Person 3 must avoid two days (363/365), and so on. Multiply, then subtract from 1.

P(some shared birthday) = 1 − (365/365 × 364/365 × 363/365 × … ) “One minus the chance that everyone dodges everyone else.” With n people there are n factors, each a little smaller.

Why so few people? It's not about your birthday. With 23 people there are 23 × 22 / 2 = 253 pairs, and each pair is another little chance for a match. Many small chances add up fast, exactly like the agent's 100 steps.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
flips = np.random.rand(1000) < 0.5   # 1,000 fair coin flips (True = heads)
flips.mean()                            # ≈ 0.5: the long-run frequency

0.99 ** 100                             # 0.366: 100 independent steps all succeed
p_no_match = np.prod([(365 - i) / 365 for i in range(23)])
1 - p_no_match                          # 0.507: birthday problem with 23 people

# Dropout: keep each of 8 neurons with probability 0.5
keep = np.random.rand(8) < 0.5          # e.g. [T, F, F, T, T, F, T, F]
🌍 Where it's used
Next-token probabilities 🧠 LLMs · 2019 GPT-2's config: vocab_size 50,257

How: at every step GPT-2 outputs one probability for each of its 50,257 tokens. Each is between 0 and 1, and together they add up to 1. “mat” after “The cat sat on the” might get 0.3.

Why this method: a single “best word” would hide how unsure the model is. A full probability list lets you sample varied text (Lesson 4.6) and measure how surprised the model is by real text (Phase 6).

Probability as long-run frequency: calibration 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 Guo et al.: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

How: a classifier is calibrated if, of all the photos where it says “90% cat”, about 90% really are cats. That's the long-run-frequency meaning of probability, checked on real data. The paper found modern deep networks are poorly calibrated: their stated confidence doesn't match how often they're right.

Why this method: doctors, self-driving cars and spam filters act on the number, not just the label. A 0.97 that's really right only 70% of the time is dangerous.

Dropout: keep each neuron with probability p 🖼️ Deep learning · 2012 Hinton et al.: Improving neural networks by preventing co-adaptation

How: during training, each neuron is switched off at random, independently, on every example. The paper randomly omitted half of the hidden units. With 4 neurons kept at 0.5 each, all four survive only 0.5⁴ = 1/16 of the time, so the network sees a different “thinned” version almost every step.

Why this method: neurons can't rely on one specific partner always being there, so they learn features that are useful on their own. That greatly reduced overfitting in the paper. (Careful: in PyTorch, nn.Dropout(p) uses p = the chance of dropping.)

ε-greedy exploration 🎮 RL · 2013 Mnih et al.: Playing Atari with Deep RL (DQN)

How: with probability ε (epsilon) pick a random action, otherwise (probability 1 − ε) pick the best-looking one. DQN started at ε = 1 and lowered it to 0.1 over the first million frames. With 4 actions and ε = 0.1, the best action is chosen 0.9 + 0.1 × ¼ = 92.5% of the time.

Why this method: always picking the best-so-far can get stuck on a lucky early result. A small, fixed chance of trying something else keeps the agent learning, and it's one line of code.

🤯 Fun fact · probability started with gambling

Around 1654, a gambler, the Chevalier de Méré, asked Blaise Pascal how to fairly split the pot of a dice game that was stopped halfway. Pascal worked it out in letters with Pierre de Fermat. That correspondence is often called the birth of probability theory (the problem of points).

Check yourself

1. You flip a fair coin 3 times. What's the probability of 3 heads in a row?
Independent flips, “and” → multiply: 0.5³ = 1/8 = 0.125. Any answer above 1 can't be a probability, which is a quick sanity check.
2. A model says “P(spam) = 0.97”. What's P(not spam)?
Spam or not spam, nothing else: the two must add up to 1. So P(not spam) = 1 − 0.97 = 0.03.
3. Why does dropout switch neurons off at random (each with some probability) instead of always removing the same ones?
Removing a fixed set would just be a smaller network. Random, independent drops change the team every step, so every neuron has to be useful alongside many different partners. At test time all neurons are used.
📝 TL;DR
  • Probability = a number from 0 to 1 = long-run frequency. Equally likely outcomes: count and divide.
  • P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Independent “and”: multiply. Non-overlapping “or”: add.
  • “At least one” = 1 − “none”: that's why 23 people suffice for a shared birthday and why long agent chains fail.
  • In AI: next-token probabilities, calibrated confidence, dropout's random masks, ε-greedy exploration.
