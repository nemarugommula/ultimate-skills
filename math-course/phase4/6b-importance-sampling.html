Phase 4 · Lesson 6b

Importance sampling: reusing someone else's samples

You want an average under one distribution, but your samples came from another. Reweight each sample and the answer comes out right. It's how PPO and GRPO reuse old rollouts, how ad systems test a new policy without running it, and how movie renderers trace light.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Expected value 𝔼[X] = Σ x · P(x): the probability-weighted, long-run average. Variance measures the swing around it (Lesson 4.4).
  • Monte Carlo: estimate an expected value by averaging random samples (Lesson 4.6).
  • A policy is a probability distribution over actions; an LLM's is a distribution over the next token (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

RL fine-tuning of an LLM is expensive mostly because of generation: write a batch of answers, score them, update the model. PPO squeezes several updates out of each batch. But after the first update the model has changed, and the answers were written by the old one. Using them as if nothing happened would be wrong.

The fix is one fraction per token, new probability ÷ old probability, and then PPO clips that fraction to between 0.8 and 1.2. By the end of this lesson you'll know why the fraction works and why it needs a leash.

Numbers first: a die you didn't roll

A friend rolled a fair die 600 times and wrote down the results: about 100 of each face. You want to know the average roll of a loaded die that lands on 6 half the time (and on each of 1–5 with probability 0.1). You can't roll the loaded die. Can you still use your friend's list?

Under the loaded die, a 6 is 3× as likely (0.5 instead of 1/6) and each other face is 0.6× as likely (0.1 instead of 1/6). So count every 6 in the list three times, and every other roll as 0.6 of a roll:

( 100 × 0.6 × (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5)  +  100 × 3 × 6 ) / 600  =  (900 + 1800) / 600  =  4.5

The true average of the loaded die is 0.1 × (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) + 0.5 × 6 = 1.5 + 3 = 4.5. ✓ The plain average of the list is 3.5, the fair die's answer, which is the wrong question.

𝔼p[ f(X) ]  ≈  (1/n) Σi wi · f(xi),    with wi = p(xi) / q(xi) and xi ~ q “The average of f under p is about the average, over samples drawn from q, of f times the weight p over q.” 𝔼p means “expected value when X follows p”; xi ~ q means “sample i was drawn from q”; Σi adds over the n samples.
p: the distribution you care about (target, new policy)q: the one your samples came from (old policy, logging policy)w: the importance weight
Why it works the q's cancel, in three lines

Average of w · f under q = Σx q(x) · (p(x) / q(x)) · f(x) = Σx p(x) · f(x) = 𝔼p[f]. The q on top and the q underneath cancel.

Check with the die's 6: q = 1/6, weight 3, and 1/6 × 3 = 0.5 = p(6). ✓ So the estimate is right on average (unbiased). One catch: q must be above 0 wherever p is. You can't reweight a face that never appears in the list.

💡 Big idea A sample from q can stand in for p if you count it p(x)/q(x) times: how much more likely this sample is under the new rule than under the old one. Weight above 1: p wanted more of these than q gave you. Below 1: q over-produced them.
🧠 Analogy: fixing a lopsided survey

You survey a town by standing outside the university. Half the people you ask are students, but only 10% of the town is. So each student's answer counts 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 of a person and everyone else's counts 0.9 / 0.5 = 1.8. Pollsters do this every election (“survey weighting”). The danger is the same too: if you met only one retiree, that one person's answer carries a huge weight and can swing the whole poll.

✏️ Worked example: six rolls by hand

Weights: faces 1–5 get 0.6, face 6 gets 3. Estimate = (sum of weight × roll) / 6.

  • Rolls 4, 6, 2, 5, 1, 3 (one of each face): 0.6 × (4 + 2 + 5 + 1 + 3) + 3 × 6 = 9 + 18 = 27, and 27 / 6 = 4.5. Spot on.
  • Rolls 6, 6, 5, 6, 2, 1 (lucky sixes): 3 × 18 + 0.6 × 8 = 54 + 4.8 = 58.8, and 58.8 / 6 = 9.8. Way too high: three sixes, each counted 3 times.
  • Rolls 3, 1, 5, 2, 4, 2 (no sixes): 0.6 × 17 = 10.2, and 10.2 / 6 = 1.7. Way too low.

Right on average, wobbly in a small batch. One roll of the weighted estimate has a spread (standard deviation) of about 6.1. Rolling the loaded die itself would give a spread of only about 1.8. Reweighting costs you variance.

The catch: a few samples get huge weights

The weight p/q is big exactly where p likes something that q rarely produced. Those rare samples carry most of the answer. Here's a handy way to measure the damage, the effective sample size (ESS):

ESS = ( Σ wi )² / Σ wi² “The total weight, squared, divided by the sum of the squared weights.” With equal weights it equals n. If one sample hogs the weight, it drops toward 1: you have n samples, but they're worth about one.

Four samples with weights [1, 1, 1, 1]: ESS = 16 / 4 = 4. Four samples with weights [0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 3.7]: same total (4), but ESS = 16 / 13.72 ≈ 1.2. The further p and q are apart, the faster ESS collapses.

With p close to q, ESS stays near n and the estimate is good. Push p two or three widths away and a handful of samples in p's region get weights in the tens or hundreds: ESS drops to single digits and the estimate jumps around from batch to batch. A wider q that covers p's region keeps the weights tame. That's the whole art: pick (or keep) q close to p.

Old samples, new policy

In RL the two distributions are two versions of the same policy. An agent chose “left” and “right” 50/50 (the old policy, πold, read “pi old”). “Right” gives reward 1, “left” 0. After an update the new policy πnew picks right 80% of the time. How good is the new policy, judged from the old log: L, R, R, L?

  • Weights: right 0.8 / 0.5 = 1.6, left 0.2 / 0.5 = 0.4.
  • Estimate: (0.4 × 0 + 1.6 × 1 + 1.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 0) / 4 = 3.2 / 4 = 0.8, exactly the new policy's true average reward. The naive average of the log says 0.5.
ρ = πnew(a | s) / πold(a | s) “The ratio rho is the new policy's probability of the action that was taken, divided by the old policy's probability of it.” ρ = 1: nothing changed. ρ = 1.6: the new policy likes this action 60% more. The PPO paper writes it rt(θ); the RL course writes ρ so it doesn't clash with the reward r.

For an LLM, the action is a token and the probabilities come from the model's softmax. A whole answer's exact weight is the product of its per-token ratios, and products explode: 100 tokens at 1.05 each gives 1.05¹⁰⁰ ≈ 131, and at 0.95 each, 0.006. GRPO instead uses one ratio per token, each clipped on its own.

🎮 Playground · The policy changed: reuse the old batch

“The cat sat on the …”. The old policy wrote 40 next tokens (the chips). A poetry judge scored each token (moon scores best). Move the update size to shift the policy toward high-scoring tokens, and watch each chip's weight ρ = πnew/πold. Then turn on PPO-style clipping and compare the estimates. “Repeat 200 batches” shows the trade: clipping wobbles less but drifts away from the truth.

Small updates: every ρ stays near 1, the reweighted estimate is accurate, and clipping barely matters. Big updates: “moon” went from 5% to a large share, so its few old samples get weights of 5, 10 or more, and the estimate swings from batch to batch. That's why PPO stops trusting the old batch beyond about ±20% and goes back to collect fresh samples.

Why PPO clips the ratio

PPO's training objective multiplies each old sample's advantage (how much better than usual it did) by ρ, and clips ρ to [1 − ε, 1 + ε] with ε = 0.2. Once the update has made an action 20% more likely (when it was good) or 20% less likely (when it was bad), that sample stops pushing. In the playground above the clip is applied to the weights themselves, the way ad-system engineers cap weights (below). Both buy the same thing: less variance, at the price of a small bias, and a policy that's never judged from data it has moved too far away from.

This is also why off-policy RL (learning from data some other, older or different policy collected) is hard: the more the policies differ, the more a few samples dominate, and the less your batch is really worth.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, torch
rolls = np.random.randint(1, 7, size=100_000)      # samples from q: a fair die
w = np.where(rolls == 6, 0.5, 0.1) / (1/6)       # weights p/q: 3 for a six, 0.6 otherwise
np.mean(w * rolls)                                 # ≈ 4.5 = average of the loaded die
w.sum()**2 / (w**2).sum()                          # effective sample size (≈ 55,600 of 100,000 here)

# PPO / GRPO: one ratio per token, from log-probabilities (safer than dividing tiny numbers)
ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)             # ρ = π_new / π_old

🌍 Where it's used
PPO's probability ratio and clip 🎮 RL · 2017 Schulman et al.: Proximal Policy Optimization

How: the old policy collects a batch; PPO then runs several epochs of minibatch updates on it. Each sample enters the objective as rt(θ) × advantage, where rt(θ) = πθ(at|st) / πθ old(at|st) is exactly this lesson's weight, clipped to [1 − ε, 1 + ε] with ε = 0.2 in the paper.

Why this method: throwing the batch away after one step wastes expensive experience; reusing it unweighted is biased; reusing it with unlimited weights lets a few samples run wild. The clip “removes the incentive for moving rt outside” the safe range (the paper's words).

GRPO's per-token ratio 🧠 LLMs · 2024 DeepSeekMath (Shao et al.)

How: for each question, the old model writes a group of G answers. Every token of every answer gets its own ratio πθ(token | question, earlier tokens) / πθ old(same), clipped like PPO's and multiplied by that answer's group-relative advantage, then averaged over tokens and answers.

Why this method: one ratio per token keeps the weights near 1, where a product over hundreds of tokens would explode or vanish. The paper notes that with a single update on fresh samples πθ old = πθ: every ratio is 1 and the clip does nothing.

Off-policy evaluation of an ad system 🏭 In production · 2013 Bottou et al.: Counterfactual Reasoning and Learning Systems (JMLR)

How: Bing's ad placement system logged what it did, with a little randomness, plus the outcomes. To ask “what if we had used a different setting?”, each logged event is reweighted by (its probability under the new setting) ÷ (its probability under the logged one). Because rare events get huge weights, the authors clip the weights, using the fifth-largest observed weight as the cap.

Why this method: a live A/B test for every idea is slow and risky for revenue. Reweighting old logs answers many “what ifs” at once, as long as the old system sometimes did what the new one would do (q > 0 wherever p > 0).

Path tracing in film rendering 🏭 In production · 1997 Physically Based Rendering: Importance Sampling

How: a pixel's brightness is an average of the light arriving from every direction, estimated with random rays (Monte Carlo, Lesson 4.6). Renderers shoot more rays toward lamps and along shiny reflections, and divide each ray's light by the probability of having chosen it. Eric Veach's 1997 thesis showed how to combine several such strategies (multiple importance sampling) and won a 2014 Academy Sci-Tech award.

Why this method: uniformly random rays mostly hit dark walls and give noisy, speckled images. Sampling where the light is gives the same right answer with far less noise per ray.

🤯 Fun fact · a one-in-3.5-million event, measured with 10,000 samples

How likely is a standard bell-curve draw (Lesson 4.3) to land above 5? About 2.87 × 10⁻⁷, roughly 1 in 3.5 million. Sample the bell curve itself a million times and you expect 0.29 hits: usually zero, so the estimate is “0”. Instead, sample from a bell curve centered at 5 and reweight each draw by p/q. We tried it: 10,000 samples gave the answer to within 1%, and the typical error at that size is about 2.4%. That trick is how engineers estimate rare failures, from network outages to financial crashes.

Check yourself

1. The old policy sampled a token with probability 0.2. After an update, the new policy gives it 0.5. What importance weight does that old sample get?
ρ = new ÷ old = 0.5 / 0.2 = 2.5: the new policy produces this token 2.5× as often, so this old sample counts 2.5 times. 0.4 is the ratio upside down; 0.3 is the difference, not the ratio. (PPO with ε = 0.2 would clip it to 1.2.)
2. You reweight 100 old samples and find that one of them carries 90% of the total weight. What does that tell you?
The estimate is still right on average, but this batch's answer rests almost entirely on one sample. Fixes: collect fresh samples from a policy closer to p, or clip the weights (accepting a little bias), as PPO and the Bing ad paper do.
3. An ad system has logs from its current policy. Engineers want to know how a new policy would have performed, without deploying it. What makes importance sampling possible here?
Reweighting needs q > 0 wherever p > 0. If the old system never showed a certain ad, no weight can tell you how it would have done. That's why Bottou et al.'s system logged with deliberate randomness.
📝 TL;DR
  • To average under p with samples from q: weight each sample by w = p(x)/q(x), then average w · f. Right on average (needs q > 0 where p > 0).
  • The weight says how much more likely the sample is under the new rule than the old one.
  • When p and q differ a lot, a few samples get huge weights: variance explodes and ESS = (Σw)²/Σw² collapses.
  • PPO and GRPO reweight old rollouts by ρ = πnew/πold per token and clip it to [0.8, 1.2]; ad systems, offline RL and film renderers use the same trick.
