Phase 4 · Lesson 5

Joint probability & the chain rule

How to get the probability of several things at once. Multiplying “next word given the words so far” is exactly how GPT scores and writes a sentence.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • P(A | B), “A given B”: the chance of A once you know B happened (Lesson 4.2).
  • Independent events multiply: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) (Lesson 4.1).
  • Logs turn multiplying into adding: log(a × b) = log a + log b (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

An LLM never sees a whole sentence and decides “that's 0.0066 likely”. It only ever answers one small question: “given the words so far, what's the next word?” Yet from those little answers it can score any sentence, any essay, any program.

The glue is one line of probability, the chain rule. Claude Shannon used a crude version of it in 1948 to generate fake English by hand. GPT uses the full version.

  • The inside cells are joint probabilities: P(spam, free) = 20/100 = 0.20. The comma is read “and”: both at once.
  • The totals on the edges are marginal probabilities, found by adding a row or column: P(spam) = 30/100 = 0.30, P(free) = 25/100 = 0.25. (“Marginal” because they're written in the margin.)
  • Conditionals zoom into one column or row: P(spam | free) = 20/25 = 0.8.

The product rule, and when you can't just multiply

If spam and “free” were independent, P(spam, free) would be 0.30 × 0.25 = 0.075. The table says 0.20. They're not independent: knowing “free” is there changes the odds of spam from 0.30 to 0.8. The general rule always works:

P(A, B) = P(B) × P(A | B) “The chance of A and B equals the chance of B, times the chance of A given B.” Check: 0.25 × 0.8 = 0.20 ✓. Independence is the special case where P(A | B) = P(A).

The chain rule: many things at once

Apply the product rule again and again and you get the chain rule of probability. For three words:

P(a, b, c) = P(a) × P(b | a) × P(c | a, b) “The chance of a, then b, then c is: the chance of a, times the chance of b given a, times the chance of c given both a and b.” Each new word is conditioned on everything before it.

For a sentence of n tokens, with Π meaning “multiply over every position t”:

P(w₁, …, wn) = Πt P(wt | w₁, …, wt−1) “Multiply, word by word, the probability of each word given all the words before it.” This is exactly what a GPT-style model computes. It's called autoregressive: each output becomes part of the input for the next step.

Real LLMs condition on the whole history. A simpler model, a bigram model, cheats: it only looks at the one previous word, P(wt | wt−1). That shortcut is called the Markov assumption: the present is enough, the deeper past is ignored.

💡 Big idea The chain rule turns one impossible question (“how likely is this entire sentence?”) into many easy ones (“how likely is the next word?”). Learn to answer the easy one well and you can score, and generate, anything.
🎮 Playground · A tiny bigram language model

A hand-made model with 15 words. At each step it shows P(next | previous word). Click a word to choose it yourself, or let the model sample. Watch the running product shrink, and its log (natural log, ln) add up.

Try “the cat sat on the robot beeped”. The model happily writes it, because after “the”, “robot” is fine, and after “robot”, “beeped” is fine. A bigram model can't remember that the sentence already had a subject. That's why real LLMs condition on thousands of previous tokens, not one.

🧠 Analogy: a choose-your-own-adventure book

Every page offers a few choices, each with a probability. The chance of one complete story is the chance of the first choice, times the chance of the second choice given you made the first, times the third given the first two… Long stories get tiny probabilities just because they have many forks, not because they're bad.

✏️ Worked example: scoring “the cat sat on the mat .”

From the playground's model: P(the | start) = 0.6, P(cat | the) = 0.35, P(sat | cat) = 0.5, P(on | sat) = 0.7, P(the | on) = 0.6, P(mat | the) = 0.15, P(. | mat) = 1.

  • Product: 0.6 × 0.35 × 0.5 × 0.7 × 0.6 × 0.15 × 1 = 0.0066.
  • Logs: ln 0.6 + ln 0.35 + … = −0.511 − 1.050 − 0.693 − 0.357 − 0.511 − 1.897 + 0 = −5.02, and e−5.02 ≈ 0.0066 ✓.
  • Why logs? A 100-token answer where every token has probability 0.5 has probability 0.5100 ≈ 8 × 10−31, dangerously close to what a computer rounds to zero. Its log is just −69.3. LLMs always work with log-probabilities (Lesson 8.5).
🐍 In code 
import math, random
P = {"<s>": {"the": 0.6, "a": 0.4},           # P(next | previous): a bigram table
     "the": {"cat": 0.35, "dog": 0.3, "robot": 0.2, "mat": 0.15}}  # ...

def log_prob(words):                       # chain rule, in log space
    prev, total = "<s>", 0.0
    for w in words:
        total += math.log(P[prev][w]); prev = w   # add ln P(w | prev)
    return total

# Generate: sample a word, feed it back in (autoregressive)
nxt = random.choices(list(P["the"]), weights=P["the"].values())[0]

🌍 Where it's used
Autoregressive LLMs: the chain rule at scale 🧠 LLMs · 2020 Brown et al.: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)

How: GPT-3 is described as “an autoregressive language model with 175 billion parameters”. It outputs P(next token | all previous tokens); generating text means sampling a token, appending it, and asking again. Training maximizes the sum of log-probabilities over real text.

Why this method: the chain rule is exact, not an approximation, so modeling the “next token” distribution well is enough to model whole documents. And every position of every document is a free training example.

Learning the joint probability of word sequences 🖼️ Deep learning · 2003 Bengio et al.: A Neural Probabilistic Language Model

How: the abstract opens with the goal: “to learn the joint probability function of sequences of words”. Their network predicts the next word from the previous few, using learned word vectors (embeddings, Lesson 1.5) instead of a lookup table of counts.

Why this method: a count table needs to have seen each exact word pair; with word vectors, a sentence about a “dog” helps with a similar sentence about a “cat”. That's the ancestor of every modern LLM.

n-gram approximations of English 📜 History · 1948 Shannon: A Mathematical Theory of Communication

How: Shannon generated text by choosing each word with the right probability given only the previous word (his “second-order word approximation”), a bigram model like the playground's. He noted the text looks more like English at each step up in order.

Why this method: he wanted to measure how predictable (how compressible) English is. That question became information theory (Phase 6), and n-gram models later became a standard tool in speech recognition and machine translation.

The Markov assumption 📜 History · 1913 Wikipedia: Markov chain

How: Andrey Markov studied the sequence of vowels and consonants in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, assuming each letter depends only on the one before. RL makes the same assumption about states: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not the whole history.

Why this method: conditioning on everything quickly becomes impossible to store or learn. Conditioning on just the present keeps the chain rule's terms small enough to estimate, which is what makes most RL algorithms possible.

🤯 Fun fact · a bigram from 1948

Shannon's hand-made bigram sentence begins: “THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER”. Every pair of neighbouring words makes sense; the whole thing doesn't. It's the same failure as “the cat sat on the robot beeped”, and the reason modern models look so much further back.

🌉 AI bridge · from one word to whole worlds

LLMs: the chain rule is how an LLM is trained (maximize Σ log P(next | context)) and how it generates (sample, append, repeat). Lesson 4.6 covers the “sample” part; Lesson 6.3 turns the log-probabilities into the cross-entropy loss.

RL: an episode's probability is also a chain: P(s₁) · π(a₁ | s₁) · P(s₂ | s₁, a₁) · … and the Markov assumption keeps each term short. See RL Lesson 2.1: the Markov property.

Naive Bayes (Lesson 4.2) is the opposite extreme: it assumes every word is independent given the class, so the chain collapses to a plain product.

Check yourself

1. A model gives P(I) = 0.2, P(love | I) = 0.1, P(math | I love) = 0.05. What's P(“I love math”)?
Chain rule: 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.05 = 0.001. Adding (0.35) isn't how “and” works; and the last term alone (0.05) ignores the first two words.
2. In the 100-email table, P(spam) = 0.3, P(free) = 0.25 and P(spam, free) = 0.2. Are spam and “free” independent?
Independent means the joint equals the product of the marginals. 0.2 ≠ 0.075, so “free” really does change the odds of spam (from 0.3 to 0.8).
3. Why do LLM libraries report log-probabilities instead of plain probabilities for a whole response?
The chain rule multiplies one probability per token. 0.5¹⁰⁰ ≈ 8 × 10⁻³¹, but 100 × ln 0.5 is just −69.3. Logs turn the long product into a sum.
📝 TL;DR
  • Joint P(A, B) = both at once. Marginals = row/column totals. Conditionals = zoom into a row or column.
  • Product rule: P(A, B) = P(B) · P(A | B). Independent only if P(A | B) = P(A).
  • Chain rule: P(w₁…wₙ) = Π P(wₜ | everything before). That's an autoregressive LLM.
  • Markov/bigram shortcut: condition on just the last item. Use log-probabilities to keep long products from vanishing.
