Law of large numbers & the bell curve (CLT)

Why averages of noisy things settle down, exactly how fast (like 1/√n), and why they always end up bell-shaped. It's the math behind batch sizes, error bars on benchmarks, A/B tests and polls.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • 𝔼[X] is the long-run average; σ (sigma), the standard deviation, is the size of a typical swing around it. One die: 𝔼 = 3.5, σ ≈ 1.71 (Lesson 4.4).
  • The Gaussian N(μ, σ²) is the bell curve, with 95% of draws within 2σ of the center (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2017, researchers (Henderson et al.) trained the same RL algorithm 10 times, changing only the random seed. They split the runs into two groups of 5 and averaged each group. The two averages looked like two different algorithms, and a standard test called them statistically different.

How many runs (or test questions, or users, or examples in a batch) do you need before an average can be trusted? This lesson gives you the answer, and it's one of the most useful formulas in all of data science: noise shrinks like 1 / √n.

Numbers first: average more dice

Roll one die: you get anything from 1 to 6, typically about 1.71 away from 3.5. Now roll 4 dice and take their average. To get an average of 6 you'd need four sixes in a row (1 in 1,296). Extreme values cancel out, so the average stays much closer to 3.5.

Two things to notice. The average gets closer and closer to 3.5: that's the law of large numbers. And the closeness improves with the square root of n: 4 times more dice only halves the miss, 100 times more makes it 10 times smaller.

x̄n → 𝔼[X]     and     SE = σ / √n x̄ is read “x-bar”: the average of n samples. “As n grows, the sample average heads to the expected value.” SE, the standard error, is read “the typical miss of an average of n”: sigma (the spread of one sample) divided by the square root of n.
💡 Big idea One sample is luck. An average of n samples is luck divided by √n. To get 10× more precise, you need 100× more data. That single fact decides batch sizes, how many seeds to run, and how big a test set must be.

The surprise: averages are always bell-shaped

Here's the deeper fact. Add up (or average) many independent random things, and the result follows a bell curve, almost no matter what the individual things looked like. Flat dice, lopsided dice, coin flips, click/no-click: average enough of them and you get a Gaussian. This is the central limit theorem (CLT).

x̄n ≈ N(μ, σ² / n)   for large n “The average of n samples is approximately normally distributed, centered on the true mean mu, with variance sigma squared over n.” (≈ is read “is approximately”.) So the 68–95–99.7 rule from Lesson 4.3 applies to averages.

The cleanest way to see it is a Galton board: a ball falls through rows of pegs and at each peg bounces left or right with probability ½. Its final position is the sum of many little random ±1 bounces.

🎮 Playground · The Galton board

Each ball makes one 50/50 left-or-right choice per row; its bin is the number of “rights”. Drop balls and watch a bell curve build itself. The dashed outline is what probability predicts. More rows = more random steps added up.

No ball “knows” about bell curves. Each just flips coins. The shape appears because there are many more paths to the middle bins than to the edges: with 10 rows there's 1 path to the far-left bin, but 252 paths to the middle one.

🎮 Playground · Averages of n dice

Each sample is the average of n dice; we draw 3,000 such averages and plot their histogram. Start at n = 1 (flat for a fair die, lopsided for the weird one), then slide n up. The purple curve is the CLT's prediction N(μ, σ²/n).

🧠 Analogy: asking a crowd

Ask one person to guess a jar's number of sweets and they may be wildly off. Ask 100 people and average: the individual over- and under-guesses largely cancel out. (If everyone shares the same bias, though, averaging can't remove it: the law of large numbers kills noise, not bias.) And if you asked 100 people again and again, those averages would themselves wobble in a neat bell shape around the truth.

✏️ Worked example: error bars for a benchmark and a poll
  • An LLM gets 80% of 500 test questions right. Each question is a Bernoulli (right/wrong) with σ = √(0.8 × 0.2) = 0.4. SE = 0.4 / √500 ≈ 0.018, about 1.8 points.
  • 95% of a bell curve lies within about 2 standard errors (1.96, precisely), so the score is 80% ± 3.5 points. A rival model at 82% isn't clearly better.
  • A poll asks 1,000 people a yes/no question with a roughly 50/50 split: σ = 0.5, SE = 0.5 / √1000 ≈ 0.016, so ± 1.96 × 0.016 ≈ ± 3.1%. That's the familiar “margin of error ±3%”.
  • To cut the poll's margin to ±1.5%, you'd need 4 times as many people: 4,000.
🐍 In code 
import numpy as np
for n in [1, 4, 25, 100]:
    avgs = np.random.randint(1, 7, size=(10_000, n)).mean(axis=1)   # 10,000 averages of n dice
    print(n, avgs.mean().round(2), avgs.std().round(2))      # ≈ 3.5, and ≈ 1.71/√n

# Error bar on a benchmark score
correct = np.random.rand(500) < 0.8                # 500 right/wrong results
se = correct.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(correct))      # standard error ≈ 0.018
print(f"{correct.mean():.3f} ± {1.96 * se:.3f}")        # e.g. 0.796 ± 0.035

🌍 Where it's used
Mini-batch gradient noise shrinks with batch size 🖼️ Deep learning · 2018 McCandlish et al.: An Empirical Model of Large-Batch Training

How: a batch gradient is the average of B per-example gradients, so its variance scales like 1/B (its noise like 1/√B). The paper measures a “gradient noise scale” and finds it predicts the largest useful batch size across ImageNet, Atari, Dota and more.

Why this method: beyond some batch size, the gradient is already accurate enough; more examples per step just cost compute. The 1/√B law tells you where that point is.

The “± 3%” margin of error in polls 📊 Data science · classic Wikipedia: Margin of error

How: each answer is a yes/no draw, and the poll's result is the average of n of them. The CLT makes that average bell-shaped, so the 95% margin is 1.96 × SE. With 1,000 people and a 50/50 split: 1.96 × 0.5 / √1000 ≈ ± 3.1%, the number Wikipedia gives.

Why this method: asking everyone is impossible. The √n law says 1,000 people already pin the answer to about ± 3%, and halving that takes 4,000, so pollsters know exactly what precision costs.

The Galton board 📜 History · 1889 Wikipedia: Galton board

How: Francis Galton built the board of pegs to demonstrate that many small random deflections add up to a normal (bell) distribution, and wrote about this “order in apparent chaos” in his 1889 book Natural Inheritance.

Why this method: it made the central limit theorem visible without any formulas: the playground above is a digital copy.

🤯 Fun fact · why the √?

When you add n independent random things, their variances add up: n × σ². The standard deviation is the square root, so the sum's spread is σ√n. Divide by n to get the average: σ√n / n = σ / √n. That's the whole reason behind the square root (and why game B in Lesson 4.4 had a spread of 9.95 × √n).

🌉 AI bridge · the √n law everywhere

Deep learning: batch size, learning rate and gradient noise are tied together by σ/√B. Lesson 7.2 (SGD and mini-batches) builds on this directly.

RL: mean, standard deviation and standard error of returns across episodes and seeds are the bread and butter of RL Lesson 0.5: one run lies, mean, spread & error bars.

Data science: A/B tests compare two averages, each with its own ± standard error. Lesson 5.5 turns that into confidence intervals and p-values.

Check yourself

1. A mini-batch of 64 examples gives a gradient estimate with a certain noise level. How big a batch do you need to cut that noise in half?
Noise ∝ 1/√B. Halving it needs √B to double, so B must grow 4×: 64 × 4 = 256. (4,096 = 64² would cut the noise by 8×.)
2. What does the central limit theorem say?
A single die is flat, not bell-shaped. But averages of many dice are bell-shaped around 3.5 with spread σ/√n. That's why error bars use the Gaussian's “± 2 standard errors”.
3. Model A scores 81.0% and model B 80.2% on a 300-question benchmark. Why might an evaluator refuse to call A better?
Each score has a ± of about 2 × 2.3 ≈ 4.5 points at 95%. A 0.8-point gap is well inside the noise. More questions (or comparing both models question by question, Lesson 5.5) shrink it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Law of large numbers: the average of n samples heads to the expected value.
  • Its typical error, the standard error, is σ/√n: 4× the data halves the noise; 100× makes it 10× smaller.
  • CLT: sums and averages of many independent things are ≈ bell-shaped, N(μ, σ²/n), so “± 2 standard errors” ≈ 95%.
  • Used for batch sizes (gradient noise), benchmark error bars, enough RL seeds, A/B tests and polls.
