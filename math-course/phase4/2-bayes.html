Conditional probability & Bayes' rule

How a probability should change when you learn something new. It powers spam filters, explains why most alarms are false, and drives Bayesian exploration in RL.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A probability is a number from 0 to 1, a long-run fraction: “count the hits, divide by the total” (Lesson 4.1).
  • Independent “and” = multiply: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) (Lesson 4.1).
🎯 Hook

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A test catches 90% of sick people and wrongly flags 9% of healthy ones. You test positive. What's the chance you're actually sick?

Most people guess around 90%. The real answer is about 9%. The same trap fools fraud detectors, security scanners and anomaly alerts. One picture makes it obvious.

Numbers first: 1,000 people

Forget percentages. Imagine 1,000 people:

  • 1 in 100 is sick → 10 sick, 990 healthy.
  • The test catches 90% of the sick → 9 sick people test positive.
  • It wrongly flags 9% of the healthy → 9% × 990 ≈ 89 healthy people test positive.

So 9 + 89 = 98 people test positive, and only 9 of them are sick:

P(sick | positive) = 9 / 98 ≈ 0.09

The vertical bar | is read “given”. P(sick | positive) means “the probability of being sick, given that you tested positive”. It's a conditional probability: we zoom in on just the people who tested positive and ask what fraction of them are sick.

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) “P of A given B”: keep only the cases where B happened, and ask how many of those also have A. Here: (sick and positive) / (all positives) = 9 / 98.
A: what we want to know (sick? spam?)B: the evidence we saw (positive test, the word FREE)
💡 Big idea P(positive | sick) and P(sick | positive) are completely different numbers (0.9 vs 0.09). Flipping them is the most common probability mistake. When the thing you're looking for is rare, most alarms are false, because the healthy crowd is so much bigger.

Bayes' rule: flipping the condition

The test maker tells you P(positive | sick). You want P(sick | positive). Bayes' rule converts one into the other. It's exactly what we did with the 1,000 people, just written with probabilities instead of counts:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B) “The new belief in A after seeing B equals: how well A explains B, times how likely A was before, divided by how likely B is overall.”

The pieces have names you'll meet again in Lesson 5.4: P(A) is the prior (belief before the evidence), P(B | A) the likelihood (how expected the evidence is if A is true), and P(A | B) the posterior (belief after).

✏️ Worked example: the hook, with Bayes' rule
  • Prior: P(sick) = 0.01. Likelihood: P(+ | sick) = 0.9.
  • P(+) counts both ways to test positive: 0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0891 = 0.0981.
  • Posterior: P(sick | +) = 0.9 × 0.01 / 0.0981 = 0.009 / 0.0981 ≈ 0.092.

Same 9% as the counting. A second positive test (independent) would start from prior 0.092 instead of 0.01 and jump to about 0.5. Evidence stacks up.

🧠 Analogy: the smoke alarm

Your kitchen smoke alarm goes off. Is the house on fire? The alarm almost always rings when there's a fire (high likelihood). But burnt toast happens hundreds of times more often than fires (low prior for fire). So when it rings, it's almost always toast. Bayes' rule is the bookkeeping that weighs “how well does fire explain the beep?” against “how rare are fires in the first place?”.

A tiny spam filter: Bayes, one word at a time

In 2002, Paul Graham described a spam filter built on exactly this rule. For every word, count how often it appears in spam and in normal mail (“ham”). Then, for a new email, ask: P(spam | these words)?

The “naive” trick is to treat the words as independent given the class, so their likelihoods just multiply (Lesson 4.1's “and” rule). That's not really true (“free” and “money” like to appear together) but it works surprisingly well.

P(spam | words) ∝ P(spam) × Πw P(w | spam) ∝ is read “is proportional to”: compute this score for spam and for ham, then divide each by their sum so they add up to 1. Π means multiply over every word w in the email.
🎮 Playground · Build an email, watch the filter think

Click words to put them in the email. Each word has (made-up) counts: how often it shows up in spam vs normal mail. The filter multiplies everything and tells you P(spam | email).

Notice how one strong word like “winner” (30× more common in spam) can outweigh a mild hint the other way. Each word multiplies the odds by its own ratio, which is why the order of words doesn't matter.

🐍 In code 
prior, sens, fpr = 0.01, 0.90, 0.09
p_pos = sens * prior + fpr * (1 - prior)       # P(+) = 0.0981, both ways to test positive
sens * prior / p_pos                         # P(sick | +) ≈ 0.092 (Bayes' rule)

# Naive Bayes spam score: multiply word likelihoods (made-up numbers)
p_w_spam = {"free": 0.30, "meeting": 0.01}
p_w_ham  = {"free": 0.02, "meeting": 0.10}
s, h = 0.5, 0.5                                # priors
for w in ["free"]: s *= p_w_spam[w]; h *= p_w_ham[w]
s / (s + h)                                  # ≈ 0.94  → probably spam
# Real libraries: sklearn.naive_bayes.MultinomialNB

🌍 Where it's used
Naive Bayes spam filtering 🏭 In production · 2002 Paul Graham: A Plan for Spam

How: every word gets a spam probability from counts in your own spam and ham folders, and an email's words are combined with Bayes' rule into one P(spam | email). Graham reported missing fewer than 5 spams per 1,000, with 0 false positives, on his own mail.

Why this method: he first spent months hand-writing rules (“contains click”). Counting words learns the rules automatically, adapts to each person's mail, and is cheap enough to run on every message. Many filters, like SpamAssassin and SpamBayes, use Bayesian techniques.

The base-rate problem in alerts 📊 Data science · classic Wikipedia: Base rate fallacy

How: Wikipedia's example: a city of 1,000,000 with 100 suspects and a scanner that's right 99% of the time. It raises about 10,098 alarms, and only about 99 are real: under 1%. Fraud detectors and anomaly alerts face the same arithmetic, which is why teams report precision, P(real | alert), not just accuracy. Lesson 5.6b builds precision, recall and F1 from a confusion matrix.

Why this method: a model that “catches 99%” sounds great, but with a tiny base rate the false alarms swamp the analysts. Bayes tells you, before deploying, how many alerts will be real.

Bayesian search for lost ships and planes 📜 History · 1968 Wikipedia: Bayesian search theory

How: split the ocean into squares and give each a prior chance of holding the wreck. Search a square and find nothing? Bayes' rule lowers that square's chance and raises all the others. Say a square had a 50% prior and the search spots the wreck 80% of the time when it's there: after an empty search it drops to 0.1 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 17%. The method helped find the submarine USS Scorpion (1968) and Air France 447's flight recorders (2011).

Why this method: an empty search is still evidence. Instead of sweeping evenly, Bayes says where to look next, which is why the US Coast Guard's search-planning software uses it.

🤯 Fun fact · the word “naive”

Naive Bayes is called naive because it assumes every word is independent of every other word once you know the class. That's obviously false for language. Yet it was one of the first methods to make spam filtering work at scale. A model can be useful even when its assumptions are wrong, as long as the decision (spam or not) comes out right.

Check yourself

1. 1 in 100 transactions is fraud. A detector flags every fraud (100%) and 10% of normal transactions. A transaction is flagged. About how likely is it to be fraud?
Out of 1,000: 10 frauds, all flagged. 990 normal, 10% flagged = 99. So 10 / (10 + 99) ≈ 0.092. The rare thing is swamped by false alarms from the huge normal crowd.
2. How do you read P(spam | “FREE”)?
The thing after the bar is what you already know. Option 1 is the flipped one, P(“FREE” | spam): the likelihood that Bayes' rule turns into what you want.
3. Why does a naive Bayes filter multiply the word likelihoods together?
That's the “naive” independence assumption. It's wrong for real language but makes the model tiny (one number per word per class) and surprisingly effective.
📝 TL;DR
  • P(A | B), “A given B”: zoom in on the cases where B happened; what fraction also have A?
  • Bayes' rule: P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B). Posterior = likelihood × prior, rescaled.
  • P(B | A) ≠ P(A | B). With a rare base rate, most positives are false. Think in 1,000 people.
  • Used in naive-Bayes spam filters, alert precision, Thompson sampling, and every LLM's P(next | context).
