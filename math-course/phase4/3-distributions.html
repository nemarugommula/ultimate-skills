Random variables & distributions

A distribution is the full list of what could happen and how likely each is. Neural networks start from one, diffusion models are built on one, and every LLM outputs one per token.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A probability is a number from 0 to 1; the chances of all possible outcomes add up to 1 (Lesson 4.1).
  • An integral is the area under a curve, a sum of many thin slices (Lesson 3.6).
🎯 Hook

Before training starts, every one of a neural network's millions of weights is set to a random number. Not just any random number: a draw from a bell curve with a very carefully chosen width. Make it a bit too wide or too narrow and a deep network may not train at all.

A diffusion image generator, like Stable Diffusion, starts each picture from pure random noise drawn from that same bell curve. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to draw from it yourself, and you'll know what “the width” means.

Numbers first: a random variable is a number decided by chance

Roll a die and call the result X. Before you roll, X could be 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, each with probability 1/6. That's a random variable: a number whose value is decided by chance. The list of its possible values and their probabilities is its distribution.

P(X = 1) = P(X = 2) = … = P(X = 6) = 1/6    and   6 × 1/6 = 1

We write X ~ (some distribution). The squiggle ~ is read “is drawn from” or “is distributed as”. Here are the four distributions you'll meet constantly in AI:

🪙 Bernoulli(p)

Yes/no. 1 with probability p, 0 otherwise. A coin, a click, a dropout mask (Lesson 4.1).

🎲 Categorical(p₁…pK)

One of K options, each with its own probability. A die, a class label, the next token.

📏 Uniform(a, b)

Any number between a and b, all equally likely. random() is Uniform(0, 1).

🔔 Gaussian N(μ, σ²)

The bell curve: values cluster around a center μ with spread σ. Weights, noise, measurement errors.

💡 Big idea A distribution is the full menu of what could happen, with a probability next to each item. AI models rarely give one answer: they give a distribution (a categorical over words, a Gaussian over numbers) and then pick from it.

The categorical: an LLM's output

After “The cat sat on the”, a language model outputs a categorical distribution over its whole vocabulary: maybe mat 0.40, sofa 0.25, floor 0.20, roof 0.10, moon 0.05. Every number is between 0 and 1, and they add up to 1. Writing a word means sampling: picking one at random with those chances.

🎮 Playground · Edit a next-token distribution, then sample

“The cat sat on the ___”. Drag the raw scores. They're divided by their total so the probabilities always add up to 1. Then sample and compare the observed frequencies (dashed markers) with the probabilities (bars).

Push one score far up and the model becomes almost certain. Make them all equal and it's a fair 5-sided die. A probability of 0.05 for “moon” means: in about 1 of 20 runs, the cat sits on the moon.

The Gaussian: the bell curve

Heights of adults, errors of a measuring device, the sum of many small random effects (Lesson 4.7 explains why): they all pile up into the same bell shape. It has two knobs:

  • μ (“mu”, the mean): where the center is.
  • σ (“sigma”, the standard deviation): how wide it is. σ² (“sigma squared”) is called the variance (Lesson 4.4). Careful: σ here means standard deviation, not the sigmoid squash from Lesson 3.2. Same Greek letter, different job.
X ~ N(μ, σ²) “X is drawn from a normal (Gaussian) distribution with mean mu and variance sigma squared.” N(0, 1), centered at 0 with width 1, is called the standard normal.

A handy rule of thumb: about 68% of draws land within 1 σ of the center, 95% within 2 σ and 99.7% within 3 σ. For N(0, 1): 68% of draws are between −1 and 1.

Mass vs density: why the peak can be above 1

A die has a probability mass on each value: P(X = 3) = 1/6. You can read it straight off the bar.

A Gaussian is continuous: X could be 0.5, 0.5001, 0.50000017… The chance of hitting any exact number is 0. So the curve shows a density: probability per unit of width. To get a real probability, take the area under the curve between two points (an integral, Lesson 3.6). The total area is always 1.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under the density curve from a to b “The probability that X lands between a and b is the area under the curve over that stretch.” Height alone isn't a probability; height × width is.
🧠 Analogy: sand on a ruler

Pour 1 kg of sand along a 1-meter ruler. A mass distribution is like 6 cups at fixed spots, each holding some sand. A density is a smooth pile: you can't ask how much sand is at exactly 0.5 m (zero!), only how much lies between 0.4 and 0.6 m. If you squeeze all the sand into 10 cm, the pile gets taller (over 1 kg per meter) but it's still 1 kg in total. That's why a narrow Gaussian's peak goes above 1.

✏️ Worked example: initializing a layer

A layer has 512 inputs. The He initialization rule (2015) draws each weight from a Gaussian with μ = 0 and σ = √(2 / number of inputs).

  • σ = √(2 / 512) = √0.0039 = 0.0625.
  • So about 68% of weights land between −0.0625 and +0.0625, and 95% between −0.125 and +0.125.
  • The peak height is 1 / (σ √(2π)) = 1 / (0.0625 × 2.507) ≈ 6.4. A density, not a probability: well above 1, and perfectly fine.

Why not just use σ = 1? Each neuron adds up 512 weighted inputs, and wide weights make those sums explode as they pass through layer after layer. The √(2 / inputs) width keeps the signal at a steady size.

🐍 In code 
import numpy as np, torch
np.random.rand()                          # Uniform(0, 1)
np.random.rand() < 0.3                    # Bernoulli(0.3): True 30% of the time
words = ["mat", "sofa", "floor", "roof", "moon"]
np.random.choice(words, p=[0.40, 0.25, 0.20, 0.10, 0.05])  # categorical
x = np.random.normal(loc=0, scale=1, size=10_000)   # N(0, 1): loc = μ, scale = σ
np.mean(np.abs(x) < 1)                     # ≈ 0.68

w = torch.empty(256, 512)
torch.nn.init.kaiming_normal_(w)           # He init: σ = √(2/512) = 0.0625
w.std()                                    # ≈ 0.0625

🌍 Where it's used
Gaussian weight initialization 🖼️ Deep learning · 2015 He et al.: Delving Deep into Rectifiers

How: every weight starts as a draw from N(0, σ²) with σ = √(2 / inputs). Glorot & Bengio's earlier “Xavier” rule (2010) uses σ = √(2 / (inputs + outputs)). PyTorch has both built in (kaiming_normal_, xavier_normal_).

Why this method: all-zero weights would make every neuron identical forever, so you need randomness. The width is tuned so signals neither blow up nor fade away as they pass through many layers; the He paper says its rule let them train extremely deep networks from scratch.

Gaussian noise in diffusion models 🖼️ Deep learning · 2020 Ho et al.: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)

How: training takes a real image and adds a little Gaussian noise, step by step, over T = 1,000 steps, until it's pure static. A network learns to undo one step. To generate, start from a fresh draw of N(0, 1) noise for every pixel and denoise 1,000 times.

Why this method: Gaussian noise is easy to sample, easy to add up (Gaussian + Gaussian is still Gaussian), and the starting point needs no data at all: every new image is just a new random draw. Lesson 8.4b builds a toy diffusion model step by step.

Gaussian policies for continuous actions 🎮 RL · 2018 Haarnoja et al.: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)

How: a robot arm's torque isn't one of a few buttons, it's any number. So the policy network outputs a μ and a σ, and the action is a draw from that Gaussian (SAC then squashes it with tanh to keep it in range).

Why this method: a categorical can't list infinitely many torques. A Gaussian needs just two numbers per action, and σ directly controls how much the robot explores.

Categorical next-token outputs 🧠 LLMs · 2019 Jay Alammar: The Illustrated GPT-2

How: the model's last layer gives one score per vocabulary token (50,257 for GPT-2), softmax (a recipe that turns any list of scores into positive numbers adding up to 1, Lesson 4.6) turns them into a categorical distribution, and the next token is sampled from it, exactly like the playground above.

Why this method: words are separate options, not points on a number line, so a categorical is the natural fit; sampling from it (rather than always picking the top word) gives varied, natural text (Lesson 4.6).

🤯 Fun fact · a “probability” of 6.4

The He-initialized layer above has a density peak of about 6.4. Densities can be any positive height, as long as the total area is 1. Only areas are probabilities. Squeeze σ down to 0.001 and the peak is about 399!

Check yourself

1. Weights are drawn from N(0, σ²) with σ = 0.1. About what fraction of the weights end up between −0.2 and +0.2?
0.2 is 2 σ from the center (2 × 0.1). The 68–95–99.7 rule says about 95% of draws land within 2 σ.
2. A Gaussian density curve has a peak height of 4. What does that mean?
Height is probability per unit width. A narrow curve (σ ≈ 0.1 gives a peak of about 4) must be tall so its total area stays 1.
3. Why do RL algorithms like SAC use a Gaussian policy for robot control, instead of a categorical one?
A categorical lists separate options (tokens, buttons). For “any torque between −2 and 2”, a Gaussian's μ says where to aim and σ says how much to explore.
📝 TL;DR
  • A random variable is a number decided by chance; its distribution lists outcomes and their probabilities. X ~ D: “X is drawn from D”.
  • Bernoulli (yes/no), categorical (one of K: the next token), uniform (all equal), Gaussian N(μ, σ²) (bell curve: center μ, width σ).
  • 68–95–99.7: share of Gaussian draws within 1, 2, 3 σ.
  • Discrete: probability mass per value. Continuous: density; probability = area, so peaks can exceed 1.
  • Used for weight init (He/Xavier), diffusion noise, Gaussian RL policies, LLM token outputs.
