Phase 4 · Recap

Recap: probability

Seven lessons, one idea: AI systems don't output answers, they output distributions, and then they count, multiply, average and sample.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🎲

4.1 Probability

P(not A) = 1 − P(A) · indep. “and” = ×

A number from 0 to 1 = long-run frequency. “At least one” = 1 − “none”.

Next-token probabilities, dropout, ε-greedy, calibration.

🔍

4.2 Bayes' rule

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B)

Update a belief with evidence. P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A); rare things make most alarms false.

Naive-Bayes spam filters, alert precision, Thompson sampling.

🔔

4.3 Distributions

X ~ N(μ, σ²) · Categorical(p)

Bernoulli, categorical, uniform, Gaussian. Mass vs density: only areas are probabilities.

Weight init, diffusion noise, Gaussian RL policies, LLM outputs.

⚖️

4.4 Expectation & variance

𝔼[X] = Σ x·P(x) · Var = 𝔼[(X − μ)²]

The probability-weighted average, and the typical swing around it.

Expected loss, expected return, baselines, batch noise.

⛓️

4.5 Chain rule

P(w₁…wₙ) = Π P(wₜ | w₁…wₜ₋₁)

Many easy “next” questions make one sentence probability. Use logs.

Autoregressive LLMs, n-grams, the Markov assumption.

🌡️

4.6 Sampling

pᵢ ∝ ezᵢ/T · top-k · top-p

Roll a loaded die with u ~ Uniform(0, 1). Temperature reshapes; top-k/p cut the tail. Monte Carlo = average random samples.

LLM decoding, π from darts, MC in RL.

🎯

4.6b Importance sampling

𝔼p[f] ≈ (1/n) Σ p(x)/q(x) · f(x), x ~ q

Reuse samples from q by weighting each by p/q. Right on average, but far-apart p and q give huge weights (tiny effective sample size).

PPO/GRPO ratio + clip, off-policy evaluation of ads, path tracing.

📏

4.7 LLN & CLT

SE = σ / √n · x̄ ≈ N(μ, σ²/n)

Averages settle down; noise shrinks like 1/√n; averages are bell-shaped.

Batch sizes, eval error bars, RL seeds, polls, A/B tests.

Explain to a friend, in 2 minutes and without symbols, how a chatbot writes a reply: it gives every possible next word a probability (4.3), reshapes those with temperature and top-p and rolls the dice (4.6), appends the word and repeats, so the whole reply's probability is a product of “next given so far” (4.5). Then explain why a benchmark score of 80% on 500 questions deserves a “± 3.5” (4.7). If you can do both, you own this phase.

🧩 Use it yourself

🧩 Problem: is the fraud model worth it?

A bank processes 10,000 card transactions a day. 0.5% are fraud. A new model flags 90% of frauds and wrongly flags 2% of normal transactions. Every flag is checked by a person at $5 each; every fraud that slips through costs $200.

  1. How many alerts per day, and what fraction are real fraud, P(fraud | alert)?
  2. What's the expected daily cost with the model, versus checking nothing?
  3. The number of frauds per day itself varies. Roughly how much (one standard deviation)?
Solution Worked out with this phase's math

1. Bayes with 10,000 people (4.2). Frauds: 0.005 × 10,000 = 50. Caught: 0.9 × 50 = 45. Normal: 9,950, false alarms 0.02 × 9,950 = 199. Alerts = 45 + 199 = 244. P(fraud | alert) = 45 / 244 ≈ 0.18. Fewer than 1 in 5 alerts is real: the base rate bites.

2. Expected value (4.4). With the model: 244 checks × $5 = $1,220, plus 5 missed frauds × $200 = $1,000, total $2,220 a day. Without it: 50 × $200 = $10,000. Even with 82% false alarms, the model saves about $7,800 a day on average.

3. Spread (4.4, 4.7). Each transaction is a Bernoulli(0.005), so the daily count has variance 10,000 × 0.005 × 0.995 ≈ 49.75 and σ ≈ 7. Most days see roughly 50 ± 14 frauds (±2σ), so judge the model over weeks, not on one day.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. An agent's task has 3 independent steps that succeed with probabilities 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5. What's the chance the whole task succeeds?
All three must succeed: independent “and” → multiply. 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.36 (Lesson 4.1).
2. A model says P(next = “Paris”) = 0.5, then P(“is” | “Paris”) = 0.4, then P(“lovely” | “Paris is”) = 0.25. What's P(“Paris is lovely”)?
Chain rule (Lesson 4.5): 0.5 × 0.4 × 0.25 = 0.05. Adding gives more than 1, which can't be a probability.
3. You want an LLM's answers to be as repeatable as possible for a coding tool. Which setting moves you toward that?
Low temperature sharpens the distribution until the top token always wins: greedy decoding (Lesson 4.6). High temperature or a wide top-k/top-p adds variety.
4. Why are a new network's weights drawn from N(0, σ²) with σ = √(2 / inputs), instead of being set to 0?
Identical (zero) weights would get identical updates forever. The He rule's width keeps each layer's outputs at a steady size (Lesson 4.3).
5. Your RL algorithm's average return over 4 seeds has a standard error of 20. How many seeds would bring it to about 10?
Standard error = σ/√n (Lesson 4.7). Halving it needs √n to double, so n × 4 = 16 seeds.
