Phase 4 · Lesson 6

Sampling, temperature & Monte Carlo

How a computer “rolls” any distribution, how temperature, top-k and top-p reshape an LLM's choices, and how random samples can compute things that are hard to calculate.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A categorical distribution: one probability per option, all adding to 1, like an LLM's next-token list (Lesson 4.3).
  • ez (exp) turns any number into a positive one, and bigger z gives much bigger ez (Lesson 0.4).
  • 𝔼 = the long-run average, which you can estimate by averaging samples (Lesson 4.4).
🎯 Hook

Ask a chatbot the same question twice and you often get two different answers. It's not broken. On purpose, it rolls dice at every token.

And when the API offers knobs called temperature, top_k and top_p, they change exactly how those dice are loaded. Turn temperature to 0 and the answers become (almost) repeatable; turn it up to 2 and the model starts writing nonsense. This lesson shows you why, with a story-writing machine you control.

Numbers first: rolling a loaded die with one random number

Computers have one basic random tool: a number u drawn uniformly between 0 and 1. How do you turn that into “mat 0.5, sofa 0.3, moon 0.2”?

Lay the probabilities end to end on a line from 0 to 1: mat takes 0 to 0.5, sofa 0.5 to 0.8, moon 0.8 to 1. Throw u at the line and pick whichever piece it lands in. If u = 0.62, it lands in sofa. Because each piece's width is its probability, each word gets picked exactly as often as it should.

u = 0.62  →  0.5 ≤ 0.62 < 0.8  →  “sofa”

Temperature: reshaping the distribution

An LLM doesn't directly output probabilities. It outputs a raw score per token, called a logit (written z). To get probabilities it uses softmax: take ez of every score (now all positive) and divide by the total (now they add to 1). Temperature T divides the scores first. Try it on scores z = [2, 1, 0] for “cat”, “dog”, “eel”:

T = 1:   e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1  → total 11.11  → [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]
T = 2:   scores ÷ 2 = [1, 0.5, 0]  → e¹ = 2.72, e0.5 = 1.65, e⁰ = 1  → total 5.37  → [0.506, 0.307, 0.186]. Flatter.

The same recipe, for any list of scores:

pi = ezi / T / Σj ezj / T “The probability of token i is e to the power (its score divided by T), divided by the same thing added up over all tokens j.” T = 1 is the model's own distribution.
  • T < 1 stretches the gaps between scores: the favourite gets even more likely. As T → 0 it always picks the top token (“greedy”).
  • T > 1 squashes the gaps: unlikely tokens get a bigger share and the text gets more surprising, then weird.
✏️ Worked example: a cold temperature, then top-p

Same scores z = [2, 1, 0], now with T = 0.5 (colder than 1):

  • Scores ÷ 0.5 = [4, 2, 0]. e⁴ = 54.6, e² = 7.39, e⁰ = 1. Total 62.99.
  • Probabilities [0.867, 0.117, 0.016]. “cat” dominates (it had 0.665 at T = 1).

Top-p with p = 0.9 on [0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05]: keep the most likely tokens until their total reaches 0.9: 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8 (not yet), + 0.15 = 0.95 ✓. Drop the last one and rescale: [0.5, 0.3, 0.15] / 0.95 = [0.526, 0.316, 0.158].

Top-k and top-p: cutting off the weird tail

Even a great model gives tiny probabilities to thousands of strange tokens. Each one is unlikely, but together they add up, and one bad pick can derail a whole paragraph. Two popular fixes:

  • Top-k: keep only the k most likely tokens, rescale them to add up to 1, sample from those.
  • Top-p (“nucleus sampling”): keep the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p (say 0.9). When the model is confident, that's 1 or 2 tokens; when it's unsure, it's many.
💡 Big idea Decoding is a choice after training. The same model can be boring (greedy), creative (T > 1), or creative-but-sane (top-p), just by changing how you sample from its distribution.

    🧠 Analogy: the restaurant menu

    Temperature is your mood: at T = 0.2 you order your usual every time; at T = 2 you might order the pickled jellyfish. Top-k is “I only consider the 3 dishes the waiter recommends”. Top-p is “I consider dishes until I've covered 90% of what people here usually order”: at a pizza place that's 2 dishes, at a tapas bar it's 15.

    Monte Carlo: compute with randomness

    Sampling isn't only for writing. It can measure things. Throw random darts at a 1 × 1 square. The quarter circle inside it has area π/4 ≈ 0.785, so about 78.5% of darts land inside. Count them and you can estimate π:

    π ≈ 4 × (darts inside the quarter circle) / (all darts) “Pi is about four times the fraction of darts that land inside.” An expected value (Lesson 4.4), estimated by averaging random samples: that's a Monte Carlo method.
    🎮 Playground · Estimate π with darts

    Throw darts uniformly at the square. Purple = inside the quarter circle (x² + y² < 1). Watch the estimate wobble, then close in on π (dashed line).

    Notice the error shrinks slowly: 100 times more darts gives only about 10 times more accuracy. The law of large numbers (Lesson 4.7) promises the average settles down; the “error ∝ 1/√n” rate (∝ means “in proportion to”) is the standard error, which comes with the central limit theorem in the same lesson. Monte Carlo is slow but almost magical in its generality: if you can simulate something, you can estimate its average. If your samples came from the wrong distribution, Lesson 4.6b shows how to reweight them, the trick PPO uses to reuse old rollouts.

    🐍 In code 
    import numpy as np
def sample(logits, T=1.0, top_p=1.0):
    p = np.exp((logits - logits.max()) / T); p /= p.sum()    # softmax with temperature
    order = np.argsort(-p); cum = np.cumsum(p[order])
    keep = order[: np.searchsorted(cum, top_p) + 1]       # smallest set reaching top_p
    q = np.zeros_like(p); q[keep] = p[keep]; q /= q.sum()    # cut the tail, rescale
    return np.random.choice(len(q), p=q)                     # roll the loaded die

xy = np.random.rand(100_000, 2)                                # Monte Carlo darts
4 * np.mean((xy**2).sum(1) < 1)                               # ≈ 3.14

    🌍 Where it's used
    Nucleus (top-p) sampling 🧠 LLMs · 2019 Holtzman et al.: The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration

    How: at each step, keep the top tokens until their probabilities reach p, drop the “unreliable tail”, rescale and sample. The paper found the nucleus usually ranges from one to about a thousand tokens, growing and shrinking with the model's confidence.

    Why this method: the paper showed maximizing probability (e.g. beam search) gives bland, repetitive text, while pure sampling wanders into gibberish via the tail. A fixed top-k can't adapt to how spread out the distribution is; top-p can.

    Decoding knobs in every LLM library 🏭 In production · 2020 Hugging Face: How to generate text

    How: generate() takes temperature, top_k (described by Fan et al., 2018) and top_p; chat APIs expose the same names. Low temperature for code and facts, higher for brainstorming and fiction.

    Why this method: retraining a model to be “more creative” is expensive; reshaping its output distribution costs nothing and can be changed per request.

    Temperature for calibration 🖼️ Deep learning · 2017 Guo et al.: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

    How: the same T knob, used for honesty instead of creativity. Divide an overconfident classifier's logits by one learned T > 1, so “99% sure” becomes, say, “90% sure” when it's really right 90% of the time.

    Why this method: the paper found this single-parameter fix “surprisingly effective” on most datasets, and it never changes which class is ranked first, so accuracy stays exactly the same.

    The birth of Monte Carlo 📜 History · 1946 Wikipedia: Monte Carlo method

    How: Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from an illness in 1946, wondered about the odds of winning a game of solitaire and realized it was easier to play many games and count. He and John von Neumann used the idea to simulate neutrons at Los Alamos; their colleague Nicholas Metropolis suggested the name, after the Monte Carlo casino.

    Why this method: the exact math was hopeless, but each random trial was easy to simulate. That trade (simple samples instead of impossible formulas) is still the deal today.

    Monte Carlo value estimates in RL 🎮 RL · 2018 Sutton & Barto, chapter 5

    How: to learn how good a state is, play complete episodes from it and average the returns you actually got. No model of the world is needed, only the ability to play.

    Why this method: the expected return is an average over countless possible futures; sampling a few hundred of them is the simplest unbiased estimate.

    🤯 Fun fact · π by dropping needles

    Long before computers, the Comte de Buffon posed a famous question in the 18th century: drop a needle on a floor of parallel wooden strips; how likely is it to cross a line? The answer involves π, so dropping many needles and counting gives a (slow) way to estimate π: a Monte Carlo method nearly two centuries before the name existed (Buffon's needle).

    🌉 AI bridge · sampling everywhere

    LLMs: every generated token is a draw from a temperature-adjusted, top-p-trimmed categorical. Methods like best-of-n and GRPO sample several answers per prompt on purpose and compare them, so randomness becomes a source of better answers and training signal (RL course Phases 7–8).

    Check yourself

    1. After temperature, the probabilities are [0.6, 0.25, 0.1, 0.05]. With top-p = 0.8, which tokens can be sampled?
    Add the top probabilities until you reach p: 0.6 is not yet 0.8; 0.6 + 0.25 = 0.85 is. So keep two tokens, rescaled to 0.6/0.85 ≈ 0.71 and 0.25/0.85 ≈ 0.29.
    2. You raise the temperature from 1 to 2. What happens to the next-token distribution?
    Dividing the logits by 2 shrinks the gaps between them, so after softmax the probabilities are more equal. With [2, 1, 0], the top token drops from 0.665 to 0.506.
    3. Why use Monte Carlo (averaging random simulations) to estimate an RL state's value, instead of computing it exactly?
    Monte Carlo trades exact formulas for simple samples. It's unbiased and model-free, at the cost of noise that shrinks like 1/√n.
    📝 TL;DR
    • To sample a categorical: lay probabilities on [0, 1], draw u ~ Uniform(0, 1), pick the piece it lands in.
    • Temperature divides logits before softmax: T < 1 sharper (T → 0 greedy), T > 1 flatter.
    • Top-k keeps the k best tokens; top-p keeps the smallest set reaching total p. Both cut the weird tail.
    • Monte Carlo: estimate an expectation by averaging random samples (π from darts, values in RL). Error ∝ 1/√n.
