Phase 0 · Lesson 3

The autonomy stack, module by module

Open the hood of a robotaxi and the loop from Lesson 0.1 becomes a team of programs, each with one job, passing data along at its own pace. Learn what each module eats and produces, and you can read any company's architecture diagram, and guess where a bug lives just from what the car did.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The loop: sense → perceive → localize → predict → plan → control (Lesson 0.1).
  • Latency and loop rate: old information and gaps between looks both turn into meters driven blind (Lesson 0.1).
  • Fallback: at Level 4 the system itself must handle failures, so it has to notice them (Lesson 0.2).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A pickup truck is being towed backwards, hanging at an angle across two lanes. A robotaxi behind it sees the pickup clearly, knows exactly where it is on the road, and still drives into it. A few minutes later, a second robotaxi hits the same pickup.

Which part of the software failed? The cameras saw it. The car knew where it was. So what went wrong? Guess before you read on.

This happened to two Waymo vehicles in Phoenix on December 11, 2023 (no injuries, minor damage, no riders aboard). Waymo's explanation: because the pickup and the tow truck pointed in persistently different directions, the car “incorrectly predicted the future motion of the towed vehicle”. Waymo updated its whole fleet between December 20, 2023 and January 12, 2024, and filed a voluntary recall (Waymo, Feb 2024). One module, one rare situation, one software fix. That's the stack at work.

🧩 Predict: in the towed-pickup crash, perception saw the truck and localization knew where the car was. Which module got it wrong, and what did it hand to the planner?
Prediction. It took a correct object (a pickup, here, this way round) and produced a wrong future: it expected the pickup to move the way a pickup pointing that way normally drives, not the way a towed one swings. The planner trusted that forecast and planned a path that wasn't actually clear. Every module downstream did its job on bad input: that's why a bug's symptom often shows up two modules away from its cause.

The modules, and what flows between them

Here is a typical modular driving stack, with the data each module consumes and produces. Rates and latencies are typical orders of magnitude (they vary a lot between companies, and most don't publish theirs).

ModuleConsumesProduces (data type)Typical rate · latency
SensorsLight, laser pulses, radio echoes, motionImages (cameras); point clouds (lidar: a 3D dot per laser hit); radar returns with speed; IMU accelerations and rotation rates; GNSS (satellite) positions; wheel speedsCameras ~10–30 Hz; spinning lidar ~10 Hz; IMU 100+ Hz
PerceptionImages, points, radarA list of objects: class, 3D box, position, velocity, plus lanes, signs and free space~10–30 Hz · tens of ms
MapSurveyed in advance (or built on the fly)Lanes, crosswalks, stop lines, speed limits, landmarks for matchingStatic; loaded by area
LocalizationLidar/camera features, IMU, wheels, GNSS, mapThe car's pose: x, y, heading (and speed), with uncertaintyOften 50–100+ Hz · a few ms
PredictionTracked objects, map, own poseFor each agent, a few possible future paths over the next several seconds, each with a probability~10 Hz · tens of ms
PlanningPredictions, map, route, own stateA trajectory: where to be, and how fast, over the next few seconds~10 Hz · tens of ms
ControlTrajectory, current pose and speedCommands: steering angle, throttle, brake~50–100 Hz · ~1 ms

Notice the pattern: each step makes the data smaller and more meaningful. Millions of pixels and points per second become a few dozen objects, then a handful of possible futures, then one trajectory, then three numbers for the pedals and wheel. Each arrow is an interface: a written-down contract (“a list of boxes with these fields, in this coordinate frame, stamped with this time”).

Numbers first: a latency budget

✏️ Worked example: how old is the world when the brakes move?

Take one camera frame through the stack, with illustrative processing times:

StepProcessing timeRunning total
Camera: expose, read out, send30 ms30 ms
Perception: find the objects60 ms90 ms
Prediction: forecast their paths20 ms110 ms
Planning: choose a trajectory50 ms160 ms

That's 160 ms of pure work. But if each module runs on its own 10 Hz timer, a fresh result can wait up to 100 ms for the next module to wake up, at every handoff. Three handoffs can add up to 300 ms of waiting. At 20 m/s (72 km/h), 160 ms is 3.2 m of driving; 460 ms is 9.2 m. So waiting can easily cost more than working. The fix isn't always faster code. Sometimes it's starting each module the moment its input arrives. Try both in the next playground.

The lesson generalizes: in a chain of independent timers, every handoff adds up to one period of waiting, so the worst case is the sum of all the periods plus all the processing. Triggering on new data removes the waiting, and then the camera rate alone sets how fresh things are and how much compute you burn. Real stacks mix both: sensors drive perception, while planning and control often stay on steady clocks so that the car's behaviour is smooth and predictable. Timing like this is why every message carries a timestamp (Phase 2, time sync).

💡 Big idea An autonomy stack is a pipeline of modules joined by interfaces. Data gets smaller and more meaningful at each step (pixels → objects → futures → a trajectory → three commands). Every interface is a place you can log, test and debug, and also a place where errors pass downstream and time is lost waiting. A failure's symptom usually appears downstream of its cause.
🧠 Analogy: a restaurant kitchen

The waiter (sensors) takes the order; the prep cook (perception) turns raw ingredients into chopped, labelled bowls; the head chef reads the room and the order tickets (prediction, planning) and decides what goes out when; the line cook (control) does exactly what the chef says, fast. If the soup tastes wrong, you check each station's output in order, and the ticket rail between stations is where food waits. A brilliant chef can't fix mislabelled salt.

🤔 Why this? (and not one giant program)
  1. The problem before. Driving involves very different problems: recognizing objects in pixels, geometry and maps, guessing human intentions, choosing safe paths, and steering a two-tonne vehicle smoothly. One program doing all of it is impossible to reason about, test, or hand to a team.
  2. What people tried. Purely reactive rules (“if something is within 5 m, brake”): fast and simple, but they can't plan around a double-parked van or anticipate a cyclist. One big monolithic program: every change risks breaking something far away, and nobody can tell which part caused a failure.
  3. Why the stack works. Each module has one job, a written interface and its own rate. You can test perception alone on logged images, swap a planner without touching perception, and replay yesterday's sensor logs through today's code. Debugging becomes a search along the chain: with 7 modules and logged interfaces, you check 7 outputs in order and find the first one that's wrong, instead of searching millions of lines blind.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Interfaces throw information away: a box saying “pedestrian, 1 m/s” has lost the fact that they're looking at their phone. Errors pass downstream and add up (a missed detection can't be fixed by a perfect planner). Waiting at each handoff adds latency (Playground 2). And each module is tuned for its own score, not for driving well overall.
  5. What came next. Learned components inside each module (neural networks for perception and prediction), joint models that merge perception and prediction, and fully learned end-to-end driving where the interfaces themselves are learned. That's the great debate of Lesson 0.4.
🧩 Predict: a warehouse robot keeps stopping and replanning in one aisle, though nothing is there. Its lidar is fine. A colleague says “the planner is buggy”. What else should you check first?
Check the modules upstream of the planner, starting with what the planner was actually given. A localization error of 30 cm makes the shelves on the floor map seem to stick into the aisle. Or a stale map (shelves were moved) disagrees with what the lidar sees. The planner may be doing exactly the right thing with wrong input. Logged interfaces let you prove it either way.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In ROS 2, each module is a node and each interface is a topic with a message type. Three command-line tools answer most “is this module healthy?” questions:

ros2 topic list                          # every interface in the running stack
ros2 topic hz    /perception/objects     # how often a module actually publishes (its real rate)
ros2 topic delay /perception/objects     # now − header.stamp: how old each message is (latency)
ros2 topic bw    /lidar/points           # bandwidth: point clouds are big
ros2 bag record -a                       # log every interface, then replay it through new code

On a PX4 drone the modules talk over uORB, a publish/subscribe bus; the listener sensor_accel command prints a topic live from the MAVLink console. Full open-source driving stacks you can read: Autoware (sensing, map, localization, perception, planning, control, vehicle interface) and Baidu Apollo; for indoor robots, Nav2 (costmaps, planners, controllers and recovery behaviours, coordinated by behaviour trees).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

The classic modular shape, with heavy learning inside. Waymo's vehicles use lidar, cameras and radar, and Waymo maps each territory in detail before driving there, which is the map box in the table above. Its December 2025 “Waymo Foundation Model” post describes a large learned model whose sensor-fusion encoder combines camera, lidar and radar. The towed-pickup recall shows the benefit of modules: Waymo could name the module (prediction) and the situation, fix it, and update the fleet.

Sources: The Waymo Driver, Waymo, Dec 2025, Waymo recall, Feb 2024

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's public statements describe the opposite direction: its Q4 2023 update says FSD v12 “utilizes end-to-end training”, meaning much of perception, prediction and planning is learned as one network from video, rather than separate hand-built modules with fixed interfaces. Inputs are the car's cameras (8 exterior cameras on a 2025+ Model Y). Tesla hasn't published a module diagram of its current stack.

Sources: Tesla Q4 2023 update, Model Y manual

🛸 Drones

PX4 is openly modular: separate modules for sensors, the EKF2 state estimator, navigation and the cascaded controllers, all exchanging uORB messages that you can list and plot. An autonomous drone like Skydio's adds perception and planning on top: six navigation cameras build a 360° picture for flying around obstacles. Prediction of other agents is usually minimal, because trees don't move.

Sources: PX4 uORB, PX4 controllers, Skydio X10

Why they differ: the same modules exist in every machine, because the problems exist (finding things, knowing where you are, deciding, steering). The differences are which boxes are hand-designed with fixed interfaces and which are learned together, and what each machine can afford: Waymo spends on sensors, maps and validation per city; Tesla bets on learning from a huge fleet's video; drones keep a tiny, fast, verifiable core and add autonomy on top.

🎤 Interview angle

“Draw the architecture of an autonomous vehicle and tell me where this failure came from.” A strong answer:

  • Draws sensors → perception → localization (with map) → prediction → planning → control, and names the data type on each arrow.
  • Debugs by walking the interfaces upstream from the symptom, using logs and replays, rather than guessing at the module that showed the symptom.
  • Mentions timing: rates, timestamps, end-to-end latency, and the cost of waiting at each handoff.
  • Knows the modular stack's weakness (lossy interfaces, compounding errors) and where learned models fit in (Lesson 0.4).

How: two vehicles misjudged the future motion of a pickup being towed backwards at an angle; the fix was a software update to the whole fleet within about a month.

Why this method: with separate modules and logged interfaces, the cause could be pinned to one module and one rare situation.

Autoware's seven stacks 🧰 Open source Autoware architecture

How: sensing, map, localization, perception, planning, control and vehicle interface, each with its own requirements and replaceable components.

Why this method: universities and companies can replace one stack (say, localization) by wrapping their component to fit Autoware's interfaces.

Baidu Apollo, version by version 🧰 Open source ApolloAuto/apollo

How: its README walks from Apollo 1.0 (GPS waypoint following on a closed course) through releases that added lidar perception, urban driving, and new deep-learning models for perception and prediction.

Why this method: a modular stack can grow one module at a time, each release upgrading specific boxes.

PX4 on uORB 🧰 Open source PX4 uORB docs

How: PX4 modules run in parallel and exchange messages over uORB, a publish/subscribe middleware; every topic can be logged and inspected.

Why this method: for flight safety you want each module small, separately testable and observable in flight logs.

🤯 Fun fact · the same bug, twice, minutes apart

In the towed-pickup incident, the tow truck didn't stop after the first contact, so a few minutes later a second Waymo vehicle met the same oddly angled pickup and made the same prediction mistake (Waymo). It's a perfect illustration of software failure: a fleet doesn't make random mistakes like tired humans; it makes the same mistake in the same situation. The upside: one fix also fixes every car.

Check yourself

1. Perception takes 60 ms and planning 50 ms. Planning runs on its own 10 Hz timer. What's the worst-case time from perception starting on a frame to a plan that uses it being ready?
60 ms of perception, then up to a full 100 ms period waiting for the planner to wake up, then 50 ms of planning: 60 + 100 + 50 = 210 ms. Triggering the planner when perception finishes would cut it to 110 ms.
2. A robotaxi brakes hard for a plastic bag blowing across an empty road. Where's the most likely root cause?
The planner and controller did the right thing for an obstacle that was really there. The mistake is the label: harmless debris versus something you must not hit. Fixing it means better perception (and perhaps carrying the uncertainty forward so the planner can choose a gentler response).
3. Why does each interface in a modular stack shrink the data (pixels → objects → trajectories → commands)?
A planner can't reason over millions of pixels, but it can reason over 30 objects with velocities. That compression is what makes modules testable and fast, and it's also the information loss that end-to-end learning tries to avoid (Lesson 0.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Stack: sensors → perception (objects) + localization (pose, with a map) → prediction (futures) → planning (trajectory) → control (commands).
  • Data shrinks and gains meaning at every step; each arrow is an interface you can log, test and replay.
  • Bugs show up downstream of their cause (the towed pickup: a prediction error that looked like a planning mistake). Debug by walking upstream.
  • Latency = processing + waiting at each handoff; triggering on new data removes the waiting but costs compute.
  • Drones and warehouse robots share the skeleton: estimator, perception, planner, controller, just different sensors, maps and rates.
