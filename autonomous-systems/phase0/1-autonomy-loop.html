Phase 0 · Lesson 1

What makes a machine autonomous: the sense → act loop

A robotaxi, a camera drone that follows a mountain biker through trees, a warehouse robot carrying shelves: on the outside they look nothing alike. Inside, they all run the same short loop, many times a second. Learn the loop and you have the map for this whole course.

🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You are the computer driving a car at 60 km/h. A pedestrian steps onto a crossing whose stop line is 25 m ahead. The brakes are excellent. But you only look at the world twice a second, and every picture reaches you half a second late.

Do you stop before the line? If not, which would you fix first: looking more often, or seeing sooner? Hold your guess. You'll get to drive this exact car in Playground 2.

In March 2004, DARPA (the US military's research agency) ran a race for driverless vehicles across the Mojave Desert. Fifteen vehicles started. None finished: the best one managed about 7.5 miles. Eighteen months later, five vehicles completed a 132-mile course, and Stanford's “Stanley” won in 6 hours 53 minutes (DARPA). Today Waymo's robotaxis have driven 271.3 million miles with nobody in the driver's seat (through June 2026, Waymo). The gap between those two stories is mostly one idea, done really well: the loop.

🧩 Predict: at 60 km/h (about 16.7 m/s), how far does the car travel while one half-second-old picture is “in the post”, before the computer has even seen the pedestrian?
8.3 m: 16.7 m/s × 0.5 s. That's about two car lengths driven with your eyes shut, and the brakes haven't even started yet. Looking only twice a second adds up to another half second of waiting for the next look. Latency and loop rate are both distance, and you'll feel it in Playground 2.

What “autonomous” actually means

A machine is autonomous when it decides what to do next from what it senses, without a person choosing each move. A dishwasher running a fixed timer isn't autonomous: it never checks whether the plates are clean. A robot vacuum that bumps into a chair, notices, and turns away is (a little).

The difference is a closed loop: measure the world, decide, act, then measure again to see what your action did. Engineers usually split the deciding into six jobs. Every lesson of this course zooms into one of them:

StepQuestion it answersOutput (example)Course phase
1 · SenseWhat are my sensors reading right now?Camera pixels, lidar distances, wheel speedPhases 1–2
2 · PerceiveWhat things are out there?“Pedestrian, 25 m ahead, walking left”Phases 4, 6
3 · LocalizeWhere am I, and which way am I facing?“Lane 2, 140 m past the junction, heading 92°”Phases 3, 5
4 · PredictWhat will everyone else do next?“The pedestrian reaches my lane in 1.8 s”Phase 7
5 · PlanWhat should I do about it?“Slow down and stop 0.5 m before the line”Phase 7
6 · ControlExactly how hard do I push the pedals and wheel?“Brake at 6 m/s², steering 0°”Phase 8

Then the car moves, the world changes, and the loop starts again. Lesson 0.3 opens each box properly; Lesson 0.4 asks whether it should be boxes at all.

Numbers first: why speed of thought is distance

✏️ Worked example: the hook, solved by hand

Stopping distance has two parts: the distance you cover before the brakes start (reacting), and the distance the brakes need (braking).

  1. Speed in m/s. 60 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 16.7 m/s.
  2. Reaction time. Each picture is 0.5 s old (the latency). The loop only looks twice a second (the loop rate, 2 Hz, so 0.5 s between looks), and here the first look that actually shows the pedestrian happens 0.75 s after they step out. So the brakes start at 0.75 s.
  3. Reaction distance. 16.7 × 0.75 = 12.5 m, driven at full speed.
  4. Braking distance. With hard braking of 8 m/s² (a typical dry-road emergency stop for a modern car): v² ÷ (2a) = 16.7² ÷ 16 = 278 ÷ 16 = 17.4 m.
  5. Total. 12.5 + 17.4 = 29.9 m. The line is 25 m away, so you stop about 4.9 m past it, on the crossing.

Now make the computer faster: 10 looks a second and 0.1 s latency. The brakes start at 0.15 s, the reaction distance shrinks to 16.7 × 0.15 = 2.5 m, and the total is 2.5 + 17.4 = 19.9 m: you stop with 5 m to spare. Same car, same brakes, same road. Only the loop changed.

stopping distance = v × (latency + wait for the next loop) + v² ÷ (2 × a) The first part is distance driven “blind”; the loop makes it worse by up to one full loop period (1 ÷ rate). The second part is physics: braking can't beat the tyres.
💡 Big idea An autonomous machine is a loop: sense, perceive, localize, predict, plan, control, then sense again to see what happened. Two numbers decide how well it copes with a fast world: the loop rate (how often it decides) and the latency (how old its information is when it acts). Every decision is about a world that has already moved on.
🧠 Analogy: online gaming on bad Wi-Fi

If you've played an online game with a 500 ms ping, you know this loop. You see an opponent where they were half a second ago, you react, and your shot lands where they no longer are. Games fight lag the same two ways robots do: send updates more often (loop rate), and have your computer predict where everyone is now from where they were heading (latency compensation). You'll try that trick with a checkbox in the next playground.

🤔 Why this? (and not a simpler machine)
  1. The problem before. Early automatic machines ran open loop: a fixed script with no checking. A factory robot arm replays the same motion forever; a cruise control from the 1950s held a throttle position, not a speed. That works when the world never changes. Roads, skies and warehouses change every second.
  2. What people tried. Remote control (a human in the loop, but radio lag and one operator per vehicle). Tracks and wires: factory carts that follow a buried wire or painted line (reliable, but blind to anything off the line). Scripted routes: drive this list of GPS points (fine until a parked truck sits on one of them).
  3. Why the loop works. Each pass compares what you wanted with what actually happened and corrects the difference, so small errors can't pile up. Drive 100 m blind with a speedometer that reads 2% low and you end up 2 m off. Re-measure your position 10 times a second and your error never grows past what one tenth of a second of driving adds, plus the sensor's own error.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need sensors, computers, and software that finishes on time, every time. Information is always a little old (latency), and the loop is blind between looks (loop rate): at speed both turn into meters. Too slow a loop doesn't just react late, it can make a machine unstable: it overcorrects, then overcorrects the overcorrection (Playground 3). And every step can be wrong: a missed pedestrian in perception poisons everything after it.
  5. What came next. Splitting the loop into modules that each run at their own speed: fast inner loops for control, slower outer loops for planning (Lesson 0.3). Ways to measure and budget latency, and to predict around it. And lately, learning big parts of the loop end to end from driving data instead of hand-writing each module (Lesson 0.4).

🏆 Challenge: at 60 km/h or faster, stop 0 to 1 m before the line. Then find the slowest loop rate that still wins with 0.1 s latency. Bonus puzzles: at 50 km/h with a 1 Hz loop and 0.5 s latency you cross the line; does 🧠 prediction save you? And is there any speed where no loop, however fast, can save you?

What you should find: the loop rate and the latency add up. At 60 km/h, 0.5 s latency loses no matter how fast you loop, but 0.2 s wins even at a lazy 2 Hz. Prediction is a real fix for stale information when there's room to brake: the computer shifts the old picture forward by how far it must have travelled since. But prediction can't give back the meters already driven before the first look. And above about 72 km/h the braking distance alone (v² ÷ 2a) is longer than 25 m: no software can stop you, only driving slower or seeing further ahead can. That's why long-range sensors matter so much at highway speed (Phase 2).

🧩 Predict: a quadcopter drone has nothing like brakes; it stays level only by constantly adjusting four motor speeds. If you run its balance loop slower, do you expect it to just react a little later, or something worse?
Something worse. A drone that tilts starts sliding sideways and tilts more, so the controller must push back hard. Push back hard with old information and you overshoot, then overshoot back the other way, bigger each time: an oscillation that ends in a crash. For balancing machines a slow loop isn't just late, it's unstable. Try it next.

Loops inside loops

Real machines don't run one loop at one speed. They nest them: a fast, simple inner loop keeps the machine steady, and slower, smarter outer loops decide where to go. The numbers below are typical orders of magnitude, not any one company's specs; the PX4 numbers come from its documentation.

MachineFast inner loopSlower outer loops
🛸 Quadcopter (PX4)Body-rate control: the loop rate is set by IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX, 400 Hz by default; the state estimator's prediction runs on the IMU (motion sensor) at 100 Hz or moreAttitude, velocity and position loops wrapped around it; obstacle avoidance and path planning slower still
🚗 CarSteering, brake and throttle control: typically tens of Hz to around 100 HzPerception and planning, typically around 10 Hz, limited by how fast sensors deliver (a typical spinning lidar turns about 10 times a second)
🤖 Warehouse robotWheel speed control: typically tens to hundreds of Hz on a motor controllerObstacle checks and path following a few to tens of times a second; route planning only when the task changes

Why does the drone need the fastest loop? A parked car is stable: do nothing and it stays put. A hovering drone is not: do nothing and it tips over within moments. The less stable the machine, the faster its innermost loop must run.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Most robots and many research cars run their loops on ROS 2 (Robot Operating System 2: a framework of “nodes” passing timestamped messages). A loop is a node with a timer. The timestamp on every message is how you measure latency, and you'll see it in every stack you ever debug.

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from rclpy.time import Time
from sensor_msgs.msg import Range
from geometry_msgs.msg import Twist

class StopForObstacle(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__("stop_for_obstacle")
        self.dist, self.age = None, 0.0
        self.create_subscription(Range, "/front_range", self.on_range, 10)  # sense
        self.cmd = self.create_publisher(Twist, "/cmd_vel", 10)                  # act
        self.create_timer(0.1, self.tick)                                        # loop rate: 10 Hz

    def on_range(self, msg):
        self.dist = msg.range
        age = self.get_clock().now() - Time.from_msg(msg.header.stamp)           # latency of this reading
        self.age = age.nanoseconds * 1e-9

    def tick(self):                                                          # decide + act
        cmd = Twist()
        if self.dist is not None and self.dist > 1.0 + 0.5 * self.age:      # stale data → more margin
            cmd.linear.x = 0.5                                                  # m/s forward
        self.cmd.publish(cmd)                                                    # else: stop

rclpy.init()
rclpy.spin(StopForObstacle())

On a PX4 drone the innermost loop rate is a parameter, not code: IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX (default 400 Hz; PX4's docs call it “the loop rate for the rate controller and outputs”). The ROS 2 publisher/subscriber tutorial is the best first hour you can spend.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

The loop runs from cameras to controls. A 2025+ Model Y has 8 exterior cameras (its owner's manual lists them). Tesla's Q4 2023 update said its FSD v12 software “utilizes end-to-end training”: much of the perceive → plan part is one neural network trained on driving video (Lesson 0.4). The product is called Full Self-Driving (Supervised), and Tesla says it requires active driver supervision. That means the human is part of the loop too (Lesson 0.2). Tesla hasn't published its loop rates or latencies.

Sources: tesla.com/fsd, Tesla Q4 2023 update, Model Y manual

🚕 Waymo

The loop has every step from this lesson, with more sensors feeding it: lidar, cameras and radar, plus detailed maps of each city made before driving there. Nobody sits behind the wheel, so the loop has no human to fall back on. That's why so much of Waymo's effort goes into the loop's safety checks. Its December 2025 post describes a “Waymo Foundation Model” that fuses camera, lidar and radar inside a large learned model. So Waymo learns big parts of the loop too, while keeping it structured.

Sources: Waymo FAQ, The Waymo Driver, Waymo, Dec 2025

🛸 Drones

PX4 is open source, so you can see every loop. It's a cascade: a position loop feeds a velocity loop, which feeds an attitude loop, which feeds the fast angular-rate loop that drives the motors (400 Hz by default). Skydio's X10 adds an autonomy loop on top: six navigation cameras give 360° vision for flying around obstacles by itself.

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, PX4 parameters, Skydio X10

Why they differ: all three run the same loop, but they place the human and the speed differently. A drone falls in moments without its inner loop, so that loop is fast, simple and open for anyone to inspect. Tesla sells cars to drivers, so it can keep a human as the backup while it learns the loop from video. Waymo removed the human, so its loop must handle everything itself, and it pays for extra sensors and maps to do so.

Choice✅ Gains❌ Costs

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through what happens between a photon hitting the camera and the brakes moving.” A strong answer:

  • Names the steps (sense, perceive, localize, predict, plan, control) and what each one hands to the next.
  • Talks about time: each step's rate and latency, and that the total latency is what matters, measured with timestamps from the sensor to the actuator.
  • Turns time into distance with a number: “0.3 s at 30 m/s is 9 m.”
  • Knows the fixes: run faster, cut latency, predict forward with timestamps, and nest loops so that stability doesn't wait on the slow parts.

Check yourself

1. A delivery robot drives at 2 m/s. Its readings are 0.2 s old and its loop runs at 5 Hz. In the worst case, how far does it travel between an obstacle appearing and its first reaction?
Worst case it waits a full loop period (1 ÷ 5 Hz = 0.2 s) plus the 0.2 s latency: 0.4 s × 2 m/s = 0.8 m. On average it waits half a period, so 0.6 m. Either way, that's distance it drives before it even starts to brake.
2. A hobbyist's drone wobbles harder and harder after they move its balance controller from 400 Hz to 30 Hz, until it flips. What's the best explanation?
An unstable machine needs a loop fast enough for its stiffness. At 30 Hz each correction is based on a tilt that is up to 33 ms old and held for 33 ms, so it keeps pushing after the drone has already swung back. Fix it by running faster, or by making the controller softer (and accepting more drift in gusts).
3. Your car's camera pipeline has a steady 150 ms latency that you can't reduce. What's the most useful software fix?
At 30 m/s, 150 ms is 4.5 m. A known, steady delay can mostly be cancelled by predicting forward from timestamps (as the 🧠 checkbox did). Looping faster only shrinks the waiting between looks, not the age of each picture.
📝 TL;DR
  • Autonomous = a closed loop: sense → perceive → localize → predict → plan → control → sense again. Cars, drones and warehouse robots all run it.
  • Loop rate and latency become distance: blind distance ≈ speed × (latency + wait for the next loop). 60 km/h × 0.75 s = 12.5 m.
  • Stopping distance = blind distance + v² ÷ 2a. Above some speed only seeing further (or going slower) helps.
  • Known delays can be predicted around with timestamps; unstable machines (drones) need fast inner loops, nested inside slower ones.
  • The course's seven colors: sensor, estimate, uncertainty, map, other agents, plan, control.
