SAE levels 0–5: who is driving?

“Self-driving” means wildly different things in ads, news and law. Engineers use one shared scale, SAE J3016, and its question isn't “how clever is the car?” It's “who has to step in when something goes wrong?” Answer that and you can place any car, robotaxi or shuttle in seconds.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The autonomy loop: sense → perceive → localize → predict → plan → control, many times a second (Lesson 0.1).
  • Latency: how old information is when you act on it; at speed it turns into meters driven “blind” (Lesson 0.1).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Three cars, today, on real roads:

  • Car A drives you across a city: it turns at junctions, stops at red lights, changes lanes. You must watch the road the whole time.
  • Car B only works on a German motorway, at up to 95 km/h. While it drives, you're allowed to watch a film.
  • Car C has nobody in the driver's seat at all, but only inside certain neighbourhoods of a few cities.

Rank them from least to most “self-driving”. Most people put A first or second, because it does the most. The official scale puts A lowest. By the end of this lesson you'll know why, and you'll be able to place any product you read about.

These are real: A is how Tesla describes Full Self-Driving (Supervised), which it says requires “active driver supervision” (Tesla); B is Mercedes-Benz DRIVE PILOT, approved in Germany for up to 95 km/h in December 2024, and Mercedes says drivers may watch TV while it's active (Mercedes-Benz); C is a Waymo robotaxi.

🧩 Predict: if Car A notices something it can't handle, who deals with it? And for Car B? And Car C?
A: you, instantly, because you were supposed to be watching all along (Level 2). B: you, but after a warning: the car asks you to take over and gives you some seconds to look up from your film (Level 3). C: the car itself: there's nobody to ask, so it must bring itself to a safe stop (Level 4). The scale measures who is the backup, not how many tricks the car can do.

The words you need (four of them)

SAE J3016 is a standard from SAE International, the US-based engineering society, first issued in January 2014 and used by regulators and companies worldwide. Its whole scale is built from four ideas:

TermPlain meaningExample
DDT: dynamic driving taskThe real-time part of driving: steering, speeding up and slowing down, plus watching the road and reacting to what happens. Choosing where to go isn't part of it.Braking for a cyclist who swerves
Fallback (DDT fallback)What happens when the system fails or reaches the edge of what it can handle: someone must take over, or bring the car to a safe stop.Sensor fault on the motorway
MRC: minimal risk conditionJ3016's term for that safe stop: “a stable, stopped condition” that lowers the risk of a crash when the trip can't continue.Pulled over, hazard lights on
ODD: operational design domainThe conditions a feature is designed to work in: road types, places, speeds, weather, time of day, traffic.“Motorways, ≤ 95 km/h, flowing traffic”

One more: ADS (automated driving system) is J3016's name for a Level 3, 4 or 5 system, the ones that do the entire DDT while engaged. Levels 1 and 2 are “driver support”: they help, but you're still the driver.

Numbers first: the handover problem

✏️ Worked example: how far does a Level 3 car go while you look up?

At Level 3 you may read or watch a film, but you must take over when the car asks. Suppose the car gives you 10 seconds (an illustrative number; real systems and regulations set their own).

  1. 95 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 26.4 m/s.
  2. In 10 s the car covers 26.4 × 10 = 264 m, about two and a half football pitches, still driving itself.
  3. So the system can't just “give up” the moment it's confused. It must keep driving safely through the warning period, and handle the case where you never respond.

Mercedes describes exactly that last case: if the driver doesn't take back control after increasingly urgent prompts and the takeover time runs out, DRIVE PILOT brakes to a standstill with its hazard lights on (Mercedes-Benz, Dec 2024). Compare Level 2: you were supposed to be watching, so the expected handover time is roughly your own reaction time, about one second, during which the car moves around 26 m.

💡 Big idea The SAE level is decided by who does the watching and who is the fallback, inside a stated ODD. It describes the feature that is engaged, not the car, and it isn't a score: J3016 itself says the levels don't imply that Level 4 is “better” than Level 3 or Level 2. A brilliant city-driving system that needs you to supervise is Level 2. A simple shuttle that never needs anyone, on one campus, is Level 4.
🧠 Analogy: the learner driver and the instructor

Level 2: a learner is driving, and you're the instructor with your foot over the dual brake. You can't look away, even when they're doing great. Level 3: the learner is good enough on motorways that you may read, but when they say “your turn” you must take over within a few seconds. Level 4: they've passed the test, but their licence only covers this town in daylight; outside it they park and wait. Level 5: a fully licensed driver, anywhere you could drive.

🤔 Why this? (and not just “self-driving” or a 1–10 score)
  1. The problem before. “Autopilot”, “self-driving”, “autonomous” and “hands-free” meant different things to every carmaker, journalist and buyer. Regulators and insurers needed to know one thing above all: who is responsible at this moment? Marketing words couldn't tell them.
  2. What people tried. Germany's federal highway research institute (BASt) published a scale of automation levels in 2013. SAE's task force largely adopted it, added a sixth level (Level 5), and issued J3016 in January 2014; it has been revised since (2016, 2018, 2021, and September 2026). Other ideas, like ranking systems by capability or by how many sensors they carry, don't answer the responsibility question.
  3. Why this works. It splits driving into roles: doing the DDT, watching, being the fallback, and the ODD where all of that holds. Each level is a specific assignment of those roles, so it's never ambiguous. A tiny example: a car that steers and brakes for you on the motorway but needs you to watch → you watch → Level 2, whatever the ad says. J3016 even bans fractions (“Level 2.5” is not a thing), because a half-level would blur who's responsible.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. The levels say nothing about how well a system drives, or how big its ODD is. A Level 4 shuttle on one campus and a Level 4 robotaxi across a city share a number. People also misread the scale as a ladder that every company climbs one rung at a time. And Level 3's handover is genuinely hard: humans who have been reading for ten minutes are slow to rebuild a picture of the road.
  5. What came next. Machine-readable ODD descriptions, such as ASAM OpenODD, so that an ODD can be checked and tested rather than just written in a brochure. Safety cases and crash data comparisons that ask how safely a system drives inside its ODD (Lesson 0.6). And regulation built on top of the roles, such as the UN rule (R157) under which Germany approved DRIVE PILOT in 2021.

The ODD is a product decision

Notice that Levels 1 to 4 all have a limited ODD. That's not an embarrassment, it's a strategy. Every condition you add (night, rain, snow, higher speeds, more road types) multiplies the situations the system must handle and prove it can handle. Waymo's service areas and DRIVE PILOT's motorway-only ODD are choices about where to start.

The winning designs look a lot like a robotaxi: city streets at city speeds, day and night, in good weather. 🧩 Predict: a robotaxi stops in front of a burst water main blocking its lane. A person at a remote operations centre looks at its cameras and tells it “it's fine to use the oncoming lane for 30 m”. Does that make it Level 2, because a human helped?
No, it's still Level 4. J3016 calls this remote assistance: event-driven advice such as a new route or permission, which doesn't include anyone driving it in real time. The car still steers, watches and handles its own fallback. A remote person actually steering it in real time would be a remote driver, and then the human is doing the DDT.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In code, the SAE level shows up as an ODD monitor: a check that runs every loop and decides whether the feature may stay engaged, and what to do when it may not. ODDs are increasingly written in machine-readable form (ASAM OpenODD defines formats such as YAML), and test scenarios at the ODD's edges are written in ASAM OpenSCENARIO and run in simulators such as CARLA ScenarioRunner. Here's the idea in plain Python (an illustration, not a library):

from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Conditions:
    road: str            # "motorway", "city", "rural"
    speed_kmh: float
    raining: bool
    sensors_ok: bool

ODD = {"roads": {"motorway"}, "max_speed_kmh": 95, "rain_ok": False}

def in_odd(c: Conditions) -> bool:
    return (c.road in ODD["roads"] and c.speed_kmh <= ODD["max_speed_kmh"]
            and (ODD["rain_ok"] or not c.raining) and c.sensors_ok)

def on_tick(c: Conditions, level: int, seconds_since_request: float, driver_took_over: bool) -> str:
    if in_odd(c):
        return "keep driving"
    if level == 3 and not driver_took_over:
        # fallback-ready user: ask, keep driving safely meanwhile, stop if nobody answers
        return "request to intervene" if seconds_since_request < 10 else "stop in lane, hazards on"
    if level >= 4:
        return "reach minimal risk condition (pull over and stop)"   # the system is the fallback
    return "hand back to driver"                                    # Level 1–2: driver was watching

The 10-second number is illustrative. The real work is in in_odd: noticing that you're leaving the ODD (rain starting, lane lines vanishing, a sensor failing) early enough to act.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's own wording places FSD (Supervised) at Level 2: its features “require active driver supervision and do not make the vehicle autonomous”. Tesla also runs a Robotaxi service in Austin: its Q3 2026 update reports nearly two million cumulative unsupervised Robotaxi miles by the end of September 2026, and Cybercab rides from September 3. Tesla doesn't state an SAE level for it. By J3016's roles, a ride where no person aboard is expected to drive would be Level 4 within its service area.

Sources: tesla.com/fsd, Tesla Q3 2026 update

🚕 Waymo

Waymo describes its vehicles as Level 4 (in its October 2026 Toyota partnership post). Its ODD is its service areas: as of its FAQ in late 2026, ride-hailing in 13 metro areas from Phoenix to Miami, plus Austin and Atlanta through Uber. Inside those areas nobody in the car is the fallback: the Waymo Driver must handle failures itself and bring itself to a safe stop.

Sources: Waymo, Oct 2026, Waymo FAQ

🚙 Mercedes (Level 3)

DRIVE PILOT is a certified Level 3 system: approved in Germany under UN R157 in December 2021 (up to 60 km/h in dense motorway traffic), in Nevada (January 2023) and California (June 2023) at up to 40 mph, and at up to 95 km/h in flowing traffic on German motorways from a December 2024 approval. Mercedes lists more than 35 sensors, including lidar, and redundant steering, braking and electrics.

Sources: 2021, Nevada, California, 95 km/h

🛸 Drones

Drones have no SAE-style ladder, but the same question applies: what happens when things go wrong? PX4 builds the fallback in as failsafes: on low battery, radio loss or data-link loss the drone switches by itself to Hold, Return or Land, ordered by severity. That's the drone version of a minimal risk condition. Skydio's X10 flies around obstacles by itself, using six navigation cameras for 360° vision.

Sources: PX4 safety & failsafes, Skydio X10

Why they differ: it comes down to who they sell to and who can be the backup. Tesla sells cars to drivers, so a supervising driver is always there, and a Level 2 product can ship everywhere at once. Waymo sells rides, so there is no driver to fall back on. It goes straight to Level 4 and grows its ODD city by city. Mercedes picked a narrow Level 3 ODD where it could accept the responsibility while the driver relaxes. Drones mostly fly with a remote pilot responsible, and failsafes cover the gaps.

🎤 Interview angle

“Is product X Level 3?” or “What's the hardest part of Level 3?” A strong answer:

  • Answers with roles: who does the DDT, who watches, who is the fallback, and within which ODD. Points out that levels describe a feature, not a car, and aren't a quality score.
  • Knows the 2 → 3 jump is about watching and the 3 → 4 jump is about fallback, and can explain an MRC.
  • On Level 3: the handover. The system must stay safe for the whole warning period (264 m at 95 km/h for 10 s), detect ODD exits early, and cope with a driver who doesn't respond.
  • Names real examples with care: Tesla's own “supervised” wording, Mercedes' certified Level 3, Waymo's Level 4 in its service areas.

The standard itself 📜 History · 2014 SAE J3016 (2021 edition)

🤯 Fun fact · there's no Level 2.5

J3016 says outright that levels are “never fractional”: calling something Level 2.5 or 4.7 is incorrect, and so is calling a very capable Level 3 system a “partial Level 4”. It also says the numbers don't rank merit or sophistication. The level is about who is responsible, and responsibility can't be split in half (SAE J3016, section 8.3).

🌉 How it connects

The level decides how much of the loop from Lesson 0.1 the machine owns. At Level 2 the human closes the loop when perception or planning fails; at Level 4 the machine needs its own fallback, which is why Lesson 0.3's stack has safety monitors and Lesson 0.6 asks how many miles prove it's safe. Phase 9 returns to safety cases, operations and remote assistance (9.6, 9.7). In RL terms, the ODD is the set of situations a policy was designed and tested for; stepping outside it is the out-of-distribution problem the offline RL lesson warns about.

Check yourself

1. A Level 3 car at 108 km/h asks you to take over and gives you 8 seconds. How far does it drive itself during that warning?
108 ÷ 3.6 = 30 m/s, × 8 s = 240 m. The system must stay safe for all of it, and have a plan for when you don't respond.
2. A car steers, brakes and changes lanes by itself on any road, but its maker requires the driver to watch the road and be ready to take over immediately at all times. What level is it?
Capability doesn't set the level; roles do. If the human must supervise continuously, the human is part of the DDT (watching) and is the fallback: Level 2, however impressive the driving.
3. What's the key difference between Level 3 and Level 4?
Both have limited ODDs (only Level 5 is unlimited), and sensors aren't part of the definition. The jump from 3 to 4 is about who handles failures: a person who is asked, or the machine on its own.
📝 TL;DR
  • SAE J3016 (current edition: September 2026) defines Levels 0–5 by roles: who does the DDT, who watches, who is the fallback, within which ODD.
  • 2 → 3: the car takes over the watching. 3 → 4: the car becomes its own fallback (reaches a minimal risk condition). 4 → 5: the ODD becomes unlimited.
  • Levels describe the engaged feature, never fractions, and aren't a quality score.
  • Today: Tesla FSD (Supervised) is Level 2 by Tesla's own wording; Mercedes DRIVE PILOT (and BMW, Honda) are certified Level 3 in narrow ODDs; Waymo is Level 4 in its service areas; nobody offers Level 5.
  • The ODD is a product decision: every condition you add multiplies the testing.
