Lidar: time of flight & point clouds

A camera measures directions and has to guess distance. A lidar fires laser pulses and times their echoes, so it measures distance directly, a million or more times a second. Here's how the stopwatch works, why far-away people turn into a handful of dots, and why fog and black cars are lidar's worst enemies.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Scale ambiguity: one camera can't tell a toy at 5 m from a car at 10 m; it measures directions, not distance (Lesson 2.1).
  • Angles to meters: a small angle θ (in radians) at distance d spans about d × θ meters. 1° ≈ 0.0175 rad, so 1° at 60 m is about 1 m.
  • Frames: a lidar reports each point in its own frame (x forward, y left, z up for most automotive units); extrinsics move it into the car's frame (Lessons 1.5 and 2.2).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Light travels about 300,000 km every second. A lidar wants to know whether a pedestrian is 30.00 m or 30.15 m away: a difference of 15 cm.

How precise must its stopwatch be? A millisecond? A microsecond? Something wilder? And a second puzzle: a lidar that puts 100 dots on a person at 20 m, how many does it put on the same person at 80 m?

Waymo thought lidar mattered enough to build its own. In 2019 it wrote that off-the-shelf sensors “simply didn't serve our needs”, so from 2011 it developed its own sensors from the ground up, including three different types of lidar (Waymo, Mar 2019). Tesla went the other way: its cars carry cameras and no lidar.

🧩 Lock in a guess: to tell 30.00 m from 30.15 m, the lidar's clock must resolve about: (a) 1 millisecond, (b) 1 microsecond, (c) 1 nanosecond.
(c) about 1 nanosecond, a billionth of a second. The pulse goes out and back, so 15 cm of extra distance is 30 cm of extra travel, and light covers 30 cm in 1 ns. A microsecond of error would be 150 m. Lidar is, above all, a very, very good stopwatch.

Numbers first: distance from a round trip

d = c · t / 2 The distance is the speed of light (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s) times the round-trip time t, halved because the pulse travels there and back. This is called time of flight (ToF).
✏️ Worked example: timing three echoes
Round-trip time tDistance c·t/2What it is
1 ns299,792,458 × 10⁻⁹ / 2 ≈ 0.15 mThe size of one “tick” of a 1 GHz clock
200 ns≈ 30 mA pedestrian across an intersection
1.334 µs (1,334 ns)≈ 200 mA stopped car at highway braking range

Flip it around for the error budget: a timing error of 0.1 ns is a range error of 1.5 cm. And a rule about firing rate falls out too: if you fire the next pulse before the last echo is back, you can't tell which pulse an echo belongs to. Firing a million pulses a second (one every 1,000 ns) means echoes from beyond c × 1,000 ns / 2 ≈ 150 m come back after the next shot and look like close objects.

What you should find: the clock tick sets the precision (error up to tick × 7.5 cm), and the pulse rate sets a hard range limit of c / (2 × rate). That's one reason a lidar's points-per-second budget is precious: it's shared between range, resolution and frame rate. Real sensors also code their pulses or use several wavelengths and receivers in parallel to stretch the budget.

From one beam to a point cloud

One pulse gives one distance along one direction: one point. Do it in millions of directions and you get a point cloud, a 3D snapshot of the world made of dots. Two main ways to sweep the beam:

DesignHow it points the laserTypical strengthsTypical weaknesses
Spinning (mechanical)A column of lasers, one per channel (also called a beam or ring), rotates 10–20 times a secondFull 360° view; mature; every channel sweeps a ring around the carMoving parts; bulky; fixed vertical spacing between rings
Solid-state (MEMS mirror, optical phased array, flash)A tiny vibrating mirror, a chip that steers light, or one flash lighting the whole sceneFew or no moving parts; smaller; can be cheaperLimited field of view per unit (usually 120° or less), so several are needed for 360°

For a spinning sensor two numbers set the resolution. Vertical: the channels split the vertical field of view, so 128 channels over 22.5° are 22.5 / 127 ≈ 0.18° apart. Horizontal: each channel fires a fixed number of times per turn (the columns), say 1,024, so 360 / 1,024 ≈ 0.35° apart. Those are the specs of a real long-range unit, the Ouster OS2: 128 channels, 22.5° vertical field of view, up to 20 Hz (Ouster OS2, checked Nov 2026).

✏️ Worked example: dots on a pedestrian

A pedestrian is 1.7 m tall and 0.5 m wide. The lidar has 0.18° between rings and 0.35° between columns.

  1. At 20 m: height covers atan(1.7 / 20) ≈ 4.9°, so about 4.9 / 0.18 ≈ 27 rings; width covers 0.5 / 20 rad ≈ 1.43°, about 4 columns. Roughly 100+ points.
  2. At 80 m: 4 × farther, so 4 × fewer rings (≈ 7) and 4 × fewer columns (≈ 1): roughly 7 points.
  3. Points fall with the square of distance: 4 × farther is about 16 × fewer points. The gap between rings is 80 × 0.0031 ≈ 0.25 m at 80 m, so a child or a dog can slip between two rings entirely.
🧩 Predict: your lidar's total points-per-second budget is fixed. You double the rotation rate from 10 Hz to 20 Hz to get fresher data. What happens to the number of points on a far pedestrian?
It roughly halves, and can drop to zero. Twice as many turns per second with the same budget means half the columns per turn, so the columns are twice as far apart. At 60 m, 0.70° between columns is wider than the person (0.48°), so they can fall between two columns. Fresh data, but fewer points: a classic lidar trade-off. Try it in the next playground.

💡 Big idea A lidar measures distance directly and precisely (centimeters), with its own light, day or night. Its weakness is density: a fixed budget of points spread over a sphere means far objects get few points, falling with the square of distance. Every lidar design trades channels, columns, frame rate and field of view against that budget.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A camera can't measure distance directly: a single image has scale ambiguity, and stereo depth error grows with the square of distance. A planner deciding whether to brake for a pedestrian needs a range it can trust, at night too, without guessing the person's height.
  2. What people tried. Ultrasonic sensors (precise, but only a few meters). Radar (long range and all weather, but blurry: it can't tell a person from a pole beside them, Lesson 2.4). Stereo cameras (dense, but depth precision collapses at range). Single-beam laser rangefinders (one distance, one direction).
  3. Why this works. Light is fast and nearly straight, so a timed pulse gives distance with no assumptions about the object: d = c·t/2, and a 0.1 ns timing error is only 1.5 cm. Fire in millions of directions and you get a 3D point cloud whose geometry you can trust directly: a 1.7 m pedestrian at 20 m shows up as about 100 points, each to a few centimeters.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Cost and complexity (spinning units, lasers, precise timing). Sparse at range: 16× fewer points at 4× the distance. Weak returns from dark, low-reflectivity surfaces (a black car), mirror-like ones (wet roads, glass) that bounce the beam away, and fog, rain, snow or exhaust that scatter it and add false points. No color and no text: it can't read a sign or a traffic light.
  5. What came next. Multiple returns per pulse to see through rain and foliage, solid-state designs for cost and reliability, 1550 nm lasers that may emit more power safely, FMCW lidar that also measures each point's speed (the Doppler trick of Lesson 2.4), and fusing lidar with cameras for meaning (2.7) and with radar for weather.

What lidar sees well, and what it doesn't

A lidar only sees light that comes back. How much comes back depends on the target's reflectivity (the fraction of light it returns) and on the air in between. Sensor datasheets therefore quote range at a reflectivity: the Ouster OS2, for example, lists 200 m range at 10% reflectivity and 400 m maximum (Ouster OS2). A typical white car reflects far more than 10%; black paint and dark clothing reflect only a few percent.

  • Dark targets: returned light falls with reflectivity and with 1/distance², so a target half as reflective is lost about 1.4 × closer (√2), all else equal.
  • Mirror-like surfaces (glass, wet asphalt, shiny paint at a glancing angle) send the beam away instead of back: missing points or phantom points “inside” reflections.
  • Fog, rain, snow, dust and exhaust: droplets scatter the pulse, weakening the real echo and creating early false echoes close to the sensor.
  • Retroreflectors (road signs, license plates) send so much light back that they can saturate the receiver and bloom into fat blobs.
🧩 Predict: in fog, a lidar records a cloud of points about 5–20 m away that aren't there. Its echo for a car 90 m ahead is also weaker. If you make the receiver more sensitive to see the car again, what happens to the fog points?
They multiply. The fog echoes are weak too, so a lower detection threshold lets more of them through. The fix is to use more than the first echo: a pulse that hits fog droplets and then the car returns two echoes. Keeping the last (or strongest) return keeps the car and drops the fog. Waymo's Honeycomb lidar, for instance, records up to four objects along each beam (Waymo, 2019).

905 nm or 1550 nm?

Most automotive lidars use near-infrared light at one of two wavelengths. The difference comes down to eyes and detectors:

905 nm1550 nm
Eye safetyThe eye focuses it onto the retina, so eye-safety rules cap the powerAbsorbed by the front of the eye before it reaches the retina, so it can safely emit more power and reach longer ranges, though not “eye-safe at any power”
DetectorsSilicon: the cheap, mature chip technologyNeeds other materials (such as InGaAs), typically costlier
Watch outRange limited by the power capReports in 2019 suggested high-power 1550 nm lidars might damage camera sensors in some circumstances

Source: Jeff Hecht, Laser Focus World, Sept 2019. Both wavelengths are in wide use; neither is “the winner”.

🧠 Analogy: echolocation with a flashlight

A bat clicks and listens; the delay tells it how far the moth is. A lidar does the same with light instead of sound, a million times a second, in a million directions. And like a bat in a rainstorm, it hears echoes from every droplet: the trick is knowing which echo is the moth.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Point clouds are just N × 3 arrays (x, y, z), often with intensity and a ring number per point. Open3D is the friendly Python library for working with them; PCL (the Point Cloud Library) is the C++ classic; ROS 2 carries them as sensor_msgs/PointCloud2. A first pass on any driving scan: shrink it, then find the ground.

import numpy as np
import open3d as o3d

# Range to points: each return is (range r, azimuth az, elevation el) in the sensor frame
r, az, el = np.array([20.0, 20.1]), np.radians([0.0, 0.35]), np.radians([-2.0, -2.0])
xyz = np.stack([r * np.cos(el) * np.cos(az), r * np.cos(el) * np.sin(az), r * np.sin(el)], axis=1)
print("round trip for 20 m (ns):", 2 * 20.0 / 299_792_458 * 1e9)   # ≈ 133.4

pcd = o3d.geometry.PointCloud()
pcd.points = o3d.utility.Vector3dVector(xyz)            # works the same for a 100,000-point scan
# pcd = o3d.io.read_point_cloud("scan.pcd")           # ...or load one from disk
down = pcd.voxel_down_sample(voxel_size=0.1)            # keep one point per 10 cm cube
plane_model, inliers = down.segment_plane(distance_threshold=0.15,
                                          ransac_n=3, num_iterations=1000)
ground = down.select_by_index(inliers)                  # the road
objects = down.select_by_index(inliers, invert=True)    # everything standing on it

segment_plane fits the road with RANSAC (Lesson 4.2) and returns the plane's [a, b, c, d] and the indices of points within 15 cm of it. On a real scan with only two points it would fail: give it a full sweep.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Waymo builds its own lidars: since 2011 it has developed its own sensors, including three types of lidar, because off-the-shelf ones didn't meet its needs. Its 5th-generation system (2020) paired a 360° lidar with more than 300 m of range with perimeter lidars at four points around the vehicle to cover the close-in blind spots. The Honeycomb perimeter lidar has a 95° vertical field of view, a minimum range of zero and up to four returns per beam. The 6th-generation Waymo Driver (rider-only from Feb 2026) uses short-range lidars alongside its cameras and imaging radar.

Sources: Waymo, 2019, Waymo, 2020, Waymo, Feb 2026

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's production cars carry no lidar. The Model Y manual lists only cameras for driving, and Tesla describes “Tesla Vision” as its camera-based system; it removed radar from 2021 and ultrasonic sensors from late 2022, replacing them with camera-based networks. Tesla has argued publicly (for example at its 2019 Autonomy Day) that cameras plus neural networks are the path to self-driving, accepting that distance must be inferred rather than measured.

Sources: Model Y manual: cameras, Tesla Vision update (archived 2023), Tesla Autonomy Day

🛸 Drones

Full 3D spinning lidars are heavy and power-hungry for a small drone, so drones mostly use cameras for obstacles (Skydio's X10 relies on six navigation cameras) and small single-beam lidar rangefinders for height. PX4 supports laser rangefinders such as LightWare's SF11/C (up to about 120 m) for terrain following and landing, and rotary lidars such as the SF45/B for collision prevention.

Sources: PX4 distance sensors, Skydio X10 specs

Why they differ: Waymo runs its own fleet in chosen cities and can afford expensive sensors that measure geometry directly, which makes safety arguments easier. Tesla sells millions of consumer cars where every sensor's cost multiplies, and bets that data and networks can infer depth from cameras. A drone's limit is weight and battery: one tiny laser pointing down is worth carrying, a car-style spinning lidar usually isn't.

🎤 Interview angle

“What does a lidar measure, and when would you not trust it?” A strong answer:

  • Gives d = c·t/2 with a number: 1 ns ≈ 15 cm, so ranging needs sub-nanosecond timing; precision is centimeters.
  • Explains density vs range: points ∝ 1/distance² (16× fewer at 4× the distance), with ring spacing and columns per turn set by a points-per-second budget.
  • Lists failure modes: low reflectivity (black cars, range quoted “at 10%”), specular surfaces, fog/rain/snow (false near returns, weakened echoes; multiple returns help), retroreflector blooming, and motion distortion within a sweep.
  • Knows the industry split: Waymo builds lidar into its stack; Tesla is camera-only; drones use small rangefinders and cameras.
🏭 In the real world
Waymo's in-house lidar family 🚗 AV · 2011+ Waymo blog, 2019

How: long-range 360° lidar on the roof plus short-range perimeter units around the car; the Honeycomb sees 95° vertically, from zero range, with up to four returns per pulse.

Why this method: the perimeter units cover the blind spots right next to the vehicle (curbs, a child beside the door) that a single roof sensor can't see.

The KITTI benchmark's Velodyne 🧪 Research · 2012 KITTI sensor setup

How: a 64-channel Velodyne HDL-64E spinning at 10 frames per second, capturing about 100,000 points per turn, calibrated to four cameras.

Why this method: dense 360° geometry with centimeter ranges became the ground truth for a decade of 3D detection and depth-estimation research (Lesson 6.2).

Long-range robotics lidar 🏢 Ouster · 2026 Ouster OS2

How: 128 channels over 22.5°, up to 20 Hz, 200 m at 10% reflectivity, ±2 cm precision, available in 32, 64 and 128 channels.

Why this method: a narrow vertical field of view concentrates the rings where far cars and people are, buying range at the cost of coverage up close.

Drone terrain following 🧰 Open source PX4 distance sensors

How: a downward single-beam laser rangefinder feeds height above ground to PX4's estimator for terrain following, precision hovering and landing.

Why this method: one beam weighs grams and draws little power, yet fixes the barometer's slow drift right where it matters: near the ground.

🤯 Fun fact · a million points a second, in 2012

KITTI's Velodyne captured about 100,000 points per turn at 10 turns a second (KITTI): roughly a million range measurements every second, each one a nanosecond-precise stopwatch reading. Today's Ouster OS2 lists up to 2.62 million points per second with dual returns (Ouster). Every one of those points still obeys d = c·t/2.

Check yourself

1. A lidar echo returns 400 ns after the pulse left. How far away is the target?
d = c·t/2 = 3 × 10⁸ m/s × 400 × 10⁻⁹ s / 2 = 120 / 2 = 60 m. Forgetting the “/2” (the pulse goes there and back) is the classic mistake, and it would give 120 m.
2. On a clear night your lidar sees a white car at 150 m but loses a black car at 90 m. Why?
Lidar brings its own light, so darkness doesn't matter, but the reflectivity of the target does. Returned signal ∝ reflectivity ÷ distance², which is why datasheets quote range “at 10% reflectivity”. Dark cars and dark clothing are hard cases; radar (2.4) and cameras help cover them.
3. A 64-channel lidar puts about 100 points on a pedestrian at 20 m. Roughly how many at 60 m?
At 3× the distance the person spans 3× fewer rings and 3× fewer columns: 100 ÷ 9 ≈ 11. Point density falls with the square of distance, which is why far-range detection needs many channels, narrow vertical fields of view, or help from cameras and radar.
📝 TL;DR
  • Time of flight: d = c·t/2. 1 ns ≈ 15 cm; 200 m ≈ 1.33 µs round trip. Pulse rate limits unambiguous range: c / (2 × rate), 150 m at 1 MHz.
  • Spinning lidars sweep channels (rings) × columns; solid-state ones steer light with mirrors, chips or a flash but see a narrower field each.
  • Points on an object fall with distance²: ~100 at 20 m, ~7 at 80 m. Faster rotation with a fixed budget means fewer points per sweep.
  • Range depends on reflectivity (specs say “at 10%”): black cars, glass, fog, rain and snow hurt; multiple returns help see through droplets.
  • Waymo builds its own lidars (long-range 360° + perimeter units); Tesla uses no lidar; drones use cameras and small laser rangefinders.
