Phase 2 · Lesson 5

IMU: gyros, accelerometers, bias & drift

A chip smaller than a fingernail tells a drone which way is up 1,000 times a second, and tells a car how it's turning inside a tunnel. It's also the sensor that drifts the fastest: a bias you can barely measure becomes tens of meters of error in a minute. Here's what it really measures, and why errors grow with time squared.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Integration: speed is the running total of acceleration × time; position is the running total of speed × time. A constant acceleration a for t seconds gives speed a·t and distance ½·a·t² (refresher: integrals in the Math course).
  • Body frame: the IMU measures in its own axes, fixed to the vehicle (x forward, y left, z up), not in the world frame. Turning those readings into world directions needs the vehicle's orientation (Lessons 1.1 and 1.3).
  • Gravity: g ≈ 9.81 m/s², pointing down. Hold on to it; it's about to behave strangely.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A drone sits perfectly still on a table. Its accelerometer, the sensor that measures acceleration, reports 9.81 m/s² pointing up.

Is it broken? The drone isn't moving, so shouldn't the acceleration be zero? And the second puzzle: you then drop the drone off the table. While it falls, accelerating downward at 9.81 m/s², what does the accelerometer read?

The answer is the key to every drone that hovers: a flight controller like PX4 lists the gyroscope and accelerometer as mandatory sensors, together with a compass and a barometer (PX4 sensor docs), and its estimator predicts the drone's motion from them at 100 Hz or more. Waymo's vehicles carry “redundant inertial measurement systems” that cross-check each other (Waymo Driver).

🧩 Lock in a guess: falling freely, the accelerometer reads (a) 9.81 m/s² down, (b) 19.6 m/s² down, (c) zero.
(c) zero. An accelerometer is a tiny weight on a spring. It measures how hard the spring pushes on the weight, which is everything except gravity, called the specific force. On the table, the table pushes the drone up with 9.81 m/s² worth of force to stop it falling: that's what the sensor reads. In free fall nothing pushes, the spring relaxes, and the reading is zero, the same “weightless” feeling astronauts have in orbit. To get the drone's real acceleration you must add gravity back, which means you need to know exactly which way is down.

What's inside an IMU

An IMU (inertial measurement unit) is two sensors in one package, usually with three axes each:

SensorMeasuresUnitsIntegrate once →Integrate twice →
Gyroscope (gyro)Rotation rate about x, y, zrad/s or °/sOrientation (roll, pitch, heading)-
AccelerometerSpecific force: acceleration minus gravitym/s² (or g)VelocityPosition

Modern IMUs are MEMS (micro-electro-mechanical systems): microscopic silicon springs and vibrating structures etched on a chip, the same kind as in your phone. They're cheap, tiny and fast (hundreds to thousands of readings per second), and that's their appeal: an IMU needs nothing from the outside world. No satellites, no light, no landmarks. It works in tunnels, under bridges, indoors and in fog. Its weakness is that it only ever measures changes, so every small error piles up.

f = a − g    (at rest on a level table: f = 0 − (0, 0, −9.81) = (0, 0, +9.81) m/s²) The accelerometer reads the specific force f: the true acceleration a minus the gravity vector g (which points down). Standing still, a is zero, so it reads +9.81 m/s² upward. In free fall a equals g, so it reads zero.

🏆 Challenge: make the forward (x) axis read +1.70 m/s² (± 0.03) in two different ways: once by only tilting (no acceleration), once by only accelerating (level). The x reading alone can't tell them apart. That's why a car braking hard can briefly “look” tilted to a cheap IMU, and why tilt errors are so dangerous.

What you should find: 10° of nose-up tilt gives 9.81 × sin 10° ≈ 1.70 m/s² on the x axis, exactly what a 1.70 m/s² push forward gives. The z axis differs only a little (9.66 vs 9.81). This is the root of the next idea: if your estimate of “which way is down” is off by a fraction of a degree, a sliver of gravity's 9.81 m/s² leaks into the horizontal directions and gets integrated as if the vehicle were accelerating.

Numbers first: why drift grows with time squared

Every real accelerometer has a bias: a small constant error, so it reads a little too high or low even when nothing happens. Biases also wander with temperature and time. Here's what a tiny bias does when you integrate it twice to get position (this is called dead reckoning: navigating from your own motion only).

✏️ Worked example: a 0.01 m/s² bias for one minute

0.01 m/s² is about 1 milli-g (1 mg = 0.0098 m/s²): a thousandth of gravity, far too small to feel. Assume everything else is perfect.

Time tVelocity error b·tPosition error ½·b·t²
1 s0.01 m/s0.005 m
10 s0.1 m/s0.5 m
30 s0.3 m/s4.5 m
60 s0.6 m/s18 m

Ten times the time, a hundred times the error. Now a tilt error: if the system thinks it's level but is tilted by just 1°, gravity leaks in as 9.81 × sin 1° ≈ 0.17 m/s², seventeen times our bias, and after 60 s that's ½ × 0.17 × 3,600 ≈ 308 m. Woodman's classic study puts it the same way: a tilt error of only 0.05° projects 0.0086 m/s² of gravity onto the horizontal axes (Woodman, Cambridge TR-696).

position error ≈ ½ · ba · t²      heading error = bg · t An accelerometer bias ba, integrated twice, gives a position error that grows with the square of time. A gyro bias bg, integrated once, gives a heading (or tilt) error that grows steadily with time, and that wrong heading then sends every later step of travel slightly the wrong way.
🧩 Predict: a car drives perfectly straight at 30 m/s for a minute. Its gyro has a bias of only 0.01 °/s. How far sideways does dead reckoning think it went? (a) a few centimeters, (b) about 1 m, (c) about 9 m.
(c) about 9 m. The heading error grows to 0.01 × 60 = 0.6° after a minute. It grows steadily, so the sideways speed error does too: v × (heading error) rises from 0 to 30 × 0.0105 rad ≈ 0.31 m/s. Integrating that ramp gives ½ × 30 m/s × 0.000175 rad/s × 60² ≈ 9.4 m: more than two lane widths, from a bias you'd struggle to see on a datasheet plot.

💡 Big idea An IMU is superb for short times and hopeless for long ones. Over a hundredth of a second it's nearly perfect, which is why every drone's control loop and every estimator's prediction step runs on it. Over a minute, biases integrated once (gyro → heading) and twice (accelerometer → position) grow into meters or worse. So IMUs are always paired with a sensor that doesn't drift but is slow or noisy (GNSS, wheel odometry, camera, lidar, a map), and the pairing is the job of the Kalman filter (Lesson 3.3).
🧠 Analogy: walking with your eyes closed

Close your eyes and walk ten steps to a door you just saw: you'll arrive within a hand's width. Try a hundred steps and you might end up in another room. Your inner ear is an IMU: superb at feeling each turn and lurch, but every small misjudgment adds up, and with no eyes to re-anchor you, the errors only grow. Open your eyes for a second (a GNSS fix, a landmark) and you snap back. That's sensor fusion.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A drone must know its tilt hundreds of times a second to stay in the air, and a car must keep tracking its motion in a tunnel, under a bridge or when the camera is blinded. GNSS updates only a few times a second and vanishes underground; cameras need light and texture; wheels slip.
  2. What people tried. Mechanical gyroscopes and gimballed platforms (accurate, but heavy and expensive; aircraft and rockets used them), a magnetic compass alone (heading only, fooled by steel and motors), wheel odometry alone (no use for drones, wrong on slippery roads, Lesson 2.6).
  3. Why this works. MEMS gyros and accelerometers measure rotation and specific force directly, need nothing from the outside world, and run at hundreds of Hz on a chip costing a few dollars (illustrative). Over a short time the errors are tiny: a 0.01 m/s² bias costs only 5 mm in 1 s. At rest, the accelerometer even gives you tilt for free, because it reads gravity: 10° of tilt shows up as 1.70 m/s² on the forward axis.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Everything drifts. Accelerometer bias → position error ½·b·t² (0.01 m/s² → 18 m in 60 s); gyro bias → heading error b·t (0.01 °/s → 0.6° per minute → about 9 m sideways at 30 m/s); tilt errors leak gravity (1° → 0.17 m/s²). Biases change with temperature and each power-up; vibration from motors adds noise; and an accelerometer can't tell tilting from accelerating.
  5. What came next. Estimate the bias: average the readings while standing still (Playground 3), or keep the biases as extra states in a Kalman filter that GNSS, wheels or cameras keep correcting (PX4's EKF2 does exactly this; Lessons 3.3 and 3.6). Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) pairs an IMU with a camera; redundant IMUs catch a faulty one. Higher grades (fiber-optic and ring-laser gyros) drift far less, at far higher cost.

Fix it: measure the bias while standing still

The simplest, most effective trick in inertial navigation: when the vehicle is known to be still (parked, a drone on the launch pad, a robot waiting at a door), the true rotation rate is zero and the true acceleration is zero. Whatever the gyro reads is gyro bias; whatever the accelerometer reads beyond gravity is accelerometer bias. Average a few seconds of readings (averaging N samples cuts the noise in the estimate by √N) and subtract it from then on. That's one reason gyro calibration wants the vehicle perfectly still, and a robot or car can repeat the trick at every stop, clamping its velocity to zero while it re-estimates the biases (a zero-velocity update).

🏆 Challenge: finish the 10-second drive with a position error under 1 m. Raw, the IMU ends up about 2–3 m off. How long do you need to stand still? Try 0.5 s, then 5 s, and press 🎲 a few times: short averages leave a noisy bias estimate, so you're sometimes lucky, sometimes not. Standing still is cheap; standing still for a long time isn't.

What you should find: subtracting even a half-second average cuts the error a lot, and a few seconds gets you under 1 m most of the time. But notice it never reaches zero. The leftover is noise integrated twice (a “random walk” that grows like t3/2) plus whatever the bias estimate got wrong, and biases drift again later. In a real vehicle the fix is continuous: a filter keeps re-estimating the bias whenever another sensor offers a reference (Lesson 3.6).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In ROS 2, IMUs publish sensor_msgs/msg/Imu: an orientation quaternion, angular_velocity in rad/s, linear_acceleration in m/s² (the message definition says “not in g's”), and a covariance matrix for each. Here's bias estimation and double integration in NumPy, plus a minimal rclpy subscriber.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
dt, g = 0.01, 9.81                                   # 100 Hz IMU
bias_true = np.array([0.03, -0.02, 0.05])            # accelerometer bias, m/s²

# 1) Sit still for 5 s. A level accelerometer at rest reads +g on z (specific force!), plus bias + noise.
still = np.array([0.0, 0.0, g]) + bias_true + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((500, 3))
bias_est = still.mean(axis=0) - np.array([0.0, 0.0, g])
print(np.round(bias_est, 3))                         # [ 0.03  -0.02   0.044]

# 2) Dead-reckon 60 s of standing still along x, with and without the correction.
ax = bias_true[0] + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(6000)  # true acceleration is 0
for name, a in [("raw", ax), ("bias removed", ax - bias_est[0])]:
    v = np.cumsum(a) * dt                            # integrate once: velocity
    x = np.cumsum(v) * dt                            # integrate twice: position
    print(f"{name:>12}: drift after 60 s = {x[-1]:6.2f} m")   # raw ≈ 55 m, bias removed ≈ 1.6 m
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from sensor_msgs.msg import Imu

class ImuEcho(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__("imu_echo")
        self.create_subscription(Imu, "/imu/data", self.cb, 50)
    def cb(self, m):
        a, w = m.linear_acceleration, m.angular_velocity    # m/s² and rad/s, body frame
        self.get_logger().info(f"az={a.z:.2f} m/s²  yaw rate={w.z:.4f} rad/s")

rclpy.init(); rclpy.spin(ImuEcho())

Two classic bugs: forgetting that a level IMU at rest reads +9.81 on z (so “subtract gravity” has a sign), and mixing frames: the IMU's axes are rarely the car's axes, so its mounting rotation is an extrinsic you must calibrate (Lesson 2.7). In ROS 2, robot_localization fuses this message with odometry and GPS (Lesson 3.3).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

The IMU is the heart of a flight controller. PX4 minimally requires a gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer (compass) and barometer. Its EKF2 estimator runs its prediction step on the IMU at 100 Hz or more and carries gyro bias and accelerometer bias as states, so GNSS, barometer, optical flow or vision measurements keep re-estimating them in flight. ArduPilot's EKF3 goes further on redundancy: with two or more IMUs it runs one filter core per IMU and uses the healthiest.

Sources: PX4 sensor hardware, PX4 EKF2, ArduPilot EKF

🚕 Waymo

Waymo lists “redundant inertial measurement systems for vehicle positioning” among its safety features: the two systems cross-check each other and take over from one another if a fault is detected. It hasn't published which IMUs it uses or how it fuses them; its public descriptions emphasize localizing against its detailed maps “even where GPS struggles, like tunnels or between skyscrapers”, exactly where an IMU bridges the gaps between other measurements.

Sources: Waymo Driver, Waymo mapping handbook (2020)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published details of the inertial sensors in its driving system or how it fuses them; its public talks (AI Day 2021 and 2022) focus on the camera networks. What's safe to say is general: every modern car already carries inertial sensors for stability control and airbags, and any camera-only system still needs to know how the car itself is moving between frames.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021 (no IMU details given)

Why they differ: for a drone the IMU is not optional: without a fast tilt estimate it falls out of the sky in under a second, so its whole estimator is built around the IMU and its biases. A robotaxi treats the IMU as the reliable short-term backbone that bridges between lidar, camera and map localization, and duplicates it for safety. Tesla's public story is about vision; its inertial sensing hasn't been part of what it presents.

🎤 Interview angle

“What does an accelerometer measure, and why does inertial navigation drift?” A strong answer:

  • Says specific force: acceleration minus gravity; +9.81 m/s² up at rest, zero in free fall. Gravity must be removed using the estimated orientation.
  • Explains the error growth with numbers: accelerometer bias → ½·b·t² (0.01 m/s² → 18 m in 60 s); gyro bias → b·t in angle; a 1° tilt error leaks 0.17 m/s² of gravity.
  • Knows the fixes: bias estimation at rest and zero-velocity updates, biases as Kalman-filter states, fusion with GNSS, wheels, vision (VIO) or lidar.
  • Mentions the practical stuff: temperature-dependent bias, vibration isolation on drones, IMU-to-body extrinsic calibration, time stamps, redundant IMUs.
🏭 In the real world
PX4's EKF2 on every PX4 drone 🧰 Open source PX4: Using the ECL EKF

How: a 24-state filter predicts with the IMU and keeps gyro and accelerometer biases among its states, corrected by GNSS, barometer, magnetometer, optical flow and vision.

Why this method: the controller needs attitude hundreds of times a second; only an IMU delivers it, and only a filter keeps its biases in check.

One filter per IMU in ArduPilot 🧰 Open source ArduPilot EKF overview

How: with two or more IMUs, EKF3 runs a separate core for each and switches to the one whose sensor data is most consistent.

Why this method: a single faulty or vibrating IMU is one of the most dangerous failures on a drone; redundancy catches it.

Redundant inertial systems on robotaxis 🚗 AV · current Waymo Driver

How: two inertial measurement systems track the vehicle's motion and cross-check each other, taking over if one faults.

Why this method: with no human driver, the system that tracks the car's own motion can't be a single point of failure.

How fast does a MEMS IMU drift? 🧪 Research · 2007 Woodman, An introduction to inertial navigation

How: measurement and simulation of a navigation system built on an Xsens MTx MEMS IMU, breaking the drift down by noise source.

Why this method: it showed that gyro errors, through tilt and gravity leakage, are the critical path for drift in nearly all such systems.

🤯 Fun fact · 150 m in one minute

In Woodman's study, a simple inertial navigation system built on a MEMS IMU (an Xsens MTx) had an average position error of over 150 m after just 60 seconds, and the main culprit wasn't the accelerometers but the gyros: small orientation errors let gravity leak into the horizontal directions (Woodman 2007). The same chips that keep a drone perfectly level can't find their way across a parking lot on their own.

Check yourself

1. An accelerometer has an uncorrected bias of 0.02 m/s². With nothing else correcting it, how far off is the dead-reckoned position after 30 s?
½ · b · t² = 0.5 × 0.02 × 30² = 0.5 × 0.02 × 900 = 9 m. Double the time to 60 s and it's 36 m: error grows with time squared.
2. A drone hovers perfectly still in the air. What does its accelerometer read?
An accelerometer measures specific force (acceleration minus gravity). Hovering, the acceleration is zero, so it reads +9.81 m/s² up: the push of the propellers, just like the push of the table. Only in free fall would it read zero.
3. A delivery robot's position estimate drifts more and more while it waits at each crosswalk, even though it isn't moving. What's the best fix?
At rest, the true velocity and rotation are zero, so anything the IMU reports is bias and noise. Clamping velocity to zero stops the drift, and averaging the readings gives a fresh bias estimate. A higher rate doesn't remove a bias.
📝 TL;DR
  • An IMU = gyroscope (rotation rate) + accelerometer (specific force = acceleration − gravity: +9.81 m/s² up at rest, 0 in free fall).
  • Biases grow when integrated: accelerometer → ½·b·t² (0.01 m/s² → 18 m in 60 s); gyro → heading b·t (0.01 °/s → 0.6°/min ≈ 9 m sideways at 30 m/s).
  • Tilt errors leak gravity: 1° → 0.17 m/s². An accelerometer can't tell tilting from accelerating.
  • Fixes: average at rest and subtract, zero-velocity updates, biases as filter states, fuse with GNSS, wheels, cameras or lidar.
  • Drones are built around the IMU (PX4 EKF2 estimates its biases); Waymo carries redundant inertial systems; Tesla hasn't published its IMU use.
