Phase 2 · Lesson 6

GNSS & RTK, wheel odometry & ultrasonics

Satellites tell you where you are on Earth, give or take a few meters, until a skyscraper bounces the signal. Wheels count how far you rolled, until they spin on ice. Ultrasonic pings measure the last meter to a wall. Three humble sensors, each with a famous way to lie.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Time of flight: distance = speed × travel time. Lidar does it with light (Lesson 2.3); here satellites do it with radio, and ultrasonic sensors with sound.
  • Bias vs noise: noise is random and averages away; a bias is a steady offset that doesn't (Lesson 2.5's IMU drift was a bias, integrated).
  • Frames: a world frame fixed to the ground, and the car's body frame (x forward, y left) that moves with it (Lesson 1.1).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Stand in an open field and your phone knows where you are to within about 5 m. Walk into a downtown street between tall buildings and it may put you on the wrong side of the road, or a block away, even though more satellites are overhead than ever. Meanwhile a survey drone with a slightly fancier receiver lands on a 2 cm mark, every time.

Same satellites, same signals. What's going on in the city, and what is the drone doing differently?

The U.S. government's own GPS site gives the numbers: GPS-enabled smartphones are “typically accurate to within a 4.9 m (16 ft.) radius under open sky”, accuracy “worsens near buildings, bridges, and trees”, and high-end receivers with dual frequencies and corrections “can enable real-time positioning within a few centimeters” (gps.gov, checked Nov 2026). By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain both numbers.

Numbers first: a clock that's off by a millionth of a second

GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) is the family name: the US GPS, Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS and China's BeiDou. Each satellite broadcasts “I'm here, and it's exactly this time.” Your receiver notes when the message arrives; travel time × the speed of light = distance to that satellite.

✏️ Worked example: why the clock matters so much
  1. Light travels 299,792,458 m/s, so 1 microsecond (µs) of travel is 300 m, and 1 nanosecond is 30 cm.
  2. Satellites carry atomic clocks. Your phone has a cheap quartz clock that can easily be off by microseconds. A 1 µs error makes every distance 300 m too long.
  3. So the receiver doesn't trust its clock. It treats the clock error as one more unknown. Unknowns: x, y, z and the clock offset = 4, so it needs at least 4 satellites. Those mis-timed distances are called pseudoranges (“fake ranges”): true range + c × clock error.

Each true range puts you on a sphere around a satellite. Three spheres meet at a point; the fourth satellite checks that the clock offset you assumed makes all four agree. That's trilateration: position from distances (not angles, which would be triangulation).

💡 Big idea GNSS is time of flight with a twist: the receiver solves for its own clock error along with its position. Anything that makes a signal arrive late (a reflection, the atmosphere) looks exactly like being farther from that satellite, and gets blended into the answer.

The city: signals that take the long way round

In a street lined with tall buildings, many satellites are hidden behind a wall. Often their signal still arrives, reflected off the building across the street. That's multipath; when the direct path is blocked entirely it's called NLOS (non-line-of-sight) reception. The reflection travels farther, so the pseudorange is too long by the extra path, typically several to tens of meters in a narrow street. Unlike noise, that error is a bias: it doesn't average away while you stand there.

🧩 Predict: your receiver has a 3D city map, so it knows which satellites are hidden behind buildings. It throws all of those out. Does the fix get better?
Often it gets worse. In a north–south street the satellites you can still see directly are mostly the ones straight up and along the street. They pin down your north–south position nicely, but say almost nothing about east–west. With bad geometry (a large “dilution of precision”), even 1 m of noise turns into many meters of error across the street. You'll see it in the next playground: tick the map filter and watch the cloud of fixes stretch sideways.

What you should find: the satellites with the longest detours (low ones on the east side, whose signals bounce off the far, western wall) do the most damage; dropping them while keeping a spread of directions brings the error down to a couple of meters. Real receivers do a version of this automatically, weighting satellites by elevation and signal strength and rejecting ones whose ranges disagree with the rest (the outlier tests of Lesson 3.7). In the deepest canyons nothing works well enough, which is why cars lean on other sensors and maps there.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A robot outdoors needs an absolute position: not “3 m from where I started” (odometry and IMUs drift, Lesson 2.5) but “here on the map”. Before satellites, that meant landmarks, radio beacons or a person reading a map.
  2. What people tried. Ground radio beacons (only where someone builds them), dead reckoning from wheels and gyros (drifts without limit), matching against stars or terrain (slow, specialized). All either local or drifting.
  3. Why this works. Satellites with atomic clocks broadcast their position and time everywhere at once. Four pseudoranges give x, y, z and your clock error; 1 µs of clock error is 300 m, which is why solving for the clock matters. The errors don't grow with time: a fix in an hour is as good as a fix now, which is exactly what drifting sensors lack.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A few meters of error even under open sky (4.9 m typical for a phone); much worse in cities from blockage and multipath; nothing in tunnels, garages or indoors; updates typically only a few to tens of times per second; and the signals are weak enough to be jammed or spoofed.
  5. What came next. RTK uses the carrier wave and a nearby base station for centimeters (next section). Fusing GNSS with an IMU and odometry in a Kalman filter covers the gaps (Lessons 3.3 and 3.6). Self-driving cars also localize against detailed maps with lidar or cameras (Phase 5), so GNSS becomes one input among many.

RTK: from meters to centimeters

Normal GNSS times the code riding on the radio signal, a pattern of bits; ESA's navigation encyclopedia puts the noise of these code measurements at about 1 m. RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) instead measures the carrier, the radio wave itself, whose phase can be tracked to about 5 mm (ESA Navipedia).

✏️ Worked example: counting waves
  1. The GPS L1 carrier is 1575.42 MHz. Its wavelength is c / f = 299,792,458 / 1,575,420,000 ≈ 19 cm.
  2. The receiver can measure where in a wave the signal is very precisely, but not how many whole waves lie between it and the satellite. That unknown whole number is the integer ambiguity. Get it wrong by one and you're off by 19 cm.
  3. A base station sits at a precisely known spot, typically within 10–20 km (Navipedia). It sees nearly the same atmospheric delays and satellite errors as your rover, so it broadcasts its measurements (or corrections) over a radio or internet link. Subtracting them cancels the shared errors.
  4. With the shared errors gone and the integers solved (“fixed”), the remaining error is a few centimeters. A modern dual-band module like the u-blox ZED-F9P advertises “centimeter level accuracy in seconds”.

The catch: you need a base station (or a correction service) and a data link, and the integer fix is fragile. Pass under a bridge, lose satellites, and the receiver may drop back to meter-level “float” accuracy until it re-solves the integers.

🧩 Predict: your robot stands still and averages 100 ordinary GNSS fixes, each with 5 m of error. Is the average now good to 5 / √100 = 0.5 m?
Only if the errors were independent, and they mostly aren't. 5 / √N is the rule for random noise (the law of large numbers: Math course 4.7). Multipath and atmospheric delays change slowly, over minutes, so 100 fixes taken in a few seconds share almost the same bias; you'd average the noise down and keep the bias. That's why RTK removes errors by differencing against a base station instead of by waiting.

Wheel odometry: counting turns, and when the turns lie

A wheel encoder counts ticks as the wheel turns: a typical one gives a few hundred to a few thousand ticks per revolution. Distance = (ticks ÷ ticks per revolution) × 2π × tyre radius. Compare the left and right wheels and you also get the turn: if the right wheel rolled farther, you turned left. This is wheel odometry, and it's in every car, robot vacuum and warehouse robot.

  • Scale error: the software assumes a tyre radius. A tyre 4% smaller than assumed (worn, under-inflated) makes every distance 4% long: 0.8 m after 20 m.
  • Slip: on ice, gravel or wet paint a wheel spins without the car moving that much. The encoder happily counts the spinning. If only one side slips, odometry thinks you turned, and a heading error grows into a big sideways error.
  • The fix: cross-check. A gyro (Lesson 2.5) measures turning directly; if the wheels claim a turn the gyro didn't feel, a wheel is slipping. Cars do this constantly for traction and stability control.

Ultrasonics: the parking sensors

The little round dimples in a car's bumper are ultrasonic sensors: a speaker sends a chirp of sound above human hearing and times the echo. Sound is slow, about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, which makes the timing easy and the hardware cheap.

✏️ Worked example: one echo
  1. The echo returns after 5.83 ms. Distance = 343 × 0.00583 / 2 = 1.00 m (divide by 2: there and back, as with lidar).
  2. On a 0 °C morning sound travels at about 331 m/s. A sensor that assumes 343 m/s reads that same 1.00 m wall as 343 × (2 / 331) / 2 ≈ 1.04 m: a 4 cm error, harmless for parking.
  3. Range is short (typically up to a few meters), the beam is wide (it says “something is there”, not exactly where), and soft or angled surfaces can absorb or deflect the ping. Good for the last meter; useless at 30 m/s on a highway.
🧠 Analogy: three ways to know where you are in a city

GNSS is asking several far-away friends with perfect watches to shout the time: great in a park, confusing between buildings where the echoes come back late. Wheel odometry is counting your own steps: precise over a block, hopeless after a slip on ice. Ultrasonics is stretching your arm out in the dark: only tells you about the wall right in front of you. A good navigator uses all three, and trusts each where it's strong.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Your receiver solves the position for you, but writing the solver once makes everything above click: it's a few iterations of least squares. On ROS 2, GNSS arrives as a sensor_msgs/NavSatFix (latitude, longitude, altitude and a position covariance) and robot_localization's navsat_transform_node turns it into the robot's map frame for fusion with odometry and IMU. On PX4 drones, RTK is a supported setup: the base station first runs a “survey-in” to learn its own position.

import numpy as np

c = 299_792_458.0                                   # m/s
sats = np.array([[15600e3,  7540e3, 20140e3],       # satellite positions (m, Earth-centred)
                 [18760e3,  2750e3, 18610e3],
                 [17610e3, 14630e3, 13480e3],
                 [19170e3,   610e3, 18390e3]])
truth, clock = np.array([-40e3, 15e3, 6370e3]), 3e-6  # receiver, and a 3 µs clock error
rho = np.linalg.norm(sats - truth, axis=1) + c * clock  # measured pseudoranges

x = np.zeros(4)                                     # [x, y, z, c·dt], start at Earth's centre
for _ in range(8):                                  # Gauss–Newton
    d = np.linalg.norm(sats - x[:3], axis=1)
    H = np.hstack([(x[:3] - sats) / d[:, None], np.ones((4, 1))])
    x += np.linalg.lstsq(H, rho - (d + x[3]), rcond=None)[0]
print(np.round(x[:3] - truth, 3), round(x[3] / c * 1e6, 3), "µs")   # [0. 0. 0.] 3.0 µs

The fourth column of H, all ones, is the clock: one shared unknown that shifts every pseudorange equally. Feed in a reflected satellite with 20 m added to its pseudorange and watch the solution move by meters, not by 20 m: least squares spreads the lie across all four unknowns.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla took ultrasonic sensors out of its cars. Its support page said that from early October 2022, Model 3 and Model Y built for certain regions (by January 2023: North America, Europe, the Middle East, Taiwan and Korea) were no longer built with the 12 ultrasonic sensors on the bumpers, with Model S and Model X following in 2023. A camera-based “occupancy network” replaced their inputs, and Park Assist, Autopark, Summon and Smart Summon were temporarily limited on those cars at first. Tesla hasn't published how its driving software uses GNSS.

Sources: Tesla support page (archived Mar 2023)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo doesn't depend on GNSS where it's weakest. In its 2020 “Driver Handbook” post on mapping, Waymo says it built detailed mapping technology that helps its cars “navigate places even where GPS struggles, like tunnels or between skyscrapers”: the car locates itself by matching what its sensors see against that map (Phase 5). Waymo hasn't published its GNSS hardware or how heavily it weights it.

Sources: Waymo, Sep 2020

🛸 Drones (PX4)

For PX4, GNSS is “recommended”, needed for missions and some automatic modes; gyros, accelerometers, a magnetometer and a barometer are the mandatory minimum. PX4 supports RTK GNSS for “centimeter-level accuracy” (survey, precision landing); its docs suggest lowering the EKF's GPS noise settings, which assume meter-level accuracy by default. Indoors or under bridges, drones switch to optical flow, rangefinders or vision; Skydio says its drones navigate “even where GPS isn't available”.

Sources: PX4 sensors, PX4 RTK, Skydio

Why they differ: a car drives in cities, where GNSS is worst, and needs lane-level accuracy everywhere, so Waymo localizes against maps and Tesla against what its cameras see; ultrasonics added little that cameras couldn't do once the networks were good enough, in Tesla's judgment. A drone flies in open sky most of the time, where GNSS is at its best and RTK is cheap to add, so it leans on satellites far more.

🎤 Interview angle

“Our robot's GPS is good in the parking lot and terrible downtown. Why, and what would you do?” A strong answer:

  • Explains pseudoranges and the clock unknown (4 satellites; 1 µs = 300 m), then multipath / NLOS: reflected signals arrive late, so ranges are biased, not just noisy.
  • Mentions geometry (dilution of precision): rejecting blocked satellites can leave a narrow spread and make errors worse.
  • Proposes fixes in layers: satellite weighting and outlier rejection; RTK where centimeters matter; fusing with IMU and wheel odometry in a Kalman filter (with the GNSS covariance taken seriously); map-based localization for cities.
  • Knows odometry's own failure modes: tyre-radius scale error and slip, caught by cross-checking against a gyro.
🏭 In the real world
Your phone's blue dot 📜 Public service · since the 1990s gps.gov: GPS accuracy

How: a single-frequency (or, in newer phones, dual-frequency) receiver solves pseudoranges for position and clock, then blends in Wi-Fi and motion sensors.

Why this method: it's free, global and needs no infrastructure beyond the satellites; 4.9 m under open sky is plenty for walking directions.

Centimeter drones and survey robots 🧰 Open source PX4: RTK GNSS

How: a base station surveys itself in for a few minutes, then streams corrections to the drone; PX4's estimator is retuned to trust the now-centimeter fixes.

Why this method: mapping, inspection and precision landing need centimeters, and open sky lets RTK hold its fix.

Dual-band RTK modules 🏢 Company · current u-blox ZED-F9P

How: a small module tracks all major constellations on multiple frequency bands and resolves RTK ambiguities quickly.

Why this method: two frequencies let the receiver measure and remove much of the atmospheric delay, so the integer fix comes in seconds instead of minutes.

Fusing GPS on ground robots 🧰 Open source robot_localization: integrating GPS

How: navsat_transform_node converts latitude and longitude into the robot's world frame so an EKF can fuse it with wheel odometry and an IMU.

Why this method: odometry is smooth but drifts; GNSS is jumpy but doesn't drift. Together they give a smooth, anchored estimate (Lesson 3.6).

🤯 Fun fact · RTK measures in wavelengths of a radio wave

The GPS L1 carrier wave is about 19 cm long (299,792,458 m/s ÷ 1575.42 MHz). RTK's centimeter accuracy comes from tracking where in that wave the signal is, to a few millimeters (ESA Navipedia quotes about 5 mm of carrier-phase noise), across a distance of over 20,000 km to the satellite. The hard part isn't measuring the fraction of a wave; it's knowing how many hundred million whole waves come before it.

🌉 How it connects

GNSS is the drift-free anchor that the Kalman filter (Lesson 3.3) blends with the drifting IMU (Lesson 2.5) and wheel odometry; Lesson 3.6 does exactly that, and Lesson 3.7 throws out multipath outliers with innovation gates. Lesson 2.7 adds the last ingredient for fusion: time stamps, because GNSS also delivers a very precise clock. Phase 5 shows how cars localize against maps when GNSS fails. The averaging rule (σ/√N) and why it needs independence are in the law of large numbers in the Math course; the least-squares solve is optimization on a tiny scale.

Check yourself

1. A receiver's clock is 2 µs off and it does not solve for the clock. Roughly how wrong is every pseudorange?
c × 2 µs = 299,792,458 × 0.000002 ≈ 600 m. That's why every receiver treats the clock offset as a fourth unknown and needs at least four satellites.
2. A delivery robot's GNSS position is fine in the suburbs but jumps 15 m sideways on one downtown street, even while it's parked. What's the most likely cause?
Reflections add extra path, so the ranges are biased, not just noisy, and they change slowly: waiting and averaging won't remove them. Weight or reject suspicious satellites, fuse with odometry and IMU, or localize against a map.
3. A warehouse robot's odometry says it drove 20.8 m, but the floor markings show 20.0 m, on a straight run with no slipping. What's the fix?
A consistent 4% overshoot is a scale error: the tyres are smaller than the radius in the code (wear, pressure, load). Drive a measured distance and set radius = assumed × 20.0 / 20.8. A gyro catches slip-induced turns, not scale; GNSS doesn't work indoors.
📝 TL;DR
  • GNSS = time of flight from satellites. The receiver solves x, y, z and its clock (1 µs = 300 m), so it needs 4 satellites.
  • Typical phone: 4.9 m under open sky. Cities add multipath/NLOS biases that don't average away; geometry matters as much as the number of satellites.
  • RTK: carrier phase (19 cm waves, millimeter precision) + a base station within ~10–20 km → centimeters, if the integer ambiguities stay fixed.
  • Wheel odometry: smooth but drifts; tyre-radius errors scale distance, slip fakes turns. Cross-check with a gyro.
  • Ultrasonics: 343 m/s echo timing, meters of range, great for parking. Tesla dropped its 12 from Oct 2022 for a camera occupancy network; Waymo leans on maps where GPS struggles; drones love RTK in open sky.
