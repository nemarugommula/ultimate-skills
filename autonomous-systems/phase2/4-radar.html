Radar: Doppler & all-weather sensing

Radar is the sensor that doesn't care about fog, rain or night, and the only common one that measures an object's speed directly, in a single echo. It's also blurry, and it famously can't tell a stopped car from a bridge. Here's why both are true, and how imaging radar fixes the second.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Time of flight: a pulse goes out and comes back, so distance = speed × round-trip time ÷ 2. Light (and radio) covers about 30 cm per nanosecond (Lesson 2.3).
  • Wavelength and frequency: for any wave, wavelength λ = speed ÷ frequency. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
  • Bearing vs range: a camera measures directions well and distance badly (Lesson 2.1). Keep that in mind: radar is the mirror image.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're driving at 90 km/h in thick fog. Your camera sees 40 m of grey. Your radar reports two echoes straight ahead, both closing at exactly 90 km/h: one 150 m away, one 180 m away. One is a car stopped in your lane. The other is a bridge over the road.

Which one should you brake for? And why are they both closing at exactly your own speed? Hold your answer: this exact puzzle shaped how driver-assistance radar was built for years.

Tesla spelled it out in 2016: radar photons “travel easily through fog, dust, rain and snow, but anything metallic looks like a mirror,” and an overhead sign or bridge where the road dips “often looks like a collision course” (Tesla blog, Sept 2016, archived). Five years later Tesla removed radar from its cars altogether, while Waymo built one of the first imaging radars for self-driving. Same physics, opposite bets.

🧩 Lock in a guess: why do the stopped car and the bridge both close at exactly 90 km/h?
Because you are doing 90 km/h and they aren't moving. Radar measures closing speed: how fast the gap shrinks. Anything standing still (a bridge, a sign, a parked car, a soda can, the road itself) closes at your own speed. A classic radar sees only range, a rough left-right angle and closing speed; with no reliable height, a stopped car and a bridge 5 m overhead look nearly the same. You'll fix this in Playground 3.

What “radar” means here

Radar (radio detection and ranging) sends out radio waves and listens for echoes. Car radars use millimeter waves: a typical automotive radar works at 76–81 GHz, where the wavelength is about 4 mm (TI, mmWave fundamentals). That buys three superpowers and one weakness:

Range from a chirp (FMCW)

Lidar times a short pulse. Timing a radio pulse to the nanosecond is hard and expensive, so most car radars use a cleverer trick called FMCW, frequency-modulated continuous wave. The radar transmits a chirp: a tone whose frequency sweeps steadily upward, say by B = 1 GHz in 50 µs. The echo is the same chirp, just late. Mix the outgoing and incoming chirps and you get their difference in frequency, the beat frequency, which is proportional to the delay and therefore to the distance.

✏️ Worked example: a car 50 m away
  1. Round-trip delay: τ = 2R / c = 2 × 50 / (3 × 10⁸) = 333 ns.
  2. Chirp slope: S = B / T = 1 GHz / 50 µs = 2 × 10¹³ Hz per second (the frequency climbs 20 MHz every microsecond).
  3. Beat frequency: while the echo is 333 ns late, the transmitter has climbed S × τ = 2 × 10¹³ × 333 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 6.7 MHz higher. Twice as far → 13.3 MHz. A frequency is easy to measure precisely (a Fourier transform, the “range FFT”), a nanosecond delay is not.
  4. Range resolution: two echoes can be told apart if their beat frequencies differ by at least one “bin”, which works out to ΔR = c / (2B). With B = 1 GHz that's 15 cm; with the full B = 4 GHz of the 77–81 GHz band, TI's paper gets 3.75 cm.
fbeat = S · 2R / c      ΔR = c / (2B) The beat frequency grows in proportion to distance. How finely you can separate two objects in range depends only on how wide a band of frequencies the chirp sweeps: more bandwidth, sharper range.

Speed in one echo: the Doppler effect

You know the Doppler effect from an ambulance siren: higher pitch coming toward you, lower going away. Radar uses the same thing. If the target closes on you at speed v, the echo comes back shifted in frequency by

fD = 2 v / λ The Doppler shift is twice the closing speed divided by the wavelength (twice, because the wave is compressed on the way out to the target and again on the way back). Short wavelength → big shift → precise speed.
✏️ Worked example: numbers at 77 GHz
  • Wavelength: λ = c / f = 3 × 10⁸ / 77 × 10⁹ ≈ 3.9 mm.
  • Closing at 1 m/s (walking pace): fD = 2 × 1 / 0.0039 ≈ 513 Hz.
  • Closing at 30 m/s (108 km/h, a stopped car seen from the highway): ≈ 15.4 kHz.
  • An older 24 GHz radar (λ ≈ 12.5 mm) sees only 4.8 kHz for the same 30 m/s: the shorter wavelength makes 77 GHz about three times more sensitive to speed.

In practice an FMCW radar sends a burst of many chirps and measures how the echo's phase creeps from chirp to chirp, a second FFT (the “Doppler FFT”). Either way, every echo arrives tagged with its closing speed, from a single radar frame. A camera or lidar has to compare positions across several frames to get the same number, and each frame's error makes that estimate noisy.

💡 Big idea Radar is a range-and-speed sensor with blurry angles: the opposite of a camera, which has sharp angles and no range. Each radar echo carries a distance (from the chirp), a closing speed (from Doppler) and a rough direction. That's why the two are such natural partners, and why removing radar means asking cameras and neural networks to estimate speed from how things move across frames.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Adaptive cruise control and emergency braking need the distance and closing speed of the car ahead, reliably, at highway range, in rain, fog and at night. Cameras of the 1990s and 2000s couldn't do that, and laser rangefinders were blinded by fog and spray.
  2. What people tried. Pulsed radar like air-traffic radar (needs very fast, expensive timing to get centimeters), stereo cameras (depth error grows quickly with distance and fails in fog), ultrasonics (a few meters at most, Lesson 2.6).
  3. Why this works. Millimeter waves slip through weather, FMCW turns a nanosecond delay into an easy-to-measure frequency, and Doppler gives speed for free. At 77 GHz, λ ≈ 3.9 mm, so 30 m/s closing gives a 15.4 kHz shift: a big, clean number, available from a single frame. It's cheap, solid-state and works at 150 m+ at night in fog.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Angles are blurry: a radar with 12 virtual antennas has a beam about 10° wide, so two cars side by side at 100 m (2° apart) merge into one blob. Classic radars had little or no elevation, so stationary things (bridges, overhead signs, parked cars) all close at your own speed and look alike; many systems simply filtered out stationary echoes, which also hides a stopped car. Metal acts like a mirror: a concave soda can can look huge, and echoes bounce off guardrails into ghost targets. Wood and plastic are nearly invisible.
  5. What came next. Imaging radar, also called 4D radar (range, left-right angle, elevation and Doppler), uses many more antennas to make a sparse point cloud with heights: the bridge goes up, the stopped car stays on the road. Waymo built one of the first for self-driving. And learned fusion combines radar's range and speed with a camera's sharp angles (Lesson 6.6).

The stationary-object problem

Back to the hook. The radar's own echoes can't distinguish “stopped car in my lane” from “bridge across my lane” without height information. For years many systems played it safe the other way: ignore anything that isn't moving, because the road is full of stationary clutter (signs, poles, guardrails, manhole covers). That kills the false alarms, and it also means the radar ignores a car stopped at the end of a traffic jam. Tesla's 2016 post describes its workaround: build up radar “snapshots” every tenth of a second, and use the fleet to record which stationary radar objects cars routinely drive past safely, a geocoded whitelist of harmless bridges and signs.

🧩 Predict: in clear weather, the classic fix is “only brake for a stationary radar echo if the camera confirms it's a car.” What happens to that rule in thick fog?
It fails exactly when you need radar most. In fog the camera can't confirm anything, so the radar's echo from the stopped car gets thrown away, and you're back to not braking. To brake in fog you need the radar to decide on its own, which means it must know height (is it overhead?) and extent (is it car-sized or soda-can-sized?): that's what imaging radar adds. Why radar angles are blurry, and how imaging radar sharpens them

A radar finds an echo's direction by comparing its arrival at several receive antennas: an echo from the left reaches the left antenna a hair earlier, which shows up as a phase difference. With antennas spaced half a wavelength apart, a rule of thumb (straight ahead) is that an array of N antennas can separate two echoes about 2/N radians apart.

  • Two cars side by side at 100 m, 3.5 m apart, are only 2 × atan(1.75 / 100) ≈ 0.035 rad ≈ 2° apart.
  • To separate them you need N ≈ 2 / 0.035 ≈ 57 antennas' worth of aperture. A small radar with 12 gets a beam about 2/12 rad ≈ 9.5° wide: one blob.
  • The trick that makes it affordable is MIMO (multiple-input, multiple-output): each of T transmit antennas is listened to by each of R receive antennas, giving T × R “virtual” antennas. 3 transmitters and 4 receivers act like 12.

What you should find: cutting fog visibility blinds the camera but leaves the radar untouched, and the radar's sharpness depends only on antenna count, not on weather. That's why imaging radars carry dozens of transmit and receive channels, and why their output is a sparse point cloud rather than a few blobs.

🧠 Analogy: a bat with a blurry eye

A bat in the dark knows exactly how far the moth is and whether it's flying toward or away (pitch shift), but its sense of exact direction is coarse. A camera is the opposite: a hawk's eye that sees every feather but has to guess how far away the rabbit is. Put the bat and the hawk in the same head and you get something better than either: that's camera + radar fusion.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Radar chips do the FFTs on board and usually hand you a list of detections. Here's what happens inside, in NumPy: simulate the beat signal of one FMCW chirp for two targets and find their ranges with an FFT. In ROS 2, the radar_msgs package defines RadarReturn (range, azimuth, elevation, doppler_velocity, amplitude) and RadarTrack messages.

import numpy as np

c, B, T = 3e8, 1e9, 50e-6          # 1 GHz sweep in 50 µs
S = B / T                            # chirp slope, Hz per second
fs, n = 20e6, 1000                  # ADC: 20 MHz × 50 µs = 1000 samples
t = np.arange(n) / fs

ranges = [30.0, 30.5]                 # two targets, 50 cm apart (resolution c/2B = 15 cm)
beat = sum(np.exp(2j * np.pi * (S * 2 * R / c) * t) for R in ranges)

spec = np.abs(np.fft.fft(beat * np.hanning(n)))
r_axis = np.fft.fftfreq(n, 1 / fs) * c / (2 * S)   # beat frequency → metres
peaks = [i for i in range(1, n // 2) if spec[i] > spec[i-1] and spec[i] > spec[i+1] and spec[i] > 0.5 * spec.max()]
print(np.round(r_axis[peaks], 2))           # [30.  30.45] (bins are 15 cm wide)

v, lam = 30.0, c / 77e9
print(f"Doppler at 30 m/s: {2 * v / lam:.0f} Hz")   # ≈ 15400 Hz

Real radars add a second FFT across chirps (Doppler) and a third across antennas (angle), then a detector such as CFAR (constant false alarm rate) that picks peaks above the local noise. Open-source tooling is thinner than for cameras and lidar; datasets with radar include nuScenes; the original Waymo Open Dataset release had lidar and cameras only.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla added radar to its cars in October 2014 and in 2016 made it a primary braking sensor, with the fleet-built whitelist of harmless bridges and signs described above. Then it reversed course: in 2021 it began “Tesla Vision” by removing radar from Model 3 and Model Y (North America from May 2021), followed by Model S and Model X in 2022, relying on cameras alone. In his CVPR 2021 talk Andrej Karpathy showed networks estimating other cars' depth and velocity from video, the job radar used to do.

Sources: Tesla blog 2016 (archived), Tesla Vision support page (archived 2023), CVPR'21 keynote

🚕 Waymo

Waymo kept radar and made it sharper. Its 5th-generation announcement (2020) described “one of the world's first imaging radar system for self-driving”. Its Waymo Driver page says radar provides “an object's distance and speed” and is “effective in rain, fog, and snow.” The 6th-generation Waymo Driver (rider-only from February 2026) uses an imaging radar improved for rain and snow. Radar's direct speed is exactly what a tracker wants on the first frame.

Sources: Waymo, Mar 2020, Waymo Driver, Waymo, Feb 2026

🛸 Drones (PX4)

Small drones mostly skip radar for obstacle avoidance (weight, power, cost) and use cameras. Where radar shines is altitude: PX4 supports radar altimeters such as the Ainstein US-D1 (0.5–50 m, 100 Hz, works “in all weather conditions and over all terrain types (including water)”) and the Smartmicro Drone Altimeter (1–175 m), useful where lasers and cameras struggle, like over water, dust or crops.

Sources: PX4: distance sensors

Why they differ: Tesla found radar's false alarms and blurry angles more trouble than help once its cameras and networks could estimate depth and speed, and dropping it cut cost and conflicting inputs. Waymo, building cars with no human backup, wants an independent sensor that keeps working in fog and measures speed directly, and paid for imaging radar to fix the angle problem. A drone mostly needs to know its height over tricky ground, so it uses radar just for that.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do so many vehicles use radar, and what are its failure modes?” A strong answer:

  • Says what it measures directly: range (FMCW beat frequency, resolution c/2B) and radial speed (Doppler, fD = 2v/λ; 77 GHz, 30 m/s → about 15 kHz), plus coarse angle.
  • Explains weather robustness (millimeter waves vs droplets) and why that complements cameras and lidar.
  • Names the failures: angular resolution (≈ 2/N rad), stationary clutter and the bridge/overhead sign problem, multipath ghosts off guardrails, weak returns from people and plastic.
  • Knows the fix: imaging (4D) radar with MIMO virtual arrays and elevation, and fusing radar's speed into trackers (Lesson 6.5).

🤯 Fun fact · the soda can that looks like a truck

Tesla's 2016 post warned that a discarded soda can with its concave bottom facing you can, to radar, “appear to be a large and dangerous obstacle,” because a dish-shaped metal surface amplifies the reflected signal “to many times its actual size,” while wood or painted plastic is “almost as transparent as glass” (Tesla, 2016). How strongly something reflects radar depends on its material and shape, not its size: the same reason stealth aircraft are faceted.

Check yourself

1. A 77 GHz radar (λ ≈ 3.9 mm) sees an echo with a Doppler shift of +5,130 Hz. What's the closing speed?
v = fD × λ / 2 = 5130 × 0.0039 / 2 ≈ 10 m/s (36 km/h). Each 1 m/s of closing speed adds about 513 Hz at 77 GHz.
2. A car with a classic radar cruise control, set to ignore stationary echoes, approaches the end of a traffic jam where the last car has fully stopped. What's the risk?
Stationary objects all close at your own speed. Filtering them out removes clutter (signs, bridges, poles) and the stopped car with it. Camera confirmation, elevation from imaging radar, or tracking the car before it stopped are the fixes.
3. Your radar can't separate two motorbikes riding side by side 100 m ahead. Which change helps most?
Side by side means same range, different angle. Bandwidth sharpens range, not angle. Angular resolution is roughly 2/N radians for N virtual antennas, so more transmitters × receivers (MIMO) is the fix. A longer wavelength would make it worse for the same antenna size.
📝 TL;DR
  • Radar (typically 76–81 GHz, λ ≈ 4 mm) sees through fog, rain and dark and measures range and closing speed directly.
  • FMCW: a chirp's echo gives a beat frequency ∝ distance; range resolution c/2B (1 GHz → 15 cm, 4 GHz → 3.75 cm).
  • Doppler: fD = 2v/λ; at 77 GHz, 1 m/s ≈ 513 Hz and 30 m/s ≈ 15.4 kHz.
  • Weaknesses: blurry angles (≈ 2/N rad), stationary objects all look alike without elevation, metal mirrors and ghosts.
  • Imaging (4D) radar adds elevation and many virtual antennas. Waymo uses it; Tesla removed radar in 2021–2022; drones use radar mainly as an altimeter.
