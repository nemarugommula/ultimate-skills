Phase 2 · Lesson 1

Cameras: the pinhole model

Every camera on a Tesla, a Waymo and a Skydio drone squashes the 3D world onto a flat grid of pixels. One tiny formula says exactly where each point lands. Learn it and you'll know why far cars look small, why a single photo can't tell a toy from a truck, and how to pick a lens.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Frames: every sensor has its own coordinate frame. A camera frame points z forward (out of the lens), x right, y down; the car's frame is x forward, y left, z up (Lesson 1.1).
  • Similar triangles: two triangles with the same angles have proportional sides. If one is twice as far, everything in it is twice as big. That's the whole lesson, really.
  • Pixels: an image is a grid. We name a pixel by its column u (counted from the left) and row v (counted from the top).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A car's camera sees a family hatchback 20 m ahead. The car is 1.5 m tall and fills 75 pixels of the image, top to bottom. The hatchback brakes, the gap grows, and now it's 40 m ahead.

How tall is it in the image now? Half as tall (37.5 px)? A quarter, because area shrinks with the square? Or does perspective shrink it in some trickier way? And the deeper question: if a camera only ever sees pixel sizes, how could it know whether that's a real car at 40 m or a toy car at 4 m?

Tesla bets its entire driving system on exactly this kind of reasoning: its Model Y manual lists eight exterior cameras and no lidar or radar for driving, and since 2021 Tesla has called this camera-only approach “Tesla Vision” (Tesla Model Y manual, checked Nov 2026). Every pixel those cameras produce follows the formula you'll learn in the next ten minutes.

🧩 Lock in a guess: 75 px tall at 20 m. At 40 m it is: (a) 37.5 px, (b) 18.75 px, (c) about 53 px.
(a) 37.5 px. Image size is inversely proportional to distance: twice as far, half as tall (and half as wide). The area it covers drops to a quarter, which is where (b) comes from, but height and width each just halve. Why? Light from the top and the bottom of the car travels in straight lines through one tiny hole; the triangle on the far side of the hole is a shrunken copy of the triangle out in the world. Double the long side, and the short side halves.

Numbers first: the camera that's just a hole

Picture a dark box with a pinhole in one wall. Light from each point in the world travels in a straight line through the hole and hits the back wall, painting an upside-down image. Put a pixel sensor on that wall and you have a camera. A real lens gathers far more light than a pinhole, but for where a point lands it behaves almost exactly like one (Lesson 2.2 handles the “almost”).

✏️ Worked example: where does a pedestrian's head land?

A camera has a focal length of f = 800 pixels (the distance from the pinhole to the sensor, measured in pixel widths) and a 640 × 480 image whose center is pixel (cx, cy) = (320, 240). It sits 1.4 m above the road, looking straight ahead.

A pedestrian 1.5 m tall stands Z = 20 m ahead and X = 2 m to the right. In the camera frame (z forward, x right, y down) their head is at (X, Y, Z) = (2, 1.4 − 1.5, 20) = (2, −0.1, 20), and their feet at (2, 1.4, 20).

  1. Column (same for head and feet): u = f·X/Z + cx = 800 × 2 / 20 + 320 = 80 + 320 = 400.
  2. Head row: v = f·Y/Z + cy = 800 × (−0.1) / 20 + 240 = −4 + 240 = 236 (just above the image center, because the head is 10 cm above the camera).
  3. Feet row: v = 800 × 1.4 / 20 + 240 = 56 + 240 = 296.
  4. Height in the image: 296 − 236 = 60 px, which is just f × 1.5 m / 20 m = 800 × 1.5 / 20 = 60.

Move them to 40 m and every offset from the center halves: u = 360, feet at row 268, height 30 px. The Z in the denominator is the entire story of perspective.

u = f · X / Z + cx     v = f · Y / Z + cy To find where a point lands: divide its sideways and up-down offsets by its distance Z, scale by the focal length f (in pixels), then shift so (0, 0) is the image's top-left corner instead of its center. Size in the image = f × real size ÷ distance.
💡 Big idea A camera measures directions, not distances. Each pixel is a ray leaving the camera at one angle; everything along that ray, near or far, lands on the same pixel. Dividing by Z is how depth gets thrown away, and getting it back is what stereo, motion, lidar and neural networks spend the rest of this course doing.

🏆 Challenge: make the 1.5 m pedestrian exactly 100 px tall (within half a pixel) while staying fully inside the image. Then try it again with a different focal length: what's the rule linking f and the distance you need?

What you should find: the height in pixels is always f × 1.5 ÷ Z, so 100 px needs Z = f × 0.015. With f = 800 that's 12 m; with f = 1600 (a “zoom” lens) it's 24 m. Drag the feet up toward the horizon line and the distance shoots off: near the horizon, one pixel of row covers many meters of road. That's why camera distance estimates get worse the farther away things are.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A perception system needs to turn pixels into positions: “that blob at column 400, row 296 is a person 20 m ahead and 2 m right.” Without a model of how the camera forms images, a pixel is just a color, and there's no way to connect it to the meters a planner needs.
  2. What people tried. Hand-made lookup tables (“row 296 means about 20 m”), measured on a test track for one camera and one mounting height. They break the moment you change the lens, the mount or the car, and they say nothing about objects off the road.
  3. Why this works. Light travels in straight lines through the lens center, so similar triangles give the exact mapping: u = f·X/Z + cx. Four numbers (f in x and y, cx, cy) describe the whole camera. Example: f = 800 px, a 1.5 m person at 20 m is 800 × 1.5 / 20 = 60 px tall; flip it around, a 60 px person must be 20 m away, if you know they're 1.5 m tall.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It throws depth away: a 1.5 m person at 20 m and a 0.75 m child at 10 m give the same 60 px. You must know f and cx, cy precisely (calibration, Lesson 2.2), and real lenses bend straight lines, especially wide-angle ones. Rolling-shutter sensors don't snap all rows at the same instant, so fast motion skews the picture (also 2.2).
  5. What came next. The intrinsic matrix K and distortion models make the pinhole precise (2.2). Stereo and motion recover depth from two views (Phase 4). Lidar measures depth directly (2.3). And neural networks learn depth from context, as in Tesla's camera-only system (Lesson 6.3).

Focal length is a zoom knob: field of view

Look at the formula again: f multiplies everything. A big f (a telephoto or “narrow” lens) makes things big but sees a thin slice of the world; a small f (a wide-angle lens) sees a broad sweep but makes everything tiny. The angle a camera can see across, its horizontal field of view (HFOV), comes from the image edge: the last column is W/2 pixels from the center, so

HFOV = 2 · atan( W / (2 f) ) The field of view is twice the angle whose tangent is half the image width divided by the focal length. Wider sensor or shorter focal length → wider view.

Detectors typically need a car to cover at least a couple of dozen pixels to recognize it reliably (a rough rule of thumb, not a law). At 4.8 px, a wide-angle camera can't really see that car at 150 m. Pixels are the currency, and you only have W of them to spread across your field of view.

🧩 Predict: you need to see a cyclist 8 m ahead and 6 m to the side (they're about to cross) and a stopped car 150 m ahead at 20 px or more. Can a single 1280-pixel-wide camera do both?
No. The cyclist is atan(6/8) ≈ 37° off-axis, so you need an HFOV of at least 74°, which with 1280 px means f ≤ 853 px. The far car needs f × 1.8 / 150 ≥ 20, so f ≥ 1667 px. The two ranges don't overlap. Your options: buy more pixels (about 2,500+ px wide, so a 4K-class sensor), or use two cameras, one wide and one narrow. Cars and drones do both. Same picture, different worlds: why one camera can't see depth

Here's the uncomfortable consequence of dividing by Z. Take any scene, make every object twice as big and twice as far: X/Z and Y/Z don't change, so not a single pixel changes. A toy car 0.75 m tall at 10 m and a real car 1.5 m tall at 20 m are the same picture. This is called scale ambiguity, and it's the central weakness of a single (monocular) camera.

🧩 Predict: one photo shows a car whose wheels touch the road at row 296. You know the camera is 1.4 m above a flat road and f = 800 px (horizon at row 240). Is the car's distance still ambiguous?
No: the road breaks the tie. The ground contact point is 296 − 240 = 56 rows below the horizon, and the ground is 1.4 m below the camera, so Z = f × 1.4 / 56 = 800 × 1.4 / 56 = 20 m. Extra knowledge (a flat road, a known camera height, a known object size) removes the ambiguity. Driver-assistance cameras have long used this flat-ground trick for distance to the car ahead, and it fails exactly where you'd expect: on hills, and for things that aren't touching the road.
🧠 Analogy: the forced-perspective photo

Tourists in Pisa “hold up” the leaning tower with one hand. The photo is honest about directions: the hand and the tower really do line up from where the camera stands. It's silent about distance, so your brain picks the wrong one. A camera's perception system faces the same trick on every frame, and it needs extra clues (where things touch the ground, how big things usually are, how they move) to pick the right world.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Projecting points is three lines of NumPy; OpenCV's cv2.projectPoints does the same with a full camera model (rotation, translation and the lens distortion of Lesson 2.2). Every perception stack, from ROS 2 image pipelines to autonomous-driving datasets, ships camera parameters in exactly this form.

import numpy as np
import cv2

K = np.array([[800.0,   0.0, 320.0],      # fx, 0, cx
              [  0.0, 800.0, 240.0],      # 0, fy, cy
              [  0.0,   0.0,   1.0]])
P_cam = np.array([[2.0, -0.1, 20.0],          # head (camera frame: x right, y down, z forward)
                  [2.0,  1.4, 20.0]])         # feet

uvw = (K @ P_cam.T).T                          # multiply by K ...
uv = uvw[:, :2] / uvw[:, 2:]                # ... then divide by Z
print(uv)                                      # [[400. 236.] [400. 296.]]

# Same with OpenCV: no rotation, no translation, no distortion
uv_cv, _ = cv2.projectPoints(P_cam, np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3), K, np.zeros(5))
print(uv_cv.reshape(-1, 2))                     # identical

Watch the frames: OpenCV wants points in the camera frame (z forward, y down). Points in the car's frame (x forward, z up) must first be rotated and shifted into it: that's the extrinsic transform from Lesson 1.5, and forgetting it is the most common reason projected points land in the sky.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Cameras are Tesla's only driving sensors. The current Model Y owner's manual lists eight exterior cameras: one above the front bumper grille, two on the windshield above the mirror, one in each door pillar, one on each front fender and one above the rear license plate (the 2020–2024 Model Y manual listed three windshield cameras instead). Mixing positions and lenses is the field-of-view trade-off you just played with: no single camera covers both the crossing cyclist and the car 150 m ahead. Tesla's public talks (AI Day 2021) describe networks that fuse all cameras into one 3D view of the world, rather than trusting any one camera's flat-ground guess.

Sources: Model Y manual: cameras, Tesla AI Day 2021

🚕 Waymo

Waymo uses cameras alongside lidar and radar, so its cameras don't have to solve depth alone: lidar points give the distance and cameras add color, texture, traffic-light state and long range. Its 5th-generation system announcement (2020) said its long-range cameras can identify pedestrians and stop signs more than 500 m away. The 6th-generation Waymo Driver (rider-only from February 2026) uses a 17-megapixel imager and fewer than half the cameras of the 5th generation: more pixels per camera, fewer cameras.

Sources: Waymo, Mar 2020, Waymo, Feb 2026

🛸 Drones (Skydio)

Skydio's X10 sees obstacles with cameras, not lidar: six navigation cameras in a trinocular arrangement on top and bottom, each with a 200° diagonal field of view, giving 360° obstacle coverage out to 20 m (Skydio's spec sheet). Such extreme wide-angle lenses are far from a pinhole at the edges, so the calibration and distortion models of Lesson 2.2 matter even more. Overlapping views also let the drone triangulate depth instead of guessing it (Phase 4).

Sources: Skydio X10 specs, Skydio Autonomy

Why they differ: all three use the same pinhole math; they differ in what else they trust. Tesla gets depth purely from cameras plus learning, so it needs many overlapping views and a lot of data. Waymo measures depth with lidar and uses cameras for meaning and range. A small drone can't carry much weight or power, so it uses a few very wide cameras and recovers depth geometrically from their overlap.

🎤 Interview angle

“Write the projection of a 3D point into an image, and tell me what a single camera can't measure.” A strong answer:

  • Writes u = f·X/Z + cx, v = f·Y/Z + cy, names the camera frame convention (z forward, y down) and says the point must first be moved into that frame by the extrinsics.
  • Does a number: f = 1000 px, a 1.5 m object at 30 m is 50 px tall; double the distance, half the size.
  • Explains scale ambiguity: a monocular camera measures bearing, not range; depth needs assumptions (flat ground, known size), a second view (stereo, motion) or another sensor.
  • Connects focal length, field of view and pixels per object, and why vehicles carry several cameras with different lenses.
🏭 In the real world
Waymo Open Dataset cameras 🧪 Research · 2020 Sun et al., CVPR 2020

How: five “high-resolution pinhole cameras” and five lidars on each car; for every camera the dataset gives its intrinsics and its pose on the car, so any 3D point can be projected into any image.

Why this method: the pinhole model plus calibration is what lets researchers paint lidar points onto images and check 3D boxes against 2D pixels.

KITTI, the classic driving benchmark 🧪 Research · 2012 KITTI sensor setup

How: two grayscale and two color 1.4-megapixel cameras with 4–8 mm lenses, plus a Velodyne lidar and a GPS/IMU, all mounted on one car and calibrated to each other.

Why this method: with known intrinsics, every lidar point gets a pixel, which made KITTI the testbed for a decade of depth and 3D detection research.

Skydio X10's navigation cameras 🏢 Company · 2023+ Skydio X10 specs

How: six 32-megapixel navigation cameras with a 200° diagonal field of view cover every direction around the drone for obstacle avoidance.

Why this method: cameras are light and low-power; extreme wide angles mean a handful of them cover the whole sphere.

OpenCV camera model 🧰 Open source OpenCV calib3d docs

How: the same pinhole model with a K matrix and distortion coefficients, used by projectPoints, solvePnP and calibrateCamera.

Why this method: one shared, well-tested camera model means calibration files and code move between projects and robots.

🤯 Fun fact · how far can a dataset camera “see” a person?

The Waymo Open Dataset's front camera images are 1920 px wide with a horizontal field of view of ±25.2° (Sun et al. 2020). Rearranging HFOV = 2·atan(W/2f) gives f = 960 / tan 25.2° ≈ 2,040 px. So a 1.5 m pedestrian 100 m away is about 2040 × 1.5 / 100 ≈ 31 px tall: enough to detect, not enough to read their body language. Every “how far can it see?” question about a camera is this one line of arithmetic.

Check yourself

1. A camera has f = 1000 px. A car 1.5 m tall is 30 m ahead. How tall is it in the image?
Image size = f × real size ÷ distance = 1000 × 1.5 / 30 = 50 px. At 60 m it would be 25 px: twice as far, half as tall.
2. A single forward camera sees a picture of a car printed on the back of a truck, and estimates its distance from the printed car's size. What goes wrong?
A monocular camera measures directions; distance comes from assumptions such as “cars are about 1.5 m tall” or “wheels touch the road”. A printed car violates them. Stereo, motion over time or a range sensor (lidar, radar) are what catch this kind of trick.
3. You need to see cross traffic at intersections and also cars stopped 200 m ahead on the highway, on a modest budget of pixels. Best camera plan?
“Wide” and “large f” contradict each other: HFOV = 2·atan(W/2f). With a fixed number of pixels, split the job: a small f buys angle, a large f buys pixels per object far away. That's why cars carry several forward cameras with different lenses, or one very high-resolution sensor.
📝 TL;DR
  • Pinhole projection: u = f·X/Z + cx, v = f·Y/Z + cy in the camera frame (z forward, x right, y down).
  • Image size = f × real size ÷ distance: twice as far, half as tall. f = 800 px, a 1.5 m person at 20 m → 60 px.
  • HFOV = 2·atan(W/2f): small f sees wide but small, large f sees far but narrow. Pixels are the budget, so vehicles carry several lenses.
  • One camera measures directions, not distance: scale ambiguity. Flat ground, known sizes, a second view or another sensor breaks the tie.
  • Tesla relies on eight cameras plus learning; Waymo adds lidar and radar for depth; Skydio flies on six ultra-wide navigation cameras.
