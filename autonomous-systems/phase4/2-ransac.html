RANSAC: fitting models when data lies

Feature matching always leaves some wrong matches behind, and a single wrong match can drag an ordinary fit anywhere. RANSAC's answer, from 1981, is gloriously simple: guess from a tiny random sample, count who agrees, repeat, keep the best. It runs inside almost every camera-geometry pipeline today.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Feature matches: pairs of “the same corner in two images”, found by comparing descriptors; some are wrong, especially on repeated patterns (Lesson 4.1).
  • Least squares: the line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical errors. Refresher on fitting by minimizing a loss: loss landscapes in the Math course.
  • “And” means multiply for independent chances: two draws that each succeed with probability 0.5 both succeed with 0.25. Refresher: probability.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You have 40 points. Half of them sit neatly on a straight line. The other half are garbage, scattered anywhere. You don't know which is which. How would you find the line?

Averaging (least squares) feels natural and fails badly: in the playground below, half garbage tilts the fitted line by 5° to 20°. Trying every pair of points works but costs 40 × 39 / 2 = 780 lines here, and about 186 billion 8-match subsets for the camera models of the next lesson with 100 matches. RANSAC finds it in about 17 random tries, with a 99% guarantee you can compute on a napkin.

The idea came from Martin Fischler and Robert Bolles at SRI International, published in Communications of the ACM in June 1981 as “Random Sample Consensus” (SRI), for working out where a camera was from landmarks in an image.

🧩 Predict before you try it: twelve points lie almost perfectly on a line. You add one wrong point, far away. How much does the least-squares line move: barely, or a lot?
A lot. Least squares squares each error, so a point 10 m off counts 100 times as much as a point 1 m off. One far outlier can outweigh all twelve good points put together. Click the far corner of the next playground and watch the red line swing.

The recipe

Instead of letting every point vote at once, RANSAC asks small groups to propose, and everybody else to vote:

  1. Sample the smallest set that defines a model: 2 points for a line, 3 for a plane, 5 matches for the camera motion of Lesson 4.3.
  2. Fit the model to just those points (exactly: two points define one line).
  3. Count the consensus: how many of all points lie within a threshold distance t of it? Those are its inliers.
  4. Repeat N times; keep the model with the most inliers.
  5. Refit with ordinary least squares on the winner's inliers only, so the final answer uses all the good data.

Why does it work? A random pair that's two garbage points proposes a garbage line, but almost nobody agrees with it: garbage points are scattered, not lined up. A pair of two good points proposes (nearly) the true line, and all the other good points agree. The truth wins the vote because good data agrees with itself and bad data doesn't.

🏆 Challenge: with 50% or more outliers, reach a 95% success rate (tilt error under 2°) using at most 15 iterations. Two knobs matter. Then push the threshold to 3: why does success drop even with 60 iterations?

What you should find: a tight-but-not-too-tight threshold, about 2 to 5 times the noise of the good points (here noise is 0.12, so 0.3 to 0.6), plus enough iterations. Too tight, and even the perfect line misses good points that wobble. Too loose, and garbage near the line sneaks into the vote and the refit, tilting the result.

How many tries? The napkin formula

✏️ Worked example: 50% outliers, a 2-point model

Let w be the fraction of good points, s the sample size, and p how sure you want to be (0.99) of drawing at least one all-good sample.

  1. Chance one sample is all good: ws = 0.5² = 0.25.
  2. Chance one sample is contaminated: 1 − 0.25 = 0.75.
  3. Chance all N samples are contaminated: 0.75N. We want that ≤ 1 − p = 0.01.
  4. Solve: N = log(0.01) ÷ log(0.75) = (−4.61) ÷ (−0.288) = 16.0, so round up: 17 iterations.
N = log(1 − p) / log(1 − ws) The number of random samples needed so that, with probability p, at least one of them contains only inliers.

Notice what drives the cost: not the number of points, but the sample size s in the exponent. At 50% outliers, the 8-point model needs 8 times the iterations of the 5-point one. That's a big reason the 5-point algorithm is the standard for calibrated cameras.

💡 Big idea When some data is lying, don't average it: let tiny random samples propose and the whole dataset vote. Good data agrees with itself; garbage scatters. The number of tries depends only on the inlier fraction and the sample size: N = log(1 − p) / log(1 − ws), so keep models minimal.
🧠 Analogy: finding the real witnesses

Twenty people say they saw a car crash; half are making things up. Pick two at random and ask them to agree on a story. If both are honest, the other honest people will nod along to that story; if one is lying, almost nobody will. Do this a dozen times and keep the story the most people nod to. You never had to figure out who the liars were first.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Feature matching leaves 10% to 50% wrong matches (repeated windows, moving cars, reflections). Least squares, the default fitting tool, gives every point a vote proportional to its squared error, so a few wrong matches pull the camera-motion estimate far off.
  2. What people tried. Fit, then delete the worst points and refit (fails when outliers have already dragged the first fit toward themselves). Robust losses such as Huber that grow slowly for big errors (good, but need a decent starting guess). Try every minimal subset (780 pairs for 40 points; hopeless for 8-point models). The Hough transform (every point votes in a grid of possible lines; memory explodes with more parameters).
  3. Why RANSAC works. It only needs one clean minimal sample, and it can tell when it has one by the vote. 50% outliers and 2-point samples: each try is clean with probability 0.25, so 17 tries give 99% confidence. No starting guess needed, any model you can fit from a few points.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Iterations explode with the outlier fraction and the sample size (70% outliers, 8 points: 70,188 tries). You must pick a threshold in pixels or meters. It's random: two runs can disagree. When the scene has two consistent motions (a big truck moving across the view), the bigger group wins even if it's the wrong one. It returns one model; finding several needs repeated runs.
  5. What came next. Adaptive N (update w from the best inlier count found so far and stop early), PROSAC (try the best-looking matches first), LO-RANSAC (polish good hypotheses locally), MLESAC/MAGSAC (score by likelihood instead of a hard threshold; OpenCV exposes MAGSAC as USAC_MAGSAC), and robust losses in the final optimization (Lesson 4.7).
🧩 Predict: with 50% outliers and a 2-point line model, you run the formula's 17 iterations. If you repeat the whole experiment many times, in what fraction of runs will at least one sample be all-inlier?
About 99%: that's what the formula promised (p = 0.99). In practice a little less, because two good points that happen to be very close together give a wobbly line even though they're both inliers, and because drawing without replacement from a finite set lowers the chance slightly. 🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You'll rarely write RANSAC yourself, except once to understand it (the Phase 4 lab does). It's built into the geometry functions: OpenCV's findEssentialMat defaults to RANSAC with a 0.999 confidence and a 1-pixel threshold; Open3D's segment_plane finds the ground in a lidar cloud the same way.

import numpy as np, cv2

def ransac_line(pts, iters=17, t=0.5, rng=np.random.default_rng(0)):
    best = None
    for _ in range(iters):
        a, b = pts[rng.choice(len(pts), 2, replace=False)]
        n = np.array([a[1] - b[1], b[0] - a[0]]); n /= np.linalg.norm(n)   # unit normal
        inl = np.abs((pts - a) @ n) < t                     # distance to the line
        if best is None or inl.sum() > best.sum(): best = inl
    return np.polyfit(pts[best, 0], pts[best, 1], 1), best    # refit on the winners

# Camera motion from matched pixels (Lesson 4.3), outliers removed by RANSAC
E, mask = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
print(int(mask.sum()), "inlier matches of", len(pts1))

# Ground plane in a lidar cloud
import open3d as o3d
plane, idx = pcd.segment_plane(distance_threshold=0.05, ransac_n=3, num_iterations=200)

The threshold is in the units of the residual: pixels for image matches, meters for point clouds. Set it to 2 to 3 times the noise of good data. mask marks which matches survived: use only those afterwards.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones

In the open-source VINS-Mono estimator used on research drones, features tracked between frames pass an outlier check “performed using RANSAC with fundamental matrix model” before they reach the optimizer; loop-closure candidates are checked with RANSAC too. A single wrong track fed to a visual-inertial filter can bend the drone's whole position estimate, so this gate is essential.

Sources: VINS-Mono

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published how its stack fits geometric models. The classic use of RANSAC in lidar pipelines is pulling out the ground: fit a plane from 3 random points, count points within a few centimeters, keep the best. Open-source libraries such as Open3D ship exactly that as segment_plane.

Sources: Open3D point cloud tutorial

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's public talks describe neural networks that turn camera video directly into 3D outputs, and Tesla hasn't said whether or where it uses RANSAC. Learned pipelines still face the same problem in another form: training data with wrong labels, handled with robust losses and auto-labeling checks (Lesson 6.7).

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021

🤖 Robots

ORB-SLAM starts a new map by computing, in parallel, a homography and a fundamental matrix with the normalized DLT and 8-point algorithms “inside a RANSAC scheme”, then picks whichever model explains the scene (a flat floor or a general 3D scene).

Sources: ORB-SLAM

Why they differ: wherever explicit geometry is computed from noisy matches (drones, SLAM robots, lidar ground removal) RANSAC or a descendant sits in front of it. Systems that learn geometry end to end move the robustness problem into training data and losses instead.

🎤 Interview angle

“You're estimating camera motion from 500 feature matches and the result is unstable. What do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Suspects outliers first and explains RANSAC: minimal sample, consensus count, best model, refit on inliers.
  • Computes N on the spot: N = log(1 − p)/log(1 − ws), e.g. 50% outliers with the 5-point model ≈ 146 iterations for 99%; knows adaptive N.
  • Sets the threshold from the noise (about 1 px for well-calibrated matches) and checks the inlier ratio as a health signal: a sudden drop means blur, a moving object filling the view, or a bad calibration.
  • Mentions the follow-up: nonlinear refinement on inliers with a robust loss (bundle adjustment, Lesson 4.7), and variants (PROSAC, LO-RANSAC, MAGSAC).
🏭 In the real world
The original paper 📜 History · 1981 Fischler & Bolles, CACM 24(6)

How: random minimal samples plus a consensus count, applied to the “location determination problem”: where was the camera, given landmarks of known position in its image?

Why this method: automated cartography had to cope with gross errors from feature detectors that least squares couldn't handle.

Drone visual-inertial odometry 🧪 Research · 2017 VINS-Mono

How: RANSAC with a fundamental-matrix model rejects bad feature tracks every frame; RANSAC with PnP checks loop closures.

Why this method: the optimizer behind it assumes Gaussian noise; RANSAC makes that assumption true by removing the gross errors.

Ground removal in point clouds 🧰 Open source Open3D segment_plane

How: sample 3 points, fit a plane, count points within a distance threshold, repeat, return the plane with the largest support.

Why this method: the ground is the biggest flat thing in a lidar sweep; everything else (cars, people, poles) is an “outlier” to the ground plane, which is exactly what you want to keep for detection.

Starting a SLAM map 🧰 Open source ORB-SLAM

How: homography and fundamental matrix fitted in parallel inside RANSAC; a score picks the model that fits the scene.

Why this method: a flat scene breaks the fundamental-matrix model and a 3D scene breaks the homography; trying both and voting avoids a bad start.

🤯 Fun fact · the exponent is the whole story

At 70% outliers a line needs 49 RANSAC iterations, but an 8-point fundamental matrix needs 70,188 (from the table above). That single exponent is why researchers spent decades shrinking minimal problems: Nistér's 2004 five-point solver (Lesson 4.3) mattered as much for RANSAC speed as for accuracy.

🌉 How it connects

RANSAC sits between matching (Lesson 4.1) and every geometric model in this phase: the essential and fundamental matrices (4.3), the camera pose in visual odometry (4.6), and the first guess that bundle adjustment polishes (4.7). It's the same idea as the outlier gates of Lesson 3.7, done before estimation instead of inside the filter, and it reappears in lidar ground removal (Phase 6) and place recognition for loop closure (Lesson 5.4). The formula is “and means multiply” from probability.

Check yourself

1. 30% of your matches are wrong and you fit a 4-point homography. How many RANSAC iterations for 99% confidence?
w = 0.7, so w⁴ = 0.24 and 1 − w⁴ = 0.76. N = log(0.01)/log(0.76) = −4.61/−0.274 ≈ 16.8, so 17. (7 is the 2-point line; 72 is the homography at 50% outliers.)
2. Your RANSAC line fit keeps including points from a nearby second line of garbage. What should you change first?
A threshold that's too loose lets nearby garbage join the vote and the refit. More iterations don't help (the best sample was already found), and bigger samples only make clean draws rarer.
3. A bus drives across a car's camera view, covering 60% of the image. RANSAC on frame-to-frame matches returns a confident camera motion. What might be wrong?
RANSAC finds the largest consistent set, not the true one. When a moving object dominates the view, its matches agree with each other perfectly. Systems guard against it with IMU checks, semantic masks for moving objects, and feature spreading across the image.
📝 TL;DR
  • Least squares lets every point pull by its squared error, so one far outlier can tilt the whole fit.
  • RANSAC: sample a minimal set, fit, count inliers within t, repeat N times, keep the best, refit on its inliers.
  • N = log(1 − p)/log(1 − ws): 50% outliers, 2-point line, p = 0.99 → 17. Cost explodes with s: 146 for 5-point, 1,177 for 8-point.
  • Threshold ≈ 2 to 3 × the inlier noise. It fails when a wrong model has the biggest consensus (a bus filling the view).
  • It guards drone VIO (VINS-Mono), SLAM initialization (ORB-SLAM) and lidar ground removal (Open3D); descendants include PROSAC, LO-RANSAC and MAGSAC.
