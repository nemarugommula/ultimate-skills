Epipolar geometry: two views of one world

When one camera sees a point, the second camera can't see it just anywhere: it must appear on one particular line. That single rule turns a 2D search into a 1D one, lets you recover how the camera moved from matched pixels alone, and is the reason stereo cameras only search along image rows.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The pinhole camera: a 3D point (X, Y, Z) in the camera's frame lands at pixel u = f·X/Z + cx, v = f·Y/Z + cy. Camera frames here are x right, y down, z forward (Lessons 2.1 and 2.2).
  • Rotation and translation: one camera's pose relative to another is a rotation R and a shift t (Lesson 1.5). Matrices just apply them in one go: refresher on matrices and matrix multiplication.
  • Matches and RANSAC: pairs of corresponding pixels from Lesson 4.1, cleaned of wrong ones by RANSAC (Lesson 4.2).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Close one eye and look at a lamp. You know its direction, but not how far away it is: it could be a small lamp nearby or a big one far away, anywhere along one line of sight. Now open the other eye instead. Where, in that second view, could the lamp possibly appear? Anywhere? On a circle? On a line?

The answer, a line, was worked out for photogrammetry long before computers. In 1981 H. C. Longuet-Higgins published in Nature a computer algorithm that recovers how two cameras are placed from just eight matched points (Longuet-Higgins, 1981). Today that idea, refined to five points, runs every time a drone or a SLAM system starts tracking.

🧩 Predict before you try it: the second camera sits directly to the right of the first, looking the same way (like your two eyes). Click a point in the left image. What will its line in the right image look like?
A horizontal line on the same row. Moving sideways only shifts things sideways in the image, so the match can only slide left or right. That's exactly the geometry stereo cameras are built (and “rectified”) to have, so their search is along a row (Lesson 4.4). Now set forward motion instead: the lines all radiate from one point in the middle of the image, the direction you're moving toward.

The rule, in words and then in one equation

Three points define a plane: the two camera centers and the 3D point. That's the epipolar plane you saw in the 3D view. Each camera sees that plane edge-on as a line in its image. So the match must sit on the line where the plane cuts image 2. Whatever the depth, the 3D point stays in that plane, so its image stays on that line.

  • The baseline is the segment joining the two camera centers.
  • The epipole is where the baseline pierces an image: the pixel where camera 2 sees camera 1 (it may be off-screen, or at infinity for pure sideways motion). Every epipolar line passes through it.
  • For pure forward motion, the epipole is the point you're heading toward, the focus of expansion (Lesson 4.5).
✏️ Worked example: why stereo matches stay on the same row

Use normalized coordinates (pixels with the camera's intrinsics removed): x = (u − cx)/f, y = (v − cy)/f. The two-view rule is one line of algebra:

x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0,    E = [t]× R x₁ and x₂ are the matched rays (x, y, 1) in each camera. R and t move camera-1 coordinates into camera 2. [t]× is the matrix that does a cross product with t. E is the essential matrix.

Stereo pair: no rotation (R = I) and camera 2 is B meters to the right, so t = (−B, 0, 0). Then

E = [ 0 0 0 ; 0 0 B ; 0 −B 0 ],   E x₁ = (0, B, −B·y₁),   x₂ᵀ E x₁ = B·y₂ − B·y₁ = 0  →  y₂ = y₁

Same row, whatever the depth and whatever B. With B = 0.12 m and f = 700 px: a match at row 240 in the left image is on row 240 in the right; only its column changes, and by how much is the depth signal of Lesson 4.4.

Notice the “2 for the direction of t”. E is unchanged if you double t: the equation is = 0, so any scale works. Two views alone can never tell you how far the camera moved, only in which direction. Hold that thought for the game below and for Lesson 4.6.

The eight-point algorithm treats E's (or F's) nine entries as unknowns; each match gives one linear equation; eight matches pin them down up to scale. Raw pixel coordinates make it numerically fragile; Richard Hartley showed in 1997 that simply normalizing the points first (center them, scale to an average distance of √2) makes it accurate (“In defense of the eight-point algorithm”). Then E is decomposed into R and t (four candidate solutions; the right one puts the points in front of both cameras).

💡 Big idea Two cameras plus one 3D point make a plane, so a pixel in one image can only match a line in the other. All of it is captured by one 3×3 matrix, x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0, which encodes the rotation and the direction of travel, but not the distance traveled. Five good matches are enough to recover it.
🧠 Analogy: a laser pointer seen from the side

Shine a laser pointer through fog. You know the dot is somewhere along the beam. A friend standing beside you sees the whole beam as a line across their view. Ask them where the dot might be: “somewhere on that line”. The epipolar line is your friend's view of your line of sight.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. To match features or measure depth you'd search the entire second image for every pixel: 300,000 candidates in a VGA image. And to know how the camera moved you'd need extra sensors or known landmarks.
  2. What people tried. Search everywhere (slow, and many look-alikes). Assume small motion and search a little window (breaks on fast motion). Known calibration targets in the scene (fine in a lab, not on a street). Photogrammetrists solved camera placement with hand-measured points and lots of arithmetic.
  3. Why epipolar geometry works. It's pure geometry: the match must lie on one line, so the search drops from 2D to 1D (a VGA row has 640 pixels instead of 300,000). The same constraint, written as x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0, turns five or eight matches into the camera's rotation and direction of travel. Stereo example: R = I, t = (−B, 0, 0) gives y₂ = y₁, the same row.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It needs calibration (for E) and at least 5 to 8 clean matches. Pure rotation (camera spins in place) or a flat scene (a wall, a floor) make it degenerate: a homography explains the matches better, which is why ORB-SLAM fits both and picks. Translation scale is lost. Rolling-shutter and unsynchronized cameras bend the lines.
  5. What came next. Hartley's normalization made the 8-point algorithm reliable (1997); Nistér's five-point solver (2004) inside RANSAC became the standard for calibrated cameras; rectification (Lesson 4.4) makes stereo lines horizontal; visual odometry (4.6) chains the poses and recovers scale with stereo or an IMU; bundle adjustment (4.7) refines everything.
🧩 Predict: in the next game you hunt for camera 2's hidden pose by making its epipolar lines pass through six matches. One of the three sliders, it turns out, doesn't change the score at all. Which one?
The baseline length. E only knows the direction of t, so a camera that moved 0.5 m and one that moved 3 m in the same direction produce exactly the same lines. This is the scale ambiguity of a single camera, and why monocular visual odometry needs stereo, an IMU, wheel odometry or a known object size to get meters (Lesson 4.6).

Why it powers stereo and visual odometry

Three jobs, one constraint:

  • Matching: search only along the line (within a pixel or two), so repeated windows elsewhere in the image can't fool you. The next playground shows how much that prunes.
  • Stereo depth: calibrate the rig once, rectify so lines are rows, then search each row (Lesson 4.4).
  • Visual odometry: estimate E from matches with RANSAC, decompose it into R and the direction of t, then triangulate points (Lesson 4.6).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

OpenCV does the estimation and decomposition; you supply matched pixel arrays (N×2, float) and the intrinsics K from calibration (Lesson 2.2).

import numpy as np, cv2

# pts1, pts2: matched pixels (N x 2), K: 3x3 intrinsics
E, mask = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC,
                               prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)   # 5-point inside RANSAC
n, R, t, mask = cv2.recoverPose(E, pts1, pts2, K, mask=mask)  # picks the 1 of 4 (R, t) with points in front
print("rotation:\n", R, "\ndirection of travel (unit length!):", t.ravel())

# Uncalibrated pixels: the fundamental matrix, then epipolar lines for drawing
F, fmask = cv2.findFundamentalMat(pts1, pts2, cv2.FM_RANSAC, 1.0, 0.99)
lines2 = cv2.computeCorrespondEpilines(pts1.reshape(-1, 1, 2), 1, F)   # a, b, c with a·u + b·v + c = 0

t from recoverPose always has length 1: two views give the direction, never the distance. Check the inlier count in mask; a scene that's mostly one plane or a camera that only rotated makes E unreliable.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's cars have exterior cameras covering 360°. In its AI Day 2021 presentation, Tesla described fusing all of the camera views into a single 3D “vector space” with a transformer network, rather than matching features camera by camera. The cameras' relative poses are fixed by the car's build and calibration, so the network can learn the multi-view geometry instead of computing epipolar lines explicitly. Tesla hasn't published the details beyond its talks.

Sources: Tesla Autopilot, Tesla AI Day 2021

🚕 Waymo

Waymo's lidar measures 3D points directly, so the car doesn't need two-view geometry to get depth. What it does need is the fixed geometry between sensors, so lidar points can be projected into each camera image. Its public research dataset shows the scale: 5 lidars (1 mid-range, 4 short-range) and 5 cameras, all calibrated to each other.

Sources: Waymo Open Dataset, Waymo Driver

🛸 Drones

Visual-inertial drones use the two-view constraint every frame: VINS-Mono rejects bad feature tracks with RANSAC on a fundamental-matrix model. Skydio's X10 carries six navigation cameras giving 360° visibility; overlapping views of a calibrated rig are what make multi-camera depth possible (Lesson 4.4).

Sources: VINS-Mono, Skydio X10

🤖 Robots

ORB-SLAM bootstraps its map from two frames by fitting both a fundamental matrix (8-point) and a homography inside RANSAC, then choosing: a flat floor makes the fundamental matrix degenerate, a 3D room makes the homography wrong. Its paper also discusses the five-point algorithm for this step.

Sources: ORB-SLAM

Why they differ: with lidar, depth is measured and epipolar geometry is just calibration bookkeeping. A drone or robot with one or two cameras must recover motion from images, so the explicit constraint (and RANSAC around it) is central. Tesla's camera-only, network-first approach learns the multi-camera geometry from data rather than writing it out.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between the essential and the fundamental matrix, and what can't you get from them?” A strong answer:

  • States the constraint: x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0 for normalized rays, u₂ᵀ F u₁ = 0 for pixels, F = K⁻ᵀ E K⁻¹; explains the epipolar plane, lines and epipoles in words.
  • Counts degrees of freedom: E has 5 (3 rotation + 2 translation direction), F has 7; minimal solvers: 5-point vs 7/8-point; Hartley normalization.
  • Knows the scale ambiguity (t is unit length) and the degenerate cases: pure rotation and planar scenes (use a homography), tiny baselines.
  • Connects it to practice: RANSAC + 5-point for VO initialization, rectification for stereo, cheirality check to pick 1 of 4 decompositions.
🏭 In the real world
The eight-point algorithm 📜 History · 1981 Longuet-Higgins, Nature 293

How: eight matched points give eight linear equations in the essential matrix's entries; solve, then factor into rotation and translation direction.

Why this method: it turned a classical photogrammetry problem into a few lines of linear algebra a computer could run.

The five-point solver 🧪 Research · 2004 Nistér, IEEE TPAMI 26(6)

How: solves the minimal calibrated case (5 matches) efficiently; used inside RANSAC.

Why this method: smaller samples mean far fewer RANSAC iterations, and it doesn't break on planar scenes the way the 8-point algorithm does.

SLAM initialization 🧰 Open source ORB-SLAM

How: fundamental matrix and homography fitted in parallel with RANSAC; a score chooses which explains the first two keyframes.

Why this method: each model fails in the other's best case (planar vs general scenes), so voting between them makes start-up robust.

Drone feature-track checks 🧪 Research · 2017 VINS-Mono

How: every new frame's tracked features must satisfy a fundamental matrix found by RANSAC; violators are dropped before optimization.

Why this method: the epipolar constraint needs no depth and no map, so it can veto bad tracks from the very first frame.

🤯 Fun fact · a theoretical chemist wrote the paper

H. Christopher Longuet-Higgins, author of the 1981 eight-point paper, was a theoretical chemist who switched to cognitive science and studied how brains (and computers) see. His two-page Nature article, “A computer algorithm for reconstructing a scene from two projections”, became one of the founding papers of computer vision (Nature 293, 133–135).

Check yourself

1. How many degrees of freedom does the essential matrix have, and so how many matches does the minimal solver need?
Translation's length is lost (x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0 holds for any scale of t), leaving 3 + 2 = 5. Each match gives one equation, so five matches suffice: Nistér's five-point algorithm. The linear 8-point algorithm uses more matches because it ignores E's internal structure.
2. A calibrated stereo rig has R = I and camera 2 is 0.2 m to the right. A feature is at row 315 in the left image. Where should you search in the right image?
With t = (−B, 0, 0) the constraint becomes y₂ = y₁: same row. The point shifts left in the right image by the disparity, which encodes depth (Lesson 4.4).
3. A drone hovers and only rotates (yaw) between two frames. Your essential-matrix estimate is garbage. Why?
E = [t]× R is zero when t = 0. Pure rotation maps image 1 to image 2 by a homography, and there's no parallax to learn depth or direction from. Systems detect this (homography fits better, tiny parallax) and wait for real motion.
📝 TL;DR
  • Camera centers + 3D point = epipolar plane, so a pixel in image 1 must match a line in image 2; all lines pass through the epipole.
  • x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0 with E = [t]× R (5 DoF, calibrated); F = K⁻ᵀ E K⁻¹ for raw pixels (7 DoF). Solve with 5-point (Nistér) or normalized 8-point (Hartley) inside RANSAC.
  • Pure sideways motion → same-row lines (stereo); forward motion → lines radiate from the focus of expansion.
  • Two views give rotation and the direction of travel, never its length; pure rotation and flat scenes are degenerate.
  • Drones and SLAM use it every frame (VINS-Mono, ORB-SLAM); Waymo's lidar measures depth directly; Tesla's networks learn multi-camera geometry.
