Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Features & matching: corners & ORB

Before a camera can measure depth or motion, it has to recognize the same spot in two pictures. Most of the image is useless for that. Corners are gold. This lesson shows why, how a laptop finds a thousand of them in a few milliseconds, and how it pairs them up between frames.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An image is a grid of numbers: each pixel is a brightness (0 = black, 1 = white here). A camera turns 3D points into pixels by the pinhole model (Lesson 2.1).
  • A gradient is “how fast brightness changes, and in which direction”: across an edge it's big, on a flat wall it's zero. Refresher: gradients in the Math course.
  • Eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix tell you how much it stretches along its two main directions. You only need the intuition; refresher: eigenvalues.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A drone snaps two photos of a street, a tenth of a second apart. To work out how it moved, you may cut out one small square patch from photo 1 and search for it in photo 2. Which patch do you pick?

  1. A patch of clear blue sky.
  2. A patch on the long straight edge of a roof.
  3. A patch on the corner of a window.

Most people pick the window corner, and they're right. The interesting question is why, and how to turn that hunch into a number a computer can compute a million times a second. That number is the heart of every visual tracker: the ORB-SLAM system (2015) extracts about 1,000 corners per frame at VGA-ish resolution and uses those same features for tracking, mapping, relocalization and loop closing (Mur-Artal et al.).

Numbers first: why a corner is special

Shift a patch by a pixel and compare it to the original. On a flat wall, nothing changes in any direction. On an edge, nothing changes if you slide along the edge. Only at a corner (or in rich texture) does every direction of shift make a visible difference. That's exactly what the heat maps showed: a flat black square, a dark valley, a single dark dot in a bright bowl.

This is called the aperture problem: seen through a small opening, a straight edge only tells you motion across it, never along it. You'll meet it again with moving images in Lesson 4.5.

✏️ Worked example: the structure tensor by hand

Instead of trying every shift, add up the gradients in the window. For each pixel take Ix (left-right change) and Iy (up-down change) and sum three numbers:

M = Σwindow [ Ix²   IxIy ;   IxIy   Iy² ] A 2×2 matrix that summarizes “in which directions does brightness change inside this window, and how strongly”. Its two eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ λ₂ are the strengths along the two main directions.
WindowGradients insideMλ₁, λ₂Verdict
Flat wallall zero[0 0; 0 0]0, 0flat: useless
Vertical edge6 pixels with Ix = 0.3[0.54 0; 0 0]0.54, 0edge: slides vertically
Corner3 pixels Ix = 0.3, 3 pixels Iy = 0.3[0.27 0; 0 0.27]0.27, 0.27corner: locked in place

The edge has more total gradient (0.54) than the corner, yet it's worse for tracking, because all of it points one way. The test isn't “lots of contrast”; it's “both eigenvalues big”.

Harris score (1988): R = det(M) − k·trace(M)², with k ≈ 0.04–0.06, avoids computing eigenvalues. With k = 0.05: corner R = 0.27·0.27 − 0.05·0.54² = 0.0729 − 0.0146 = +0.058; edge R = 0 − 0.05·0.54² = −0.015. Positive means corner, negative means edge, near zero means flat. Shi–Tomasi (1994) simply uses the smaller eigenvalue λ₂ as the score, and showed it predicts which features track well.

💡 Big idea A good feature is a patch you can't slide without it looking different. Two strong gradient directions in one small window (λ₁ and λ₂ both large) pin it in x and y. Everything else in geometric vision, from stereo depth to bundle adjustment, is built on top of these pinned-down points.
🧠 Analogy: meeting a friend at a station

“Meet me on the platform” is useless: the platform is a long edge. “Meet me under the big clock” works: there's only one clock. “Meet me at the third bench” works too, but only if you can count benches without getting confused, which is the repeated-pattern problem you'll hit in the matching playground.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. To estimate motion or depth from images you need the same 3D point in two pictures. Comparing every pixel with every pixel of a 1-megapixel image is 1012 comparisons, and most pixels (sky, walls, road) are ambiguous anyway.
  2. What people tried. Track edges (they're everywhere, but you can't tell motion along them). Track bright blobs or hand-placed markers (works in a lab, not on a street). Moravec's 1980 detector tried a few shifts directly; it was noisy and favored some directions.
  3. Why corners work. The structure tensor sums gradients in a window; both eigenvalues big means the patch is locked in 2D. Corner example: λ = 0.27 and 0.27 → Harris R = +0.058; edge example: λ = 0.54 and 0 → R = −0.015. Picking only the top few hundred such points cuts the search by orders of magnitude, and each one can be matched with confidence.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. White walls, fog, night and motion blur give few corners. Repeated patterns (windows, fences, tiles) give many corners that look alike. Corners on moving objects or reflections lie about the camera's motion. Harris also isn't scale-invariant: a corner seen from far away can look like a blob up close.
  5. What came next. FAST (2006) finds corners with a few pixel comparisons; SIFT (2004) adds scale and a robust descriptor; BRIEF (2010) and ORB (2011) make descriptors binary and fast; outlier-proof fitting with RANSAC cleans up the bad matches (Lesson 4.2). Learned detectors and matchers (such as SuperPoint and LightGlue) now compete with them; see the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (3D vision basics).

FAST: corners in a handful of comparisons

Harris needs gradients, products and sums for every pixel. On a drone's small computer at 30 frames per second, that adds up. FAST (Rosten & Drummond, 2006) asks a cheaper question: look at the 16 pixels on a circle of radius 3 around a candidate. If 9 in a row (in the common FAST-9 version) are all brighter than the center by a threshold t, or all darker, it's a corner.

Compare with Harris: a 5×5 window means 25 gradient products per pixel, for every pixel. FAST does a few integer comparisons for most pixels and stops. That's the trick that made feature tracking cheap enough for phones and small drones.

Descriptors: a fingerprint for each corner

A corner's position isn't enough: to match corner #17 in frame 1 with the right corner in frame 2 you need a descriptor, a short fingerprint of the patch around it. SIFT's descriptor is 128 numbers (histograms of gradient directions): accurate, but slow to compute and compare. BRIEF (Calonder et al., 2010) does something almost silly: pick 256 random pairs of pixels in the patch and record one bit per pair, “is pixel A darker than pixel B?”.

✏️ Worked example: Hamming distance in one instruction

Two 8-bit descriptors (real ones are 256 bits = 32 bytes):

corner in frame 11 0 1 1 0 0 1 0
candidate in frame 21 0 0 1 1 0 1 0
XOR (1 where they differ)0 0 1 0 1 0 0 0

Count the 1s: the Hamming distance is 2. A CPU does this with one XOR and one “popcount” instruction per 64 bits, so comparing two 256-bit descriptors is 4 XORs and 4 popcounts. Two random patches differ in about half their bits (≈ 128 of 256); the same corner seen again typically differs in far fewer.

Plain BRIEF has one weakness: its pixel pairs are fixed to the image axes. Rotate the camera and every pair now compares different pixels. ORB (“Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF”, Rublee et al., 2011) fixes that: it measures each corner's orientation from its intensity centroid (the patch's “center of brightness” is off to one side; the direction from the corner to it is the patch's arrow), then rotates the BRIEF pattern to that arrow before taking the bits. It also detects on an image pyramid (the image shrunk by 1.2× several times) so a corner can be found at different scales. The paper reports ORB being two orders of magnitude faster than SIFT while performing as well in many situations.

🧩 Predict: rotate a patch by 90° and recompute plain BRIEF (pairs fixed to the image axes). Out of 64 bits, about how many will flip? And how small a rotation already breaks it?
Far more than you'd hope: on this patch about 22 of 64 at 90°, and around 30 to 39 beyond that. A completely unrelated patch would differ in about half (32). After a big rotation each pair compares two pixels that have little to do with the original pair, so many bits become coin flips. Plain BRIEF already passes 16 wrong bits at about 15°, while the steered (ORB) curve stays down near 3 to 8 at every angle.

Find the angle where plain BRIEF passes 16 of 64 bits (a quarter wrong): that's where it stops being a reliable match. A small amount of “noise” is added to every pixel, like a real camera, so even the steered version is never exactly 0.

Matching, and the ratio test

With descriptors in hand, matching is simple: for each corner in frame 1, find the corner in frame 2 with the smallest Hamming distance (“brute force” for a thousand features is a million XOR-popcounts, a few milliseconds). The hard part is knowing when the best match is wrong.

David Lowe's trick from the SIFT paper: also look at the second-best match. If the best is much better than the runner-up, the match is distinctive. If they're close, you're probably looking at one of several look-alikes (or at a corner that isn't visible in frame 2 at all). Keep a match only if

ratio = dbest / dsecond best < τ   (Lowe used τ = 0.8) Best match at distance 20 bits, runner-up at 110 → ratio 0.18: keep. Best 60, runner-up 64 → ratio 0.94: two look-alikes, throw it away.

In Lowe's experiments, rejecting matches with a ratio above 0.8 removed 90% of the false matches while discarding less than 5% of the correct ones. Try to beat that on a scene with repeated windows:

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

OpenCV has every detector and descriptor in this lesson. ORB's defaults (500 features, pyramid scale 1.2, 8 levels, FAST threshold 20) are a fine start; ask for 1,000–2,000 features for visual odometry.

import cv2

img1 = cv2.imread("frame1.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
img2 = cv2.imread("frame2.png", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

orb = cv2.ORB_create(nfeatures=1000)              # FAST + Harris ranking + rotated BRIEF
kp1, des1 = orb.detectAndCompute(img1, None)      # des: N x 32 bytes (256 bits)
kp2, des2 = orb.detectAndCompute(img2, None)

bf = cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_HAMMING)                 # Hamming distance for binary descriptors
pairs = bf.knnMatch(des1, des2, k=2)                 # best and second-best for each feature
good = [m for m, n in (p for p in pairs if len(p) == 2)
        if m.distance < 0.8 * n.distance]           # Lowe's ratio test
print(len(kp1), "features,", len(good), "good matches")

# Shi–Tomasi corners for optical-flow tracking (Lesson 4.5)
corners = cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(img1, maxCorners=300, qualityLevel=0.01, minDistance=8)

Use NORM_HAMMING for ORB, BRIEF and BRISK, and NORM_L2 for float descriptors like SIFT. crossCheck=True in BFMatcher keeps only mutual best matches (it can't be combined with the ratio test in knnMatch, so pick one).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones

Hand-crafted features are alive and well here, because they're cheap and predictable. The open-source VINS-Mono visual-inertial estimator, flown on research drones, tracks features between frames with the KLT tracker (Lesson 4.5), detects new Shi–Tomasi corners to keep 100–300 features per image, and rejects outliers with RANSAC. Skydio's X10 carries six navigation cameras for 360° obstacle avoidance; Skydio hasn't published whether its pipeline uses classic or learned features.

Sources: VINS-Mono, Skydio X10

🚕 Waymo

Waymo's lidar measures 3D directly (its own page: lidar “paints a 3D picture of the vehicle's surroundings”), so the car doesn't need to match corners between camera frames to get depth. Cameras spot traffic lights, construction zones and other objects, a job for neural networks. Waymo hasn't published using hand-crafted features in its stack.

Sources: Waymo Driver

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's cars have exterior cameras with 360° visibility and no lidar. In its public talks (AI Day 2021) Tesla described neural networks that take the raw camera images and learn their own features end to end, rather than a FAST/ORB front end. Tesla hasn't published the internals beyond those talks.

Sources: Tesla Autopilot, Tesla AI Day 2021

🤖 Robots & AR

ORB-SLAM builds its whole map from ORB features: FAST corners at 8 scale levels with factor 1.2, about 1,000 corners for 512×384 to 752×480 images and 2,000 for KITTI's 1241×376. Its successor ORB-SLAM3 runs with monocular, stereo, RGB-D and inertial sensors.

Sources: ORB-SLAM, ORB-SLAM3

Why they differ: a drone's small computer must estimate its own motion hundreds of times a second with a guaranteed cost, so fast, well-understood corners plus an IMU win. A car with lidar gets geometry for free and spends its compute on understanding the scene. Tesla, with cameras only, bet on networks that learn whatever features best predict depth and motion from huge amounts of video.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why are corners good features, and how would you match them between frames?” A strong answer:

  • Explains the aperture problem: flat patches and edges can slide without changing; corners can't. Structure tensor, both eigenvalues large, Harris R or Shi–Tomasi min λ.
  • Knows the cost ladder: FAST for detection speed, binary descriptors (BRIEF/ORB) with Hamming distance, SIFT when distinctiveness matters more than speed.
  • Matches with ratio test or cross-check, spreads features over the image (a grid), and knows that the remaining outliers are removed by geometry + RANSAC, not by descriptors.
  • Names failure modes: textureless scenes, repeated patterns, motion blur, moving objects, lighting changes; and the learned alternatives.
🏭 In the real world
Visual SLAM on robots and headsets 🧰 Open source ORB-SLAM (2015)

How: ORB features at 8 pyramid levels, spread over a grid; the same features serve tracking, mapping, relocalization and loop closing.

Why this method: binary descriptors are fast enough for real time on a CPU and good enough to recognize a place seen minutes earlier.

Drone visual-inertial odometry 🧪 Research · 2017 VINS-Mono

How: Shi–Tomasi corners tracked frame to frame with KLT optical flow, 100–300 per image, outliers rejected with RANSAC, fused with the IMU.

Why this method: tracking a few hundred corners is cheap and smooth at camera rate; the IMU covers fast motion between frames.

The ORB paper 📜 History · 2011 Rublee et al., ICCV 2011

How: oriented FAST corners plus a rotation-steered, decorrelated BRIEF pattern.

Why this method: SIFT and SURF were patented and slow; ORB was free, fast and built into OpenCV, so it became the default for real-time robotics.

Harris corners 📜 History · 1988 Harris & Stephens, Alvey Vision Conference

How: the structure-tensor score R = det − k·trace², from Plessey Research in the UK.

Why this method: it was a robust, rotation-invariant improvement on Moravec's shift test, cheap enough for 1980s hardware.

🤯 Fun fact · a corner detector trained by machine learning, in 2006

FAST's authors didn't hand-write the order of pixel tests. They ran a slow, exhaustive detector on training images and used machine learning to build a decision tree that reaches the same answers with as few comparisons as possible (Rosten & Drummond, ECCV 2006). Learning to make classic vision faster, years before deep learning took over.

Check yourself

1. A window's structure tensor has eigenvalues λ₁ = 0.40 and λ₂ = 0.01. Using k = 0.05, what is the Harris score R, and what is the patch?
det = 0.40 × 0.01 = 0.004; trace = 0.41, so k·trace² = 0.05 × 0.168 = 0.0084. R = 0.004 − 0.0084 ≈ −0.004: negative, an edge. One strong direction, one weak one: the patch can slide along the edge.
2. Your drone flies past a long glass office facade with hundreds of identical windows. Matching gives many wrong matches. What's the best first fix?
Identical windows make the best and second-best matches nearly equal, so the ratio test discards them. Look-alikes that still slip through break the geometry the true matches agree on, which RANSAC exploits (Lesson 4.2). More features just add more look-alikes, and ORB descriptors are bits: Hamming is the right distance.
3. Why does ORB measure an orientation for each corner?
Plain BRIEF's pairs are fixed to the image axes, so after a big rotation about half the bits flip, like a random patch (Playground 2). Steering the pattern by the intensity-centroid orientation keeps the bits stable.
📝 TL;DR
  • Good features are patches that can't slide unnoticed: both structure-tensor eigenvalues large (Harris R = det − k·trace², Shi–Tomasi = min λ). Flat → useless, edge → aperture problem.
  • FAST finds corners with a few pixel comparisons (9 contiguous of 16 on a circle); ORB = oriented FAST + rotated BRIEF, 256-bit descriptors compared by Hamming distance (XOR + popcount).
  • Match by nearest descriptor, then keep only distinctive matches: Lowe's ratio test (0.8 removed 90% of false matches, under 5% of correct ones in his tests).
  • Repeated patterns and look-alikes survive descriptor tests; geometry and RANSAC remove them next.
  • Drones and SLAM systems run classic corners (VINS-Mono, ORB-SLAM); lidar cars get depth without them; Tesla learns features with networks.
