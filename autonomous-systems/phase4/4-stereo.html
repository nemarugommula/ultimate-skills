Stereo depth & disparity

Two cameras side by side, like your eyes, see each object at slightly different columns. That shift, the disparity, is depth: Z = f·B/d. It's cheap and passive and works on any texture, but its error grows with the square of distance. This lesson shows the formula, how matching along a row works, and how to size a rig.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Pinhole projection: u = f·X/Z + cx, with the focal length f in pixels (Lesson 2.1). Calibration gives f, cx, cy and lens distortion (Lesson 2.2).
  • Epipolar lines: for two cameras side by side with no rotation, a point's match is on the same image row (Lesson 4.3).
  • Derivatives to see how an error in d becomes an error in Z: refresher on derivatives.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Hold a finger 20 cm in front of your nose and blink left eye, right eye, left, right. It jumps a lot. Now do it with a lamp across the room: it barely moves. Your brain turns that jump into distance.

A stereo camera with its lenses 12 cm apart can measure a car 10 m away to about ±0.3 m. How well does it do at 50 m: ±1.5 m (five times worse, since it's five times farther), or much worse? Commit to a guess; the first playground answers it.

This is no lab curiosity. Subaru's EyeSight driver-assist uses two color cameras mounted near the rear-view mirror, and Subaru describes the system as processing stereo images to find the vehicles in front (Subaru, Subaru media). The famous KITTI driving dataset was recorded with stereo pairs about 0.54 m apart (KITTI setup).

Numbers first: one formula

✏️ Worked example: f = 700 px, B = 0.12 m

Two identical cameras, B meters apart, looking the same way. A point at depth Z appears at column uL in the left image and uR in the right. Similar triangles give:

d = uL − uR = f·B / Z    ⟺    Z = f·B / d Disparity d (in pixels) is the focal length times the baseline divided by depth. Near things shift a lot, far things barely.
Depth Zdisparity d = 700 × 0.12 / Zerror if d is off by ¼ px
5 m16.8 px± 0.07 m
10 m8.4 px± 0.30 m
20 m4.2 px± 1.19 m
50 m1.68 px± 7.4 m

Where does the last column come from? Change d by a small δd and Z changes by

δZ ≈ Z² / (f·B) × δd Take the derivative of Z = fB/d: dZ/dd = −fB/d² = −Z²/(fB). Depth error grows with the square of depth.

At 50 m: 50² / (700 × 0.12) × 0.25 = 2500 / 84 × 0.25 = 7.4 m. Five times farther than 10 m, so 25 times the error, not five. The hook's answer is “much worse”: at 50 m this rig can't tell a car at 45 m from one at 55 m.

🧩 Predict: you double the baseline. By how much does the depth error at 50 m shrink? And what's the catch for things very close to the cameras?
It halves: δZ ∝ 1/B. The catch: close objects now have huge disparities (a point 1 m away with f = 700 and B = 0.9 m shifts 630 px, nearly the whole image), so the matcher must search much further, the two views look more different, and near objects may be visible to only one camera. Wide baselines are for far range; narrow ones for close range. Some cars and drones carry more than one pair for that reason.
💡 Big idea Depth from stereo is just Z = f·B/d, and its error is δZ ≈ Z²/(f·B)·δd. Doubling distance quadruples error; doubling the baseline or the focal length halves it. So a stereo rig is sized for a range: pick the farthest distance you need and the error you can tolerate, then solve for f·B.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. One camera can't measure depth: a small near car and a big far one make the same picture (Lesson 4.3's scale ambiguity). Active sensors (lidar, radar, ultrasonics) measure range but cost money, power and weight, and lidar was very expensive for years.
  2. What people tried. Known object size (“cars are about 1.8 m wide”: breaks on vans and kids). Ground-plane geometry (“things higher in the image are farther”: breaks on hills). Motion parallax from one moving camera (needs motion and knowing how far you moved).
  3. Why stereo works. A fixed, calibrated baseline gives metric depth from a single instant, with no motion and no assumptions about the scene: Z = fB/d. With f = 700 px and B = 0.12 m, a 8.4 px disparity means 10 m, every time. It's passive (no emitted light), dense (every textured pixel gets a depth) and uses cheap cameras.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Error ∝ Z²: ±0.3 m at 10 m becomes ±7.4 m at 50 m on a small rig. It needs texture: white walls, sky, fog and glare give no match. Repeated patterns fool it; reflections and transparent glass lie. Calibration must hold to a fraction of a pixel; a bumped camera ruins it. Night needs light.
  5. What came next. Semi-global matching (SGM, Hirschmüller 2005/2008) smooths disparities along many directions and became the classic; active stereo adds a projected IR pattern for texture; multi-camera rigs give 360° coverage (drones); learned stereo networks and monocular depth networks now compete (Lesson 6.6, and the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (3D vision basics)); lidar sidesteps the Z² problem by timing light (Lesson 2.3).

Rectification: making every match a row search

Real rigs aren't perfect: one camera is tilted a fraction of a degree, or rolled slightly. Then matches are no longer on the same row, and a row search finds the wrong pixel. Rectification warps both images (a rotation of each camera's rays, computed once from calibration) so the epipolar lines become exactly horizontal and aligned. OpenCV does it with stereoRectify plus a remap per frame. Try it by hand:

Block matching: finding d along the row

Once images are rectified, for each left pixel you slide a small window along the same row of the right image and pick the shift with the smallest difference. The simplest score is the sum of absolute differences (SAD) of the pixel brightnesses in the window:

SAD(d) = Σwindow | L(u + k) − R(u + k − d) |    →    d* = argmin SAD Try every disparity from 0 to a maximum; keep the one whose right-image window looks most like the left one.

The window size is the classic dilemma. One pixel alone is too ambiguous: dozens of right pixels have the same brightness. A large window averages away noise but smears across object boundaries, so thin things (a pole, a cyclist) get the background's depth. Find the sweet spot yourself:

Then tick the blank wall and inspect a pixel inside it: what does the cost curve look like when there's nothing to match? (Pixels hidden from the right camera behind the objects are left out of the score.)

What you should find: a window of about 9 to 25 pixels wins here. Below that, noise creates false minima; above it, the box and wall drown out the pole. On the blank wall the cost curve is flat, so the “winner” is random: real matchers detect that (no clear minimum, or a failed left-right consistency check) and report “no depth” rather than a wrong one.

🧠 Analogy: finding your place in a song

Someone plays two seconds of a song and asks where it is in the track. One note is hopeless: that note appears a hundred times. A two-second phrase is easy. A two-minute excerpt is easy too, but if the band changed key halfway through it, you'll place it badly. Window size in block matching is the length of the phrase.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

OpenCV has block matching (StereoBM) and semi-global matching (StereoSGBM); both expect rectified 8-bit images and return disparity ×16 as integers.

import cv2, numpy as np

# once, from stereo calibration (cv2.stereoCalibrate): K1, D1, K2, D2, R, T
R1, R2, P1, P2, Q, _, _ = cv2.stereoRectify(K1, D1, K2, D2, size, R, T)
m1 = cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap(K1, D1, R1, P1, size, cv2.CV_32FC1)
m2 = cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap(K2, D2, R2, P2, size, cv2.CV_32FC1)

# every frame
L = cv2.remap(left, *m1, cv2.INTER_LINEAR); Rr = cv2.remap(right, *m2, cv2.INTER_LINEAR)
sgbm = cv2.StereoSGBM_create(minDisparity=0, numDisparities=128, blockSize=5,
                             P1=8 * 5**2, P2=32 * 5**2, uniquenessRatio=10)
disp = sgbm.compute(L, Rr).astype(np.float32) / 16.0    # pixels; ≤ 0 means "no match"

f, B = P1[0, 0], -P2[0, 3] / P2[0, 0]                 # focal length (px) and baseline (m)
Z = np.where(disp > 0, f * B / disp, np.nan)           # depth map in meters
xyz = cv2.reprojectImageTo3D(disp, Q)                     # or a full point cloud

numDisparities must be a multiple of 16 and sets the closest depth you can see (Zmin = fB / numDisparities). P1/P2 are SGM's smoothness penalties; the 8·blockSize² and 32·blockSize² values follow OpenCV's own documentation example for one-channel images.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (Skydio)

Obstacle avoidance on Skydio drones comes from cameras, not lidar. The Skydio 2 Enterprise datasheet lists “six 4K navigation cameras for omnidirectional computer vision”; the X10 has six 32 MP navigation cameras and 360° obstacle avoidance. Overlapping views from a calibrated multi-camera rig are exactly what multi-view depth needs. Its operator manual warns that avoidance degrades in low light (without the NightSense option) and near transparent or reflective surfaces: the textureless/glass failure you just saw.

Sources: Skydio X10, Skydio 2 Enterprise datasheet

🚕 Waymo

Waymo doesn't need stereo for depth: its lidar “paints a 3D picture” of the surroundings, and radar gives an object's distance and speed. Lidar's range error is roughly constant with distance instead of growing with Z², which matters at highway speeds. Cameras spot traffic lights, construction zones and other objects.

Sources: Waymo Driver

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's cars are camera-only (radar removed from Model 3/Y in North America from May 2021). In his CVPR 2021 keynote, Andrej Karpathy, then Tesla's head of AI, showed neural networks estimating other cars' depth and velocity from video, trained with data from the fleet. Tesla hasn't described using a classic stereo pair; its networks infer depth from multiple overlapping cameras and motion.

Sources: Tesla Vision, CVPR'21 keynote

🚗 Driver assist (Subaru)

Subaru's EyeSight puts two color cameras near the rear-view mirror (a third camera on some newer models widens the view), and Subaru's media material says the system processes stereo images to detect vehicles, obstacles and lanes ahead. Mounting behind the windshield keeps the cameras clean and protected from bumper knocks.

Sources: Subaru EyeSight, Subaru media

Why they differ: a drone needs light, cheap, all-around depth at short range (a few to tens of meters), where stereo's Z² error is small: a perfect match. A robotaxi needs accurate depth at 100 m+ in any light, so it pays for lidar. Driver-assist cars need forward depth at low cost behind the windshield. Tesla bets that networks can learn depth from cameras and fleet data well enough without either lidar or a dedicated stereo pair.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a stereo system for a robot that must see obstacles up to 20 m.” A strong answer:

  • Writes Z = fB/d and δZ ≈ Z²δd/(fB), then sizes it: with f = 700 px, δd = 0.25 px and ±0.5 m at 20 m, f·B ≥ 400·0.25/0.5 = 200 px·m, so B ≈ 0.29 m.
  • Checks the near end: the closest object sets the disparity search range (Zmin = fB / dmax).
  • Mentions rectification, synchronized shutters (global shutter if moving fast), calibration stability (rigid mount, temperature), and texture: an IR projector indoors.
  • Knows the matcher options (block matching, SGM, learned) and the failure modes: textureless, repeated, reflective, low light, occlusions; left-right consistency checks.

Why this method: lidar gives the true depth, so stereo algorithms can be scored pixel by pixel on real streets.

Semi-global matching 📜 History · 2005 Hirschmüller, TPAMI 2008

How: matching costs plus penalties for disparity jumps, aggregated along many 1D paths across the image.

Why this method: nearly the quality of global optimization at a fraction of the cost; it runs on FPGAs and GPUs in cars and robots, and OpenCV ships a version as StereoSGBM.

Skydio drones 🏢 Company Skydio X10

How: six navigation cameras give 360° visibility for obstacle avoidance.

Why this method: cameras are light and low-power, and at drone ranges the depth error from multi-view geometry is small.

🤯 Fun fact · your eyes are a 6 cm rig

With a baseline of roughly 6 cm, the same Z² law means your stereo depth sense is excellent within arm's reach and fades over tens of meters; beyond that you lean on cues like size, perspective and motion. That's why you can thread a needle but can't judge whether a distant plane is 5 or 10 km away.

🌉 How it connects

Stereo is the epipolar constraint of Lesson 4.3 made convenient by rectification, with matching costs like the descriptors of 4.1. Optical flow (4.5) is the same search without the row constraint, over time instead of between cameras. Stereo visual odometry (4.6) uses the fixed baseline to fix monocular scale. Learned stereo and monocular depth are Lesson 6.6; lidar's constant-ish range error is Lesson 2.3. The error formula is a first-order derivative.

Check yourself

1. A rig has f = 800 px and B = 0.25 m. A point has disparity 4 px. How far is it, and roughly how uncertain if δd = 0.25 px?
Z = 800 × 0.25 / 4 = 50 m. δZ = 50² / (800 × 0.25) × 0.25 = 2500 / 200 × 0.25 = 3.1 m.
2. Your stereo depth error at 30 m is ±2 m and you need ±0.5 m. Which change does it?
δZ ∝ δd / (f·B). A 4× improvement needs f·B × 4 (doubling both does it) or δd ÷ 4. Doubling B alone only halves it to ±1 m; a bigger window may reduce noise but blurs edges.
3. A robot's stereo camera reports nonsense depths on a freshly painted white corridor wall. Why, and what helps?
Matching needs something to match. On a blank surface the SAD cost curve is flat (Playground 3), so the minimum is random. Active stereo adds texture with a projector; good pipelines also flag low-confidence pixels as “no depth”.
📝 TL;DR
  • Disparity is depth: Z = f·B/d. f = 700 px, B = 0.12 m: 8.4 px ↔ 10 m.
  • Error grows with Z²: δZ ≈ Z²·δd/(f·B). ±0.3 m at 10 m becomes ±7.4 m at 50 m. ±1 m at 50 m needs f·B ≈ 625 px·m (e.g. B ≈ 0.9 m at 700 px).
  • Rectify first (rows = epipolar lines), then match along rows; window size trades noise against smeared edges; SGM adds smoothness.
  • Fails on textureless, repeated, reflective and dark scenes; calibration must hold to a fraction of a pixel.
  • Skydio flies on multi-camera vision; Subaru EyeSight uses a windshield stereo pair; Waymo measures depth with lidar; Tesla infers it with networks.
