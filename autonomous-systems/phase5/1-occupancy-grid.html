Phase 5 · Lesson 1

Occupancy grids: a map made of maybes

Chop the world into little squares and keep, for each one, a running bet: “is something here?” Every lidar beam nudges the bets. This 1985 idea still draws the map under almost every warehouse robot and robot vacuum, and its descendants run inside self-driving cars.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Lidar: a sensor that fires laser beams and times the echo to measure the distance to the first thing each beam hits (Lesson 2.3). A 2D lidar spins one beam in a flat circle: hundreds of distances per turn.
  • Bayes' rule: start with a belief, see evidence, update the belief in proportion to how well it explains the evidence (Lesson 3.2). Refresher: Bayes' rule in the Math course.
  • Odds: a probability of 0.8 is odds of 0.8 : 0.2 = 4 to 1. A logarithm turns multiplying into adding: log(a × b) = log a + log b.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A robot's lidar beam hits something 4.0 m away. What did it just learn? Most people say “there's a wall at 4 m”. That's only half the news, and the less useful half.

The beam flew through 4 m of air before it hit anything. So every spot along the way is (probably) empty. A robot needs that empty space far more than the wall: free space is where it's allowed to drive. Now the twist: the same spot can be seen as empty by one beam and as occupied by another, a second later. Who do you believe?

In 1985, Hans Moravec and Alberto Elfes at Carnegie Mellon faced exactly this with cheap, blurry sonar sensors. Their answer, published as “High resolution maps from wide angle sonar” (ICRA 1985), was to stop deciding and start keeping score: a grid of cells, each with a probability of being occupied, updated by every reading. Elfes named it the occupancy grid (IEEE Computer, 1989).

One cell, many opinions

Zoom in on a single 25 cm square of floor. Over a few seconds, beams pass through it or end in it. A beam that ends in the cell is a hit (evidence: occupied). A beam that passes through is a miss (evidence: free). The cell starts at 50/50: unknown.

🧩 Predict: a hit adds 0.85 and a miss subtracts only 0.41. Why would anyone make evidence for “free” weaker than evidence for “occupied”?
Because a miss is less reliable. A beam that passes through a cell may have only clipped its corner, and the cell might hold a thin chair leg the beam happened to slip past. A beam that ends in a cell almost certainly hit something there. So a hit says “70% occupied” (log-odds +0.85) and a miss says only “60% free” (log-odds −0.41). These are the default numbers of OctoMap, a widely used open-source 3D mapping library (source).

Numbers first: why add instead of multiply

✏️ Worked example: 3 hits and 2 misses

Bayes' rule for a yes/no question says: new odds = old odds × (evidence odds). A hit has odds 0.7 : 0.3 ≈ 2.33. A miss has odds 0.4 : 0.6 ≈ 0.67. Start at 1 (50/50).

probability5.65 ÷ 6.65 = 0.851 ÷ (1 + e−1.73) = 0.85

Same answer, but the right column only adds. That matters for three reasons:

  1. Speed. A lidar sweep touches thousands of cells, 10 to 20 times a second. An add is the cheapest thing a computer does.
  2. No overflow. 1,000 hits multiply the odds to 2.331000 ≈ 10368, which is bigger than the largest double-precision number (about 10308): the computer stores “Infinity”, and no number of misses can ever bring it back. The log-odds is just 847. (Press 🎯×1000 above.)
  3. Symmetry. 0 means unknown, positive means occupied, negative means free. Easy to threshold, easy to clamp.
ℓcell ← ℓcell + { +0.85 if the beam ends here, −0.41 if it passes through }     P(occupied) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−ℓ) Each cell keeps one number, its log-odds ℓ. Hits add, misses subtract, and you only convert back to a probability when you need to draw the map or plan a path.
💡 Big idea An occupancy grid is thousands of tiny, independent Bayes filters, one per cell, each answering a yes/no question. Treating cells as independent is a white lie (walls are continuous), but it makes the update a single addition per cell per beam, and the lie washes out with enough beams.
🧠 Analogy: a tally sheet for every floor tile

Imagine a security guard with a clipboard showing a grid of the warehouse floor. Every time a camera sees a box on a tile, the guard adds a tick to that tile; every time the camera sees clear floor there, the guard rubs out half a tick. Nobody trusts a single glance. After an hour, tiles with many ticks are “definitely boxes”, tiles with many rub-outs are “definitely floor”, and tiles nobody ever looked at stay blank: unknown, which is different from free.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Early robots stored maps as lists of geometric shapes: lines for walls, polygons for objects. That needs you to recognize a wall before you can store it, which blurry sonar and noisy lidar can't do reliably. One bad reading creates a phantom wall that is never removed.
  2. What people tried. Mark a cell occupied the first time anything is seen there (every noise spike becomes a permanent obstacle). Keep the last reading only (the map flickers, and anything out of view is forgotten). Count hits and divide by looks (closer, but it ignores that a miss is weaker evidence than a hit, and has no notion of “unknown”).
  3. Why this works. Each cell runs Bayes' rule, so every reading counts by how trustworthy it is, and noise averages out. In log-odds the update is one addition: 3 hits and 2 misses give 3 × 0.85 − 2 × 0.41 = 1.73, or 85% occupied. Free, occupied and unknown fall out naturally (ℓ < 0, ℓ > 0, ℓ = 0), and a planner can treat unknown cells with caution.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Memory grows with area ÷ resolution²: 4 million cells for a 100 m × 100 m floor at 5 cm, and 800 million in 3D. It assumes the world is static: a person who stood still for a while leaves a “ghost” that takes many misses to erase. It also assumes you know the robot's pose exactly; if the pose drifts, walls smear (that's the next lesson). Cells near the edge of a beam get wrong votes, so thin obstacles can be erased.
  5. What came next. Clamping the score so cells can change their mind quickly (OctoMap), octrees that store big empty regions as a single node (OctoMap, 2013), signed distance fields that store distance to the nearest surface for drone planners (voxblox), layered costmaps that add safety margins for path planners (ROS 2 Nav2), and learned occupancy networks that predict 3D occupancy straight from cameras (Lesson 6.3).

Which cells does a beam cross? Bresenham's line

To update the grid you need, for every beam, the list of cells it passes through. In 1965, Jack Bresenham at IBM needed the same thing to drive a pen plotter: which grid points should the pen visit to draw a straight line? His algorithm uses only integer additions and comparisons, no division and no floating point (IBM Systems Journal, 1965). Robots still use it for exactly this job.

✏️ Worked example: a beam from cell (0, 0) to cell (6, 2)

The line rises 2 cells over 6. Bresenham steps one cell to the right each time and keeps a running “error”: how far the true line is above the current row. Whenever the error passes half a cell, it also steps up one row.

Result: (0,0) (1,0) (2,1) (3,1) (4,1) (5,2) (6,2). The robot sits in (0,0); cells 1 to 5 get a miss (−0.41) and (6,2), where the beam ended, gets a hit (+0.85). A 2D lidar with 360 beams and a 10 m range at 5 cm cells updates up to 360 × 200 = 72,000 cells per sweep: that's why each update must be one cheap addition.

🧩 Predict before the next playground: you stand in the middle of the room below and take one full 360° scan. Roughly what fraction of the room's free floor do you think you'll map: 30%, 60% or 90%?
About 30% from the dead center. Interior walls and furniture cast shadows: every beam stops at the first thing it hits, so whole rooms behind a wall stay unknown. Mapping is mostly about choosing where to look from. Robots that do this on their own are doing exploration: drive to the border between known-free and unknown space (the “frontier”) and scan again.

What you should find: scanning from the middle of a big open area wastes beams on space you've already seen; scanning from doorways and just around corners reveals whole new rooms. At 10 cm cells with 90 beams, beams fan out: at 8 m away, neighbouring beams are 2π × 8 ÷ 90 ≈ 0.56 m apart, so most cells between them are never touched. Finer cells need more beams, more memory and more time.

The trick behind OctoMap (Hornung et al., Autonomous Robots 2013): most of a 3D volume is either empty air or unknown, so store it as an octree. A cube that is all one value is a single node; only cubes containing a surface get split into 8 children, then 8 again, down to the finest size.

Ghosts: when the world moves

The grid assumes nothing moves. A person who stands in one spot for 8 seconds, seen by a 10 Hz lidar, collects about 80 hits: a log-odds of 80 × 0.85 = 68. To erase that at −0.41 per miss takes 68 ÷ 0.41 ≈ 167 misses, about 17 seconds of the robot staring at the empty spot. Until then the planner sees a ghost: an obstacle that isn't there. Walk around and you leave a ghost trail.

The classic fix is clamping: never let the score go beyond a limit. OctoMap's defaults clamp to [−2, +3.5], so a cell is never more than 97.1% sure it's occupied or 88.1% sure it's free. A clamped cell can flip back in 3.5 ÷ 0.41 ≈ 9 misses. The price: a real wall that gets grazed by unlucky beams also flips faster.

The lesson generalizes: an occupancy grid can be fast to forget (good for moving people and doors) or slow to forget (good for walls that some beams only graze), but not both with one number. Real systems split the problem: a static map built once, plus a short-lived obstacle layer for things that move (next section), or they track moving objects separately (Lesson 6.5).

From occupancy to “how bad is it to drive here?”: costmaps

A path planner doesn't just want “occupied or not”; it wants a cost, so it can keep a safe distance from walls. ROS 2's navigation stack, Nav2, builds a layered costmap on top of occupancy (Nav2 costmap docs):

LayerWhat it adds
Static layerThe saved map from a mapping run (the slow-to-forget part).
Obstacle / voxel layerLive lidar or depth-camera data: marks what beams hit and clears what they pass through (raytracing, like Bresenham). The voxel layer does it in 3D columns.
Inflation layerSpreads cost outward from obstacles (the inflation_radius), so plans keep clear of walls by more than the robot's radius.

Each cell ends up as one byte. In Nav2's source: 0 = free, 253 = so close to an obstacle that the robot's body would touch it (“inscribed”), 254 = lethal (an obstacle), 255 = no information (cost_values.hpp). Planners like A* (Lesson 7.2) then search this grid.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The log-odds update is a few lines of NumPy (the Phase 5 lab has you write it). In practice: Nav2 costmap_2d on ROS 2 ground robots, slam_toolbox or Cartographer to build the static 2D map (Lesson 5.4), OctoMap for 3D occupancy with clamping, voxblox for drones that need distances to surfaces.

import numpy as np
L_HIT, L_MISS, L_MIN, L_MAX = 0.85, -0.41, -2.0, 3.5     # OctoMap's defaults, as log-odds

def bresenham(i0, j0, i1, j1):
    """All grid cells from (i0, j0) to (i1, j1), integers only."""
    cells, dx, dy = [], abs(i1 - i0), -abs(j1 - j0)
    si, sj, err = (1 if i0 < i1 else -1), (1 if j0 < j1 else -1), dx + dy
    while True:
        cells.append((i0, j0))
        if (i0, j0) == (i1, j1): return cells
        e2 = 2 * err
        if e2 >= dy: err += dy; i0 += si
        if e2 <= dx: err += dx; j0 += sj

def update(L, robot_cell, end_cell, hit):
    ray = bresenham(*robot_cell, *end_cell)
    for (i, j) in ray[:-1]:                      # the beam flew through these: free
        L[j, i] = max(L_MIN, L[j, i] + L_MISS)
    i, j = ray[-1]
    L[j, i] = min(L_MAX, L[j, i] + L_HIT) if hit else max(L_MIN, L[j, i] + L_MISS)

L = np.zeros((45, 80))                            # 0 = unknown everywhere
update(L, (0, 0), (6, 2), hit=True)
P = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-L))                           # back to probabilities to draw or plan

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla has no lidar, so it can't raytrace beams. At AI Day 2022 it presented an occupancy network: a neural network that takes video from all the car's cameras and predicts which parts of the 3D space around the car are occupied, plus how they move. It is the occupancy-grid idea, learned from data instead of counted from beams (Lesson 6.3). Tesla's AI page describes networks that turn video from all cameras into the road layout in a bird's-eye view.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2022 (video), tesla.com/AI

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published how its onboard maps of free space are built. Its researchers have published occupancy flow fields (2022): a bird's-eye-view grid that predicts, for each cell, whether it will be occupied in the next seconds and how things there move, and Waymo ran an occupancy-flow prediction challenge on its open dataset the same year. Its detailed prior maps are the subject of Lesson 5.5.

Sources: Occupancy Flow Fields (arXiv 2022), Waymo Open Dataset challenge

🛸 Drones

A drone flies in 3D, so a flat grid isn't enough. Open-source drone stacks use OctoMap octrees or voxblox, which stores, for each voxel, the distance to the nearest surface so a planner can check clearance instantly. Skydio says its drones “constantly map the environment as they fly through it” to avoid obstacles autonomously.

Sources: OctoMap, voxblox (arXiv 2016), Skydio autonomy

🤖 Robots

Warehouse and service robots on ROS 2 store a 2D occupancy map from a mapping run and plan on Nav2's layered costmap at around 5 cm per cell, with live lidar marking and clearing obstacles on top.

Sources: Nav2 costmap_2d

Why they differ: with lidar you can measure free space beam by beam, so counting hits and misses works and is easy to verify. With cameras only, free space has to be inferred, so Tesla trains a network to output the grid. Drones need 3D and fast clearance checks, so they pay for octrees or distance fields. Indoor robots live on flat floors, where a 2D grid is cheap and enough.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you build a map for a warehouse robot, and what goes wrong?” A strong answer:

  • Explains log-odds per cell with numbers (+0.85 hit, −0.41 miss) and why: additions, no overflow, 0 = unknown.
  • Mentions raytracing free space (Bresenham), and that unknown ≠ free for safety.
  • Does the resolution and memory math out loud and picks something like 5 cm for 2D.
  • Names the failure modes: dynamic objects / ghosts (clamping, separate static and live layers), pose error smearing the map (needs SLAM, Lesson 5.2), glass and mirrors that lidar sees through or bounces off.
🏭 In the real world
The first occupancy grids 📜 History · 1985 Moravec & Elfes, ICRA 1985

How: wide-angle sonar readings from a mobile robot were combined, cell by cell, into probability maps of empty and occupied space.

Why this method: sonar was too blurry to recognize walls from one reading, but many blurry readings, combined by probability, gave a sharp map.

OctoMap 🧰 Open source octomap.github.io

How: a probabilistic 3D occupancy map stored as an octree, with log-odds updates and clamping (default hit 0.7, miss 0.4, clamp 0.12 to 0.97).

Why this method: 3D grids are huge; an octree stores empty and unknown regions compactly, and clamping keeps the map responsive to change.

Nav2 layered costmaps 🧰 Open source Nav2 docs

How: a static layer from the saved map, an obstacle layer that marks and clears from live lidar, and an inflation layer for safety margins, all merged into one byte per cell.

Why this method: splitting slow-changing walls from fast-changing obstacles avoids the ghost-versus-erased-wall dilemma you hit in Playground 3.

Predicting future occupancy 🧪 Research · 2022 Occupancy Flow Fields

How: a network predicts a bird's-eye-view grid of where road users will be over the next seconds, plus their motion in each cell.

Why this method: a grid handles any number of agents and shapes, and a planner can check a trajectory against it cell by cell.

🤯 Fun fact · OctoMap is never more than 97.1% sure

In OctoMap's source, the clamping thresholds are set to probabilities of 0.1192 and 0.971: log-odds of almost exactly −2 and +3.5 (AbstractOccupancyOcTree.cpp). A deliberately humble map: no matter how many times it has seen a wall, it keeps a 3% doubt, just so it can change its mind in a few scans when the wall turns out to be a door.

Each cell is a two-state Bayes filter (Lesson 3.2). This lesson assumed the robot knows exactly where it is; Lesson 5.2 drops that assumption and meets SLAM. Check yourself

1. A cell starts unknown (log-odds 0) and gets 2 hits (+0.85 each) and 1 miss (−0.41). Roughly what's its probability of being occupied?
Log-odds = 0.85 + 0.85 − 0.41 = 1.29, and 1 ÷ (1 + e−1.29) ≈ 0.78–0.79. Simple vote counting gives 0.67 because it treats a miss as being as strong as a hit; Bayes weighs each by how reliable it is.
2. A forklift parks for 10 minutes in a warehouse aisle, then leaves. Your robot's map keeps showing it for a long time and robots route around an empty aisle. What's the most likely fix?
Ten minutes of hits pile up a huge log-odds that takes ages of misses to cancel: a ghost. Clamping caps the score so a few misses flip it back; Nav2 goes further and keeps live obstacles in their own layer on top of the static map.
3. A 200 m × 100 m warehouse is mapped in 2D at 5 cm cells, one byte per cell. How much memory?
200 ÷ 0.05 = 4,000 columns and 100 ÷ 0.05 = 2,000 rows: 8 million cells = 8 MB. Easy for any computer. The same building in 3D, 10 m tall at 5 cm, is 200 times more: 1.6 GB, which is why 3D maps use octrees.
📝 TL;DR
  • An occupancy grid is a per-cell Bayes filter: hits add +0.85, misses add −0.41 in log-odds; 0 = unknown, > 0 occupied, < 0 free.
  • Log-odds turn multiplying into adding: fast, no overflow, and a clean meaning for 0.
  • Every beam marks its end cell and clears every cell it crossed (Bresenham's line, integers only).
  • Memory grows with 1/resolution² (1/resolution³ in 3D): 4 MB for 100 m × 100 m at 5 cm; octrees for 3D.
  • Moving things leave ghosts; clamping (OctoMap: [−2, +3.5]) and separate static and live layers (Nav2) fix it. Tesla learns the grid from cameras; drones use 3D octrees and distance fields.
