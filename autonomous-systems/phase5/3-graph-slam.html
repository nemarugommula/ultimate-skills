Phase 5 · Lesson 3

Graph SLAM: pose graphs, springs & loop closure

Instead of one giant filter, keep every pose the robot has been at and every “I moved this much” and “I've been here before” as a spring between them. Then let the springs settle. This one idea runs inside nearly every modern SLAM system, from ROS robots to drones.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Pose = position and heading (x, y, θ in 2D). Odometry = how far the robot thinks it moved between two poses; small errors add up into drift (Lesson 5.2).
  • Least squares: choose the numbers that make the sum of squared errors smallest. With Gaussian noise this is also the most likely answer: see maximum likelihood in the Math course.
  • Gradient descent and curvature: walk downhill on an error surface; using the curvature too (Gauss–Newton) takes bigger, smarter steps. Refreshers: gradient descent, Taylor & curvature.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A robot drives a 48 m loop around a warehouse, 24 legs of 2 m. Its odometry says it ended up 6.7 m from where it started. But its camera clearly recognizes the starting dock: it's back at the start.

So the odometry is wrong by 6.7 m. Where do you put the blame? All on the last leg (a 6.7 m jump)? Spread evenly over the 24 legs? Or something smarter?

The answer, first published for laser scans by Feng Lu and Evangelos Milios (Autonomous Robots, 1997), is to treat every measurement as a spring and let the whole network relax. Today it's the back-end of slam_toolbox in ROS 2, LIO-SAM, ORB-SLAM and many more.

Nodes, edges and springs

PieceWhat it isAs a spring
NodeA pose the robot was at (x, y, θ), one every metre or soA bead you can move
Odometry edge“From node 7 to node 8 I moved 2.0 m forward and turned 1°”A spring with that rest length, stiffness = how much you trust it
Loop-closure edge“Node 24 is the same place as node 0” (from recognizing it)A spring tying two far-apart beads together

Each spring's energy is its error squared, weighted by its stiffness. Optimizing the graph means finding the bead positions with the least total energy. Try it by hand in one dimension.

✏️ Worked example: the corridor, solved

Energy = Σ stiffness × (measured − actual)². With every stiffness = 1, the 0.6 m disagreement is shared by five springs in a ring (four odometry + one loop), each taking 0.6 ÷ 5 = 0.12 m:

  • each odometry leg shrinks by 0.12 m: beads at 0, 2.08, 3.86, 5.84, 7.72 m;
  • the loop spring stretches by 0.12 m (7.72 instead of 7.6);
  • energy = 5 × 0.12² = 0.072, versus 0.36 if you left everything at odometry and the loop took all 0.6 m.

Make the loop 10× stiffer and it takes less of the blame: bead 4 lands at 7.61 m (energy 0.088). Make it 10× weaker and odometry wins: 8.03 m (energy 0.026). The general rule: the error is split in proportion to each spring's softness (its variance), which is exactly what a Kalman gain does too.

minimize over all poses   E = Σedges eijᵀ Ωij eij,    eij = (what edge ij measured) − (what the current poses say) e is each spring's stretch (a 3-number error in 2D: dx, dy, dθ), Ω is its stiffness (the information matrix, the inverse of its covariance). Add up every spring's energy and find the poses that make it smallest: weighted least squares.

How the optimizer actually moves the beads: Gauss–Newton

In 2D the springs involve rotations, so the energy isn't a simple bowl. Gauss–Newton handles that by repeating three steps (Math course: curvature, loss landscapes):

  1. Linearize: around the current poses, approximate how each edge's error changes if you nudge its two poses (a small Jacobian J per edge).
  2. Build and solve one linear system H Δx = −g, with H = Σ Jᵀ Ω J and g = Σ Jᵀ Ω e. H is sparse: each edge only touches its two poses, so a 10,000-pose graph is a 30,000 × 30,000 matrix that is mostly zeros, and sparse solvers handle it in milliseconds to seconds.
  3. Update x ← x + Δx, and repeat until the energy stops dropping (usually 3 to 10 iterations). Levenberg–Marquardt adds a safety brake for when the linearization is poor.
💡 Big idea Graph SLAM separates two jobs. The front-end turns sensor data into edges: odometry, scan matches, and loop closures (“I've been here”). The back-end is a pure optimizer that finds the poses that best agree with all the edges. Because it keeps every pose, it can re-decide the past: one new loop closure straightens the entire trajectory, minutes after the drift happened.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. EKF-SLAM (Lesson 5.2) keeps a dense covariance over all landmarks: O(n²) per update, so it chokes on big maps. It also commits to each estimate as it goes: once the filter has linearized around a wrong pose, it can't go back and fix that choice.
  2. What people tried. Distribute the loop error evenly along the path (ignores that some legs are more trustworthy and that the error is mostly in heading). Particle filters over whole paths (FastSLAM: works, but big loops need huge numbers of particles). Filters that drop old poses (lose the ability to fix the past).
  3. Why this works. Keep every pose and every measurement and solve them together as weighted least squares. The system is sparse, so it scales; each solve re-linearizes around the current best guess, so it can fix old mistakes. Numbers: the corridor's 0.6 m disagreement split five ways is 0.12 m per spring and energy 0.072 instead of 0.36; the 2D loop goes from 3.6 m average error to about 0.15 m in three iterations.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It's only as good as its edges. A single false loop closure (two identical aisles, a repeated corridor) is a very stiff lie, and least squares will bend the whole map to honour it. Gauss–Newton needs a decent starting guess; from a terrible one it can fall into a wrong minimum. Batch solves grow with the graph, so long missions need incremental solvers or sparsification.
  5. What came next. Robust kernels (Huber, Cauchy) and methods built for SLAM such as switchable constraints (2012) and dynamic covariance scaling (2013) that let the optimizer down-weight edges that don't fit. Incremental solvers (iSAM2 in GTSAM) update the solution as nodes arrive instead of starting over. Factor graphs generalize the idea to IMU, GPS and landmark measurements (LIO-SAM, Lesson 5.4).

Robust kernels: springs that give up on liars

Plain least squares charges error². A spring stretched 10 m pulls 100 times harder than one stretched 1 m, so outliers dominate. A robust kernel changes the charge for big errors:

KernelCharge for a large error eEffect on a liar
Squared (plain)e²Pulls harder the more it lies
Hubergrows like |e| beyond a thresholdPulls with constant force: still bends the map, less
Cauchygrows like log(e²)Pull fades as the error grows
DCS (dynamic covariance scaling)scales the edge's stiffness by min(1, 2Φ ÷ (Φ + χ²))²Edges far beyond Φ are effectively switched off

One catch, which you can feel in the playground: a true loop closure after big drift also starts with a huge error, so a strict kernel might reject it too. The playground's optimizer therefore starts forgiving (Φ = 1000) and gets stricter each iteration (down to 1), a trick known as graduated non-convexity. The true loop pulls the map into shape first; the lie is left alone, stretched and ignored.

Finding loop closures: “have I been here before?”

The back-end can only fix drift if the front-end detects the revisit. With cameras, the standard trick is a bag of visual words: cluster image features (like the ORB features of Lesson 4.1) into a vocabulary of “words”, describe each image by how often each word appears, and compare images by their word histograms, like a search engine compares documents. DBoW2 does this for binary features (Gálvez-López & Tardós, IEEE T-RO 2012) and is used by ORB-SLAM. Lidar systems compare scan shapes instead.

A high similarity is only a candidate. Identical aisles, repeated offices and similar corridors produce perceptual aliasing: different places that look the same. So candidates are verified geometrically: match the features and check, with RANSAC (Lesson 4.2), that a single consistent rigid motion explains them.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

GTSAM (Georgia Tech, factor graphs, pip install gtsam), g2o (general graph optimization, C++) and Google's Ceres Solver (nonlinear least squares, used by Cartographer and, by default, slam_toolbox) are the standard back-ends. This is GTSAM's own 2D pose-graph example (5 poses, one loop closure), with the loop wrapped in a Huber robust kernel:

import math, numpy as np, gtsam

PRIOR_NOISE = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.3, 0.3, 0.1]))     # σx, σy (m), σθ (rad)
ODOMETRY_NOISE = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.2, 0.2, 0.1]))
LOOP_NOISE = gtsam.noiseModel.Robust.Create(
    gtsam.noiseModel.mEstimator.Huber.Create(1.345), ODOMETRY_NOISE)          # a spring that gives up on liars

graph = gtsam.NonlinearFactorGraph()
graph.add(gtsam.PriorFactorPose2(1, gtsam.Pose2(0, 0, 0), PRIOR_NOISE))          # pin the first pose
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(1, 2, gtsam.Pose2(2, 0, 0), ODOMETRY_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(2, 3, gtsam.Pose2(2, 0, math.pi / 2), ODOMETRY_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(3, 4, gtsam.Pose2(2, 0, math.pi / 2), ODOMETRY_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(4, 5, gtsam.Pose2(2, 0, math.pi / 2), ODOMETRY_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(5, 2, gtsam.Pose2(2, 0, math.pi / 2), LOOP_NOISE))   # loop closure

initial_estimate = gtsam.Values()                                      # deliberately wrong starting guesses
initial_estimate.insert(1, gtsam.Pose2(0.5, 0.0, 0.2))
initial_estimate.insert(2, gtsam.Pose2(2.3, 0.1, -0.2))
initial_estimate.insert(3, gtsam.Pose2(4.1, 0.1, math.pi / 2))
initial_estimate.insert(4, gtsam.Pose2(4.0, 2.0, math.pi))
initial_estimate.insert(5, gtsam.Pose2(2.1, 2.1, -math.pi / 2))

parameters = gtsam.GaussNewtonParams()
parameters.setRelativeErrorTol(1e-5); parameters.setMaxIterations(100)
result = gtsam.GaussNewtonOptimizer(graph, initial_estimate, parameters).optimize()
marginals = gtsam.Marginals(graph, result)                              # uncertainty of each pose after solving
for i in range(1, 6):
    print(result.atPose2(i), marginals.marginalCovariance(i).diagonal())

Versions as of Nov 2026: gtsam 4.3.0 on PyPI. The same graph in g2o or Ceres has the same three ingredients: variables (poses), residuals (edges) with weights, and a solver. Swapping GaussNewtonOptimizer for LevenbergMarquardtOptimizer is a one-line change.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Waymo builds its maps from many drives of sensor-equipped cars and says that its vehicles automatically upload data when they detect changes, so the map can be updated and shared with the fleet. Stitching thousands of drives into one consistent map is exactly the kind of large least-squares alignment a pose graph solves, but Waymo hasn't published its mapping back-end.

Sources: Waymo Driver Handbook: mapping (2020)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published whether its cars run a pose-graph SLAM. Its public material (AI Day talks, tesla.com/AI) focuses on neural networks that read the road layout from all cameras, rather than on building and optimizing a prior map.

Sources: tesla.com/AI, AI Day 2022 (video)

🛸 Drones

Visual-inertial SLAM systems used on drones, such as ORB-SLAM3, detect revisits with a bag of words (DBoW2), verify them geometrically, then correct the trajectory with pose-graph optimization; ORB-SLAM3 can also merge separate maps when it recognizes a place. Skydio's 3D Scan software automates capturing a structure so it can be turned into a 3D model, and works without GPS thanks to visual navigation.

Sources: ORB-SLAM3, Skydio 3D Scan (2021)

🤖 Robots

slam_toolbox (ROS 2) builds a pose graph from 2D lidar scan matches and loop closures and, by default, solves it with Ceres. LIO-SAM builds a factor graph of lidar odometry, IMU and GPS factors with GTSAM.

Sources: slam_toolbox, LIO-SAM

Why they differ: a robotaxi company can run its big pose-graph optimizations offline, in a data centre, over many drives, and ship the result as a map. A drone or a warehouse robot must close loops onboard, in real time, with a small computer. Tesla's public strategy avoids depending on a detailed prior map, so the optimization question moves to its training pipeline instead (Lesson 6.7).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain pose-graph SLAM and what happens with a false loop closure.” A strong answer:

  • Front-end vs back-end; nodes = poses, edges = relative measurements with information matrices.
  • Weighted least squares solved by Gauss–Newton / Levenberg–Marquardt; the system is sparse (each edge touches two poses).
  • Loop closures fix accumulated drift; detection by place recognition (bag of words, scan context) plus geometric verification.
  • A false closure bends the whole map; defences: strict verification, robust kernels (Huber, Cauchy, DCS, switchable constraints), and consistency checks across several closures.
🏭 In the real world
The first pose graph 📜 History · 1997 Lu & Milios, Autonomous Robots

How: keep the robot's past poses as variables, turn laser scan matches into relative constraints, and solve them all at once.

Why this method: aligning scans one pair at a time accumulates error; solving globally makes the map consistent.

A tutorial everyone reads 🧪 Research · 2010 Grisetti et al., A Tutorial on Graph-Based SLAM

How: spells out the error functions, Jacobians and sparse solve for 2D and 3D pose graphs.

Why this method: it turned graph SLAM from a research idea into something engineers could implement.

Surviving false loop closures 🧪 Research · 2012–2013 Switchable constraints, DCS

How: give each loop closure a weight the optimizer can turn down when the edge disagrees with everything else.

Why this method: loop-closure detectors are never perfect, and one bad edge must not destroy a map.

slam_toolbox 🧰 Open source Macenski & Jambrecic, JOSS 2021

How: lidar scan matching, loop closure and a Ceres-solved pose graph, with tools to continue, merge and edit maps.

Why this method: the SLAM library the Nav2 documentation recommends for ROS 2 robots.

🤯 Fun fact · “have I been here?” in 5 milliseconds

DBoW2's README reports about 3 ms to turn an image's binary features into a bag-of-words vector and about 5 ms to search a database of more than 19,000 images for matches (DBoW2). The trick is the same as a web search engine's: an inverted index from each word to the images containing it.

Check yourself

1. Three odometry springs (stiffness 1 each) and one loop-closure spring (stiffness 1) form a ring that disagrees by 0.8 m. At the least-squares optimum, how much does each spring take?
Equal stiffness, four springs in a ring: the 0.8 m is shared equally, 0.2 m each, for an energy of 4 × 0.2² = 0.16. Any other split costs more (all 0.8 on one spring costs 0.64).
2. After a warehouse robot adds a loop closure between two identical-looking aisles, its whole map bends into a banana shape. What's the best fix?
More iterations just converge more precisely to the wrong, banana-shaped answer. The edge itself is a lie (perceptual aliasing). Geometric verification catches many; robust kernels such as DCS or switchable constraints catch the rest by letting the optimizer turn that spring off.
3. Why can a pose graph with 10,000 poses (a 30,000 × 30,000 system) be solved quickly?
H = Σ JᵀΩJ only has non-zero blocks where two poses share an edge: a band along the diagonal (odometry) plus a few off-diagonal blocks (loop closures). Sparse Cholesky factorization (inside g2o, GTSAM and Ceres) solves that far faster than a dense inverse would.
📝 TL;DR
  • Pose graph: nodes = poses, edges = odometry and loop closures, each a spring with a stiffness (information matrix).
  • Optimizing = weighted least squares; errors split in proportion to softness (corridor: 0.6 m ÷ 5 = 0.12 m each).
  • Gauss–Newton: linearize, solve a sparse H Δx = −g, repeat; usually converges in a handful of iterations.
  • Loop closures need detection (bag of words) and verification (RANSAC); one false closure can wreck the map, so use robust kernels (Huber, Cauchy, DCS, switchable constraints).
  • Tools: GTSAM, g2o, Ceres; used in slam_toolbox, LIO-SAM, ORB-SLAM3. Waymo builds maps from many drives but hasn't published its back-end.
