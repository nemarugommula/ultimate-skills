Phase 5 · Lesson 5

HD maps vs mapless driving

Waymo drives with a detailed, centimetre-scale memory of every street it serves. Tesla says its cars read the road live from cameras, with only a navigation map. Both work, both have failure modes, and the reasons are a great lesson in engineering trade-offs.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Prior: what you believe before today's measurements (Lesson 3.1). A map is a prior about the world.
  • Localization against a map: finding your pose by matching what you see to what the map says (Lessons 3.5, 5.6). SLAM builds such maps (5.2–5.4).
  • Kalman gain as trust: when two sources disagree, weight each by how uncertain it is (Lesson 3.3).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're driving home on a road you know by heart. Tonight there's thick fog and the lane lines are worn away: you can barely see them. You still stay in your lane. How? Your memory of the road fills the gap.

Next morning, a construction crew has shifted the lanes 2 m to the left with fresh yellow paint and cones. Your memory now says the wrong thing. You follow the new paint without a second thought. How did you know, both times, which to trust?

That is the HD-map debate in one commute. In August 2026 Waymo wrote that “HD maps are a powerful ‘prior’”, one that lets it run a fully autonomous service from a rider's first trip and that an AI-driven mapping system keeps continuously updated (Waymo, Aug 2026). Tesla's owner's manual says FSD (Supervised) mainly uses its cameras, with map and navigation data for the route and speed (Tesla manual).

What's actually in an HD map?

A navigation map (Google Maps, the car's sat-nav) knows roads as lines, with names, speed limits and turn restrictions: enough for a human to pick a route. An HD map (high-definition map) is built for the robot: every lane, at centimetre-level detail.

Waymo's 2020 mapping post lists speed limits, lane lines, traffic signals, “lane merges, stop signs, crosswalks, and so much more” (Waymo, 2020); its 2016 post adds curb heights and traffic-light locations, and says the map lets the car know where it is “to within 10cm of accuracy” (Waymo, 2016). The map format of the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, which Waymo released with 3D maps for over 100,000 driving segments, encodes lanes, road lines, road edges, stop signs, crosswalks, speed bumps and driveways (map.proto).

🧩 Predict: roughly how much storage does one kilometre of road take: (a) as an HD vector map of lanes, (b) as a dense 3D point-cloud localization map? Kilobytes, megabytes or gigabytes?
Back-of-envelope, illustrative numbers (real formats vary a lot): (a) 5 lane lines × 1 point per metre × 3 coordinates × 4 bytes ≈ 60 KB/km. (b) A 60 m-wide corridor of road and building fronts sampled every 10 cm is 6 million points × 12 bytes ≈ 72 MB/km. For a 10,000 km city: about 0.6 GB of lanes, but about 0.7 TB of points. For comparison, Mobileye says the production cars feeding its crowdsourced map send “small data packets (10kb/km)” (Mobileye REM).

How a map is built and kept fresh

  1. Survey drives: Waymo describes “manually driving our sensor equipped vehicles down each street”; lidar builds a 3D picture of the street.
  2. Alignment: all those drives are stitched into one consistent 3D model, the large pose-graph job of Lesson 5.3.
  3. Annotation: lanes, lights, signs and crosswalks are extracted and labelled (largely automated, checked by people; Waymo's 2016 post talks about “categorizing interesting features on the road”).
  4. Freshness: roads change every day. Waymo says that every time its cars “detect changes on the road, they automatically upload the data”, so the map can be updated and shared with the fleet. Mobileye crowdsources its map from production cars instead of dedicated survey vehicles.

Map-trusting vs perception-trusting, in one drive

The puzzle from the hook, as a playground. A car drives a 260 m stretch at 12 m/s (43 km/h). Its lane is 3.6 m wide and the car is 1.8 m wide, so it can be at most 0.9 m off the lane centre before it touches a line (or a cone). It has two opinions about where the lane centre is:

  • the map: very precise, but only as fresh as its last update;
  • its perception of the painted lines: always current, but noisy, and much worse in fog or over worn paint.

✏️ Worked example: why no fixed blend works

Let w be the weight on perception, so the car aims for (1 − w) × map + w × perception.

  • Construction: the map is 2.5 m wrong and perception is roughly right, so the error is about (1 − w) × 2.5 m. To stay under 0.9 m you need (1 − w) × 2.5 < 0.9, so w > 0.64.
  • Fog: the map is right and perception wanders by up to about 2 × 1.2 = 2.4 m (two standard deviations), so the error is about w × 2.4 m. To stay under 0.9 m you need w < 0.38.

No w satisfies both. The fix is to let the weight change: trust perception when it's confident and the map seems stale, trust the map when perception is blind. That's a Kalman gain again: w = σ²map ÷ (σ²map + σ²perception). Perception reports its own σ (small in clear air, big in fog); the map's σ jumps when perception keeps disagreeing with it by more than 3 combined standard deviations, which is exactly how a fleet spots map changes.

💡 Big idea An HD map is a prior, not the truth. It's priceless when live sensing is weak (fog, worn paint, occlusions, far-away traffic lights) and dangerous when the world has changed. Map-based systems must detect when the map is stale; mapless systems must perceive everything live, every time, including what a map would have told them for free.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Early self-driving prototypes perceived everything live. Lane lines vanish under snow, glare and wear; traffic lights 100 m away are a few pixels; which light controls which lane is often impossible to tell from the camera alone; GPS is only good to metres. Doing it all live, perfectly, was beyond the perception of the time.
  2. What people tried. Navigation maps + GPS (fine for routing, far too coarse to know your lane). Pure live perception (works in good conditions, brittle in bad ones). Lane-keeping on painted lines only (driver-assist systems do this, and hand back control when lines fade).
  3. Why this works. An HD map turns hard real-time questions into look-ups: “where are the lanes, which light is mine, where's the stop line” are answered before the car arrives, and the onboard computer can spend its effort on what's “dynamic or new”, as Waymo puts it. With 10 cm localization, a mapped stop line is known to about 10 cm even in fog, while the fog example above shows perception alone wandering by metres.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Building: survey drives and annotation for every street. Scaling: every new city must be mapped first. Freshness: construction, repainting and new signs make the map wrong, and a car that trusts a stale map follows lanes that no longer exist (the construction zone above). Storage and updates: dense localization layers are tens of megabytes per kilometre (illustrative).
  5. What came next. Fleet change detection and automated map updates (Waymo's AI-driven mapping system); crowdsourced lightweight maps from production cars (Mobileye's REM, ~10 KB/km uploads); and online mapping: networks such as HDMapNet (2021) and MapTR (ICLR 2023) that predict the vectorized HD map, lane by lane, from the car's own cameras as it drives, often helped by a standard navigation map. Lesson 6.4 covers learned lanes and topology.

The scalability argument, both ways

🗺️ HD-map-first (Waymo)👀 Mapless / navigation map (Tesla, as publicly stated)
StrengthStrong prior: lanes, lights, stop lines known in advance; frees onboard compute; very robust in bad visibilityWorks on any road the car has never seen; no mapping fleet; no stale-map failures
WeaknessMust map before serving a city; must detect and fix changes quicklyMust perceive everything live, every drive, including what a map would give for free
Scaling costGrows with kilometres of road served and how fast they changeGrows with the amount of training data and compute (Lesson 9.5)

The arguments are converging: map-based teams automate mapping and lean more on live perception for change; mapless teams use navigation maps as a weak prior and train networks to predict lane-level maps online. The real question is not “map or no map” but how strong a prior, and how quickly you notice it's wrong.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Real HD-map formats vary (Lanelet2 and OpenDRIVE are common open ones; companies use their own). The easiest way to touch real lane-level map data is the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, whose scenarios include map features; here's the idea of reading them (field names from the dataset's map.proto and scenario.proto):

# pip install waymo-open-dataset-tf-2-12-0   (package name depends on your TF version; see the repo)
import tensorflow as tf
from waymo_open_dataset.protos import scenario_pb2

for record in tf.data.TFRecordDataset("training.tfrecord-00000-of-01000"):
    scenario = scenario_pb2.Scenario.FromString(record.numpy())
    for f in scenario.map_features:                         # one entry per lane, line, crosswalk…
        kind = f.WhichOneof("feature_data")                  # 'lane', 'road_line', 'road_edge', 'stop_sign', 'crosswalk', …
        if kind == "lane":
            pts = [(p.x, p.y, p.z) for p in f.lane.polyline]     # the lane centre line, in metres
            print(f.id, len(pts), "points, speed limit", f.lane.speed_limit_mph, "mph")
    break

The exact pip package name and TensorFlow version change between releases; check the repository's README before installing. For online map construction from cameras, the MapTR code (GitHub) trains on the nuScenes and Argoverse 2 datasets.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Maps first. Sensor cars drive every street; lidar builds a 3D picture that is processed into a detailed map with lanes, signals, stop signs and crosswalks; cars upload detected changes so the map can be updated and shared. In Aug 2026 Waymo called HD maps “a powerful prior”, “incredibly helpful in poor visibility and complex thoroughfares”, kept fresh by an AI-driven mapping system.

Sources: Waymo 2020, Waymo Aug 2026

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's manual says FSD (Supervised) mainly uses its cameras and is more accurate with the most recent map data; navigation and other map-derived data set speed and route. Tesla's AI page describes networks that turn video from all cameras into the road layout. In his CVPR 2021 keynote, Andrej Karpathy (then Tesla's head of AI) argued that the lidar-plus-HD-map approach is expensive to keep up to date at scale. So: navigation maps yes, Waymo-style HD maps no, as publicly stated.

Sources: Tesla manual, tesla.com/AI, CVPR'21 keynote

🛸 Drones

Most autonomous drones fly mapless: they map their surroundings live as they fly (Skydio: “constantly map the environment as they fly through it”). Mapping is often the job: Skydio's 3D Scan automates capturing a structure to build a 3D model or digital twin.

Sources: Skydio autonomy, Skydio 3D Scan

🚙 Mobileye

A middle path: Mobileye's REM builds a crowdsourced, continuously updated map (the Roadbook) from production cars that send small packets of about 10 KB per km, rather than from dedicated survey vehicles.

Sources: Mobileye REM

Why they differ: Waymo runs driverless service in specific cities, where a strong, checked prior buys safety margin and the cost of mapping is paid per service area. Tesla sells supervised driving on every road its owners use, where mapping everything to HD detail and keeping it fresh would be enormous, so it puts the load on perception and data. Drones fly where nobody has mapped (and often make the map). Mobileye sits in between by mapping with the cars it's already in.

🎤 Interview angle

“Should our robotaxi use HD maps?” A strong answer:

  • Lists what an HD map gives (lane geometry and topology, traffic-light-to-lane association, stop lines, localization layer) and what it costs (mapping, annotation, storage, freshness).
  • Treats the map as a prior with a staleness detector: compare live perception with the map, gate disagreements, update the map, and degrade gracefully (slow down, remote assistance) when both are uncertain.
  • Explains why Waymo and Tesla chose differently (service area vs everywhere; lidar vs cameras) and mentions online mapping (HDMapNet, MapTR) as the middle ground.
  • Puts numbers on it: lane 3.6 m, car 1.8 m → 0.9 m margin; localization needs to be ~10 cm (Lesson 5.6).
🏭 In the real world
Waymo's detailed maps 🚗 AV 🏢 Waymo · 2016–2026 Waymo blog

How: survey drives with sensor cars, lidar 3D models processed into maps with lanes, signals, crosswalks and curbs; fleet-detected changes uploaded automatically.

Why this method: a strong prior for driverless service, especially in poor visibility, and it lets onboard compute focus on what's new.

Crowdsourced maps 🏢 Mobileye REM

How: production cars with Mobileye systems send compact road data (about 10 KB/km) that is aggregated into a continuously updated map.

Why this method: scales with cars already on the road instead of a dedicated survey fleet.

Maps predicted online 🧪 Research · 2023 MapTR (ICLR 2023)

How: a transformer predicts lane dividers, road boundaries and crosswalks as vector polylines directly from surround-view cameras, frame by frame.

Why this method: gives lane-level structure without an offline HD map; follow-ups (MapTRv2, IJCV 2024) improve accuracy.

Maps as research data 🏢 Waymo · 2021 Waymo Open Motion Dataset

How: object trajectories and 3D maps for over 100,000 segments released for research.

Why this method: motion prediction (Lesson 7.1) leans heavily on map context: lanes tell you where cars can go.

🤯 Fun fact · 10 KB per kilometre

Mobileye says the cars feeding its crowdsourced map send data in “small data packets (10kb/km)” (Mobileye). That's less than one photo from your phone for an entire kilometre of road. The trick: send only what matters (lane geometry, signs, landmarks as compact descriptions), never raw images.

Check yourself

1. A car's lane is 3.6 m wide and the car is 1.8 m wide. A construction crew shifts the lane 2.5 m and the car aims for 0.6 × map + 0.4 × perception, with perception correct. Roughly how far off is it?
Error ≈ 0.6 × 2.5 m = 1.5 m, but the margin is only (3.6 − 1.8) ÷ 2 = 0.9 m. A fixed blend that leans on a stale map fails; the trust must shift to perception when the two persistently disagree.
2. Which is the strongest argument for an HD map in a driverless robotaxi?
Maps never replace perception (pedestrians, cars and construction aren't in them) and they do go stale. Their value is the prior: hard questions answered in advance and checked offline. Waymo's Aug 2026 post makes exactly this point.
3. Overnight, a city repaints an intersection with a new turn lane. What should a well-designed map-based car do the next morning?
That's staleness detection: gate the map against live sensing, shift trust locally, degrade gracefully, and feed the change back (Waymo describes cars uploading detected changes). The map is fixed for everyone after one car notices.
📝 TL;DR
  • HD map = lane geometry + topology + traffic control + a localization layer, at centimetre scale. Navigation maps are just roads.
  • Built by survey drives, alignment (pose graphs), annotation; kept fresh by fleet change detection (Waymo) or crowdsourcing (Mobileye, ~10 KB/km).
  • Maps shine when sensing is weak (fog, worn paint) and fail when the world changes (construction). No fixed blend handles both: trust by confidence, with staleness detection.
  • Waymo: HD maps as a powerful prior (Aug 2026). Tesla, as publicly stated: cameras plus navigation maps, no HD maps. Online mapping (HDMapNet, MapTR) is the middle ground.
