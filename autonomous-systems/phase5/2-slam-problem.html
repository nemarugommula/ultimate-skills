Phase 5 · Lesson 2

SLAM: the chicken-and-egg problem

To know where you are, you need a map. To draw a map, you need to know where you are. Robots exploring somewhere new have neither, so they build both at once: Simultaneous Localization And Mapping. Here's why that's hard, and the surprising trick that makes it work.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Odometry: estimating motion from wheel turns or an IMU. Small errors pile up, so the estimate drifts (Lessons 2.5 and 2.6).
  • The Kalman filter: predict (move, grow the uncertainty), then correct (pull toward a measurement, shrink it), with a covariance matrix P holding how unsure each number is and how the errors are linked (Lesson 3.3). The EKF (extended Kalman filter) does the same for curved models by linearizing them (Lesson 3.4).
  • Occupancy grids: a map of free, occupied and unknown cells, built assuming the robot's pose is known (Lesson 5.1). Pose = position plus heading.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A robot explores a building it has never seen. It drives a big loop, spotting landmarks (a pillar, a door, a fire extinguisher) along the way. Its wheels drift, so by the end of the loop it's ± 0.8 m unsure of where it is, and the landmarks it saw last are just as fuzzy.

Then it spots the very first pillar again, one it mapped precisely at the start. Obviously the robot's own fix improves. But here's the strange part: the landmarks it saw a minute ago, on the far side of the building, and is not looking at, also get sharper. How can seeing one thing improve your knowledge of things you can't see?

That question sits at the heart of a 40-year research problem. Its first probabilistic answer was the “stochastic map” of Randall Smith, Matthew Self and Peter Cheeseman (1987–1990), and the name SLAM first appeared at a robotics symposium in 1995, as Hugh Durrant-Whyte and Tim Bailey recount in their tutorial (IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine, 2006).

✏️ Worked example: how fast does drift smear a map?

A gyro that drifts 0.6° per metre sounds tiny. After driving d metres, the heading is off by 0.6 × d degrees. The sideways error is roughly the heading error × distance, summed along the way: about ½ × (0.6° in radians) × d².

10 m6°0.5 m

47 m (one lap)28°11.6 m if it drove straight

On a closed lap the turns partly cancel (you head east, then west), so the real end error is much smaller than the straight-line number, which is why the playground's answer surprises people. Still: errors that grow with d² make every wall you map late in the run land in the wrong place. A wall seen twice, early and late, shows up twice: the classic doubled corridor of a broken SLAM map.

The trick: estimate the robot and the map together

EKF-SLAM, the first workable answer, does something bold: put the robot and every landmark into one big state vector, and run one Kalman filter over all of it.

state = [ x, y, θ  |  ℓ1x, ℓ1y  |  ℓ2x, ℓ2y  |  … ]     size 3 + 2n     P is (3 + 2n) × (3 + 2n) Three numbers for the robot's pose, two per landmark. The covariance P stores every uncertainty and, crucially, how every pair of errors is linked.
  1. Predict (the robot moves): only the robot's part of the state changes; its uncertainty grows. Landmarks don't move.
  2. Correct (the robot measures a landmark's range and bearing: “3.1 m away, 40° to my left”): the innovation pulls both the robot and that landmark toward agreement.
  3. New landmark? Add two numbers to the state, placed where the robot believes it sees it. Its uncertainty starts as the robot's uncertainty plus the sensor's.
💡 Big idea: correlations are the map's memory When a landmark is first added, its error is the robot's error at that moment (plus sensor noise). The filter remembers that link in the off-diagonal entries of P. Later, if a measurement shows the robot was 0.5 m further east than it thought, the filter knows every landmark seen from that drifted pose was probably also 0.5 m east, and moves them all. That's the answer to the hook: you're not measuring the far landmarks again; you're learning how wrong the pose was when you placed them.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Map with odometry alone and the map inherits all the drift: walls double, loops don't close. Localize against a map you don't have and there's nothing to match. Hand-surveying every building before a robot can enter it doesn't scale.
  2. What people tried. Map, then localize, then re-map, alternating with the latest best guess (errors feed each other and never settle). Estimate each landmark separately with its own little filter, ignoring links (cheap, but overconfident: the filter forgets that all those landmarks share the robot's error, so it can't fix them together and becomes too sure).
  3. Why this works. One filter over robot + landmarks keeps the correlations, so information flows: a single re-sighting fixes the robot and, through P's off-diagonals, every landmark placed from a drifted pose. In the playground, closing the loop drops the robot's 95% ellipse from about 0.6 m to about 0.1 m, and the worst landmark's from about 0.73 m to about 0.45 m, from re-seeing one or two landmarks.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. P has (3 + 2n)² entries and every update touches all of them: O(n²) per measurement. 1,000 landmarks → 4 million entries (32 MB of doubles), updated many times per second. Linearization errors pile up and make it overconfident over long runs. And it trusts data association: match an observation to the wrong landmark once and the error is spread, with full confidence, over the whole map.
  5. What came next. FastSLAM (2002): a particle filter over robot paths, with a tiny independent filter per landmark inside each particle, which avoids the giant matrix. Then graph SLAM: keep all poses and constraints and solve them as one sparse least-squares problem, which scales to huge maps and can revisit old decisions (Lesson 5.3). Almost every modern system (slam_toolbox, Cartographer, ORB-SLAM, LIO-SAM) is graph-based.

A lidar sees hundreds of features per second, and a city has millions. That's why EKF-SLAM is now mainly a teaching tool and a component of small systems, and why the field moved to sparse graphs, where each measurement only touches the two things it connects.

Data association: “which landmark is this?”

The math above silently assumed that, when the robot sees a pillar, it knows which pillar. In reality pillars look alike. Picking the right match is called data association, and getting it wrong is the most common way SLAM breaks.

The naive rule is “pick the closest predicted landmark”. The better rule uses the uncertainty: measure distance in units of standard deviations, the Mahalanobis distance. A landmark with a long, thin ellipse may be a better explanation for a measurement that's 1.5 m away along its long axis than a sharp landmark 0.9 m away across its narrow side. If even the best match is more than about 3 standard deviations away, it's probably a new landmark (this cutoff is the same gate you met in Lesson 3.7).

dM² = (z − ẑ)ᵀ S⁻¹ (z − ẑ)     match = smallest dM;   new landmark if every dM > 3 z is what you measured, ẑ is where landmark k should appear, and S is how uncertain that prediction is (landmark + robot + sensor). Divide the gap by the uncertainty, in every direction.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You'll rarely deploy EKF-SLAM, but writing its update once is the best way to understand correlations. The core, in NumPy (state x, covariance P, landmark k at index j = 3 + 2k):

import numpy as np

def observe(x, P, j, z, R):
    """EKF-SLAM correction with one range-bearing measurement z = [r, b] of the landmark at x[j:j+2]."""
    dx, dy = x[j] - x[0], x[j + 1] - x[1]
    q = dx * dx + dy * dy; r = np.sqrt(q)
    z_hat = np.array([r, np.arctan2(dy, dx) - x[2]])
    H = np.zeros((2, len(x)))                              # only 5 columns are non-zero…
    H[:, [0, 1, 2]] = [[-dx / r, -dy / r, 0], [dy / q, -dx / q, -1]]
    H[:, [j, j + 1]] = [[dx / r, dy / r], [-dy / q, dx / q]]
    S = H @ P @ H.T + R
    K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)                         # …but K fills every row: that's the correlation
    y = z - z_hat; y[1] = np.arctan2(np.sin(y[1]), np.cos(y[1]))  # wrap the bearing error
    d2 = y @ np.linalg.solve(S, y)                         # Mahalanobis distance², for data association
    return x + K @ y, (np.eye(len(x)) - K @ H) @ P, d2

In practice on a ROS 2 robot you'd run slam_toolbox (graph-based 2D lidar SLAM, the one the Nav2 docs recommend) and on a camera or drone ORB-SLAM3; both are Lesson 5.4.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Waymo largely sidesteps online SLAM on public roads: it maps first, then localizes against that map. Its 2020 handbook post says the mapping starts by “manually driving our sensor equipped vehicles down each street”, building a 3D picture with lidar, then processing it into a detailed map. A Waymo 2016 post says the car uses the map to know where it is “to within 10cm of accuracy” (Lessons 5.5, 5.6).

Sources: Waymo, Sep 2020, Waymo, Dec 2016

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published whether or how its cars run SLAM. What it does say publicly: FSD (Supervised) mainly uses its cameras and is more accurate with recent map data, using navigation data for the route; its owner's manual says that. The approach Tesla has described in talks puts the effort into networks that read the road from video, rather than into a prior lidar map (Lesson 5.5).

Sources: Model Y owner's manual (FSD), tesla.com/AI

🛸 Drones

A drone exploring a building or flying under a bridge has no prior map and often no GPS, so it must do SLAM (or at least visual-inertial odometry: camera + IMU motion tracking, Lesson 3.6). Skydio says its drones “constantly map the environment as they fly through it”; open-source drones commonly run visual-inertial SLAM such as ORB-SLAM3 or VINS.

Sources: Skydio autonomy, ORB-SLAM3 (arXiv 2020)

🤖 Robots

Warehouse and service robots usually run SLAM once to build the floor map (driven by a person or exploring), save it, then switch to localization-only for daily work. The Nav2 docs walk through exactly this with slam_toolbox.

Sources: Nav2 SLAM tutorial

Why they differ: SLAM is hardest when you must trust it immediately in an unknown place. A robotaxi company can drive its cities first and turn SLAM into an offline, carefully checked mapping job; a drone or a robot exploring a new building can't, so it runs SLAM live. Tesla's stated bet avoids depending on a detailed prior map at all, which moves the burden onto live perception.

🎤 Interview angle

“What makes SLAM hard, and why did EKF-SLAM fall out of favour?” A strong answer:

  • States the chicken-and-egg plainly: pose errors smear the map; map errors mislead the pose.
  • Explains that the correlations in the joint covariance are what let one loop closure fix the whole map.
  • Gives the O(n²) cost with a number (1,000 landmarks → 4 M entries) and the move to sparse graph optimization.
  • Names data association as the main failure mode, and Mahalanobis gating (plus geometric checks) as the defence.
🏭 In the real world
The stochastic map 📜 History · 1987–1990 Smith, Self & Cheeseman

How: represent the robot and all mapped objects in one state with one covariance matrix, and update it as the robot moves and observes.

Why this method: it was the first consistent way to keep track of how uncertain spatial relationships depend on each other: the foundation of EKF-SLAM.

FastSLAM 🧪 Research · 2002 Montemerlo, Thrun, Koller & Wegbreit, AAAI 2002

How: a particle filter over the robot's path; each particle carries its own small Kalman filter per landmark.

Why this method: given a known path, landmarks are independent, so the giant covariance matrix disappears and cost grows gently with the number of landmarks.

slam_toolbox in ROS 2 🧰 Open source GitHub

How: 2D lidar scan matching plus a pose graph with loop closures; outputs an occupancy grid; supports mapping, then localization-only on the saved map.

Why this method: graph-based SLAM scales to large buildings and is the SLAM library Nav2's documentation recommends.

Visual-inertial SLAM on drones 🧰 Open source ORB-SLAM3

How: camera features plus IMU, a map of 3D points, loop closing and multiple maps that can be merged.

Why this method: cameras and IMUs are light and cheap, which is what a small aircraft can carry; the IMU keeps tracking through fast motion.

🤯 Fun fact · SLAM got its name in 1995

The problem was studied for a decade before it had a catchy name. According to Durrant-Whyte and Bailey's tutorial, the acronym SLAM was first presented in a survey at the 1995 International Symposium on Robotics Research (IEEE RAM, 2006). Before that, people wrote “concurrent mapping and localization”, which, you'll agree, makes a much worse T-shirt.

Check yourself

1. An EKF-SLAM map has 500 landmarks. About how many numbers does the covariance matrix P hold?
The state has 3 + 2 × 500 = 1,003 numbers, so P is 1,003 × 1,003 ≈ 1.0 million entries (8 MB of doubles), and each measurement update touches all of them. That O(n²) growth is why large maps use sparse graphs instead.
2. After a long loop, a robot re-observes the first landmark it mapped. Why do landmarks it is not currently observing also become more certain?
Each landmark was placed from a drifting pose, so its error is tied to the robot's error at that time. The joint covariance remembers those ties; when the loop closure reveals how far the robot had drifted, the gain K spreads the correction to every correlated landmark.
3. In a parking garage full of identical pillars, a SLAM map suddenly folds onto itself after the robot turns a corner. What's the most likely cause?
Lookalike landmarks are the classic trap. A filter that accepts the wrong match spreads the error everywhere with full confidence. Defences: Mahalanobis gating, matching several landmarks jointly, geometric verification, and graph back-ends that can undo a bad match (Lesson 5.3).
📝 TL;DR
  • SLAM: build the map and find yourself in it at the same time. Pose errors smear maps; bad maps mislead poses.
  • EKF-SLAM: one state [robot | all landmarks], one covariance. Correlations let one loop closure shrink every ellipse.
  • Cost is O(n²): 1,000 landmarks → 4 million covariance entries. Modern systems use sparse pose graphs (5.3).
  • Data association is the weak spot: use Mahalanobis distance and a gate (≈ 3σ), not “closest dot”.
  • Waymo maps first, then localizes; drones and exploring robots run SLAM live; Tesla hasn't published its approach beyond relying on cameras and navigation maps.
