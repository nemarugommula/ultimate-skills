Phase 3 · Lesson 3

The Kalman filter: two noisy guesses, one better answer

GPS jumps around. Wheels and IMUs drift. Blend them the right way and you get an estimate better than either one alone. This 1960 idea flew to the Moon and runs in nearly every drone today.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Belief: the robot never knows its position exactly; it keeps a best guess plus how unsure it is (Lesson 3.1).
  • A bell curve (Gaussian) is described by two numbers: its center, the mean, and its width, the standard deviation σ. Variance is σ². Refresher: distributions and variance in the Math course.
  • The Bayes filter loop: predict where you'll be from how you moved, then correct with what you measure (Lesson 3.2).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Your delivery robot has been rolling for a minute on wheel odometry alone. It thinks it's at 100 m along the street, give or take 5 m. Now a GPS fix arrives: 103 m, give or take 2 m.

Where is the robot? 100? 103? Halfway, at 101.5? And here's the stranger question: after combining them, should you be more sure than the GPS alone, or less? Hold your guess; the first playground lets you lock it in.

In the late 1950s, NASA Ames engineer Stanley Schmidt faced this exact problem for a spacecraft going to the Moon, with computers far weaker than a phone. He adapted Rudolf Kalman's brand-new filter into what became the Schmidt–Kalman filter for Apollo navigation; the FAA later added it to its aircraft-tracking systems (NASA).

🧩 Predict before you try it: unlock the sliders and drag the GPS to 115, far from the prediction. Will the green fused curve get wider because the two sources disagree?
No, and it surprises almost everyone. The fused width depends only on the two widths, never on how far apart the centers are. A basic Kalman filter has no idea that a 12 m disagreement is suspicious; it just splits the difference by trust. That blind spot is why real systems add outlier gates (Lesson 3.7).

Numbers first: the blend, by hand

✏️ Worked example: the puzzle, solved

Write each guess as a mean and a variance (σ²):

meanσvariance σ²
Prediction (odometry)100 m5 m25 m²
Measurement (GPS)103 m2 m4 m²
  1. How much to trust the GPS, the Kalman gain: K = 25 ÷ (25 + 4) = 0.86. The prediction is the shakier one, so the measurement gets 86% of the say.
  2. Move toward the measurement by that fraction: 100 + 0.86 × (103 − 100) = 100 + 2.59 = 102.59 m. Not the midpoint: much closer to the GPS.
  3. New uncertainty: (1 − K) × 25 = 0.14 × 25 = 3.45 m², so σ ≈ 1.86 m.

That last line answers the strange question: 1.86 m is better than the GPS's 2 m, and much better than the odometry's 5 m. Two imperfect witnesses, combined honestly, beat the best one. The difference (103 − 100 = 3 m) has a name, the innovation: the part of the measurement you didn't already expect.

💡 Big idea A Kalman filter is a weighted average that knows its own weights. Every step it predicts (move the guess, and grow the uncertainty because motion is imperfect), then corrects (pull toward the measurement in proportion to how much more you trust it, and shrink the uncertainty). Uncertainty breathes: out on every prediction, in on every measurement.
🧠 Analogy: the ship's navigator

Before satellites, a navigator plotted the ship's position from speed, heading and time: dead reckoning. Every hour the pencil mark got less trustworthy, because currents and wind push the ship around. Then a star sighting or a lighthouse gave a fix. A careful navigator didn't jump the mark straight onto the fix, and didn't ignore it either: a rough sighting nudged the mark a little, a sharp one moved it nearly all the way. A Kalman filter is that navigator, doing it 100 times a second, with the nudge computed exactly.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. GPS alone jumps around by meters from one fix to the next and arrives only a few times a second. An IMU or wheel odometry alone is smooth and fast but drifts: small errors add up, so after a minute you can be meters off and never know it. Neither is good enough to steer a car in a lane or hold a drone in place.
  2. What people tried. Use the best sensor and ignore the rest (throws information away). Average them 50/50 (a bad sensor drags you off). A fixed blend, like a complementary filter that trusts the IMU for fast changes and GPS for slow ones (works, and drones still use it, but the weights are hand-tuned and can't react when GPS gets worse in a city canyon).
  3. Why the Kalman filter works. It sets the weights from the uncertainties themselves, and updates those uncertainties every step. Prediction variance 25, measurement variance 4 → gain 25/(25+4) ≈ 0.86 → fused variance ≈ 3.4 m². When the GPS gets worse, its variance rises and the gain drops automatically. For linear motion and Gaussian noise, no other blend has smaller average squared error.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You must give it a motion model and honest noise levels (Q for motion, R for the sensor); lie and it either jitters or lags. It assumes the world is linear and the noise is a single bell curve. Turning cars, rotating drones and range-and-bearing sensors are nonlinear; one wild GPS jump (a reflection off a building) gets blended in as if it were true. Its matrix math grows with the cube of the state size.
  5. What came next. The EKF linearizes the curves at each step (Schmidt's Apollo version was an early one), the UKF pushes a few sample points through them instead (Lesson 3.4), particle filters handle many-bump beliefs like “I'm in one of three identical hallways” (Lesson 3.5), and gating plus robust tricks reject the wild measurements (Lesson 3.7).

The loop, in two steps

Here's the whole one-dimensional filter. x is the estimate, P its variance, z a measurement with variance R, and Q how much uncertainty one step of motion adds.

Predict   x ← x + v·Δt    P ← P + Q

Correct   K = P / (P + R)    x ← x + K (z − x)    P ← (1 − K) P Predict: move by what you commanded (v·Δt) and get a bit less sure. Correct: compute the trust K, move toward the measurement by K of the gap, and get more sure.

Read K like a dial. If P is huge (you're lost), K → 1 and you jump to the measurement. If R is huge (the sensor is junk), K → 0 and you ignore it. Everything else is in between, set by the numbers.

🏆 Challenge: make the Kalman filter's error less than half the raw GPS error, with the bias at 3% or more. Q and R are on a ×10 log scale: 0 means the honest values, +1 means ten times bigger.

What you should find: with honest settings, the filter already clearly beats raw GPS, but the bias slowly drags it off, because the filter thinks odometry errors are random noise that cancels out. Bias doesn't cancel. Raise Q a notch and the filter leans more on GPS, which fixes the drift. Push R far down and the estimate jitters with every fix; push it far up and it rides the drifting odometer. The sweet spot is a range, not a point. (Real systems go further and estimate the bias itself as an extra state; Lesson 3.6.)

From one number to a whole state

A real car or drone tracks several numbers at once: position and velocity in x and y, often heading, and sensor biases. The same two steps work, written with matrices (a refresher: matrix multiplication). Each symbol has the same job as before:

SymbolSize (for x, y, vx, vy)Plain meaning
x, P4 numbers, 4×4Best guess of the state, and how uncertain each part is, plus how the errors are linked (P's off-diagonal entries).
F4×4The motion model: “position += velocity × Δt, velocity stays”. Predict: x ← Fx, P ← FPFᵀ + Q.
H2×4What the sensor sees: GPS measures position, not velocity, so H just picks out x and y.
Q, R4×4, 2×2Noise added by one step of motion, and the sensor's noise.
K4×2The gain, now a matrix: K = PHᵀ (HPHᵀ + R)⁻¹. Then x ← x + K(z − Hx), P ← (I − KH)P.

Here's the magic: GPS never measures velocity, yet the filter estimates it. Because F links position to velocity, the off-diagonal parts of P learn that “if I'm further ahead than I thought, I'm probably faster than I thought”, and K spreads each position correction into the velocity too.

🧩 Predict: in the next playground the car drives through a tunnel with no GPS. While it's inside, will the uncertainty ellipse grow equally in every direction, or mostly one way?
Mostly along the direction of travel. Being unsure of your speed by 1 m/s puts you about 2.5 m ahead or behind after the 2.5 s it takes to cross, but barely moves you sideways. 🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The filter is a dozen lines of NumPy; write it yourself once (the Phase 3 lab does). In practice you'll use FilterPy for experiments in Python, robot_localization in ROS 2 to fuse wheel odometry, IMU and GPS on a ground robot, and the estimators built into flight stacks (PX4 EKF2, ArduPilot EKF3) on drones.

import numpy as np

dt = 0.1
F = np.array([[1, dt], [0, 1]])            # state = [position, velocity]
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])                 # GPS sees position only
Q = 0.5 * np.array([[dt**4/4, dt**3/2], [dt**3/2, dt**2]])  # random accelerations
R = np.array([[5.0**2]])                    # GPS σ = 5 m
x, P = np.array([0.0, 10.0]), np.diag([100.0, 4.0])

def kf_step(x, P, z=None):
    x, P = F @ x, F @ P @ F.T + Q                 # predict: move, grow uncertainty
    if z is not None:                         # correct (only when a fix arrives)
        S = H @ P @ H.T + R                       # how big the innovation should be
        K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)            # the gain
        x = x + K @ (z - H @ x)
        P = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P
    return x, P

# Same thing with FilterPy:  pip install filterpy
from filterpy.kalman import KalmanFilter
kf = KalmanFilter(dim_x=2, dim_z=1)
kf.x, kf.F, kf.H, kf.P, kf.Q, kf.R = x.copy(), F, H, P.copy(), Q, R
kf.predict(); kf.update(np.array([1.3]))

The 15 booleans pick which of x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, their velocities and linear accelerations each sensor contributes. Fusing the same quantity from two sensors that secretly share an error source (two odometry topics from the same wheels) makes the filter overconfident: a classic bug.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

This is where Kalman filters are most visible. PX4's EKF2 is a 24-state extended Kalman filter: attitude (as a quaternion), velocity, position, gyro and accelerometer biases, magnetic field and wind. The IMU, at 100 Hz or more, drives the predict step; GPS, barometer, magnetometer, rangefinder, optical flow and vision are corrections. Each sensor has a delay setting so old measurements are fused at the right moment, and each passes an innovation check (a gate in standard deviations) before it's trusted. ArduPilot's EKF3 runs one filter “core” per IMU and uses the healthiest.

Sources: PX4 EKF docs, ArduPilot EKF overview

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its estimator internals, but its design is the classic modular one: many sensors fused into a state estimate. The 6th-generation Waymo Driver (rider-only operations began February 2026) combines cameras, lidar and imaging radar. Radar measures an object's speed directly through the Doppler effect (Lesson 2.4): exactly the kind of measurement that tightens a tracker's velocity estimate on the first frame instead of after several. The standard open baseline for 3D tracking on driving data, AB3DMOT, is a Kalman filter plus matching (Lesson 6.5).

Sources: Waymo, Feb 2026, AB3DMOT

🚘 Tesla

Tesla took radar out of Model 3 and Model Y in North America from May 2021 (“Tesla Vision”). In his CVPR 2021 keynote, Andrej Karpathy, then Tesla's head of AI, described neural networks that estimate other cars' depth and velocity straight from video: work that used to be done by fusing radar returns in a tracker. Tesla hasn't published how it estimates the car's own position; its public talks focus on the networks.

Sources: Tesla support, CVPR'21 WAD keynote

Why they differ: a drone has no wheels and can't stop to think, so a fast, well-understood filter over cheap sensors is the core of its autopilot. Waymo can afford sensors that measure distance and speed directly, so classic fusion stays at the heart of its stack. Tesla bet that enough video and data can teach a network to infer what radar used to measure, trading sensor cost for learning (Lessons 0.4 and 9.2).

Choice✅ Gains❌ Costs

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain a Kalman filter to me, and tell me how you'd tune one.” A strong answer:

  • Explains predict and correct in one sentence each, and the gain as relative trust: K = P/(P+R) in 1D, with a number.
  • Knows Q is model distrust and R is sensor distrust: R from the sensor's datasheet or a log of it standing still; Q tuned until the filter's own uncertainty matches its real errors.
  • Checks consistency with the innovations: they should look like zero-mean noise of the size the filter predicts (NIS/NEES tests). Too big means overconfident; too small means too timid.
  • Knows the failure modes: nonlinear models (EKF/UKF), biases (add them as states), outliers (gates), correlated or delayed measurements (time stamps, fusion horizons, as in PX4).
🏭 In the real world
Navigating Apollo to the Moon 📜 History · 1960s McGee & Schmidt, NASA TM (1985)

How: NASA Ames picked up Kalman's 1960 filter almost as soon as it was published and turned it into a practical navigation tool, the extended Kalman filter, with formulations small enough for onboard computers.

Why this method: trajectory measurements arrived one at a time and were noisy; the filter folded each one into a running estimate without storing the whole history, which was all a 1960s flight computer could afford.

Every PX4 drone's state estimate 🧰 Open source PX4: Using the ECL EKF

How: a 24-state EKF predicts with the IMU and corrects with GPS, barometer, magnetometer, rangefinder, optical flow and vision, each with its own delay and innovation gate.

Why this method: the controller needs attitude and velocity hundreds of times a second, and no single sensor gives them; the filter also estimates the IMU's biases so the drone doesn't slowly drift.

Tracking every car around you 🧪 Research · 2020 AB3DMOT: 3D multi-object tracking baseline

How: a 3D detector finds boxes in each lidar frame; a Kalman filter per object predicts where each box will be next; the Hungarian algorithm matches predictions to new detections.

Why this method: simple, very fast and strong enough that it became the baseline other trackers are compared against (Lesson 6.5).

Ground robots on ROS 2 🧰 Open source robot_localization

How: EKF and UKF nodes fuse any number of odometry, IMU, pose and GPS topics into one smooth estimate, with per-sensor configuration of which quantities to use.

Why this method: warehouse and delivery robots get a robust estimate by configuration rather than custom math.

🤯 Fun fact · your flight lands on time thanks to the Moon

The same Schmidt–Kalman filtering that was developed for Apollo navigation was later added to the FAA's aircraft-tracking calculations, helping air traffic control know where planes are (NASA). One idea, from spacecraft to airliners to the quadcopter in your neighbour's garden.

🌉 How it connects

The Kalman filter is the Bayes filter of Lesson 3.2 when every belief is one bell curve. Lesson 3.4 bends it to curved motion (EKF, UKF); 3.5 drops the bell-curve assumption (particle filters); 3.6 fuses real GPS, IMU and cameras; 3.7 throws out the lies. Later you'll meet it again tracking other cars (6.5), inside SLAM (5.2) and as the “state estimate” every controller consumes (Phase 8). Its math, a weighted average of two bell curves, is Bayes' rule with Gaussians: see Bayes' rule in the Math course.

Check yourself

1. Your prediction has variance 9 m² and a measurement also has variance 9 m². What's the fused variance?
K = 9 ÷ (9 + 9) = 0.5, so you land halfway, and the variance is (1 − 0.5) × 9 = 4.5 m². Two equally good, independent sources halve the variance: σ drops from 3 m to about 2.1 m.
2. A drone's position estimate twitches every time a GPS fix arrives, even while it hovers still. What's the most likely tuning mistake?
A small R (or a large Q) pushes the gain K toward 1, so every noisy fix yanks the estimate. Measure the GPS noise while stationary, set R to match, and let the IMU-driven prediction smooth between fixes.
3. A car enters a 10-second tunnel with no GPS. What happens inside the filter?
Predict steps still run (the car is still moving), each adding Q, so P grows, mostly along the road. With no corrections it never shrinks. At the exit K is close to 1 and the estimate snaps toward the first fix: exactly the “breathing” you saw in Playground 3.
📝 TL;DR
  • Predict (move, grow uncertainty by Q), then correct (gain K = P/(P+R), pull toward the measurement, shrink uncertainty).
  • The fused estimate beats every input: variances 25 and 4 give 3.45. Disagreement doesn't widen it, so you need outlier gates.
  • With matrices it estimates things no sensor measures, like velocity from GPS positions.
  • Q too small → lags and drifts; R too small → jitters. Biases need their own states; curves need the EKF, UKF or particles.
  • Drones run it openly (PX4 EKF2, ArduPilot EKF3); Waymo-style stacks fuse lidar, radar and cameras; Tesla moved much of this work into networks trained on video.
