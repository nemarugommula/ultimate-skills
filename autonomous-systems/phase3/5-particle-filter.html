Phase 3 · Lesson 5

Particle filters & Monte Carlo localization

What if the robot has no idea where it is, or could be in one of five places at once? A single bell curve can't say that. A crowd of guesses can: move them, score them against the map, keep the good ones. This is how most ROS robots find themselves in a building.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The Bayes filter loop (Lesson 3.2): predict by moving the belief with the motion model (it blurs), correct by multiplying by the measurement's likelihood and normalizing (it sharpens). The histogram filter does it on a grid.
  • Kalman, EKF and UKF (Lessons 3.3 and 3.4) keep one bell curve. They fail when the belief has several bumps, and they can lock onto a wrong answer after a bad start.
  • Sampling: drawing random examples in proportion to probabilities (refresher: sampling in the Math course). Lidar (Lesson 2.3) returns a distance at each of many angles.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You switch on a delivery robot in an office floor it has a perfect map of. It has a lidar, but no idea where it is. Its first scan says “corridor, walls 1.5 m away on both sides”, which is true in a dozen places. What should its belief look like now, and how would you store it?

A bell curve can only say “around here”. A grid can say anything, but this floor (24 × 13.5 m) at 10 cm and 2° steps is 240 × 135 × 180 = 5.8 million cells, every one updated on every scan. In 1999, Dellaert, Fox, Burgard and Thrun proposed storing the belief as a few thousand random samples instead. Their Monte Carlo localization could find a robot without knowing its start, and was “faster, more accurate and less memory-intensive than earlier grid-based methods” (ICRA 1999). Today ROS 2's navigation stack, Nav2, starts it by default.

The idea: a crowd of guesses

A particle is one concrete guess of the state: “I'm at x = 3.2 m, facing 90°”. Keep a few hundred of them. Where many particles crowd together, the robot probably is. That's the whole representation: no formula, just a cloud. The particle filter runs the Bayes loop on the cloud in three moves:

1. Predict   move every particle by the odometry u, plus its own random noise (so the cloud spreads, like the blur of Lesson 3.2)
2. Weight   wi ∝ p(z | particle i): “if I were here, how likely is the reading I actually got?” Then normalize so the weights add to 1.
3. Resample   draw N new particles, each a copy of an old one picked with probability wi. Good guesses multiply, bad ones vanish. Predict blurs, weighting sharpens, resampling moves the effort to where the probability is. It works for any motion model and any sensor, because each particle only needs “move me” and “how well do I explain this reading?”.
✏️ Worked example: five particles and a door

A robot in a corridor has 5 particles at x = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 m. There are doors at 4 m and 8 m. Its door sensor says “door!”, and it's right 90% of the time: p(“door” | at a door) = 0.9, p(“door” | at a wall) = 0.1.

  1. How many particles really count? The effective sample size ESS = 1 / Σ w² = 1 / (3 × 0.048² + 2 × 0.429²) = 2.67 of 5. Most of the belief now rides on two particles.
  2. Systematic resampling: one random start (say 0.1), then pointers every 1/5: 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9. Each picks the particle whose running total first passes it: 0.1 → x = 4, 0.3 → 4, 0.5 → 6, 0.7 → 8, 0.9 → 8.
  3. New cloud: 4, 4, 6, 8, 8, all with weight 1/5. The belief now has two bumps, at both doors, which no single bell curve can express. The next move and reading will decide between them.
💡 Big idea A particle filter doesn't describe the belief with a formula; it is a sample of the belief. More particles where the robot probably is, none where it can't be. That buys any shape (two bumps, a ring, a banana) for a price you set yourself: the number of particles.
🧩 Predict before you play: in the playground below, a drone flies along a valley and measures the ground height under it. If you press Sense and Resample five times without ever flying, does the cloud end up in one place?
No. It sharpens around every spot where the ground has the height you measured, often 4 to 6 places, and stays split. Standing still can't tell apart places that look the same. Only moving lets the sequence of heights (the shape of the hills) single out one place. And without motion noise, repeated resampling makes many identical copies, so the cloud loses variety: a hint of the deprivation problem later in this lesson.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Kalman-type filters (KF, EKF, UKF) hold one bell curve, so they can't represent “in one of these three corridors”, can't start from “no idea” (global localization), and lock onto a wrong answer after a bad first guess (Lesson 3.4). Grid filters (Lesson 3.2) can, but the cells explode: 5.8 million for one office floor in 3D pose.
  2. What people tried. Many Kalman filters at once (multi-hypothesis tracking): works, but needs rules for creating, merging and deleting hypotheses. Coarse grids: cheap but blurry. Grids that refine only where needed: complex. Meanwhile radar trackers (1993) and computer vision (1998) had found the sampling trick under other names.
  3. Why this works. Samples put the effort where the probability is: once localized, a few hundred particles cover a 1 m blob instead of 5.8 million cells covering the whole building. Weighting is just “score this guess”, so any sensor works, including a full lidar scan against a map. Example: five particles and a door reading → weights 0.048, 0.429, 0.048, 0.429, 0.048 → resampled to 4, 4, 6, 8, 8: two bumps, kept honestly.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Cost grows with particles × beams per update. The number of particles needed grows with the volume to cover (the curse of dimensionality): a 3-number robot pose is fine, a 15-number drone state is not. If no particle lands near the truth (too few particles, a bad early convergence, or a kidnapped robot), weighting can't fix it: particle deprivation. Symmetric buildings fool it like they'd fool you.
  5. What came next. KLD-sampling (Fox, 2001) adapts the particle count: many while lost, few once localized. Augmented MCL injects random particles when the fit to the map suddenly drops, to recover from kidnapping. Likelihood fields make lidar scoring fast. Rao-Blackwellized particle filters give each particle its own map: FastSLAM and GMapping (Phase 5). For big states, robots go back to EKFs and factor graphs.

Monte Carlo localization: particles against a map

In Monte Carlo localization (MCL), each particle is a pose (x, y, heading) and the measurement is a whole lidar scan. To weight a particle, imagine the scan taken from that pose and ask how well it lines up with the map's walls. Doing this by ray casting for every particle and beam is slow, so the standard trick is a likelihood field:

  1. Once, before driving: for every map cell, store the distance to the nearest wall (a distance map).
  2. For each particle and beam: put the beam's endpoint where it would land from that particle; look up its distance d to the nearest wall.
  3. Score the beam with a bell curve in d (close to a wall = likely) plus a small constant for random junk readings, so one bad beam can't wipe out a good particle. Combine all beams into the particle's weight.

This is exactly what Nav2's AMCL does by default (laser_model_type: likelihood_field, a bell curve of width sigma_hit = 0.2 m mixed with a random part z_rand, using up to max_beams = 60 beams). In the playground below, 24 beams are scored this way for every particle, 5 times per simulated second.

🧩 Predict: the robot below starts lost, with particles spread over the whole floor. It drives the corridor loop. Will 1,000 particles always find it? Will 50?
1,000 usually find it, typically within 10 to 20 s, once the robot passes something distinctive (a corner, the round table, the shelf). Not always (in our tests, about 3 starts in 4 succeed within 40 s): this floor is nearly symmetric, and sometimes the cloud settles on the “mirror image” (the opposite corridor, facing the other way) and stays there, confidently wrong, because no particle is left near the truth. 50 particles find it only sometimes: with so few guesses spread over the whole floor and every heading, often none starts close enough. That's the deprivation problem; the injection checkbox is the cure.

🧠 Analogy: a search party with radios

You've lost a hiker in a national park. You send 1,000 volunteers to random spots. Every few minutes the hiker radios a description (“I see a lake to my left and a cliff ahead”). Volunteers whose surroundings match keep going and call in friends; those who see nothing like it are recalled and re-sent to where the matching volunteers are. Within an hour the whole search party is standing around the hiker. And if the hiker is airlifted somewhere else without telling anyone (the kidnap), a search party with no one far away will never notice. Unless you always keep a few scouts wandering at random.

🧩 Predict: in the next playground, the drone's valley gets 10 times longer (600 m instead of 60 m). To succeed as often as before, how many particles do you need?
About 10 times more. Particles must cover the space you might be in, so the count grows with its size: about 100 particles give 95% success in the 60 m valley, while in the 600 m one even 1,000 particles only get close to 90%. Now imagine adding dimensions: a 2D floor multiplies area, heading multiplies again. That's the curse of dimensionality, and why particle filters stop at small states.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A particle filter is about 15 lines of NumPy; write one (the Phase 3 lab does). On a real ROS 2 robot you'll configure Nav2's AMCL, which follows this lesson step by step: odometry motion model, likelihood-field lidar model, KLD-sampling and random-particle recovery. FilterPy provides the resampling routines.

import numpy as np
from filterpy.monte_carlo import systematic_resample
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

N = 1000
particles = np.column_stack([rng.uniform(0, 24, N), rng.uniform(0, 13.5, N),
                             rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, N)])        # x, y, heading: "no idea"
weights = np.full(N, 1 / N)

def pf_step(particles, weights, d, dtheta, scan_likelihood):
    # 1. predict: odometry + each particle's own noise
    th = particles[:, 2] + dtheta + rng.normal(0, 0.02, N)
    step = d + rng.normal(0, 0.05 * abs(d) + 0.01, N)
    particles[:, 0] += step * np.cos(th); particles[:, 1] += step * np.sin(th)
    particles[:, 2] = (th + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi
    # 2. weight: how well does the scan fit the map from each particle?
    weights = weights * scan_likelihood(particles)   # e.g. a likelihood field lookup
    weights /= weights.sum()
    # 3. resample only when the effective sample size gets small
    if 1.0 / np.sum(weights**2) < N / 2:
        idx = systematic_resample(weights)
        particles, weights = particles[idx], np.full(N, 1 / N)
    return particles, weights
# Nav2 AMCL (nav2_params.yaml), a few of the knobs from this lesson; values are the documented defaults
amcl:
  ros__parameters:
    min_particles: 500          # KLD-sampling range
    max_particles: 2000
    laser_model_type: "likelihood_field"
    max_beams: 60
    sigma_hit: 0.2              # width of the beam's bell curve (m)
    z_hit: 0.5
    z_rand: 0.5                 # "this beam is junk" share
    update_min_d: 0.25          # update after 25 cm of travel...
    update_min_a: 0.2           # ...or 0.2 rad of turning
    recovery_alpha_fast: 0.0    # 0 = off; docs suggest 0.1 to enable kidnap recovery
    recovery_alpha_slow: 0.0    # docs suggest 0.001
    robot_model_type: "nav2_amcl::DifferentialMotionModel"

AMCL needs a starting guess: it waits for an initial-pose message, or uses initial_pose from its parameters when set_initial_pose is true. It publishes the map → odom transform, so the rest of the stack sees the robot in the map frame (Lesson 1.6).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🤖 Indoor robots (ROS 2 Nav2)

This is where particle filters rule. Nav2's AMCL localizes a robot with a 2D lidar in a known map; its default localization launch starts it. Defaults (checked Nov 2026): 500 to 2,000 particles with KLD-sampling, a likelihood-field lidar model, 60 beams, updates every 0.25 m or 0.2 rad, and optional random-particle recovery. Its README says it is largely a port of ROS 1's AMCL “without any algorithmic changes”.

Sources: Nav2 AMCL configuration, nav2_amcl

🚕 Waymo

Waymo's public description (Sep 2020): the Waymo Driver locates itself using highly detailed custom maps, built by driving each street with its lidar, which complement what its sensors see and help “even where GPS struggles, like tunnels or between skyscrapers”. That's map-based localization, the same job as MCL, outdoors. Which algorithm does the matching (particles, scan matching, learned) is not public.

Sources: Waymo Driver Handbook: mapping (2020)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published how its cars localize. Its public talks (AI Day 2021, CVPR 2021) are about neural networks that build a picture of the surroundings from the car's cameras. Whether any particle-style or map-matching step sits behind them is not documented.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, CVPR'21 WAD keynote

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

The estimators documented by PX4 (EKF2, 24 states) and ArduPilot (EKF3) are extended Kalman filters, not particle filters: a drone's state (attitude, velocity, position, biases, wind) is far too big to cover with particles, and GPS usually removes the “where in the world am I?” ambiguity that particles are best at.

Sources: PX4 EKF docs, ArduPilot EKF overview

Why they differ: an indoor robot has no GPS, a small 3-number pose and a building that repeats itself, which is exactly the problem particles solve cheaply. A drone has GPS and a large state, so a Kalman-type filter fits. Waymo localizes against its own prebuilt maps but hasn't published the algorithm; Tesla says it relies on cameras and networks and hasn't published its localization at all.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your warehouse robot sometimes localizes in the wrong aisle after a restart and never recovers. What's happening and what do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Names it: symmetric environment plus particle deprivation: the cloud converged on a look-alike aisle and no particle remains near the truth.
  • Fixes in order: give AMCL an initial pose (from the last saved pose or a dock), enable recovery (augmented MCL), raise particles while lost (KLD's max), add distinctive features (reflectors, markers, different shelf ends).
  • Detects it: monitor the scan-to-map fit and the particle spread; a good fit with a tight cloud is fine, a bad fit with a tight cloud is a red flag.
  • Knows the limits: particles for a 3-number pose in a known map; Kalman-type filters or factor graphs for big states; SLAM when there's no map (Phase 5).
🏭 In the real world
Monte Carlo localization 🧪 Research · 1999 Dellaert, Fox, Burgard & Thrun, ICRA 1999

How: the robot's belief is a set of randomly drawn pose samples, moved with odometry and weighted by sonar or laser readings against a map.

Why this method: it can represent any belief, so it localizes without a known start, and beat grid methods on speed, accuracy and memory.

KLD-sampling 🧪 Research · 2001 Fox, NIPS 2001

How: while resampling, keep drawing particles until they cover enough distinct cells of the pose space for a chosen error bound.

Why this method: thousands of particles while lost, a few hundred once localized; AMCL's “adaptive” comes from this.

Every Nav2 robot 🧰 Open source nav2_amcl

How: odometry motion model, likelihood-field or beam lidar model, KLD-sampling, fast/slow fit averages for random-particle recovery; publishes the map → odom transform.

Why this method: 2D lidar plus a saved map is the cheapest reliable indoor localization, and a 3-number pose is small enough for particles.

Tracking outlines in video 🧪 Research · 1998 Isard & Blake, CONDENSATION, IJCV 1998

How: a set of samples of an object's outline is propagated with a motion model and reweighted by image evidence each frame.

Why this method: in cluttered images several outlines look plausible at once; a Kalman filter's single bell curve “cannot represent simultaneous alternative hypotheses”, in the authors' words.

🤯 Fun fact · invented three times

The same idea appeared in three fields within six years, under three names: the bootstrap filter for radar tracking (Gordon, Salmond & Smith, IEE Proceedings F, 1993), CONDENSATION for tracking shapes in video (Isard & Blake, 1998), and Monte Carlo localization for robots (Dellaert, Fox, Burgard & Thrun, 1999). Today they're all called particle filters, or sequential Monte Carlo.

🌉 How it connects

A particle filter is the Bayes filter of Lesson 3.2 with the grid replaced by samples, and it fixes what defeated the Kalman, EKF and UKF filters of Lessons 3.3 and 3.4: several hypotheses and lost starts. Lesson 3.6 goes back to Kalman-type filters for GPS + IMU fusion (big state, no ambiguity), and 3.7 adds outlier gates. In Phase 5 each particle carries its own map (FastSLAM, GMapping), and in Phase 6 particles track other agents. The math: sampling and Bayes' rule in the Math course.

Check yourself

1. Four particles have normalized weights 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1. What's the effective sample size?
ESS = 1 / Σ w² = 1 / (0.49 + 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01) = 1 / 0.52 ≈ 1.9. Four particles, but the belief rides on about two of them; below N/2 = 2, so it's time to resample.
2. A Nav2 robot is localized. Someone carries it to another room. AMCL stays confidently at the old spot, with a tight cloud. Which setting is most likely the culprit?
This is the kidnapped robot problem. Once every particle sits in the old room, weighting can't find the new one: particle deprivation. With recovery on (the docs suggest 0.1 and 0.001), a sudden drop in scan fit triggers random particles across the map, and the cloud regrows around the truth. Or simply send a new initial pose.
3. Why don't PX4 and ArduPilot use a particle filter for the drone's main state estimate?
Particles must cover the space of possible states, and that volume grows exponentially with the number of dimensions: fine for a 3-number pose, hopeless for 24. With GPS there's no “which corridor?” ambiguity, so an EKF's single bell curve is accurate and far cheaper.
📝 TL;DR
  • Particles are concrete guesses; their crowd is the belief. Loop: predict (move + noise), weight by p(z | particle), resample when ESS < N/2.
  • They hold any shape: several hypotheses, global “no idea” starts. Five particles and a door → 4, 4, 6, 8, 8: two bumps.
  • MCL scores each particle's lidar scan against the map, fast with a likelihood field (distance to the nearest wall).
  • Failure modes: deprivation, kidnapping, symmetric places, and the curse of dimensionality (10× the space → about 10× the particles).
  • Nav2 AMCL (500 to 2,000 particles, KLD-sampling, optional recovery) localizes most ROS robots; drones use EKFs; Waymo matches its own maps; Tesla hasn't published its method.
