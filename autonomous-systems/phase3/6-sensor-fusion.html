Phase 3 · Lesson 6

Sensor fusion in practice: GPS + IMU & visual-inertial

A fast sensor that drifts, a slow sensor that doesn't, and a filter in between. This lesson builds the estimator that flies every PX4 drone, keeps your phone's AR furniture on the floor, and lets a robot cross a tunnel without losing itself.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The Kalman loop: predict (move the guess, grow the uncertainty by Q), then correct (gain K = P/(P+R), pull toward the measurement, shrink the uncertainty). Lesson 3.3.
  • The EKF: the same loop for curved models, using a straight-line (Jacobian) approximation at the current guess (Lesson 3.4).
  • IMU (inertial measurement unit): a gyroscope measures how fast you rotate (°/s), an accelerometer measures acceleration plus gravity (m/s²). Both have a small, slowly changing offset called bias (Lesson 2.5).
  • GNSS (global navigation satellite systems: GPS, Galileo, BeiDou, GLONASS): a position fix a few times a second, good to a few meters, worse between tall buildings (Lesson 2.6). We'll say “GPS” for short.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Your drone's accelerometer has a tiny bias: it reads 0.05 m/s² too high, about half a percent of gravity. You switch off GPS and fly on the IMU alone, integrating acceleration into velocity and velocity into position.

How far off is the position estimate after one minute? About 1 m? 10 m? 100 m? Commit to a number, then open the box below.

That number is why no real vehicle navigates on an IMU alone, and why every PX4 drone runs a 24-state filter in which the IMU drives the prediction at 100 Hz or more and GPS, barometer, magnetometer and cameras correct it (PX4 docs). Two of those 24 states are not positions at all: they're the IMU's own biases.

🧩 Your guess: 0.05 m/s² of bias, 60 seconds, no GPS. How many meters of error?
About 90 m. A constant acceleration error integrates twice: error = ½ × b × t² = ½ × 0.05 × 60² = 90 m. After 10 s it's only 2.5 m, which is why the problem hides at first; it grows with the square of time. Halve the time, quarter the error; double it, four times the error. An IMU is a superb short-term sensor and a terrible long-term one.

The pattern every vehicle uses: fast predict, slow correct

Each sensor is good at one thing and bad at another. The trick is to give each the job it's good at.

IMU samples arrive 20 times between two GPS fixes.
✏️ Worked example: one GPS interval, step by step

IMU at 100 Hz (Δt = 0.01 s), GPS at 5 Hz. The car is at 0 m, driving 10 m/s; the accelerometer reads 2 m/s². Variance grows by Q = 0.01 m² per IMU step.

  1. Predict, 20 times (one per IMU sample): each step does p ← p + v·Δt + ½a·Δt², v ← v + a·Δt. After 0.2 s: v = 10 + 2 × 0.2 = 10.4 m/s, p = 10 × 0.2 + ½ × 2 × 0.2² = 2.04 m. Variance: 1 + 20 × 0.01 = 1.2 m² (if it started at 1 m²).
  2. Correct, once: GPS says 2.9 m with R = 4 m² (σ = 2 m). K = 1.2 ÷ (1.2 + 4) = 0.23; new p = 2.04 + 0.23 × 0.86 = 2.24 m; new variance (1 − 0.23) × 1.2 = 0.92 m².
  3. Repeat. The controller gets a fresh estimate at 100 Hz, even though GPS only speaks at 5 Hz.

Notice the asymmetry: the IMU is not a “measurement” here. It's the input that moves the state, exactly like the commanded speed v·Δt in Lesson 3.3. PX4 says it plainly: its EKF “uses the IMU data for state prediction only”.

Bias as a state: let the filter learn the sensor's mistake

The hook's 90 m came from one constant number. If the filter knew it, it could subtract it. Nobody tells the filter the bias, but GPS quietly reveals it: when the IMU keeps predicting “you're further ahead than GPS says”, fix after fix, in the same direction, that pattern is a bias, not noise.

state = [ p, v, b ]    Predict: v ← v + (ameasured − b)·Δt,   p ← p + v·Δt,   b ← b (it changes only slowly) Add the bias to the state, subtract it from every IMU reading, and let the GPS corrections tune it. The covariance P links “I'm drifting ahead” to “my bias guess is too small”, exactly like it linked position to velocity in Lesson 3.3.

🧩 Predict: with the bias state off, is the filter at least fine before the tunnel, while GPS is still arriving?
No, and it's worse than raw GPS. The filter believes the IMU is only randomly noisy, so it keeps trusting the biased prediction and lags behind every fix: switch the outage off and the RMS error with the defaults is still around 10 m, five times the GPS's own 2 m. A bias isn't noise that averages away; it's a model error. Give it a state, and the same filter beats GPS and coasts through the tunnel.
💡 Big idea The fast, drifting sensor predicts; the slow, drift-free sensor corrects; and anything that makes the fast sensor drift (biases, scale factors, even wind) becomes extra state that the corrections can learn. When the slow sensor disappears, the learned biases are what keep you on the road.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A controller needs position, velocity and attitude hundreds of times a second. GPS gives a few noisy fixes a second, late, and vanishes in tunnels. An IMU gives perfect smoothness and drifts: 0.05 m/s² of bias is 90 m after a minute.
  2. What people tried. GPS only, interpolated (jittery, delayed, blind in tunnels). IMU only, dead reckoning (fine for seconds, lost after a minute; military-grade IMUs drift far less but cost far more). A plain Kalman filter on top (better, but a bias isn't random noise, so it still drags the estimate, as Playground 1 shows). A fixed complementary blend (great for attitude, below, but can't learn biases or weigh a degraded GPS).
  3. Why this works. The IMU becomes the motion model's input, so you get 100+ Hz estimates for free; GPS bounds the drift; and bias states let every correction teach the filter about the sensor's mistake. In Playground 1 at 2 Hz GPS and a 0.3 m/s² bias, adding one bias state takes the tunnel error from about 60 m to a few meters.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. More states to tune (bias random-walk noise, initial bias uncertainty). States must be observable: a bias can only be learned if the motion and the corrections reveal it (a drone hovering perfectly still can't separate some biases from tilt). Rotations make the model nonlinear, so it's an EKF, with all of Lesson 3.4's linearization risks. Delayed or wrong corrections (multipath, a late timestamp) poison it; that's Lesson 3.7.
  5. What came next. Error-state filters for better-behaved rotations, tightly coupled fusion of raw satellite and camera measurements, visual-inertial odometry (VIO) for GPS-free flight, and optimization over a sliding window of past states (pose graphs, Lesson 5.3) instead of a single filter step.

Loosely vs tightly coupled: how deep do you plug the sensor in?

A GPS receiver doesn't really measure position. It measures the distance to each satellite (a pseudorange), and solves for position from those. To solve for x, y, z and its own clock error it needs at least 4 satellites. That gives you two ways to fuse it:

Loosely coupledTightly coupled
What enters the filterThe receiver's finished answer: position (and velocity) with an uncertaintyThe raw measurements: one pseudorange (and Doppler) per satellite
With 3 satellites in a city canyonNo fix → no correction at allStill 3 useful measurements, each one a correction
Camera versionVisual odometry outputs a pose; the filter fuses the poseThe filter fuses each tracked image feature (as MSCKF, OpenVINS and VINS-Mono do)
CostSimple, modular, swap receivers freelyNeeds a detailed sensor model, more compute, more ways to get it subtly wrong
🧩 Predict: loosely coupled systems are simpler. So why do people bother with tightly coupled ones in exactly the places they matter most?
Because the hard cases are partial information. Downtown, a receiver may see only two or three clean satellites; loosely coupled gets nothing, tightly coupled still gets two or three constraints. A camera in a dim corridor may track only a dozen features; a pose solver may fail, but each feature still constrains the motion. Tightly coupled fusion never throws away a measurement just because there isn't a complete answer.

Error-state EKF: estimate the mistake, not the state

Flight and VIO filters rarely run a “plain” EKF on the full state. They split it in two:

  • a nominal state: integrate the IMU at full rate with the exact nonlinear equations (rotate, add gravity, integrate twice), with no uncertainty bookkeeping;
  • a small error state: how far the nominal state is off (say 0.5° of tilt and 0.3 m), with its covariance. The Kalman filter runs on this. After each correction, the error is folded into the nominal state and reset to zero.

Why bother? Three reasons. The error is always small, so the straight-line approximation of Lesson 3.4 is very accurate. Rotations become well-behaved: orientation is a quaternion with 4 numbers that must keep length 1 (Lesson 1.4), but a small rotation error is just 3 numbers with no constraint. The MSCKF paper does exactly this, describing the orientation error as a small “error quaternion” (Mourikis & Roumeliotis, 2007), and PX4's EKF2 docs say it uses an “error-state” formulation to keep rotation uncertainty well-behaved (PX4). And the fast IMU loop stays cheap, with the matrix math running only on the error.

🧠 Analogy: the hiking map and the pencil correction

You walk with a compass and a step count, and you draw your route in ink as you go: that's the nominal state. Every time you reach a signpost you don't redraw the whole route; you note “I'm 40 m east of where my ink says”, with how sure you are, and shift the ink from here on. You only ever do careful arithmetic on the small correction, never on the whole journey.

Visual-inertial odometry: a camera, an IMU, and the scale problem

Indoors, under bridges, or for a drone between trees, there's no GPS. A camera can take over the correcting job: track features from frame to frame and you get the motion very precisely. But a single camera can't measure scale. A video of a 1:10 model town flown through at 1 m/s looks exactly like the real town flown at 10 m/s.

The IMU fixes that. Its accelerometer measures in m/s², real units. Match the camera's motion to the IMU's accelerations and the scale pops out. A tiny example: the camera's track implies an acceleration of 0.25 “camera units”/s² while the IMU feels 1.0 m/s² → scale = 1.0 ÷ 0.25 = 4 m per camera unit. That's visual-inertial odometry (VIO), the core of GPS-free drones and of phone AR.

The complementary filter: attitude with one line of code

Before any Kalman filter, a drone needs to know which way is down. It has two clues, with opposite flaws:

  • the gyro, integrated: smooth and instant, but its bias makes it drift (1°/s of bias is 60° off after a minute);
  • the accelerometer: at rest it measures gravity, so it tells you the tilt directly, with no drift. But it's noisy, and every vibration or turn adds accelerations that look like tilt.

So trust the gyro for fast changes and the accelerometer for the slow average. With a blend weight α (alpha) close to 1:

θ ← α · ( θ + ωgyro·Δt ) + (1 − α) · θaccel Each step: rotate by what the gyro says, then lean a little (1 − α) toward the accelerometer's tilt. The gyro's drift gets pulled back; the accelerometer's noise gets averaged out.
✏️ Worked example: what α means in seconds

The blend has a time constant τ = α·Δt ÷ (1 − α): roughly, “the accelerometer's opinion takes over after τ seconds”. At 100 Hz (Δt = 0.01 s) with α = 0.98: τ = 0.98 × 0.01 ÷ 0.02 = 0.49 s.

  • A gyro bias of 1°/s now leaves a steady error of only bias × τ ≈ 1 × 0.49 = 0.49°, instead of 60° after a minute.
  • Accelerometer wobbles faster than about 1/τ ≈ 2 rad/s are mostly filtered out.
  • Bigger α (say 0.998, τ ≈ 5 s): less accelerometer noise, but 5× the bias error. Smaller α: the reverse. The best α is a trade-off you can feel in the next playground.

🏆 Challenge: with vibration on, a gyro bias of at least 1°/s and accelerometer noise of at least 3°, keep the RMS tilt error under 1°. Too small an α lets the vibration through; too big lets the bias drift in. Then set the bias to 0: which α wins now, and why?

What you should find: the window is narrow, around α ≈ 0.98, τ ≈ 0.5 s. That's the complementary filter's limit: one fixed number trades noise against drift. A Kalman filter does the same blend but picks the weight from the uncertainties, and can estimate the gyro bias as a state, so the trade-off disappears. Yet the complementary filter is still everywhere: it's tiny, it can't diverge, and PX4 itself uses one to carry EKF2's delayed estimate forward to the present (Lesson 3.7).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

On a ground robot with ROS 2, robot_localization gives you the whole recipe by configuration: its docs suggest two EKFs, one fusing only continuous data (wheels, IMU) in the odom frame for smooth local control, and one adding GPS in the map frame, because GPS “jumps”; navsat_transform_node converts latitude/longitude into that frame. On drones you configure PX4 EKF2 or ArduPilot EKF3 by parameters. For attitude on a microcontroller, a complementary filter (or the open-source x-io Fusion AHRS library) is a few lines. For VIO, start from OpenVINS (an MSCKF-based EKF) or VINS-Mono (sliding-window optimization).

import numpy as np

# Complementary filter for tilt (roll) from a gyro + accelerometer, 100 Hz
dt, alpha = 0.01, 0.98                  # time constant = alpha*dt/(1-alpha) ≈ 0.49 s
roll = 0.0
def step(roll, gyro_x, acc_y, acc_z):
    roll_acc = np.arctan2(acc_y, acc_z)           # tilt seen by gravity (rad)
    return alpha * (roll + gyro_x * dt) + (1 - alpha) * roll_acc

# 1D GPS + IMU with an accelerometer-bias state: x = [p, v, b]
F = np.array([[1, dt, -0.5*dt**2], [0, 1, -dt], [0, 0, 1]])
B = np.array([0.5*dt**2, dt, 0])                  # how the measured acceleration enters
def predict(x, P, a_meas, Q):
    return F @ x + B * a_meas, F @ P @ F.T + Q     # v += (a_meas - b)*dt, p += ...
# robot_localization: the GPS-aware "map" EKF (ekf_global.yaml, abridged)
ekf_filter_node_map:
  ros__parameters:
    frequency: 30.0
    world_frame: map                # the odom-frame EKF uses world_frame: odom
    odom0: /wheel/odometry
    odom0_config: [false, false, false,  false, false, false,
                   true,  true,  false,  false, false, true,
                   false, false, false]
    odom1: /odometry/gps             # output of navsat_transform_node
    odom1_config: [true,  true,  false,  false, false, false,
                   false, false, false,  false, false, false,
                   false, false, false]   # GPS gives x, y only
    imu0: /imu/data
    imu0_config: [false, false, false,  false, false, true,
                  false, false, false,  false, false, true,
                  true,  false, false]

Two classic bugs: fusing GPS into the controller's odom estimate (the robot twitches at every GPS jump), and an IMU whose axes or frame don't match the robot's (the filter “learns” a huge bias that is really a mounting error). Check frames first (Lesson 1.6).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot, Skydio)

PX4's EKF2 is a 24-state EKF: attitude quaternion, velocity, position, gyro and accelerometer biases, earth and body magnetic field, wind. The IMU (at least 100 Hz; the default prediction period EKF2_PREDICT_US is 10,000 µs) drives prediction only; GPS, baro, magnetometer, rangefinder, optical flow and vision correct it. GPS must pass quality checks for 10 s (by default) before it's used; the documented minimums include at least 6 satellites, a horizontal accuracy under 3 m and a PDOP (satellite geometry) under 2.5. PX4 also says EKF2 uses an “error-state” formulation, for the rotation uncertainty. ArduPilot's EKF3 runs one filter core per IMU and flies on the healthiest. Skydio says its drones navigate “even where GPS isn't available” and avoid obstacles with navigation cameras; it doesn't publish the estimator.

Sources: PX4 EKF2, PX4 parameters, ArduPilot EKF, Skydio

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its localization filter. What it has said: its cars know “what the road ahead looks like” from prebuilt maps, which help them “navigate places even where GPS struggles, like tunnels or between skyscrapers”, and they detect road changes by “cross-referencing the real-time sensor data with its on-board map” (2020). That is fusion with a map as one of the sensors: lidar and cameras matched against the map play the role GPS plays for a drone. The 6th-generation Driver (rider-only since Feb 2026) pairs cameras, lidar and imaging radar so that “lidar and radar provide the necessary redundancy” when a camera's view is limited.

Sources: Waymo, mapping (2020), Waymo, Feb 2026

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published how it estimates the car's own position, or how it fuses GPS, IMU and cameras. What it has said publicly: Model 3 and Model Y in North America dropped radar from May 2021 (“Tesla Vision”), and in his CVPR 2021 keynote Andrej Karpathy, then head of AI, showed networks estimating other cars' depth and velocity from video alone. So the public story is about perceiving others from cameras; the ego-motion estimator isn't described.

Sources: Tesla support, CVPR'21 WAD keynote

🤖 Robots & phones

ROS 2 robots use robot_localization (two EKFs plus navsat_transform_node). Your phone runs VIO: Apple's ARKit docs say it uses “visual-inertial odometry”, combining the motion sensors with computer vision on the camera image. Research VIO such as VINS-Mono (aimed at drones and AR) and OpenVINS (an MSCKF-based EKF) is open source.

Sources: robot_localization GPS docs, ARKit, VINS-Mono

Why they differ: a drone has no wheels and no map, so an IMU-driven EKF with GPS, baro and vision corrections is its sense of self, and it's documented in the open. A robotaxi drives a mapped area, so matching lidar and cameras to the map (Lesson 5.6) gives it corrections where GPS fails; the details are proprietary. Tesla's public talks focus on learned perception from cameras, and it hasn't described its localization at all.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design the state estimator for a drone with an IMU, GPS and a downward camera.” A strong answer:

  • IMU drives the prediction at its full rate; GPS, baro, magnetometer and camera are corrections, each with its own noise, delay and gate.
  • Includes gyro and accelerometer biases as states (and says what motion makes them observable).
  • Uses an error-state formulation for attitude (3-number errors, not quaternion components).
  • Knows loosely vs tightly coupled and when each is worth it; knows a monocular camera needs the IMU for scale.
  • Mentions time stamps and delays, outlier gates and what happens when GPS drops out (Lesson 3.7).
🏭 In the real world
PX4 EKF2 on open-source drones 🧰 Open source PX4: Using the ECL EKF

How: a 24-state EKF with IMU-driven prediction and bias states; GPS, baro, magnetometer, rangefinder, optical flow and external vision as corrections, each with a delay parameter.

Why this method: the attitude and velocity controllers need fast, smooth, drift-free estimates, and no single sensor provides them.

MSCKF: vision-aided inertial navigation 🧪 Research · 2007 Mourikis & Roumeliotis, ICRA 2007

How: an EKF keeps a sliding window of recent camera poses; a feature seen from several poses becomes a constraint between them, without putting the feature's 3D position in the state.

Why this method: cost grows only linearly with the number of features, so a camera plus IMU can run in real time; it's the ancestor of many VIO systems, including OpenVINS.

VINS-Mono 🧪 Research · 2017 Qin, Li, Shen: VINS-Mono

How: one camera plus an IMU, fused tightly by optimization over a sliding window, with loop detection and pose-graph optimization for long runs.

Why this method: the minimal sensor set, a camera and an IMU, that every drone and phone already has; its authors target drone state estimation and AR.

GPS on ROS 2 ground robots 🧰 Open source robot_localization

How: a continuous odom-frame EKF (wheels + IMU) for control and a map-frame EKF that adds GPS through navsat_transform_node.

Why this method: controllers hate jumps; planners need a globally correct position. Two estimates give each what it needs.

Check yourself

1. A complementary filter runs at 200 Hz (Δt = 0.005 s) with α = 0.99. The gyro has a bias of 2°/s. Roughly what steady tilt error does the bias leave?
τ = α·Δt ÷ (1 − α) = 0.99 × 0.005 ÷ 0.01 ≈ 0.5 s, and the steady error ≈ bias × τ = 2 × 0.5 ≈ 1°. Without the accelerometer pulling it back (α = 1) it would indeed grow by 2° every second.
2. A delivery robot's position estimate is great on open streets but drifts 20 m after every long underpass, always in the direction of travel. GPS and wheel odometry are fused in an EKF without bias states. What's the best first fix?
Drift in a consistent direction is a model error, not random noise. A bias or scale-factor state lets the filter learn it from GPS while it's available, and remove it when GPS disappears (Playground 1).
3. A drone with one camera and an IMU flies a long, perfectly straight line at constant speed, and its VIO slowly gets the distance wrong. Why?
A monocular camera knows motion only up to scale; the accelerometer supplies the meters, but only when there's acceleration to compare. Constant velocity looks identical at every scale, as the flat misfit showed in Playground 2. A little excitation (turns, speed changes) or another sensor (GPS, a rangefinder) fixes it.
📝 TL;DR
  • The IMU predicts at 100+ Hz; GPS, baro, magnetometer and cameras correct. A 0.05 m/s² bias alone is 90 m after a minute (½·b·t²).
  • Make biases states: GPS calibrates them, and they carry you through outages (tens of meters → a few meters in Playground 1).
  • Loosely coupled fuses finished answers; tightly coupled fuses raw satellites or features and works with partial information.
  • Error-state EKFs track the small mistake (3 numbers for attitude), not the full state.
  • VIO: camera for precise motion, IMU for scale, which needs acceleration. Complementary filter: α = 0.98 at 100 Hz is τ ≈ 0.5 s.
  • PX4 EKF2 (24 states) and ArduPilot EKF3 do all of this in the open; Waymo leans on its maps; Tesla hasn't published its localization.
