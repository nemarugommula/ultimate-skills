Nonlinear filters: EKF & UKF

The Kalman filter assumes the world moves in straight lines. Real cars turn, real sensors measure angles, and a bell curve pushed through a curve comes out bent. Two fixes run in most robots and drones: bend the math with a tangent line (EKF), or send a few scouts through the curve (UKF).

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The Kalman filter (Lesson 3.3): predict (move the guess, grow the uncertainty P by Q), then correct (gain K = P/(P+R), pull toward the measurement, shrink P). Exact only when motion and sensor are straight-line (linear) functions.
  • A slope (derivative) says how much the output changes per unit of input; near any point, a smooth curve looks like its tangent line. Refresher: derivatives and Taylor & curvature in the Math course.
  • The uncertainty ellipse (Lesson 3.3): a 2D bell curve drawn as the gold region where the truth lies 95% of the time.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A robot knows exactly where it starts, but its compass is poor: its heading is 0° give or take 40°. It drives exactly 10 m straight ahead. On average, how far ahead of the start does it end up?

“10 m, obviously”, says the Kalman filter's way of thinking. Is it? Lock in your guess in the first playground.

This isn't a toy question. In 2005, Stanford's robot car Stanley won the DARPA Grand Challenge, a secret desert route through the Mojave driven with nobody aboard, in 6 h 53 min (an average of 19.1 mph). Its position and heading came from an unscented Kalman filter (the second fix in this lesson) tracking 15 numbers 100 times a second from GPS, a GPS compass, an IMU and the wheels (Thrun et al., 2006).

💡 Big idea A Kalman filter only works with bell curves, and a bell curve stays a bell curve only through straight-line functions. Through a curve it comes out bent (a banana, a crescent, a lopsided lump). Every nonlinear filter is a way of answering: which bell curve best stands in for the bent one? The EKF answers with the tangent line at the center. The UKF answers by pushing a handful of carefully chosen points through the real curve.

Where the curves come from

Two things make almost every real robot nonlinear. Both involve angles, and angles mean sines and cosines.

Part of the filterLinear (KF is exact)Nonlinear (real life)
Motion model f“position += velocity × Δt” along a straight trackA turning car: x += v·cos(θ)·Δt, y += v·sin(θ)·Δt, θ += ω·Δt. Heading uncertainty swings the whole future path.
Sensor model hGPS measures x and y directlyRadar, lidar and cameras measure range √(dx² + dy²) and bearing atan2(dy, dx) − θ to a landmark or another car.
Orientation in 3D(never linear)A drone's attitude: rotating the accelerometer's reading into the world frame multiplies by sines and cosines of roll, pitch and yaw (Lesson 1.3).

Fix 1, the EKF: use the tangent line

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) keeps the Kalman recipe and makes one swap. Wherever the KF multiplied by a matrix F or H, the EKF uses the slope of the real function at the current best guess. A table of slopes of a many-input function is called its Jacobian: one row per output, one column per state variable. Replacing a curve with its tangent like this is called linearizing.

Predict   x ← f(x, u)    P ← F P Fᵀ + Q    with F = ∂f/∂x at x

Correct   y = z − h(x)    H = ∂h/∂x at x    S = H P Hᵀ + R    K = P Hᵀ S⁻¹
x ← x + K y    P ← (I − K H) P Move the best guess with the real, curvy f, and predict the measurement with the real h. But move the uncertainty with the tangent-line slopes F and H, because only straight lines keep a bell curve a bell curve.
✏️ Worked example: the Jacobian of a range-bearing sensor

The robot thinks it's at (0, 0) facing along x (θ = 0). A landmark sits at (4, 3). So dx = 4, dy = 3.

  1. Predicted measurement h: range √(16 + 9) = 5 m; bearing atan2(3, 4) − 0 = 36.9°.
  2. Slopes of range: step the robot 1 m toward +x and the range shrinks by dx/r = 0.8 m; toward +y, by dy/r = 0.6 m. Turning doesn't change range. Row: [−0.8, −0.6, 0].
  3. Slopes of bearing (radians per meter): dy/r² = 0.12 for x, −dx/r² = −0.16 for y, and −1 for heading (turn left 1°, the landmark appears 1° further right). Row: [0.12, −0.16, −1].

That 2×3 table is H. Notice the 1/r²: a landmark 2 m away gives bearing slopes 6× bigger than one at 5 m. Close landmarks are where the tangent line goes wrong fastest, as the next playground shows.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. The Kalman filter is exact only for straight-line models. A turning car, a drone's rotating body and every range-and-bearing sensor are curved, so the KF has nothing to multiply by: there is no single matrix F or H.
  2. What people tried. Ignore the curve and use a fixed matrix from one operating point (fine for a rocket on a planned trajectory, wrong as soon as you turn). Throw away the angles and only filter positions (loses the heading, which steers everything). Keep the full distribution on a grid (the histogram filter of Lesson 3.2: exact, but a 3D pose grid at 10 cm and 1° over a parking lot has millions of cells).
  3. Why this works. Recompute the straight-line approximation every step, at the current best guess. Over one small time step, and within a small uncertainty, a smooth curve really is nearly straight. Example: bearing to a landmark 50 m away changes almost exactly linearly over a 1 m position error; the EKF is then essentially as good as a KF. NASA's Apollo navigation filter was an early EKF (Lesson 3.3).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You must derive (and debug) the Jacobians; a sign error silently ruins the filter. It fails when the curve bends a lot inside the uncertainty: a wide heading spread (the banana, 2.2 m of bias), a landmark very close, a function with zero slope at the guess (the EKF then reports zero spread). And a bad initial guess puts the tangent in the wrong place, so the filter can lock onto a wrong answer and become confidently wrong.
  5. What came next. The iterated EKF re-linearizes at the corrected guess; the error-state EKF linearizes a small error instead of the whole state, the standard for drone attitude (Lesson 3.6); the unscented Kalman filter (UKF) drops Jacobians and pushes sample points through the real curve (below); particle filters drop the single bell curve entirely (Lesson 3.5).
🧩 Predict: in the next playground, pick the curve x² (think “power grows with the square of speed”) and center the input bell curve at 0 with σ = 1. The real outputs are spread out between 0 and about 4. What spread will the EKF report?
Zero. The tangent to x² at 0 is flat, so the EKF concludes the output never changes: f(0) = 0 with no uncertainty at all. The true output averages 1 with a spread of about 1.41. The UKF's three points land at 0, 3 and 3, and give exactly the right average (1) and spread (1.41). Whenever the slope is zero at the guess (passing right next to a landmark, flying exactly level), an EKF can become blind.

Fix 2, the UKF: send scouts through the curve

Julier and Uhlmann's idea (1997): it's easier to approximate a bell curve than a curved function. So don't touch the function. Pick a few sigma points that have exactly the mean and spread of your belief, push each through the real f or h, and fit a new bell curve to where they land. This recipe is the unscented transform; a Kalman filter that uses it for predict and correct is the UKF.

1. Pick 2n + 1 points: the mean, and the mean ± √(n + κ) × each “square root column” of P
2. Push each through the real function: Yi = f(Xi)
3. Refit   mean = Σ wi Yi    P = Σ wi (Yi − mean)(Yi − mean)ᵀ (+ Q) n is the number of state variables. For one variable with κ = 2 the points sit at the mean and ±√3 σ, with weights ⅔, ⅙, ⅙: exactly the numbers in the worked example. For a 3-number robot pose it's 7 points; for PX4's 24-state drone filter it would be 49.

🧠 Analogy: the road ahead in fog

You want to know where a winding road goes in the fog. The EKF looks at the road right under your wheels and assumes it continues straight in that direction. The UKF sends three scouts: one straight ahead, one a bit left, one a bit right. Each walks the real road for a while and radios back where they ended up. Three reports describe a bend far better than one tangent, but if the road zigzags between the scouts, they'll all miss it.

When both fail: a bad first guess

Both filters correct the guess using the uncertainty they believe. If you start with a wrong heading but tell the filter it's certain, every measurement that disagrees is blamed on the cheapest explanation: position, not heading. The filter settles into a wrong answer and stays there, with a small, smug ellipse. This is called divergence (or being inconsistent: the real error is far bigger than the filter's own uncertainty says).

🧩 Predict: the robot below drives a circle and sees one lighthouse (range and bearing, twice a second). Its starting heading is 120° wrong, but the filter is told “± 5°”. After 40 seconds of good measurements, will it have fixed its heading?
No. It ends up driving a parallel circle about 6 m away from the truth, with a tiny ellipse. Bearings to one landmark can be explained by “I'm somewhere else” as well as by “I'm facing elsewhere”, and the filter, sure of its heading, picks the first. The cures: admit the uncertainty (a wide initial σ), add information (a second landmark makes the heading observable at once), or keep several hypotheses alive (particle filters, Lesson 3.5).

What you should find: the filter choice matters far less than honesty. Telling the filter “± 5°” when the truth is 120° off is the real bug; with ± 60° to ± 90° both filters usually recover, and the UKF does so a little more reliably (in our tests with ten noise seeds: UKF 10 of 10, EKF 7 or 8 of 10). Engineers' rule: initialize wide, and let the measurements shrink the ellipse.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

FilterPy has both filters with the same predict/update loop as the Kalman filter; you supply f, h and (for the EKF) the Jacobian. In ROS 2, robot_localization ships an ekf_node and a ukf_node with the same configuration format. Always wrap angle differences into (−180°, 180°]: a bearing residual of 359° is really −1°.

import numpy as np
from filterpy.kalman import ExtendedKalmanFilter, UnscentedKalmanFilter, MerweScaledSigmaPoints

LM = np.array([9.0, 3.0])                          # a known landmark (the lighthouse)
wrap = lambda a: (a + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi

def hx(x):                                     # state x = [px, py, heading]
    dx, dy = LM - x[:2]
    return np.array([np.hypot(dx, dy), wrap(np.arctan2(dy, dx) - x[2])])

def H_jacobian(x):                             # the worked example, in code
    dx, dy = LM - x[:2]; q = dx*dx + dy*dy; r = np.sqrt(q)
    return np.array([[-dx/r, -dy/r, 0], [dy/q, -dx/q, -1]])

def residual(a, b):                             # z - h(x) with the bearing wrapped
    y = a - b; y[1] = wrap(y[1]); return y

ekf = ExtendedKalmanFilter(dim_x=3, dim_z=2)
ekf.x = np.array([15.0, 3.4, 0.0]); ekf.P = np.diag([1, 1, np.radians(90)**2])  # honest!
ekf.R = np.diag([0.3**2, np.radians(3)**2])
z = np.array([6.1, 2.9])                            # one range-bearing reading
ekf.update(z, HJacobian=H_jacobian, Hx=hx, residual=residual)

def fx(x, dt, v=1.5, w=0.3):                       # unicycle motion: the curvy f
    return np.array([x[0] + v*np.cos(x[2])*dt, x[1] + v*np.sin(x[2])*dt, x[2] + w*dt])

points = MerweScaledSigmaPoints(n=3, alpha=0.1, beta=2.0, kappa=0.0)
ukf = UnscentedKalmanFilter(dim_x=3, dim_z=2, dt=0.1, hx=hx, fx=fx, points=points)
ukf.x, ukf.P, ukf.R = ekf.x.copy(), ekf.P.copy(), ekf.R
ukf.predict(); ukf.update(z)                     # no Jacobians anywhere
# ROS 2 launch: the same robot_localization config, unscented instead of extended
Node(package='robot_localization', executable='ukf_node', name='ukf_filter_node',
     parameters=['config/ukf.yaml'])

Check a hand-written Jacobian against a finite difference ((h(x + ε) − h(x − ε)) / 2ε for each state) once, in a unit test. It catches the sign errors that otherwise surface as “the filter is a bit off in turns”.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

Flight controllers are EKF country. PX4's EKF2 is a 24-state extended Kalman filter whose attitude is a quaternion (Lesson 1.4); attitude is what makes it nonlinear, because every accelerometer reading must be rotated into the world frame before it predicts velocity. ArduPilot's EKF3 runs one EKF “core” per IMU and flies on the healthiest. Both are open source, so you can read every Jacobian.

Sources: PX4 EKF docs, ArduPilot EKF overview

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published which filters its estimator uses. What it has said (Sep 2020): the Waymo Driver knows where it is by comparing what its sensors see, built around its own lidar, with highly detailed maps it builds in advance, which helps “even where GPS struggles, like tunnels or between skyscrapers”. Matching a scan to a map is a strongly nonlinear measurement of exactly the kind this lesson is about; how Waymo fuses it isn't public.

Sources: Waymo Driver Handbook: mapping (2020)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published how it estimates the car's own position, or which filters (if any) it uses. Its public talks describe neural networks doing the nonlinear work instead: at CVPR 2021, Andrej Karpathy showed networks estimating other cars' depth and velocity straight from video, and AI Day 2021 described networks that turn the car's camera video into a 3D picture of the surroundings. Any filter inside is not documented.

Sources: CVPR'21 WAD keynote, Tesla AI Day 2021

🤖 Robots & research cars

Stanley (2005) used a 15-state UKF at 100 Hz, and switched to a stricter motion model (“the car only moves where it points”) during GPS outages. ROS 2 robots get both an EKF and a UKF node from robot_localization; most start with the EKF and switch only if turns or close landmarks hurt.

Sources: Thrun et al. 2006, robot_localization

Why they differ: a drone's curves are known physics (rotations, gravity), small enough to linearize every few milliseconds on a microcontroller, so a carefully derived EKF is the reliable, auditable choice. A research car with odd sensors and messy models valued the UKF's “no Jacobians” convenience. Waymo's hardest nonlinearity is matching sensor data to a map, and Tesla bets that networks can learn the nonlinear mapping from cameras to the world; neither has published the filter that sits around it.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your EKF works on straight roads and goes wrong after sharp turns. What do you check?” A strong answer:

  • Jacobians: compare them to finite differences; check angle wrapping in residuals and states.
  • Consistency: are the innovations (measurement minus prediction) bigger than S says? Plot the normalized innovation; if it's too big in turns, the linearization or Q is wrong (Lesson 3.7 has the test).
  • Initialization: an over-confident initial P, especially on heading, causes lock-in.
  • Model: a constant-velocity model can't turn; use a turn-rate model, raise Q on heading, or try a UKF. If the belief is genuinely multimodal, no bell-curve filter will do: particle filter (3.5).
🏭 In the real world
Apollo navigation 📜 History · 1960s McGee & Schmidt, NASA TM (1985)

How: NASA Ames linearized the spacecraft's curved orbital dynamics around the current estimate at each step: what we now call the extended Kalman filter.

Why this method: orbits are smooth and well modeled, so a tangent-line approximation over each short step was accurate, and cheap enough for a 1960s computer.

Stanley wins the desert race 🚗 AV · 2005 Thrun et al., J. Field Robotics 2006

How: a UKF estimated 15 state variables (3D position, velocity and orientation, plus accelerometer and gyro biases) at 100 Hz from GPS, a GPS compass, an IMU and wheel encoders.

Why this method: the team notes that sigma-point linearization often gives lower error than the EKF's Taylor expansion, and needs no explicit Jacobians.

Every PX4 drone 🧰 Open source PX4: Using the ECL EKF

How: a 24-state EKF predicts with the IMU and corrects with GPS, barometer, magnetometer, optical flow and vision, each nonlinear in the attitude quaternion.

Why this method: runs hundreds of times a second on a flight controller, with innovation checks that catch bad sensors.

Ground robots on ROS 2 🧰 Open source robot_localization

How: configurable EKF and UKF nodes fuse wheel odometry, IMU and GPS for a robot's 2D or 3D pose.

Why this method: one config file, either filter; the motion model (an omnidirectional 3D kinematic model) is already nonlinear in heading.

🤯 Fun fact · named after a deodorant

Jeffrey Uhlmann recalls that colleagues started calling his method the “Uhlmann filter”, which he couldn't use, so he needed a name. One evening, alone in the lab while everyone else was at the Royal Opera House, he noticed a colleague's deodorant on a desk: unscented. He picked it precisely because it meant nothing, and within months other researchers were using it as if it always had (Uhlmann, First-Hand history, ETHW).

Check yourself

1. A UKF tracks a 4-number state (x, y, speed, heading). How many sigma points does the standard unscented transform push through the motion model each step?
2n + 1 = 2 × 4 + 1 = 9: the mean, plus one point on each side along each of the 4 directions of the uncertainty. That's why the UKF stays cheap for small states: 9 function calls instead of deriving a 4×4 Jacobian.
2. A robot's heading is 0° ± 60° and it drives 10 m. The EKF says the average end point is 10 m ahead. Roughly where is the true average?
Sideways errors cancel, but every turned path ends short of 10 m along the road. With σ = 60° = 1.047 rad the average is 10 × e−σ²/2 = 10 × 0.578 ≈ 5.8 m. The EKF's tangent line can't see this bias; the UKF's sigma points (0°, ±104°) give about 5.9 m.
3. Your EKF starts with the heading 90° wrong but P says ±2°. After a minute of good landmark measurements the estimate is still several meters off, and its ellipse is tiny. What's the best first fix?
The filter is overconfident: it believes its heading, so it explains every disagreement as a position error and locks in. A UKF with the same lie behaves almost the same. A wide initial P lets the first measurements rotate the estimate; if you truly don't know the start, use a particle filter for global localization (Lesson 3.5).
📝 TL;DR
  • A bell curve stays a bell curve only through straight lines; turns, angles and range-bearing sensors bend it.
  • EKF: move the mean with the real f and h, move the uncertainty with their slopes (Jacobians) at the current guess.
  • UKF: push 2n + 1 sigma points through the real function and refit. No derivatives; catches the banana's bias (7.84 m, not 10).
  • Both fail on strong curvature inside the uncertainty, zero slopes (EKF), wiggles (UKF) and overconfident starts. Initialize wide.
  • Drones fly on EKFs (PX4 EKF2, ArduPilot EKF3); Stanley won with a UKF; Waymo and Tesla haven't published their filters.
