Phase 6 · Lesson 3

Occupancy networks: “something is there” beats “a car is there”

A box detector only sees what it was taught to name. The road is full of things nobody named: a fallen ladder, a mattress, a trailer with a strange shape. Occupancy networks stop naming and start asking one question about every little cube of space: is it filled?

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bird's-eye view (BEV) grids: the scene from above in 0.5 m cells, built from cameras by lifting pixels with a depth guess or by letting map cells query the images (Lesson 6.2).
  • A voxel is a 3D pixel: a small cube of space, say 0.4 m on a side. A voxel grid is a 3D array of them.
  • Box detectors output a class and a 3D box per object, and need a confidence threshold to decide which outputs count (Lesson 6.1).
  • Occupancy grid (classic robotics): a 2D map where each cell holds the probability it's blocked, built from lidar or sonar. Lesson 5.1 builds these for mapping; here a network predicts one in 3D from cameras.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Your detector knows three classes: car, pedestrian, cyclist. A ladder falls off a van and lies across your lane, 25 m ahead. What does the detector output? And if you lower the confidence threshold until it finally shows something, what else shows up?

Tesla described this exact trap at CVPR 2022. Its object network was confused by a pickup truck that looked like a fence, by statues and mannequins that look like people, and by a crouched pedestrian that looks like a pile of trash; Ashok Elluswamy called defining the right list of classes “an impossible fight”. Tesla's answer was an occupancy network that takes all 8 cameras and predicts, for every voxel around the car, whether it's occupied, and how it's moving, in about 10 milliseconds (CVPR'22 WAD keynote).

🧩 Predict: the ladder gets a confidence of 0.16 as a “cyclist?”. A shadow, a puddle and a painted arrow score 0.19 to 0.24 as “obstacles?”. If you lower the threshold to 0.15 so the ladder counts, how many phantom obstacles do you also brake for?
All three. Every flat, harmless thing that scored above the ladder now counts too, so the car slams on the brakes for a shadow 14 m ahead (a “phantom braking” event) before it ever reaches the ladder. A threshold can't separate “odd but real” from “odd but harmless” when the network has no class for the odd thing. Occupancy asks a different question (is space physically filled?), which shadows and paint fail and ladders pass. Try it below.

Why boxes and pixels weren't enough

Before occupancy networks, Tesla's talk listed two common ways to represent “general obstacles” from cameras, and why each broke:

RepresentationWhat it outputsWhy it broke (per Tesla, CVPR 2022)
Image-space drivable spaceEach pixel: drivable or notIt's in pixels, not meters; the few pixels near the horizon swing wildly when projected to 3D; no overhangs, walls or occlusion
Dense depthEach pixel: a distanceLooks great close up, but far away walls wave and curve; each camera's depth disagrees at the seams; can't see behind things; blurs edges between a car and the background
3D boxes with classesCar / pedestrian / cyclist + boxOnly for named classes; an odd shape gets the wrong class or none; protrusions (a ladder on a roof rack) don't fit a cuboid

An occupancy network keeps the best part of each: it's in 3D meters (like boxes), it's dense (like depth, every location gets an answer), and it's class-free (like drivable space, it just says filled or not). Tesla's version predicts a probability per voxel from all eight cameras at once, “no stitching of independent predictions”, both static things (walls, trees) and moving ones, even through occlusions.

✏️ Worked example: how many voxels?

Use the public Occ3D-nuScenes benchmark's grid: ±40 m around the car, from 1 m below to 5.4 m above it, in 0.4 m voxels.

  1. Across: 80 m ÷ 0.4 = 200 voxels. Along: 200. Up: 6.4 m ÷ 0.4 = 16. Total = 200 × 200 × 16 = 640,000 voxels.
  2. Halve the voxel size to 0.2 m: 400 × 400 × 32 = 5.12 million. 8× more, because all three dimensions double.
  3. Store 1 byte of occupancy plus a 2-number flow (4 bytes as 16-bit floats) per voxel: 640,000 × 5 B ≈ 3.2 MB at 0.4 m, ≈ 26 MB at 0.2 m, and real networks keep many more feature channels inside.
  4. Occ3D-Waymo uses 0.05 m voxels over ±80 m: 3,200 × 3,200 × 128 ≈ 1.3 billion voxels per frame, which is why it's a label format, not something a car predicts densely at 100 Hz.

Resolution costs cubically. That's the central engineering trade of this lesson, and Playground 2.

💡 Big idea Stop asking “what is there?” first. Ask “is anything there, and where is it going?” for every small cube of space, then add class labels on top only where they help the plan (a pedestrian may step out; a cardboard box won't). The planner needs to avoid hitting things; it doesn't need to know that the thing is a ladder.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Box detectors cover a fixed list of classes. The road's long tail (debris, odd trailers, overturned cars, a pickup that looks like a fence) is exactly what they miss or mislabel, and image-space drivable space or per-pixel depth get noisy and inconsistent beyond a few tens of meters.
  2. What people tried. More classes (“debris”, “other”): the list never ends and labels are inconsistent. Lower thresholds: catches the odd thing, plus every shadow (phantom braking). Per-camera depth or drivable space, projected to 3D: noisy at range, seams between cameras. Lidar-based occupancy grids work well but need a lidar.
  3. Why this works. Geometry is class-free: a ladder fills voxels; a shadow doesn't. Learned from many cameras at once, the network makes one consistent 3D volume. Tiny numbers: 200 × 200 × 16 = 640,000 voxels at 0.4 m is small enough that Tesla says its version runs in about 10 ms, i.e. it could run at 100 Hz, faster than its cameras' 36 frames per second.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Memory and compute grow cubically with resolution, so thin things (a ladder 12 cm tall, a cable, a curb) need fine voxels exactly where it's expensive. Training labels need dense 3D ground truth (from lidar, multi-trip reconstruction or auto-labeling, Lesson 6.7). Camera-only occupancy still guesses depth, so far occupancy is soft. Flat hazards (a pothole, black ice) aren't “occupied” at all.
  5. What came next. Occupancy flow (predicting where filled voxels will move: Tesla, and Waymo's “Occupancy Flow Fields” for motion forecasting), semantic occupancy (a class per voxel, as in the Occ3D benchmarks with a “general object” class for the unnamed), continuous queries at arbitrary points instead of a fixed grid (Tesla, AI Day 2022), and supervision by rendering the predicted volume back into camera images, the NeRF idea.

How an occupancy network is built (plain words)

Tesla has described its design twice, at CVPR 2022 and AI Day 2022. In plain words:

  1. Normalize and encode each camera. Rectify away each camera's lens quirks; feed raw 12-bit photon counts (16× the dynamic range of a normal 8-bit image, and no image processor in the loop, per AI Day 2022) into image backbones that output feature maps.
  2. 3D queries ask the images. A fixed set of 3D positions, each with a positional code, attends to the image features of all cameras (the same pull idea as BEVFormer in Lesson 6.2, but in 3D). Details of attention: the Deep Learning course, Phase 6 (self-attention).
  3. Low resolution, then upsample. Doing this at every fine voxel is too expensive, so features are made at a coarse resolution and upsampled (deconvolutions) to the output grid. AI Day 2022 added an MLP (a small network) you can query at any 3D point for finer detail.
  4. Add time. Features from a short buffer of past frames are aligned using the car's own motion, which lets the network output occupancy flow: how each filled voxel moves.
  5. Supervise with geometry. Train on auto-labeled 3D occupancy (“without any human in the loop”, AI Day 2022), and additionally by rendering the predicted volume into held-out camera images and comparing, like a neural radiance field (NeRF). 3D vision background: the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (3D vision).

Occupancy flow: where will the filled space be?

Occupancy alone is a snapshot. Following a car on the highway, its voxels are filled right in front of you. If the planner assumed those voxels were standing still, it would brake for a car that's driving away at your own speed. Tesla's CVPR 2022 talk made exactly this point and added occupancy flow: a velocity for each filled voxel (or higher derivatives), estimated from several time steps. Waymo's research reached the same idea from the forecasting side: Occupancy Flow Fields (2022) predict future occupancy and flow of all agents together.

🧩 Predict: a cyclist crosses your lane 30 m ahead from the left at 3 m/s while you drive at 14 m/s. A planner that sees only the current occupancy: does it brake too early, on time, or too late?
Too late. While the cyclist is still left of your lane, current occupancy says “your lane is clear”. By the time the voxels in your lane fill, you're about 2 seconds away and need hard braking, too late here. With flow over a 1.5–2 s horizon, the planner sees the cyclist's future voxels crossing your path and eases off early and smoothly.

🧠 Analogy: a blind person's cane vs a field guide

A field guide lets you name birds you've seen pictures of; show it a bird it doesn't list and it's useless. A cane doesn't know what anything is, but it never fails to tell you something is in the way. Occupancy is the cane, swept over the whole space around the car 100 times a second, plus a sense of which way the obstacles are moving. You still want the field guide for things whose identity changes what you do: a child behaves differently from a bin.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Open benchmarks: Occ3D (nuScenes and Waymo versions), with dense, visibility-aware voxel labels. For geometry experiments, Open3D voxelizes point clouds; for a quick occupancy grid, NumPy is enough.

import numpy as np
import open3d as o3d

# A 3D occupancy grid from a lidar (or reconstructed) point cloud, NumPy only
def occupancy(points, voxel=0.4, lo=(-40, -40, -1), hi=(40, 40, 5.4)):
    lo, hi = np.array(lo), np.array(hi)
    shape = np.ceil((hi - lo) / voxel).astype(int)              # (200, 200, 16) for the defaults
    idx = np.floor((points[:, :3] - lo) / voxel).astype(int)
    ok = np.all((idx >= 0) & (idx < shape), axis=1)
    grid = np.zeros(shape, dtype=bool)
    grid[tuple(idx[ok].T)] = True
    return grid                                                 # True = at least one point fell in it

# The same with Open3D (sparse: only filled voxels are stored)
pcd = o3d.geometry.PointCloud(o3d.utility.Vector3dVector(points[:, :3]))
vg = o3d.geometry.VoxelGrid.create_from_point_cloud(pcd, voxel_size=0.4)
filled = [v.grid_index for v in vg.get_voxels()]

Careful with “no points = empty”: a voxel with no lidar returns may be unobserved (hidden behind a truck), not free. Occ3D's labels mark visibility for exactly this reason; Phase 5's occupancy grids keep three states: free, occupied, unknown.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

The occupancy network is the base of Tesla's publicly described stack: “the base geometry layer” under lanes and objects (AI Day 2022). As presented: 8 cameras in, one volumetric occupancy and occupancy flow out; runs about every 10 ms on the FSD computer; raw 12-bit photon counts as input; RegNet/BiFPN image backbones; 3D position queries with attention; trained on auto-labeled data with no human in the loop, plus NeRF-style rendering losses. Tesla also said the same occupancy network was ported to its Optimus robot with new training data.

Sources: CVPR'22 WAD keynote, Tesla AI Day 2022

🚕 Waymo

Waymo's lidar measures occupancy directly, so the hard part shifts to prediction: its research on Occupancy Flow Fields forecasts future occupancy and flow of all agents, and StopNet (ICRA 2022) predicts trajectories and occupancy at scale. The Occ3D-Waymo occupancy benchmark is built from the Waymo Open Dataset. What its production stack uses internally isn't published.

Sources: Occupancy Flow Fields, Waymo research, Occ3D

🛸 Drones

For a drone, a 3D occupancy map is the natural world model: it flies among branches, wires and walls, none of which need a class. Skydio says its drones “constantly map the environment” as they fly and can build 3D models onboard. Its manual notes limits: obstacle avoidance doesn't avoid moving objects and can be degraded near transparent or reflective surfaces. Internal map formats aren't published.

Sources: Skydio autonomy, Skydio X10

Why they differ: Tesla has no lidar, so a learned occupancy network is how it gets class-free geometry at all. Waymo gets geometry from lidar and spends learning on how that geometry will move. A drone lives in 3D clutter where classes barely matter, so occupancy-style maps are its default, with tight limits on weight and compute.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your detector missed a mattress on the highway. What would you change?” A strong answer:

  • Says lowering thresholds or adding a “debris” class won't scale (phantom braking, endless ontology), and proposes a class-free geometric layer: occupancy (lidar or learned from cameras) with flow.
  • Does the memory math: 200 × 200 × 16 = 640k voxels at 0.4 m; halve the voxel and pay 8×; spend resolution near and in-path.
  • Distinguishes free, occupied and unknown, and explains why unobserved space must not be treated as free.
  • Talks about data: dense 3D labels from lidar or auto-labeling, rare-hazard mining, and evaluation on long-tail sets rather than average IoU.
🏭 In the real world
Tesla's occupancy network 🏢 Tesla · 2022 CVPR'22 keynote

How: multi-camera video → 3D queries attend to image features → voxel occupancy and occupancy flow, about every 10 ms.

Why this method: the object network kept fighting classification dilemmas (fence-like pickup, mannequins); occupancy avoids hitting things regardless of what they are.

Occ3D benchmarks 🧪 Research · 2023 Occ3D

How: a label pipeline (voxel densification, occlusion reasoning, image-guided refinement) turns nuScenes and Waymo data into dense voxel labels: 0.4 m (200×200×16) and 0.05 m grids, with a “general object” class.

Why this method: boxes “struggle to handle general, out-of-vocabulary objects”; the field needed a shared yardstick for occupancy.

Occupancy Flow Fields at Waymo 🚗 AV Mahjourian et al., 2022

How: predict future occupancy grids and per-cell flow for all agents together, instead of one trajectory per agent.

Why this method: a grid handles any number of agents, and their uncertainty, at a fixed cost.

Classic occupancy grids on robots 📜 History Occupancy grid mapping

How: each cell keeps a probability of being blocked, updated from range sensors (Lesson 5.1).

Why this method: robots have avoided unnamed obstacles this way for decades; the new part is predicting it in 3D from cameras.

🤯 Fun fact · “there is no such thing as stationary obstacles”

Tesla's team started the project to handle only static things like walls and trees, leaving moving objects to the object network. Then they changed their minds; in Elluswamy's words, “any object when enough force is applied will move”, so moving and static obstacles went into the same framework (CVPR'22 WAD keynote).

🌉 How it connects

Occupancy is the 3D, learned cousin of the occupancy grids of Lesson 5.1. It's built with the camera-to-BEV machinery of 6.2 (queries attending to images) and complements the boxes of 6.1. Its flow feeds prediction and planning (Phase 7) as cost maps; its dense labels come from the auto-labeling of 6.7. Probability per voxel is plain probability (Math course); the Bayesian update of grids is Bayes' rule.

Check yourself

1. An occupancy grid covers ±40 m with 6.4 m of height at 0.4 m voxels (640,000 voxels). You switch to 0.2 m voxels over the same volume. How many voxels now?
All three dimensions double: 400 × 400 × 32 = 5.12 million, 2³ = 8× more. That's why fine resolution is spent only where it matters.
2. On a test drive the car brakes hard behind a lead vehicle that is accelerating away. Perception shows the lead car's voxels correctly. Most likely missing piece?
Instantaneous occupancy says “filled”, not “moving away at 14 m/s”. Without flow, a planner must assume the worst and brakes. Tesla's CVPR 2022 talk used this exact highway-following example to motivate occupancy flow.
3. Your camera occupancy network marks a region behind a parked truck as empty, though nothing there was ever seen. What's the right way to treat it?
“Not seen” isn't “free”. Occ3D's labels track visibility, and Tesla's talk described reasoning about occlusions and creeping forward at blind turns. Treating unknown as free is how you hit the car hidden behind the truck.
📝 TL;DR
  • Box detectors only see named classes; lowering thresholds brings phantom obstacles. Occupancy asks “is this voxel filled?” for everything, class-free.
  • Tesla (CVPR 2022, AI Day 2022): 8 cameras → one volumetric occupancy + occupancy flow, about every 10 ms; 3D queries attend to image features; auto-labeled and NeRF-style supervision.
  • Resolution costs cubically: 200 × 200 × 16 = 640k voxels at 0.4 m (Occ3D-nuScenes), 8× more at 0.2 m. Spend fine voxels near and in-path; thin hazards need them.
  • Flow turns a snapshot into a prediction: no braking behind moving cars, early easing for crossing cyclists.
  • Unobserved ≠ free. Waymo measures occupancy with lidar and researches occupancy flow for forecasting; drones live in 3D maps by default.
