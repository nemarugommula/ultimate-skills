Phase 6 · Lesson 5

Multi-object tracking: association, Hungarian, SORT & ByteTrack

A detector says “there are two people” 10 times a second. A planner needs to know “person #7 is walking left at 1.4 m/s and has been for 3 seconds”. Tracking turns a flicker of boxes into a cast of characters with names, histories and velocities, and the hard part is never mixing up the names.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The Kalman filter: predict where something will be from its velocity, then correct with each new measurement, weighted by trust (Lesson 3.3). Here every tracked object gets its own little filter.
  • Detections: per frame, a list of boxes with confidence scores from a detector (Lessons 6.1, 6.2). They have no names: box 3 now isn't necessarily box 3 next frame.
  • IoU, intersection over union: overlap of two boxes, 0 to 1 (Lesson 6.1). Distance between centers works too, especially in 3D.
  • Process noise q: how much the filter expects the velocity to change between steps. Small q = “they walk in straight lines”; large q = “they may turn any moment” (Lesson 3.3).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Two pedestrians cross paths in front of your car. For half a second the one behind is half hidden, and your detector gives it a shaky 0.3 confidence or misses it entirely. When they separate, which one is which? If your tracker gets it wrong, the planner suddenly believes the person who was walking away is now walking toward the road.

This name-swapping, called an ID switch, is the classic tracking failure. In 2021 the ByteTrack paper showed a surprisingly simple fix: don't throw away the low-confidence boxes. Using them in a second matching pass cut ID switches on the MOT17 validation set from 291 to 159 compared with the classic SORT tracker, and the full tracker hit 80.3 MOTA at 30 frames per second (Zhang et al.). You'll rebuild that trick and tune it until the swap disappears.

Tracking-by-detection, in four steps

Almost every production tracker, for cars, drones and robots, follows the same loop every frame:

  1. Predict. Move every existing track forward with its own constant-velocity Kalman filter: “#7 was at (4.0, 2.1) moving +1.4 m/s in x, so now it's at about (4.14, 2.1)”. Its uncertainty grows.
  2. Associate. Decide which new detection belongs to which track: an assignment problem over a cost matrix (distance or 1 − IoU between every track's prediction and every detection).
  3. Update. Matched tracks run the Kalman correction with their detection.
  4. Manage the lifecycle. Unmatched detections may be born as new tentative tracks (confirmed after a few hits in a row); unmatched tracks coast on prediction and die after a maximum age without a match.

Step 2 is where IDs get swapped, so let's look at it closely.

✏️ Worked example: greedy vs optimal matching

Two tracks A and B (predicted positions) and two detections a and b, with costs = distance in meters:

detection adetection b
track A0.92.9
track B0.81.1
  • Greedy (take the cheapest pair first, repeat): B–a (0.8) first, then A is stuck with b (2.9). Total = 3.7 m.
  • Optimal: A–a + B–b = 0.9 + 1.1 = 2.0 m. Almost half the total, and the IDs are right.

Greedy grabs the locally best pair and forces a bad pair elsewhere. The Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955) finds the assignment with the smallest total cost in O(n³) time for n tracks: instant for the dozens of objects around a car. Trying every assignment instead would take n! steps: 3.6 million for just 10 objects.

💡 Big idea Tracking is filtering plus bookkeeping. The Kalman filter makes a good guess of where each object will be; the assignment step decides who is who by solving a small optimization every frame; the lifecycle rules decide who is real. Most ID switches are not filter failures but bookkeeping failures: a track died too early, a gate was too wide, or a weak detection was thrown away.

🧩 Predict: in the crossing game below, the half-hidden pedestrian's boxes score 0.2–0.4 while they overlap. With a plain SORT-style tracker (score threshold 0.5, tracks die after 3 missed frames), what happens to that pedestrian's ID?
Its boxes are below the threshold, so they're ignored; the track gets no match for several frames and dies. When the pedestrian reappears at full confidence, a new track is born with a new ID: an ID switch, even though nobody was confused. Fix it by keeping tracks alive longer and, better, by matching leftover tracks to the low-score boxes (ByteTrack). But keep tracks alive too loosely and they start stealing each other's boxes.

What you should find: turning on the second pass alone isn't enough if tracks still die after 3 frames; keeping them for 6 or more is needed. Then the motion model matters: with very small q the filter insists on straight lines and misreads the slight wobble in the walk at the crossing point; with moderate q it follows. A very tight gate refuses the noisy boxes; a very loose one lets a coasting track grab the wrong person. Tuning a tracker is balancing those four knobs.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Detectors work frame by frame: no identity, no velocity, misses during occlusion, flicker. A planner needs each object's history and velocity to predict its future (Phase 7), and a pedestrian that blinks out for 5 frames must not be forgotten.
  2. What people tried. Nearest neighbour (each track takes the closest detection): two tracks can claim the same box, and greedy choices swap IDs. Multiple-hypothesis tracking (keep every possible association history and decide later): very accurate, but the number of hypotheses explodes. Appearance only: fails for look-alike objects and in the dark.
  3. Why this works. A Kalman prediction makes the right detection the closest one most of the time; the Hungarian algorithm then finds the globally best one-to-one assignment (greedy 3.7 m vs optimal 2.0 m in the example). Lifecycle rules (confirm after k hits, delete after max age) filter clutter and bridge short gaps. ByteTrack adds one more pass with the low-score boxes, which are often real but occluded objects: on MOT17 validation that alone cut ID switches from 291 to 159 versus SORT.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Every knob trades two failures: a wide gate or long max age bridges occlusions but lets tracks steal neighbours' boxes; a low threshold recovers occluded objects but tracks clutter. Constant velocity fails on sharp turns; camera motion breaks image-space prediction (ByteTrack's paper notes the Kalman filter fails with large camera motion on BDD100K). Long occlusions need appearance or a better motion model.
  5. What came next. Appearance re-ID (DeepSORT and successors), 3D tracking in BEV with velocity from the detector (CenterPoint), learned association (transformers that match objects across frames, like Waymo's STT), and offline auto-labeling that stitches tracks through occlusion using future frames (Lesson 6.7).

Scoring a tracker: MOTA, IDF1, HOTA

You can't improve what you can't count. Three metrics dominate, each with a different idea of “good”:

MOTA = 1 − (FN + FP + IDSW) ÷ GT Multi-object tracking accuracy: start at 100% and subtract every miss (false negative), every false track (false positive) and every ID switch, as a fraction of all ground-truth objects summed over frames. Simple, but dominated by detection quality; one ID switch costs as little as one miss.
  • IDF1 (identity F1, 2016) asks: over the whole video, what fraction of detections carry the right long-term identity? It punishes swaps much harder than MOTA does.
  • HOTA (higher-order tracking accuracy, 2020) balances detection, association and localization in one number, and splits into sub-scores to show which one is weak. It's now the headline metric on many benchmarks. The TrackEval library computes all three.
✏️ Worked example: MOTA by hand

A 100-frame clip with 10 pedestrians in every frame: GT = 1,000. Your tracker misses 80 (FN), adds 50 false boxes (FP), and switches IDs 10 times. MOTA = 1 − (80 + 50 + 10) ÷ 1,000 = 0.86. Notice the 10 swaps barely dent the score. A pedestrian whose ID swapped with another's, though, might get the other person's velocity, which is why AV teams watch IDF1, HOTA and raw switch counts too.

🧠 Analogy: a nursery teacher on a field trip

Every few seconds the teacher glances at the playground and sees some kids (detections). She doesn't re-learn who's who each time: she expects Mia near the slide because Mia was running that way (prediction), pairs each kid she sees with the name that fits best overall, not just the first one that comes to mind (Hungarian), and when Leo ducks behind a tree she doesn't decide he's gone after one glance (max age), nor does she count a coat on a bench as a new child until she's seen it move a few times (hits to confirm). A blurry glimpse of a sleeve behind the tree still counts as “probably Leo” (ByteTrack).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The assignment step is one SciPy call; the filter is FilterPy or a dozen NumPy lines (Lesson 3.3). Reference trackers: SORT and ByteTrack (official repos), AB3DMOT for 3D; metrics: TrackEval (MOTA, IDF1, HOTA).

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

def associate(track_xy, det_xy, gate=1.0):
    """Hungarian matching on center distance with a gate. Returns matches, unmatched tracks, unmatched detections."""
    if len(track_xy) == 0 or len(det_xy) == 0:
        return [], list(range(len(track_xy))), list(range(len(det_xy)))
    cost = np.linalg.norm(track_xy[:, None, :] - det_xy[None, :, :], axis=2)   # (T, D) distances
    rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(cost)                                      # optimal one-to-one
    matches = [(r, c) for r, c in zip(rows, cols) if cost[r, c] <= gate]            # gate rejects far pairs
    mt, md = {r for r, _ in matches}, {c for _, c in matches}
    return matches, [t for t in range(len(track_xy)) if t not in mt], [d for d in range(len(det_xy)) if d not in md]

# ByteTrack's idea in two calls: high-score boxes first, then leftover tracks vs low-score boxes
hi, lo = dets[scores >= 0.6], dets[(scores < 0.6) & (scores >= 0.1)]
m1, left_tracks, new_dets = associate(pred_xy, hi)
m2, lost, _ = associate(pred_xy[left_tracks], lo)        # low-score boxes never start new tracks

linear_sum_assignment handles rectangular matrices (more detections than tracks or the reverse). Apply the gate after solving, or set gated entries to a large cost before solving, never to inf for a whole row (it can make the problem infeasible).

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's public talks describe networks that output object kinematics directly: “position, orientation, depth, velocity” in the vector space, using a video module with a feature queue so an object occluded for a moment is remembered (AI Day 2021). Offline, its auto-labeler uses “the benefit of hindsight”: past and future frames give exact velocities and let tracks be stitched through occlusions, such as pedestrians persisting while cars pass in front of them. Its in-car association logic isn't public.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, CVPR'21 WAD keynote

🚕 Waymo

Waymo publishes tracking research: STT (ICRA 2024) is a transformer that does association and state estimation together from long histories of detections, with new metrics that also score velocity and acceleration; SoDA learns soft data association. Its open dataset gives 12.6 million 3D boxes with tracking IDs, the basis of public 3D tracking benchmarks. Radar's direct speed measurement helps velocity from the first frame (Lesson 2.4).

Sources: STT, Waymo research, Waymo Open Dataset

🛸 Drones

Follow-me filming and security drones live on tracking. Skydio's Shadow mode tracks people and vehicles, keeps them in frame, and reacquires them automatically when they briefly move out of view; Skydio says it predicts motion from “speed, direction, and visual appearance”: motion model plus appearance, the DeepSORT recipe in spirit. It runs on the X10's onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin. The architecture isn't published.

Sources: Skydio X10, Skydio autonomy

Why they differ: Tesla pushes tracking into the networks (video in, kinematics out) and leans on fleet auto-labels. Waymo has lidar boxes with good 3D positions and radar speeds, so classic and learned association both work well, and it researches learning both jointly. A drone tracks one subject at close range with appearance as the main cue, on a small onboard GPU.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a multi-object tracker for pedestrians around a robotaxi.” A strong answer:

  • Lays out predict → associate → update → lifecycle, with a constant-velocity (or constant-turn) Kalman filter per track in BEV.
  • Uses Hungarian on a gated cost (distance, IoU, Mahalanobis distance from the filter's covariance, plus appearance), and explains greedy's failure with a 2×2 example.
  • Explains birth/death parameters and ByteTrack's low-score pass, and what each knob trades.
  • Measures with MOTA, IDF1 and HOTA, knows MOTA under-weights ID switches, and evaluates crowded and occluded scenes separately.
🏭 In the real world
SORT 🧰 Open source abewley/sort · paper

How: Kalman filter on box center, area and aspect ratio; Hungarian on IoU; minimal lifecycle rules.

Why this method: showed detection quality matters most and a simple tracker at 260 Hz can match complex ones; still the baseline everyone extends.

ByteTrack 🧪 Research · 2021 ifzhang/ByteTrack

How: two association passes, high-score then low-score boxes; YOLOX detector; 30-frame track buffer.

Why this method: low-score boxes are often occluded people; using them raised IDF1 by 1–10 points across 9 trackers.

AB3DMOT for lidar 🧪 Research · 2019 Weng et al.

How: 3D Kalman filter + Hungarian on 3D boxes, with 3D tracking metrics.

Why this method: lidar gives metric 3D boxes, so a simple filter is strong; 207.4 FPS made it the standard 3D baseline.

STT at Waymo 🚗 AV Jing et al., ICRA 2024

How: a transformer consumes appearance, geometry and motion over long histories and is trained for association and state estimation jointly.

Why this method: planners need accurate velocity and acceleration, not just correct IDs; heuristics for both were brittle.

🤯 Fun fact · a Latin manuscript beat Kuhn by 65 years

Harold Kuhn published the Hungarian method in 1955 and named it after the Hungarian mathematicians Dénes Kőnig and Jenő Egerváry, whose work it built on. In 2006 it came out that Carl Gustav Jacobi had already solved the assignment problem in the 19th century; his solution was published after his death, in 1890, in Latin (Wikipedia).

Check yourself

1. Over a clip, GT = 200 object-frames. Your tracker has 20 misses, 10 false positives and 2 ID switches. What's its MOTA?
MOTA = 1 − (20 + 10 + 2) ÷ 200 = 1 − 0.16 = 0.84. The 2 switches cost only 1 point, which is why IDF1 and HOTA are reported alongside it.
2. In a crowded crosswalk your tracker keeps giving pedestrians new IDs right after they pass behind each other. Detections of the hidden person score around 0.3; your threshold is 0.6 and tracks die after 2 missed frames. Best first fix?
The occluded person's boxes exist but are thrown away, and the track dies too soon. ByteTrack's second pass uses them only to continue existing tracks, so clutter can't spawn new ones. Lowering the threshold globally would create ghost tracks.
3. Costs: track A to detections (a, b) = (1, 4); track B = (2, 8). What do greedy and Hungarian pick, and their totals?
Greedy grabs the cheapest pair (A–a = 1) and is forced into B–b = 8. The optimum gives A the second-best option so B can take its much better one: 4 + 2 = 6.
📝 TL;DR
  • Tracking-by-detection: predict each track (constant-velocity Kalman), associate (cost matrix + Hungarian, gated), update, then birth/death rules.
  • Greedy can be far from optimal (3.7 m vs 2.0 m); Hungarian is optimal in O(n³). SORT = Kalman + Hungarian at 260 Hz; DeepSORT adds appearance (45% fewer ID switches).
  • ByteTrack: a second pass matches leftover tracks to low-score boxes (τ = 0.6, IoU ≥ 0.2, 30-frame buffer): 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, 63.1 HOTA at 30 FPS; ID switches 291 → 159 vs SORT.
  • MOTA = 1 − (FN + FP + IDSW) ÷ GT; IDF1 and HOTA weight identity much more.
  • Every knob trades failures: gate, max age, thresholds, q. Tesla puts kinematics in networks and hindsight auto-labels; Waymo researches learned association (STT); Skydio's Shadow reacquires subjects after brief occlusion.
