Phase 6 · Lesson 1

3D object detection from lidar: PointNet → PointPillars

A lidar hands you a bag of 100,000 dots in no particular order. Image networks want a tidy grid. This lesson is the story of how people turned that bag into boxes around every car and pedestrian, fast enough to run 60 times a second.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Point cloud: a lidar fires laser pulses and times their echoes; each echo becomes one 3D point (x, y, z), often with an intensity. Dense up close, sparse far away (Lesson 2.3).
  • Bird's-eye view (BEV): the scene seen from straight above, x forward and y left, like a map. Cars drive on the ground, so most of what a planner needs lives in this flat picture.
  • A convolutional neural network (CNN) slides small learned filters over a regular grid of pixels. You don't need the internals here: the Deep Learning course, Phase 3 (CNNs) has them.
  • A 3D box for an object: center (x, y, z), size (length, width, height) and heading (yaw). Seven numbers per car.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Write down the lidar points of one parked car as a list: 300 rows of (x, y, z). Now shuffle the rows. Is it still the same car? Of course. But feed both lists to a normal neural network and it sees two completely different inputs, because row 1 is now row 217.

And here's the second trap. Lay a fine grid over the scene so you can use image tricks: almost every cell is empty. In the PointPillars paper, a typical 64-beam lidar sweep at 0.16 m cells fills only 6,000 to 9,000 columns of the grid, about 97% empty. So: how do you build a detector that doesn't care about order, doesn't waste effort on empty air, and still runs in real time?

In 2018 a team at nuTonomy (an Aptiv company) answered with PointPillars: 62 detections per second, 2–4× faster than the methods it beat, and a slimmer version at 105 Hz (Lang et al.). By the end of this lesson you'll have built its first step with your own hands.

🧩 Predict: the far edge of a lidar sweep is 60 m away. A pedestrian there is about 0.6 m wide. With a typical 0.2° gap between laser beams, roughly how many beams across hit them: about 3, about 30, or about 300?
About 3. At 60 m, 0.2° spans 60 × 0.0035 ≈ 0.21 m, so 0.6 m of person catches about 3 beams across (times a handful of vertical rings). The same person at 6 m catches about 30. That 10× swing in density with distance is why lidar detectors must handle both crowded and nearly empty regions in one scan.

Why a point cloud is awkward for a CNN

Numbers first. Here is what one sweep looks like compared with a camera image:

Camera imageLidar sweep
StructureRegular grid: pixel (row 412, col 900) always has neighboursA list of points; row order means nothing
Fill100% of pixels have a value~97% of BEV cells at 0.16 m are empty (PointPillars, 64-beam lidar)
DensitySame everywhereFalls off roughly with distance squared
What it measures wellColor, texture, classExact range and shape in meters

Three properties cause the trouble: the points are unordered (any shuffle is the same scene), sparse (most of space is air) and uneven (a hundred points on a near car, five on a far pedestrian). People tried three workarounds before PointNet: project the cloud into images (a range image, or a BEV picture with hand-made features like "max height in this cell"), chop it into a dense 3D voxel grid and run 3D convolutions (slow, mostly empty), or hand-craft features per object. Each threw away information or time.

PointNet: one tiny network per point, then take the max

PointNet (Qi et al., Stanford, 2017) had a beautifully simple idea. If order mustn't matter, only use operations that ignore order:

  1. Run the same small network on every point separately. It's a multi-layer perceptron (MLP): a few layers of "multiply by weights, add, clip negatives to zero". PointNet's grows each 3-number point into 64, then 1,024 numbers, its features.
  2. Max-pool: for each of the 1,024 feature slots, keep the largest value over all points. That gives one 1,024-number summary of the whole cloud, the global feature.
  3. A final small MLP turns that summary into a class (car? chair?) or, with extra pieces, a box.

The max of a set doesn't care what order you list it in: max(3, 9, 1) = max(1, 3, 9) = 9. That's the whole trick. The paper calls max a symmetric function, and proves a surprising bonus: the summary only depends on a small critical set of points (at most one per feature slot). Lose other points and nothing changes. Try it.

🏆 Challenge: delete 5 or more points while the max-pooled feature stays exactly the same. Which points can you never remove? (Shuffle first and notice which number changes.)

What you should find: shuffling scrambles the concatenated version but never the max. And only the 3 points that hold a column's maximum matter; the other 5 can vanish. That's why PointNet shrugs off missing points, and also its weakness: the global max ignores local structure (how neighbouring points relate). PointNet++ (2017) fixed that by running little PointNets on neighbourhoods, a pattern you're about to see again inside PointPillars.

From one object to a whole street: voxels, then pillars

PointNet classifies one object's points. A street has 100,000 points and dozens of objects. You need to know where things are, which is exactly what grids are good at. The next ideas mix the two.

Method (year)Turn points into…ThenSpeed reported
VoxelNet (Apple, 2017)3D cubes (voxels); a small PointNet-style “voxel feature encoder” per non-empty cube3D convolutions, then a region proposal network225 ms per sweep (4.4 Hz), too slow to deploy
SECOND (2018)VoxelsSparse 3D convolutions that skip empty cubes20 Hz
PointPillars (nuTonomy, 2018)Tall columns (pillars) with no vertical split; one PointNet per pillarScatter into a 2D pseudo-image, then an ordinary fast 2D CNN62 Hz; slimmer version 105 Hz
points → bin by (x, y) into pillars of 0.16 m → keep up to N = 100 points per pillar, up to P = 12,000 pillars → each point gets 9 numbers (x, y, z, intensity, offsets to the pillar's mean and center) → tensor P × N × 9 → shared MLP + max per pillar → 64 numbers per pillar → scatter back to the grid → pseudo-image 64 × H × W → 2D CNN → boxes Group points into columns, summarize each column with a tiny PointNet, paint the summaries back onto a flat grid, and now it's “an image”: every 2D detection trick applies, at 2D speed.

Why pillars and not cubes? A car's height is small compared with its length, and objects rarely stack on top of each other on a road. Dropping the vertical split loses little and turns slow 3D convolutions into fast 2D ones. The cost is a hard cap: a pillar with more than N points gets randomly sampled down, and anything beyond P pillars is dropped. Those two numbers, and the cell size, are your memory dials.

✏️ Worked example: how big is the grid?

Take a detection area 64 m ahead by 36 m wide, with 0.16 m pillars.

  1. Grid: 64 ÷ 0.16 = 400 cells by 36 ÷ 0.16 = 225 cells = 90,000 pillars possible.
  2. A typical sweep fills about 2,000 of them, so 97.8% are empty.
  3. The pseudo-image stores 64 float32 numbers (4 bytes each) per cell: 90,000 × 64 × 4 = 23 MB, dense, mostly zeros.
  4. The pillar tensor stores the points: 2,000 pillars × 100 points × 9 numbers × 4 bytes = 7.2 MB.
  5. Double the cell size to 0.32 m and the grid shrinks 4× (to 22,500 cells): the pseudo-image drops to about 5.8 MB. But each pillar now holds about 4× as many points, so more of them overflow the cap of N.

That's the whole trade in one example: finer cells = more detail and more memory; coarser cells = cheaper, but crowded pillars and lost shapes. (Real grids are bigger: OpenPCDet's KITTI config uses 0.16 m over 69.12 m × 79.36 m, a 432 × 496 grid.)

🏆 Challenge: your car's chip has a 15 MB budget for this stage. Fit under it while dropping at most 10% of the points and keeping the pedestrian and the pole in separate pillar blobs (no pedestrian pillar touching a pole pillar). The paper's defaults (0.16 m, about 100) are way over budget. Memory here is float32, 64 channels per cell, illustrative of the real cost.

What you should find: pillar size is the big dial, because the pseudo-image grows with 1/size². Coarsen to about 0.3 m and memory falls under budget, but now the near car's pillars overflow and you drop too many points, so raise N; go past about 0.4 m and the pedestrian's pillars merge into the pole's. The winning zone is narrow, which is exactly the tuning real teams do per sensor and per chip.

💡 Big idea Every lidar detector answers one question: how do I turn an unordered, sparse bag of points into something regular enough for fast learned filters? PointNet's answer is “shared MLP + max” (order-free). Voxel methods answer “a 3D grid”. PointPillars combines them: PointNet inside each column, a 2D grid across columns. The grid buys speed; the PointNet keeps the details the grid would blur.
🧠 Analogy: counting votes by district

You have a million unsorted ballots. Reading them in a random order gives the same result, so you never rely on order (PointNet). But to see where people voted what, you sort ballots into districts first, summarize each district in one line, and draw a map of the summaries (pillars and the pseudo-image). Big districts are cheap to map but blur neighbourhoods; tiny ones show detail but the map gets huge, and the busiest polling station still has to be sampled when it overflows.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Lidar gives exact 3D shape, but as an unordered, ~97%-empty, unevenly dense list. Image CNNs, which had just become very good at detection, need a full regular grid. Hand-made rules (“a cluster of points about 4.5 m long is a car”) broke on trucks, people, and anything partly hidden.
  2. What people tried. Project to images (range images, BEV maps with hand-made height and density features): fast, but the hand-made features lose shape. Dense 3D voxels with 3D convolutions (VoxelNet): learns its own features, but 225 ms per sweep. Sparse 3D convolutions (SECOND): 20 Hz, better, still 3D. PointNet on raw points: order-free and elegant, but one global max can't localize dozens of objects in a street.
  3. Why this works. PointPillars learns its features (PointNet per pillar), then hands a flat grid to a mature 2D CNN. Tiny numbers: a 64 m × 36 m area at 0.16 m is 90,000 cells, but only about 2,000 hold points, so the expensive learned step runs on 2,000 pillars, not 90,000 cubes per height layer. Result: 62 Hz, and more accurate than the slower methods on the KITTI benchmark.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Throwing away vertical structure hurts stacked or overhanging things (a branch above a car). The caps P and N randomly drop points in dense areas. Far objects have a handful of points and few pillars, so small, distant pedestrians are the classic miss, and pedestrians get confused with poles and tree trunks (the paper's own failure analysis). It also needs lots of labeled 3D boxes.
  5. What came next. CenterPoint (2020) keeps a BEV grid but predicts object centers as peaks in a heat map instead of matching thousands of pre-shaped “anchor” boxes, which handles any heading and adds velocity, and it makes tracking a simple nearest-center match. Range-image detectors (Waymo's RSN) target long range; multi-frame and offline detectors use whole sequences (Lesson 6.7); camera BEV methods do the same job without lidar (Lesson 6.2); occupancy grids drop boxes entirely (Lesson 6.3).

CenterPoint: find the middle, then the rest

Box-based detectors like PointPillars place pre-shaped anchor boxes at every grid cell (PointPillars uses two headings per anchor, 0° and 90°) and learn to nudge them. A car parked at 45° doesn't fit either anchor well. CenterPoint (Yin, Zhou, Krähenbühl, 2020) asks a simpler question per BEV cell: how likely is an object's center right here? The peaks of that heat map are the objects; from each peak it reads off size, heading and velocity. A second stage refines each box with point features. It reached 65.5 NDS on nuScenes (NDS, the nuScenes detection score, blends accuracy of position, size, heading, velocity and class into one 0–100 number) and ranked first among lidar-only entries on the Waymo Open Dataset when published. Bonus: since it predicts velocity, tracking becomes “match each center to the nearest predicted center from the last frame”, a greedy closest-point match (Lesson 6.5).

Scoring a box: 3D IoU and BEV IoU

How do you say a predicted box is “right”? The standard yardstick is IoU, intersection over union: the overlap volume divided by the combined volume. 1 means perfect, 0 means no overlap. (Full treatment of IoU in 2D: the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (detection basics, IoU, NMS).)

  • 3D IoU uses volumes, so it also punishes a wrong height or z position. KITTI counts a car as detected at 3D IoU ≥ 0.7.
  • BEV IoU drops height and compares the footprints seen from above: rotated rectangles. Cheaper, and it's what matters most for not hitting things.
✏️ Worked example: BEV IoU of two boxes

Two 2 m × 2 m squares, the second shifted 1 m forward. Overlap = 1 m × 2 m = 2 m². Union = 4 + 4 − 2 = 6 m². IoU = 2 ÷ 6 = 0.33. Rotate the second one by 45° about the same center instead: the overlap is an octagon of about 3.31 m², union ≈ 4.69 m², IoU ≈ 0.71. For rotated rectangles you clip one polygon against the other (the Sutherland–Hodgman algorithm) and measure the area of what's left; the lab does exactly that.

Non-maximum suppression: one box per object

A detector fires several overlapping boxes on the same car. Non-maximum suppression (NMS) cleans up: sort boxes by score; keep the best; delete every other box whose IoU with it is above a threshold; repeat with the next survivor. PointPillars uses NMS at 0.5 IoU. The threshold is a bet: too low and you delete a real neighbour; too high and duplicates survive.

🧩 Predict before you play: in a tight parking lot two cars sit side by side, 30 cm apart. Someone sets the NMS threshold to a strict 0.1 “to be safe”. What happens?
A whole car disappears. The detector's boxes are a bit wider than the cars, so the best box on car A overlaps the best box on car B by an IoU of about 0.2. With a threshold of 0.1 that counts as a “duplicate”, and NMS deletes the second car. A strict threshold isn't safe; it's a different failure. Check it in the playground.

🏆 Challenge: pick an NMS threshold that keeps both cars (one surviving box with IoU ≥ 0.5 to each truth) and removes every duplicate. Then find both edges of the winning window: what overlap number sets each edge?

The window's lower edge is the overlap between the two neighbouring cars' best boxes (about 0.19); the upper edge is the overlap of the weakest-overlapping duplicate (about 0.51). Every crowded scene has its own window, which is why detectors in parking lots and crowds struggle, and why CenterPoint-style “one peak per object” heads need less NMS fiddling. Also notice: the highest-scoring box is not the best-fitting one on car A (look at its IoU with the truth). Scores and localization quality are different things.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

OpenPCDet and MMDetection3D are the two big open-source toolboxes; both ship PointPillars, SECOND, CenterPoint and friends with configs for KITTI, nuScenes and the Waymo Open Dataset. Open3D loads and views point clouds. First, pillarization in plain NumPy (the lab grows this):

import numpy as np

def pillarize(pts, res=0.16, x_range=(0, 64), y_range=(-18, 18), max_pts=100):
    """pts: (M, 3+) array of x, y, z, ... → dict pillar_id -> (≤ max_pts, D) array"""
    keep = (pts[:, 0] >= x_range[0]) & (pts[:, 0] < x_range[1]) & (pts[:, 1] >= y_range[0]) & (pts[:, 1] < y_range[1])
    pts = pts[keep]
    ix = ((pts[:, 0] - x_range[0]) // res).astype(int)
    iy = ((pts[:, 1] - y_range[0]) // res).astype(int)
    nx = int(np.ceil((x_range[1] - x_range[0]) / res))
    ids = ix + nx * iy                                   # one integer per pillar
    order = np.argsort(ids, kind="stable")
    uniq, start = np.unique(ids[order], return_index=True)
    groups = np.split(pts[order], start[1:])
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    return {int(u): (g if len(g) <= max_pts else g[rng.choice(len(g), max_pts, replace=False)])
            for u, g in zip(uniq, groups)}

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Lidar is central to the Waymo Driver, and its research team publishes lidar detectors. RSN, Range Sparse Net (CVPR 2021) works on the lidar's native range image to pick out foreground points, then runs sparse convolutions only on those, aimed at real-time detection at the longer ranges newer lidars reach. 3D Auto Labeling (CVPR 2021) runs a big detector offline over whole sequences to make labels as good as humans' (Lesson 6.7). Its public Waymo Open Dataset has 12.6 million 3D box labels with tracking IDs on lidar data, the benchmark CenterPoint topped. What runs in today's car isn't published; its Dec 2025 post describes a sensor-fusion encoder over camera, lidar and radar (Lesson 6.6).

Sources: RSN, 3D Auto Labeling, Waymo Open Dataset, Waymo research

🚘 Tesla

No lidar at all. In its public talks Tesla describes networks that turn its eight cameras into a 3D “vector space” of objects with position, orientation, depth and velocity (AI Day 2021), and an occupancy network that predicts which 3D voxels are filled (CVPR 2022, AI Day 2022). The detection problem is the same as in this lesson; the input is pixels. In the CVPR 2022 talk Tesla said what it showed used no radar, lidar, ultrasonics or HD maps.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, CVPR'22 WAD keynote (Elluswamy)

🛸 Drones

Spinning automotive lidars are heavy and power-hungry for a small drone, so consumer and enterprise drones mostly see with cameras. Skydio's X10 uses six navigation cameras for 360° coverage and an onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin GPU, and its Shadow mode tracks people and vehicles. Skydio doesn't publish its network architectures. Lidar does fly on larger mapping and inspection drones, where point clouds are usually processed after the flight.

Sources: Skydio X10, Skydio autonomy

Why they differ: Waymo buys lidar so range and shape are measured, not guessed, and spends its learning on what lidar alone can't tell (class, intent). Tesla bets that cameras plus fleet-scale data can learn the geometry, saving sensor cost on millions of cars. A drone's weight and power budget rule out big lidars, so it leans on cameras and an efficient onboard GPU.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you detect cars in a lidar point cloud, and what breaks?” A strong answer:

  • Names the three properties: unordered, sparse, uneven density, and why a plain CNN can't eat a point list.
  • Explains PointNet's shared MLP + max pool as permutation invariance, and PointPillars as “PointNet per column, then a 2D CNN on a pseudo-image”, with the knobs: cell size, P, N.
  • Knows BEV vs 3D IoU, NMS and its threshold failure in crowds, and that CenterPoint predicts centers instead of anchors.
  • Talks about failure modes and data: far pedestrians with few points, poles vs people, rain and exhaust returns, the long tail of odd vehicles, and evaluating per distance band, not just one average number.
🏭 In the real world
PointPillars at nuTonomy 🧪 Research · 2018 Lang et al.

How: PointNet per pillar → pseudo-image → 2D CNN; final kernels built with NVIDIA TensorRT.

Why this method: a self-driving company needed a lidar detector that was both accurate and real-time on car hardware: 62 Hz, 2–4× faster than the methods it beat on KITTI.

CenterPoint 🧪 Research · 2020 Yin, Zhou, Krähenbühl

How: BEV heat map of object centers, regress size, heading and velocity, refine with point features; greedy closest-center tracking.

Why this method: rotated objects don't fit fixed anchors; points don't have orientations. 65.5 NDS on nuScenes and first among lidar-only Waymo entries at publication.

RSN at Waymo 🚗 AV Sun et al., CVPR 2021

How: cheap 2D convolutions on the dense range image select foreground points; sparse convolutions run only on those.

Why this method: long-range lidar means huge BEV grids; skipping background points keeps detection real-time at range.

OpenPCDet and MMDetection3D 🧰 Open source OpenPCDet · MMDetection3D

How: shared data loaders, voxelizers and heads for dozens of lidar detectors and datasets.

Why this method: almost every research comparison and many startups' first detector start from these configs.

🤯 Fun fact · the same team built the benchmark

Three of PointPillars' authors, Alex Lang, Sourabh Vora and Holger Caesar, are also authors of nuScenes, the first public dataset with a full self-driving sensor suite (6 cameras, 5 radars, 1 lidar) (Caesar et al.). Building the detector and the yardstick in the same building turns out to be a great way to find out what the yardstick misses.

Check yourself

1. A 64 m × 40 m area is split into 0.16 m pillars, and a sweep fills 3,000 of them. About what fraction of pillars is empty?
64 ÷ 0.16 = 400 and 40 ÷ 0.16 = 250, so 100,000 pillars. 3,000 filled means 97,000 empty: 97%, the same sparsity the PointPillars paper reports for a 64-beam lidar.
2. After deployment, your detector keeps missing one of two cars parked side by side, but only in tight parking lots. The NMS threshold is 0.1. Best first fix?
NMS deletes any box overlapping a better one by more than the threshold. Adjacent cars' boxes often overlap by 0.1–0.2, so 0.1 suppresses one of them. Raise the threshold into the window between the neighbour overlap and the duplicate overlap, as in Playground 3.
3. Why does PointNet use a max over points instead of concatenating all the per-point features into one long vector?
A point cloud is a set: shuffling it doesn't change the scene. Concatenation changes when the order changes; max (a symmetric function) doesn't. It also handles any number of points, and the result depends only on a small critical set.
📝 TL;DR
  • Point clouds are unordered, ~97% empty in a BEV grid, and much denser near than far: awkward for CNNs.
  • PointNet: the same MLP on every point, then max-pool → order-free; only a small critical set of points matters.
  • VoxelNet (3D voxels, 225 ms) → SECOND (sparse 3D, 20 Hz) → PointPillars (PointNet per column → 2D pseudo-image → 2D CNN, 62 Hz).
  • Knobs: cell size (memory ∝ 1/size²), points per pillar N, pillars P. Small far objects and poles vs people are the classic failures.
  • CenterPoint predicts center heat-map peaks plus size, heading and velocity. Score boxes with 3D or BEV IoU; clean up with NMS, whose threshold must sit between neighbour overlap and duplicate overlap.
