Phase 6 · Lesson 6

Early vs late fusion & monocular depth

A camera knows what things are but has to guess how far. A lidar knows exactly how far but barely knows what. Radar knows how fast. Where you combine them, early, in the middle or at the end, decides what survives when one of them is blinded. And Tesla's bet is that a network can learn the missing depth from pictures alone.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The pinhole model: an object of height H at distance Z appears f·H/Z pixels tall (Lesson 2.1). Height and distance are tangled together.
  • Lidar points get sparse with distance (about 1/Z²) and dark objects return fewer of them (Lesson 2.3). Radar measures range and radial speed directly through the Doppler effect, in rain and fog (Lesson 2.4).
  • Extrinsic calibration: the exact pose of each sensor on the car; time sync: every measurement stamped on one clock (Lesson 2.7).
  • BEV, the top-down grid every sensor can be projected into (Lesson 6.2), and painting points with class scores (PointPainting, below).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A photo shows a car exactly 120 pixels tall. Is it a real car 12.5 m away or a toy car 2.5 m away? Both give exactly the same picture (do the arithmetic: 1,000 × 1.5 ÷ 12.5 = 1,000 × 0.3 ÷ 2.5 = 120). So how can any network get depth from one image?

And yet in June 2021, Andrej Karpathy, then Tesla's head of AI, told the CVPR autonomous-driving workshop that Tesla had started shipping cars with no radar three weeks earlier, quoting Elon Musk: when radar and vision disagree, which one do you believe? Vision “has much more precision, so better to double down on vision than do sensor fusion” (CVPR'21 WAD keynote). Waymo, meanwhile, describes fusing camera, lidar and radar “over time” in its driver (Waymo, Dec 2025). This lesson gives you the tools to judge both bets.

Fusion is how you combine their strengths. The big design decision is when: at the raw data, at learned features, or at the final objects.

BEVFusion's authors put the early-fusion problem crisply: projecting camera features onto lidar points “throws away the semantic density of camera features”, because a camera image has far more pixels than there are lidar points. Fusing in BEV keeps both. The deep-learning internals are in the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (3D vision).

🧩 Predict: a black car 30 m ahead returns only 2 lidar points (too few for the lidar detector, which needs 5), and the camera sees it clearly but guesses its distance 9% short. Which fusion level gets both its class and its range right?
Feature-level. Late fusion only gets the camera's object (right class, wrong range), because the lidar never produced an object to match. Early fusion needs at least ~3 points to paint and detect. Feature-level fusion adds weak lidar evidence (2 points, but exact range) to strong camera evidence in the same BEV cell, and gets both. Combining uncertain evidence is what middle fusion is for. What you should find: late fusion detects everything in clear weather but often with the wrong range or no class, because each sensor's doubts are thrown away before combining. Early fusion is precise until calibration drifts: a 1° error moves a painted point 0.8 m sideways at 45 m, off a cyclist entirely. Feature fusion combines weak evidence and tolerates small misalignment, which is why it leads the benchmarks; its cost is that it's one big learned system you must validate as a whole.

💡 Big idea The earlier you fuse, the more information survives, and the more you depend on perfect alignment and on every sensor being healthy. The later you fuse, the more independent and debuggable each pipeline is, but each one's uncertainty is thrown away before the others can help. Middle (BEV feature) fusion is the modern compromise: learned alignment, combined weak evidence, and training with sensors randomly switched off so the network learns to cope when one is blinded.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Each sensor fails differently: cameras guess depth and go blind in glare; lidar gets sparse with range and darkness, and can't read a traffic light; radar sees speed but blurs shape. One sensor alone has holes a car can drive into.
  2. What people tried. Late fusion (match each sensor's boxes): modular and robust to one sensor dying, but an object neither pipeline was sure of disappears, and attributes are whichever sensor reported them. Early fusion (paint points, project features): rich, but hostage to calibration and timing; a 1° error is 0.8 m at 45 m.
  3. Why this works. Fusing learned features in a shared BEV grid lets weak evidence add up: 2 lidar points (exact range) plus a confident camera class gives a correct object neither sensor could report alone. The network learns small misalignments, and training with sensors randomly dropped (as Waymo's CramNet does with “sensor modality dropout”) keeps it working when one goes dark.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. More sensors cost money, power and calibration effort; every sensor must stay calibrated and synchronized. Joint networks are large and must be validated as a whole; disagreement between sensors (radar sees a car under a bridge, vision doesn't) must be resolved somehow, which is exactly the argument Tesla gave for dropping radar.
  5. What came next. Temporal fusion over many frames (Waymo's Sensor Fusion Encoder fuses “over time”), camera–radar fusion for cheaper cars (CramNet), foundation-model encoders, and on the other side, vision-only stacks that learn depth and velocity from enormous auto-labeled video datasets (Tesla) and monocular depth models trained on tens of millions of images (Depth Anything).

Monocular depth: learning to guess distance

Back to the puzzle. Geometry alone can't separate a toy car at 2.5 m from a real one at 12.5 m: that's scale ambiguity. A network that predicts depth from one image (monocular depth) gets around it with learned cues, each of which can fail:

  • Size priors: cars are about 1.5 m tall, people about 1.7 m. Works until the object isn't typical (a toy, a truck, a child).
  • Ground contact: where the object touches the ground, plus the known camera height, gives depth, exactly IPM from Lesson 6.2. Works until the ground isn't flat (a hill crest).
  • Context and texture: road texture gets finer with distance, lanes converge, things far away are hazier and partly hidden by nearer ones.
  • Motion: as the car moves, near things slide across the image faster than far things (motion parallax), which is why video helps.
image height hpx = f · H ÷ Z   ⇒   (H, Z) and (k·H, k·Z) give the same image for any k One equation, two unknowns. Every monocular depth method breaks the tie with a prior: an assumed size, an assumed ground, or patterns learned from millions of images.

MiDaS and Depth Anything: depth models trained on everything

Two research lines made monocular depth practical:

  • MiDaS (Ranftl et al., 2019) trained on a mix of datasets, including 3D films, with a loss that's invariant to depth range and scale. It predicts relative depth (this is twice as far as that) rather than meters, which sidesteps the scale ambiguity instead of solving it.
  • Depth Anything (2024) built a “data engine” that auto-annotated about 62 million unlabeled images with a teacher model, then trained a student on them; fine-tuned on metric datasets (NYUv2 indoors, KITTI driving) it outputs meters. Depth Anything V2 replaced labeled real images with synthetic ones, scaled the teacher, and released models from 25 million to 1.3 billion parameters.

Relative depth is fine for many robotics tasks; metric depth (meters) is what a car's planner needs, and it's only as good as the training data's cameras and scenes match yours. Learned depth also degrades with distance: errors of several percent of range are typical. The self-supervised tricks behind these models are in the Deep Learning course, Phase 9 (self-supervised learning).

🎮 Playground 3 · Depth error vs range: stereo, mono, lidar

How the 1σ depth error grows with distance. Stereo (two cameras a baseline B apart) has error ≈ Z² · σd ÷ (f · B): quadruples when distance doubles. The learned mono curve is a stand-in at 8% of range. Lidar is a few centimeters at any range it gets returns from.

✏️ Worked example: where depth error comes from
  1. Stereo, f = 1,000 px, B = 0.3 m, σd = 0.25 px: at 20 m, error = 20² × 0.25 ÷ (1,000 × 0.3) = 100 ÷ 300 = 0.33 m; at 60 m, 3,600 × 0.25 ÷ 300 = 3.0 m. Nine times worse at three times the distance.
  2. Monocular with a size prior: a car that's really 1.6 m tall, assumed 1.5 m: every distance is off by 1.5 ÷ 1.6, about 6%: 3.8 m at 60 m, no matter how sharp the image.
  3. Calibration for fusion: 1° of extrinsic error moves a projected point by tan(1°) × Z ≈ 0.0175 × 45 m = 0.79 m at 45 m. More than a cyclist's half-width.
🧠 Analogy: three witnesses at a trial

One witness had great eyesight but no sense of distance (camera), one measured everything with a tape but was colour-blind (lidar), one only noticed how fast things moved (radar). Late fusion reads their written statements and picks bits from each. Early fusion makes them watch the same tape together, but if their clocks or seats were mismatched they argue about different moments. Feature fusion lets the jury hear everyone's hunches before anyone writes a statement. The vision-only bet is to train one witness so well, on so many cases, that you no longer need the others.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Painting lidar points (early fusion) is a projection plus an array lookup. Monocular depth models are one call in Hugging Face Transformers. BEVFusion (MIT) and MMDetection3D have feature-level fusion models.

import numpy as np

def paint_points(pts_lidar, seg_scores, K, T_cam_from_lidar):
    """PointPainting: append the image segmentation scores at each lidar point's pixel.
    pts_lidar (N, 3) · seg_scores (H, W, C) class probabilities · K 3×3 · T 4×4 lidar→camera"""
    P = np.c_[pts_lidar, np.ones(len(pts_lidar))] @ T_cam_from_lidar.T     # into the camera frame (z forward)
    front = P[:, 2] > 0.5
    uv = (P[front, :3] @ K.T)
    uv = (uv[:, :2] / uv[:, 2:]).round().astype(int)                          # pixel coordinates
    H, W, C = seg_scores.shape
    inside = (uv[:, 0] >= 0) & (uv[:, 0] < W) & (uv[:, 1] >= 0) & (uv[:, 1] < H)
    scores = np.zeros((len(pts_lidar), C))
    idx = np.flatnonzero(front)[inside]
    scores[idx] = seg_scores[uv[inside, 1], uv[inside, 0]]
    return np.c_[pts_lidar, scores]          # (N, 3 + C): feed to any lidar detector

# Monocular depth with Depth Anything V2 (relative depth; metric checkpoints also exist)
from transformers import pipeline
from PIL import Image
pipe = pipeline(task="depth-estimation", model="depth-anything/Depth-Anything-V2-Small-hf")

Painting is only as good as T_cam_from_lidar and the timestamps: project a few frames and look before trusting it (Lesson 2.7). Relative depth maps need a scale (from lidar, a known camera height, or a metric model) before a planner can use them.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Vision only, by choice. Radar was removed from Model 3 and Model Y in North America from May 2021. At CVPR 2021 Karpathy argued radar's depth and velocity can be learned from video, and that working on “the radar stack and the sensor fusion stack” was a distraction; he showed vision depth and velocity tracking a lead car. At CVPR 2022 Tesla said its system used eight cameras and no radar, lidar, ultrasonics or HD maps. Depth comes from networks trained on auto-labeled video (Lessons 6.2, 6.3, 6.7).

Sources: CVPR'21 WAD keynote, Tesla Vision, CVPR'22 keynote

🚕 Waymo

Multi-sensor by design. Its 6th-generation driver (rider-only from February 2026) pairs cameras with lidar and an imaging radar improved for rain and snow; its Dec 2025 post describes a Sensor Fusion Encoder that “fuses camera, lidar, and radar inputs over time”. Its research covers feature fusion of lidar and camera over time (4D-Net, better on distant objects) and camera–radar fusion that keeps working when a sensor fails (CramNet).

Sources: Waymo, Dec 2025, Waymo, Feb 2026, 4D-Net, CramNet

🛸 Drones

Weight decides. Skydio's X10 uses six navigation cameras for 360° vision plus an onboard Jetson Orin; its ThermalBoost mode fuses color and thermal imagery into one view to reveal people and vehicles. Depth for obstacle avoidance comes from its multi-camera vision. Flight controllers like PX4 fuse IMU, GNSS, barometer and optical flow in an EKF (Lesson 3.3): classic state fusion rather than perception fusion.

Sources: Skydio X10, PX4 EKF

Why they differ: Waymo runs a fleet of purpose-built robotaxis where the safety case comes first and sensor cost is spread over many rides, so it buys redundancy: when one sensor is blinded another still measures. Tesla sells millions of consumer cars where every dollar and every disagreement between sensors matters, and bets that data plus learning closes the depth gap. A drone can't carry the weight or power of lidar and radar arrays, so it fuses cameras with each other and with thermal.

  • Defines early / feature / late with an example of each (PointPainting, BEVFusion, box-level matching in a tracker).
  • Walks through failure cases with numbers: 1° calibration error = 0.8 m at 45 m; a dark car with 2 lidar points; sun glare on one camera.
  • Mentions robustness engineering: modality dropout in training, health monitoring of each sensor, a late-fusion safety layer that still works if the learned fusion misbehaves.
  • Explains monocular depth: scale ambiguity, the cues networks learn, relative vs metric depth, and why error grows with range.
🏭 In the real world
PointPainting 🧪 Research · 2019 Vora et al.

How: project lidar points into an image segmentation output and append its class scores to each point; run any lidar detector.

Why this method: at the time lidar-only detectors beat fusion methods on benchmarks; painting improved PointRCNN, VoxelNet and PointPillars with almost no change to them.

BEVFusion 🧪 Research · 2022 Liu et al. (MIT) · code

How: camera features lifted to BEV (Lesson 6.2) and lidar features in BEV are concatenated and fused; an optimized BEV pooling cut that step's latency by more than 40×.

Why this method: one shared space keeps both geometry and camera semantics, and supports detection and map segmentation alike.

Waymo's sensor fusion 🚗 AV Waymo, Dec 2025

How: a Sensor Fusion Encoder fuses camera, lidar and radar over time into objects, semantics and embeddings, alongside a driving vision-language model for harder reasoning.

Why this method: each sensor covers the others' blind spots; fusing over time adds motion cues.

Depth Anything V2 🧰 Open source GitHub

How: a large teacher trained on synthetic labeled images pseudo-labels real images; students from 25M to 1.3B parameters; metric fine-tunes for indoor and outdoor scenes.

Why this method: real depth labels are scarce and noisy; scale comes from data and distillation.

🤯 Fun fact · learning depth from 3D movies

MiDaS added an unusual training source: 3D films. The left and right views of a stereo movie encode depth up to an unknown scale and shift, exactly what a scale-invariant loss can learn from (Ranftl et al.). Hollywood's stereo rigs became a depth sensor.

Check yourself

1. A stereo camera has f = 800 px, baseline 0.2 m and disparity noise 0.25 px. What's its 1σ depth error at 40 m?
Error ≈ Z² · σd ÷ (f · B) = 1,600 × 0.25 ÷ (800 × 0.2) = 400 ÷ 160 = 2.5 m. At 80 m it would be 10 m: stereo depth degrades with distance squared.
2. After a minor fender-bender, your early-fusion (PointPainting) detector starts missing cyclists beyond 40 m, while lidar-only detection of cars is fine. Most likely cause?
Calibration errors grow with distance and hurt narrow objects first. Re-calibrate (and monitor calibration online); feature-level fusion with learned alignment is more tolerant of small shifts.
3. A monocular depth network says a car is 20 m away. It's actually a 4 m-tall delivery truck at 53 m. Which cue most likely misled it?
Scale ambiguity: f·H/Z is the same for (1.5 m, 20 m) and (4 m, 53 m). If the network leans on a car-sized prior it gets the scale wrong; ground contact or class recognition (“that's a truck”) would fix it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Cameras give class, lidar gives range, radar gives speed in bad weather; each fails differently.
  • Early fusion (PointPainting) is rich but needs perfect calibration and a healthy camera; late fusion is modular but discards uncertain evidence; feature (BEV) fusion combines weak evidence and can be trained to survive a missing sensor.
  • Calibration matters with distance: 1° ≈ 0.8 m at 45 m. Stereo error ≈ Z²σd/(fB).
  • Monocular depth is ambiguous in scale (f·H/Z); networks use size priors, ground contact, context and motion, each with failure cases. MiDaS learns relative depth; Depth Anything scales with 62M auto-labeled images.
  • Tesla: vision only since 2021, depth and velocity learned from video. Waymo: camera + lidar + radar fused over time. Drones: multi-camera plus thermal, within tight weight limits.
