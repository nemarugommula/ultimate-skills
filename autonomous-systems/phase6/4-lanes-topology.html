Lanes & road topology: from painted lines to a lane graph

Finding the paint is the easy half. The hard half is knowing that this lane leads to that one through an intersection with no paint at all, and that you may turn left only from the left lane. That's road topology, and it's where lane detection turned into something like language.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • BEV, bird's-eye view: a top-down metric grid around the car, built from cameras with learned depth or attention (Lesson 6.2).
  • Least squares: choose curve parameters that minimize the sum of squared errors to the data. A polynomial y = a + b·x + c·x² is the usual lane model.
  • Outliers: measurements that don't follow the model at all (a crack in the asphalt detected as paint). One bad point can drag a least-squares fit far off, as one wild GPS fix did to the filter of Lesson 3.3.
  • A graph: nodes plus directed edges. Here a node is a lane segment and an edge means “you can drive from this one into that one”.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're in the right of two lanes, waiting at a red light. In 40 m the paint ends and the intersection begins: no lines, a few arrows on the asphalt, maybe a sign. Which lanes on the far side can you legally end up in, and how does a car know? No pixel shows the answer: it's a relationship between lanes, not a thing you can see.

At AI Day 2022, Tesla described treating this as a language problem: its lanes network outputs “a special language that we developed at Tesla for encoding lanes and their connectivities”, where “the words and tokens are the lane positions in 3D space”, predicted one token at a time like a sentence, with special tokens for where lanes fork (Tesla AI Day 2022). Waymo takes the opposite route and drives with its own highly detailed maps of every lane. This lesson walks the road from one to the other.

✏️ Worked example: why one outlier hurts

Five paint points at lateral offsets (m): 1.75, 1.76, 1.74, 1.75, and one crack at 3.25. Fit the simplest model, a constant (a horizontal line), by least squares: that's just the mean, (1.75 + 1.76 + 1.74 + 1.75 + 3.25) ÷ 5 = 2.05 m. One bad point moved the lane line 30 cm, enough to put a wheel on the line. The median (1.75 m) ignores it. RANSAC (random sample consensus) generalizes that idea to curves:

  1. Pick the minimum number of random points that define a curve (3 for a parabola) and fit them exactly.
  2. Count inliers: points within a threshold, say 0.3 m, of that curve.
  3. Repeat many times; keep the curve with the most inliers; refit by least squares using only its inliers.

If 25% of points are outliers, a random 3-point sample is all-inliers with probability 0.75³ ≈ 0.42, so 20 tries miss with probability 0.58²⁰ ≈ 0.00002. Cheap and robust.

🏆 Challenge: with outliers on, get the RMS error under 10 cm and the error at 55 m under 10 cm (a lane-keeping controller looks that far ahead at speed). Then try degree 4 without outliers, and a huge threshold: what goes wrong?

What you should find: no polynomial degree rescues least squares from outliers; RANSAC with a parabola (degree 2) nails it. Degree 1 can't bend; very high degrees wiggle in the gap and beyond the data. A threshold much larger than the outliers' offset lets them back in. Real lane pipelines also fit in BEV with a lane-width prior and track the coefficients over time with a Kalman filter, so one bad frame can't yank the line.

From lines to a lane graph

A car doesn't steer to paint; it steers along lane centerlines, and it needs to know how they connect. A lane graph encodes that:

  • Nodes: lane segments (a centerline polyline, with attributes: width, speed limit, turn type).
  • Edges: successor (this lane continues into that one), left/right neighbour (you may change lanes here), plus where lanes fork (one lane splits into a straight lane and a turn lane) and merge.
  • At intersections the edges go through paint-free space; their legality comes from rules, arrows and signs, not geometry alone.

Planning then becomes route search on this graph (“left lane → left turn → second exit”), and prediction uses it too: other cars mostly follow its edges (Lesson 7.1). Waymo's public Motion Dataset ships exactly this kind of map with every scene: lane centers, lane boundaries, road boundaries, crosswalks, speed bumps, stop signs and driveways (Waymo Open Dataset).

💡 Big idea Lane perception has two layers: geometry (where is each lane, as a curve in meters) and topology (which lane leads where). Geometry you can fit from pixels; topology is a structured output, a graph, whose errors are rare but dangerous: one wrong edge sends the car straight from a left-turn-only lane. That's why the field moved from per-pixel masks to vector and graph outputs, and why Tesla borrowed sequence models from language.
🧩 Predict: a 4-way intersection, each road with 2 lanes in each direction. If every incoming lane may go straight plus one turn (left lane turns left, right lane turns right), how many lane-to-lane connections are there through the intersection?
16. 4 approaches × 2 lanes × 2 choices each. Now imagine a five-way junction with a slip lane and a bus-only lane, and it's clear why hand-coding topology doesn't scale, and why both HD maps and learned lane graphs exist. Build the 16 yourself below.

The OpenLane-V2 benchmark (NeurIPS 2023) was built for exactly that, scoring lane–lane and lane–traffic-element relationships.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Classical lane detection found the painted lines in front of the car and fitted a curve, which is enough for highway lane keeping. City driving needs every lane around you, through unpainted intersections, with which lane leads where: a graph, not a picture.
  2. What people tried. Hough transforms and polynomial fits: fragile in shadow, rain and worn paint. Per-pixel segmentation (SCNN, LaneNet): robust lines, but a fixed number of lanes or pixels you must still group and connect. HD maps: survey every lane ahead of time and drive with the map, accurate but costly to build and to keep fresh when roads change.
  3. Why this works. Predicting vector lanes and their connections directly in BEV matches what the planner consumes. MapTR models each map element as an ordered set of points and runs camera-only at 25.1 FPS; Tesla models the graph as a token sequence so a network trained like a language model can say “this lane forks here”. Tiny numbers: 16 legal connections in a simple 4-way junction, and one wrong edge is enough to drive straight from a turn-only lane.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Needs huge amounts of labeled lane geometry and topology (Tesla described auto-labeling it with multi-trip reconstruction, Lesson 6.7). Occluded intersections, faded paint, construction zones and unusual local rules are where predicted graphs go wrong; evaluating “is the graph right?” is itself hard.
  5. What came next. Online HD-map building (HDMapNet, MapTR and successors) that blends sensor evidence with cheap navigation maps; topology benchmarks such as OpenLane-V2; and end-to-end driving models that use lanes as an internal hint rather than a hard input (Lessons 0.4 and 9.2 discuss end-to-end driving).

Vector maps: MapTR and the “language of lanes”

HDMapNet (2021) framed the task: build the local HD map online from the car's own cameras and/or lidar, and output vectorized map elements in BEV. MapTR (ICLR 2023) made it clean and fast: each map element (lane divider, road edge, crosswalk) is a set of points with equivalent orderings: a closed crosswalk outline is the same shape whichever corner you start from, and a lane divider is the same line read forwards or backwards. Matching predictions to labels while allowing those orderings stabilized training; MapTR-nano runs at 25.1 FPS on an RTX 3090 with cameras only.

Tesla's AI Day 2022 lanes network adds topology on top, in three parts (as presented):

  • a vision component that turns the camera video into a dense feature tensor;
  • a lane guidance module that encodes coarse road-level navigation map data (“not an HD map”) with hints like lane counts and intersection layout;
  • a language component that “captions” the dense tensor: it predicts a lane point (an index into a coarse 3D grid, then refined), then the next token, and so on, with fork tokens that point back to an earlier token, until an end-of-sentence token. The autoregressive idea is in the Deep Learning course, Phase 7 (autoregressive models).
🧠 Analogy: dictating a subway map

Describe a subway map to a friend over the phone. You don't read out pixels; you say: “The red line starts at Central, goes to Park, then Bridge. At Park, the green line branches off to the airport.” That's a sequence of tokens where “branches off at Park” refers back to something you already said. Tesla's lane “sentences” work the same way, with lane positions as words and forks as references back.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

For classical lanes: OpenCV's Canny + HoughLinesP, and NumPy polynomial fits. For vector maps: the MapTR repo on nuScenes and Argoverse 2. For topology: the OpenLane-V2 devkit. For lane graphs in your own code: NetworkX.

import cv2, numpy as np, networkx as nx

# Classical: edges → line segments (each row x1, y1, x2, y2 in pixels)
edges = cv2.Canny(gray, 50, 150)
segs = cv2.HoughLinesP(edges, rho=1, theta=np.pi / 180, threshold=40, minLineLength=30, maxLineGap=20)

# Robust fit in BEV: RANSAC a parabola y = c2 x² + c1 x + c0 through lane points (x forward, y left)
def ransac_poly(x, y, deg=2, thr=0.3, iters=50, rng=np.random.default_rng(0)):
    best = None
    for _ in range(iters):
        i = rng.choice(len(x), deg + 1, replace=False)
        c = np.polyfit(x[i], y[i], deg)
        inl = np.abs(np.polyval(c, x) - y) < thr
        if best is None or inl.sum() > best.sum():
            best = inl
    return np.polyfit(x[best], y[best], deg), best        # refit on the inliers

# Topology: lanes as nodes, “can drive into” as directed edges
G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edge("S_left", "N_left", turn="straight")
G.add_edge("S_left", "W_left", turn="left")
G.add_edge("W_left", "W_far", turn="straight")
route = nx.shortest_path(G, "S_left", "W_far")            # ['S_left', 'W_left', 'W_far']
🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

No HD maps in what it shows (CVPR 2022). At AI Day 2022 Tesla presented its lanes network: vision features + a lane guidance module from coarse navigation maps + an autoregressive “language of lanes” decoder with fork tokens. Labels come from auto-labeling: multi-trip reconstruction that Tesla said “can replace 5 million hours of manual labeling with just 12 hours on cluster for labeling 10,000 trips”, about 30 minutes per new trip. Tesla also uses those lane-graph labels to generate simulated intersections automatically.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2022, CVPR'22 WAD keynote

🚕 Waymo

Waymo drives with its own highly detailed custom maps, which it calls the driver's “memory”: lane merges, stop signs, crosswalks, speed limits and more, so perception can focus on what's changed and what's moving (2020 Driver Handbook post). Its open Motion Dataset publishes the same style of road graph per scene: lane centers and boundaries, road edges, crosswalks, stop signs, speed bumps, driveways.

Sources: Waymo Driver Handbook: maps, Waymo Open Dataset

🛸 Drones

Lanes and lane topology are a road-vehicle problem: the sky has no lanes. A drone's “topology” is free 3D space and its flight plan (waypoints, geofences), and its perception builds volumetric maps for obstacle avoidance (Lesson 6.3). Delivery and inspection drones fly pre-planned routes; Skydio's drones map their surroundings in 3D as they fly.

Sources: Skydio autonomy

Why they differ: Waymo serves chosen cities, so mapping every lane in advance is affordable and makes topology nearly certain. Tesla sells cars that drive anywhere, so it must infer topology on the fly from cameras plus coarse navigation maps. A drone has no lanes; its version of the problem is free-space geometry.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you give a planner the lanes at an intersection?” A strong answer:

  • Separates geometry (centerline polylines in BEV, robustly fitted and tracked) from topology (a directed lane graph of successors, neighbours, forks, merges).
  • Knows RANSAC vs least squares with a number (the probability an all-inlier sample is drawn), and why curves are tracked over time.
  • Compares HD maps vs online mapping: cost, freshness, change detection, and the hybrid of a coarse navigation map plus learned local topology.
  • Talks about evaluation: geometric error by distance and topology correctness per connection, with construction zones and faded paint as test sets.

Check yourself

1. 30% of your lane points are outliers. RANSAC fits a parabola from 3 random points per try. About how many tries for a 99% chance that at least one sample is all inliers?
P(all 3 inliers) = 0.7³ = 0.343. Need 1 − (1 − 0.343)ᵏ ≥ 0.99 → k ≥ ln(0.01) ÷ ln(0.657) ≈ 11. That's why RANSAC is cheap for small models.
2. In testing, your car tries to go straight from a lane marked “left turn only”. The lane lines in BEV are perfect. Where is the bug most likely?
Geometry can be perfect while topology is wrong. Turn restrictions come from arrows, signs and map priors, and must be represented as which edges exist. Evaluate topology per connection, not just line position.
3. Why does MapTR treat a crosswalk outline as a set of points with several equivalent orderings?
Like PointNet's order problem in 6.1: the label's ordering is arbitrary. Matching against all equivalent orderings means the network is only penalized for wrong shapes, not for starting at a different corner.
📝 TL;DR
  • Lanes went classical (Hough + fit) → segmentation (SCNN, LaneNet instances) → vectors and graphs (HDMapNet, MapTR at 25 FPS, lane graphs).
  • Fit lane curves robustly: least squares is wrecked by outliers; RANSAC (sample, count inliers, refit) is cheap, then track the coefficients over time.
  • Topology = a directed lane graph of successors, neighbours, forks, merges; a 4-way 2-lane junction already has 16 connections, and one wrong edge is dangerous.
  • Tesla (AI Day 2022): vision + coarse-map lane guidance + an autoregressive “language of lanes” with fork tokens, auto-labeled by multi-trip reconstruction.
  • Waymo drives with detailed custom maps of every lane; drones have no lanes, only 3D free space.
