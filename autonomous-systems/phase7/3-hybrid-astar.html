Phase 7 · Lesson 3

Planning for cars: Hybrid A* & kinematic limits

Grid A* happily plans 45° kinks and spins on the spot. A car can do neither. Add the car's heading to the search, move only along arcs the steering wheel can actually make, and you get Hybrid A*: the planner Stanford's robot used to park itself in 2007, written by a researcher who now co-runs Waymo.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A* on a grid: expand squares in order of cost so far + a guess of the cost to go (Lesson 7.2).
  • Pose: position (x, y) plus heading θ, the direction the car points (Lesson 1.1).
  • The bicycle model, in words: a car moves like a bicycle with a fixed back wheel and a steerable front wheel; it can only drive along its heading, and its heading changes at a rate set by speed and steering angle. Lesson 8.2 builds it properly.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Take the grid A* path from the last lesson and hand it to a real car. Its first move is a 45° turn within half a meter. What turning radius would that take, and what does your car actually have? And here's the parking version: the empty spot is right there, 3 m to your left, and your car points along the aisle. Why can't the planner just say “go left 3 m”?

In the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge, robot cars had to park themselves, make U-turns on blocked roads and navigate parking lots in traffic. Stanford's car Junior did it with Hybrid A*, replanning every 50–300 ms, and crossed the finish line 19 minutes after the winner, Carnegie Mellon's Boss (Dolgov et al. 2008, DARPA). The paper's first author, Dmitri Dolgov, is now Waymo's co-CEO (Waymo).

🧩 Predict: a grid path turns 45° between three squares 0.5 m apart. A typical car with a 2.7 m wheelbase and 30° of steering lock turns on a circle of about 4.7 m radius. How much tighter is the grid's turn than the car can manage?
About 7× tighter. A circle through three points 0.5 m apart that turns 45° has radius 0.5 ÷ (2 sin 22.5°) ≈ 0.65 m, while the car's minimum radius is 2.7 ÷ tan 30° ≈ 4.68 m. The car physically can't follow it; it will cut the corner or overshoot, and in a tight lot that means hitting something. Playground 1 lets you watch it happen.

Numbers first: what a car can and can't do

✏️ Worked example: turning circles and a three-point turn

A car with wheelbase L = 2.7 m (front axle to rear axle) and maximum steering angle δ = 30°:

  1. Minimum turning radius of the rear axle: R = L ÷ tan δ = 2.7 ÷ 0.577 = 4.68 m.
  2. A U-turn in one go swings the rear axle through a half circle, 2R = 9.4 m across, plus the body sticking out on both sides. On a 7 m wide street that's impossible.
  3. So you reverse: forward-left, back-right, forward-left. Each gear change (a cusp) costs time and annoys passengers, but buys a turn the car otherwise can't make.
  4. Sideways is never directly possible. To shift 3 m left while keeping the same heading, a car needs an S-shaped move (left arc, then right arc), several meters long. That's the nonholonomic constraint: the car's velocity always points along its heading.

So a car's plan lives in a 3D space, (x, y, θ), not on a 2D grid, and it may only move along arcs with radius ≥ R, forward or backward.

💡 Big idea Hybrid A* = A* over (x, y, θ) cells, where each node remembers the car's exact continuous pose, and children are made by driving short arcs (full left, straight, full right, and in-between, forward and reverse). Every path it returns is drivable by construction. It gives up A*'s guarantee of the shortest path (two poses in one cell get merged), and buys feasibility.

How Hybrid A* searches

  1. Cells, but continuous states. The space is cut into cells (here 0.5 m × 0.5 m × 5° of heading). When an arc lands in a cell, the node stores the exact pose it reached, not the cell's center. That's the “hybrid”: discrete bookkeeping, continuous motion.
  2. Children by simulation. From each pose, drive each primitive a short distance, checking the whole car's outline for collisions along the way.
  3. Costs that encode driving taste. Length, plus penalties for reversing, for switching gear and for steering. Raise the gear-change penalty and the planner prefers one long reverse to three short shuffles.
  4. Two heuristics, take the larger. Ignore obstacles but respect the turning radius (precomputed once, since it doesn't depend on the map), and ignore the turning radius but respect obstacles (a 2D Dijkstra from the goal that knows about dead ends). Both are admissible, so their maximum is too.
  5. Shoot for the goal. Every so often, try an analytic expansion: the exact shortest car path from this node to the goal ignoring obstacles (a Reeds–Shepp curve); if it's collision-free, you're done. This snaps onto the exact goal pose, which the coarse primitives would rarely hit.
  6. Then smooth. The Urban Challenge version polished the result with numerical optimization (conjugate gradient), trading smoothness against obstacle distance: trajectory optimization, Lesson 7.7.

The heuristics matter enormously. In the paper's parking example, plain Euclidean distance expanded 21,515 nodes; the turning-radius-aware heuristic 1,465; but a dead-end map blew that up to 68,730, fixed by combining it with the obstacle-aware one: 10,588 (Dolgov et al. 2008).

🧠 Analogy: a chess player who only knows legal moves

Grid A* is a chess program that lets the knight slide diagonally because it's shorter: fast, and illegal. Hybrid A* only ever considers legal moves (arcs the car can drive), so every line it finds can be played. It's slower to search, but it never hands you a plan the pieces can't follow. Reeds–Shepp curves are the endgame tablebase: when you're close, look up the perfect finish instead of searching.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Grid planners produce paths with sharp kinks and assume the robot can turn on the spot. Cars have a minimum turning radius (about 4.7 m for L = 2.7 m and 30° lock) and can't move sideways, so a grid path through a tight lot is undrivable, and padding obstacles enough to be safe for a long car closes the very gaps you need.
  2. What people tried. Plan on a grid, then smooth (smoothing can't invent the reverse moves a tight spot needs). Exact shortest car paths: Dubins (1957, forward only) and Reeds–Shepp (1990, with reverse) curves, which ignore obstacles. Search over continuous controls directly, which is drivable but slow without a good guide.
  3. Why this works. Search only drivable arcs, so feasibility is built in; keep A*'s speed with a grid for bookkeeping and two admissible heuristics; snap to the goal with Reeds–Shepp. In this lesson's lot, it finds a 2-gear-change park in about 3,000 node expansions, well under a second in your browser.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Not optimal (cell merging) and not complete at coarse resolutions (a narrow passage can fall between primitives). Paths are only as smooth as the primitive set, so a smoothing step usually follows. It plans a path (where), not a timed trajectory around moving cars (when), so it suits parking lots and U-turns, not highway traffic.
  5. What came next. State lattice planners precompute richer primitives that connect lattice points exactly (Lesson 7.5); Nav2's Smac planner ships Hybrid-A* and State Lattice for robots; learned guidance replaces hand heuristics (Tesla's AI Day 2021 parking demo, Lesson 7.2); and optimization polishes the result (Lesson 7.7).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

On ROS 2 robots and small vehicles, Nav2's Smac Hybrid-A* planner is the off-the-shelf version, with Dubins (forward only) or Reeds–Shepp motion models and penalties just like Playground 3. OMPL provides Dubins and Reeds–Shepp state spaces for sampling planners, including space.distance() for the exact shortest car-path length.

# Nav2 planner server: Smac Hybrid-A* (values from the Nav2 docs' example)
planner_server:
  ros__parameters:
    planner_plugins: ["GridBased"]
    GridBased:
      plugin: "nav2_smac_planner::SmacPlannerHybrid"
      motion_model_for_search: "REEDS_SHEPP"   # or "DUBIN" (forward only, the default)
      minimum_turning_radius: 0.40              # meters (a small robot; a car is ~5)
      angle_quantization_bins: 72               # 5° heading cells
      reverse_penalty: 2.0                      # reversing costs 2× (must be ≥ 1)
      change_penalty: 0.0                       # penalty for changing turn direction
      non_straight_penalty: 1.2
      analytic_expansion_ratio: 3.5             # how often to try the exact shot to the goal
# OMPL: shortest path length for a car that can reverse (pip install ompl)
from ompl import base as ob
space = ob.ReedsSheppStateSpace(4.68)        # minimum turning radius in meters
a, b = space.allocState(), space.allocState()
a.setX(0.0); a.setY(0.0); a.setYaw(0.0)
b.setX(0.0); b.setY(3.0); b.setYaw(0.0)       # 3 m to the left, same heading
print(space.distance(a, b))                   # several meters: no sideways moves for cars
🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Hybrid A* comes from Waymo's own family tree: Dolgov, Thrun, Montemerlo and Diebel wrote it for Stanford's Urban Challenge car, and Dolgov is now Waymo's co-CEO. Waymo hasn't published what its current planner uses in parking lots and pick-up zones; its December 2025 post describes a foundation model whose decoder generates the car's trajectories, checked by a separate onboard validation layer (Lesson 7.6).

Sources: Dolgov et al. 2008, Waymo, Dec 2025

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's AI Day 2021 parking-lot demo is this lesson's problem: A* on a lattice of car motions with a Euclidean heuristic expanded 400,000 nodes, a route-guided version 22,000, and a neural-network-guided tree search 288. Tesla said then that it searches over many maneuvers and finishes with a continuous optimization of the trajectory. What ships today isn't published in that detail.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021

🛸 Drones

A multicopter has no turning radius: it can stop, rotate and fly sideways, so plain grid or sampling paths are fine for it (Lessons 7.2, 7.4). Fixed-wing drones are the opposite: they can't stop and have a minimum turn radius, so planners use Dubins-style paths; OMPL ships Dubins and 3D extensions it describes as useful for UAVs and underwater vehicles.

Sources: OMPL state spaces, Nav2 Smac Hybrid-A*

Why they differ: the constraint decides the planner. Cars and fixed-wing aircraft are nonholonomic, so their planners must search drivable (or flyable) motions; multicopters and holonomic warehouse robots can use simpler geometric paths. Tesla and Waymo both face the same parking problem; Tesla described a learned guide for the search, Waymo's public story is the classic algorithm plus learned models today.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why doesn't A* work for a car, and how does Hybrid A* fix it?” A strong answer:

  • Names the nonholonomic constraint and the minimum turning radius R = L / tan δ, with a number.
  • Explains the state (x, y, θ), motion primitives, continuous poses inside discrete cells, and why that loses optimality but guarantees drivability.
  • Describes the two admissible heuristics (non-holonomic without obstacles; holonomic with obstacles) and Reeds–Shepp analytic expansion.
  • Mentions cost shaping (reverse and gear-change penalties), collision checking of the full footprint, smoothing afterwards, and that it's for low-speed unstructured spaces, not traffic.
🏭 In the real world
Junior parks itself 📜 History · 2007 Dolgov et al., STAIR 2008

How: Hybrid A* over (x, y, θ) with Reeds–Shepp expansions, then conjugate-gradient smoothing; replanning in 50–300 ms as the lidar revealed the lot.

Why this method: the Urban Challenge required parking and U-turns on blocked roads, in maps only discovered while driving.

Nav2 Smac Hybrid-A* 🧰 Open source Nav2 docs

How: Dubins or Reeds–Shepp primitives, 72 heading bins by default, penalties for reversing, direction changes and non-straight motion, an analytic expansion and a built-in smoother.

Why this method: car-like and Ackermann robots (and big forklifts) need drivable paths, not grid kinks.

Reeds–Shepp curves 📜 History · 1990 Pacific Journal of Mathematics

How: proved that a car that can reverse needs at most 2 reversals for the shortest path between two poses, and listed a family of at most 68 candidate paths, given by formula.

Why this method: an exact, instant answer to “how would a car get there with no obstacles”: perfect as a heuristic and a final shot.

Tesla parking lots 🏢 Company · 2021 Tesla AI Day 2021

How: a lattice search over car motions, with heuristics improved from straight-line to route-guided to a learned network (400,000 → 22,000 → 288 nodes).

Why this method: lots have no lanes; the search must be fast enough to replan while people and cars move.

🤯 Fun fact · two reversals are always enough

Reeds and Shepp proved in 1990 that for a car that can drive forward and backward with a fixed minimum turning radius, the shortest path between any two poses needs at most two reversals. Every “seven-point turn” you've ever made was, mathematically speaking, showing off (Reeds & Shepp 1990). With obstacles in the way, of course, all bets are off: that's what the search is for.

🌉 How it connects

This is Lesson 7.2's A* with a richer state and drivable edges. The arcs come from the kinematic bicycle model (8.2), and a tracker (8.3, pure pursuit) turns the path into steering commands. Sampling planners (7.4) offer a different route to drivable paths in high dimensions; state lattices and Frenet planners (7.5) take the primitives idea onto roads at speed; 7.7 optimizes the result, as Junior did. The search itself is a shortest path in a state graph: the same idea as planning in an MDP with deterministic moves.

Check yourself

1. A delivery van has a 3.5 m wheelbase and 35° of steering lock (tan 35° ≈ 0.70). What's its minimum turning radius (rear axle)?
R = L ÷ tan δ = 3.5 ÷ 0.70 = 5.0 m. A one-shot U-turn would need a road about 2R = 10 m wide plus the body; on narrower streets the planner must use reverse moves.
2. Your Hybrid A* parks reliably but does five back-and-forth shuffles where a human would do one reverse. What do you change first?
The planner minimizes the cost you gave it. With no price on switching gear, five short shuffles can be slightly shorter than one clean maneuver. Price the switches (Playground 3) and it changes its taste; removing reverse entirely can make the spot unreachable.
3. Why does Hybrid A* take the maximum of two heuristics instead of just one?
Turning-radius-aware but obstacle-blind, it wanders into dead ends (68,730 nodes in the paper's example); obstacle-aware but turn-blind, it underestimates tight maneuvers. Taking the larger keeps admissibility and prunes both mistakes (10,588 nodes). Hybrid A* still isn't optimal, because of cell merging.
📝 TL;DR
  • Cars are nonholonomic: no sideways motion, minimum turning radius R = L / tan δ (≈ 4.7 m for 2.7 m and 30°). Grid paths ask for ~0.65 m turns.
  • Hybrid A* searches (x, y, θ) with drivable arc primitives, storing continuous poses in discrete cells: drivable, not optimal.
  • Two admissible heuristics (turning-aware, obstacle-aware), take the max; Reeds–Shepp shots snap to the exact goal (≤ 2 reversals needed in free space).
  • Costs shape behaviour: reverse and gear-change penalties turn shuffling into clean parking.
  • Junior used it in 2007 (replanning in 50–300 ms); Nav2 ships it; Tesla showed a learned-guided version of the same search.
