Phase 7 · Lesson 1

Predicting other agents: multimodal futures

Before a car can plan, it has to guess what everyone else will do next. The catch: a car at a junction might go left, straight or right, and the “average” of those is a path nobody drives. Here's how prediction went from straight lines to sets of weighted guesses, and how Waymo's research measures them.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Tracking: perception gives every nearby car, cyclist and pedestrian an ID, a position and a velocity, updated about 10 times a second (Lesson 6.5, multi-object tracking).
  • A trajectory is a list of future positions at fixed times, e.g. one point every 0.5 s for the next 5 s.
  • Probability: several outcomes, each with a chance, summing to 1. Refresher: probability and expectation (averages) in the Math course.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A car is 26 m before a junction, doing 10 m/s (36 km/h). Where will it be in 5 seconds? The simplest answer, “it keeps going exactly as it is”, says 50 m straight ahead. If it actually turns left, how wrong is that guess? 2 m? 10 m? 20 m? Lock in a number in the first playground.

That simple guess is called the constant-velocity baseline, and Waymo's 2021 motion-forecasting dataset paper measured it: on their standard validation set, for vehicles 8 seconds ahead, constant velocity was off by 11.0 m on average, while a learned model allowed six guesses was off by 1.34 m (Ettinger et al., WOMD). This lesson is about that gap: why one guess isn't enough, and how to grade a set of guesses.

Why one guess can't work

A car approaching a junction has a handful of distinct futures: straight, left, right, stop. These are modes: separate bumps in the probability of where it will be, with nothing in between. Nobody drives “40% left”. Now think about how you'd train a network to output one trajectory. The natural loss is the squared distance to what really happened, and the single answer that minimizes average squared distance is the average of the futures (that's what an average is: see expectation).

🧩 Predict: half the drivers at this junction turn left and half turn right. A network trained with plain squared error outputs the average path. Where does that path end after 5 s?
Roughly in the middle of the junction, nearly stopped: the left-turners end up to the west, the right-turners to the east, and their average sits between them. It's a perfectly “optimal” prediction by the squared-error loss, and a physically absurd one: no driver parks in the box. A planner trusting it would happily drive around both real futures. Numbers first: grading a set of guesses

The fix is to let the model output K trajectories, each with a probability. But how do you score that? You can't compare the truth with all K (most are supposed to be wrong). The standard answer: score the closest guess.

✏️ Worked example: minADE, minFDE and a miss, by hand

Three future time steps. The car really goes (0, 10) → (−2, 18) → (−8, 22): a left turn. The model offers K = 2 guesses (meters):

t = 1t = 2t = 3ADE (average error)FDE (final error)
truth(0, 10)(−2, 18)(−8, 22)
guess A straight(0, 10)(0, 20)(0, 30)(0 + 2.83 + 11.31) ÷ 3 = 4.7111.31
guess B left(0, 9)(−3, 17)(−9, 21)(1 + 1.41 + 1.41) ÷ 3 = 1.281.41
average of A and B(0, 9.5)(−1.5, 18.5)(−4.5, 25.5)(0.5 + 0.71 + 4.95) ÷ 3 = 2.054.95
  1. minADE (minimum average displacement error) = the best guess's ADE = 1.28 m.
  2. minFDE (minimum final displacement error) = the best final error = 1.41 m.
  3. Miss: no guess ends within a tolerance of the truth. With a 2 m tolerance, guess B is inside, so this is not a miss. The miss rate is the fraction of cases that are misses.
  4. The single average of A and B scores 2.05 m and ends 4.95 m off: a miss. Two honest guesses beat one compromise.

Waymo's benchmark uses a smarter tolerance: it rotates the error into the car's own direction and allows more slack along the road than across it, growing with time (1 m across and 2 m along at 3 s; 1.8 m and 3.6 m at 5 s; 3 m and 6 m at 8 s), scaled down for slow agents (WOMD challenge). This lesson's playgrounds use the 5 s values.

💡 Big idea The future is multimodal: a few distinct outcomes, not one blurry one. So predict a set of K trajectories with probabilities, train so the closest one fits, and grade with minADE, minFDE and miss rate. The probabilities matter too: the planner needs to know that “left” is 25% and “U-turn” is 0.1%, which is what metrics like mAP (mean average precision) reward.
minADEK = min over k of  (1/T) Σt ‖ ŝtk − st ‖     minFDEK = min over k of ‖ ŝTk − sT ‖ For each of the K predicted trajectories, average the distance to the truth over the T future steps (or take only the last one); keep the best. Miss rate = share of cases where even the best guess ends outside the tolerance.
🧠 Analogy: a weather forecast that says “40% rain”

A forecaster who must give one number for tomorrow's rainfall, half the days dry and half the days stormy, would say “a light drizzle” every day and be wrong every day. A good forecaster says “40% chance of storms, otherwise dry”. You can't plan a picnic around a drizzle that never comes; you can plan around “bring an umbrella just in case”. Multimodal prediction is the storm-or-dry forecast for every car on the road.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A planner needs to know where everyone will be for the next several seconds. Early systems extrapolated each track with physics (constant velocity, or a Kalman filter's motion model): fine for 1 s, badly wrong at junctions, lane changes and stops.
  2. What people tried. Physics models (constant velocity or constant turn rate). Maneuver classifiers that pick “left/straight/right” and then draw a hand-made path. One learned trajectory trained with squared error, which collapses to the impossible average. Sampling many futures from a generative model, which works but needs lots of samples.
  3. Why this works. Predict K weighted trajectories and train only the best-matching one (plus the probabilities). Waymo's MultiPath (2019) fixed a set of anchor trajectories, typical shapes found in data, predicted a probability for each plus small corrections, and matched sampling methods with an order of magnitude fewer trajectories. On the Waymo dataset, vehicles at 8 s: constant velocity 11.0 m minADE with a 95% miss rate; a learned model with K = 6, 1.34 m and 25%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. minADE rewards spraying guesses (K = 100 always “covers” something), so benchmarks fix K (6 on Waymo's) and add probability-aware scores. Predicting each agent separately gives inconsistent futures: two cars both predicted to go first through the same gap. Rare maneuvers (U-turns, reversing out of a driveway) have little data. And prediction can't be perfect: other drivers react to you.
  5. What came next. Wayformer (2022) fused map, traffic lights and agent histories with plain attention; MotionLM (2023) turned trajectories into discrete “motion tokens” and predicted all interacting agents jointly, like a language model writing a sentence; and the newest work predicts and plans in one model (Lesson 7.7, and Phase 9).

What the state of the art looks like

Model (Waymo research)YearThe idea in one line
MultiPath2019A fixed set of anchor trajectories; predict a probability for each plus offsets with uncertainty, giving a mixture of bell curves at each time step, in one forward pass.
Waymo Open Motion Dataset2021Over 100,000 scenes of 20 s at 10 Hz (more than 570 hours, 1,750 km of roads, six US cities), mined for merges, lane changes and unprotected turns, with labels for which agents interact.
Wayformer2022A simple, uniform attention model; just concatenating map, signals and agents early (“early fusion”) and attending over everything gave state-of-the-art results on Waymo's and Argoverse's benchmarks.

The attention machinery behind Wayformer and MotionLM is explained in the Deep Learning course, Phase 6 (self-attention). The benchmark task: given 1 s of history and the map, predict up to 8 agents 8 s into the future, leaderboard ranked by a probability-aware score (Soft mAP) with miss rate as a secondary metric.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The metrics are a few lines of NumPy; write them once (the Phase 7 lab does). For real data, the Waymo Open Dataset repository has the motion data format, tutorials and the official metric code; other public benchmarks include Argoverse 2 and nuScenes prediction.

import numpy as np

def min_ade_fde(preds, truth):
    """preds: (K, T, 2) guesses; truth: (T, 2). Returns minADE, minFDE, index of best guess."""
    err = np.linalg.norm(preds - truth[None], axis=-1)   # (K, T) distances
    ade, fde = err.mean(axis=1), err[:, -1]
    return ade.min(), fde.min(), int(ade.argmin())

def is_miss(preds, truth, heading, lat_tol=1.8, lon_tol=3.6):
    """Waymo-style miss at the final step: rotate errors into the agent's frame."""
    c, s = np.cos(heading), np.sin(heading)
    d = preds[:, -1] - truth[-1]                         # (K, 2) final offsets
    lon, lat = d[:, 0] * c + d[:, 1] * s, -d[:, 0] * s + d[:, 1] * c
    return not np.any((np.abs(lat) < lat_tol) & (np.abs(lon) < lon_tol))

truth = np.array([[0, 10], [-2, 18], [-8, 22]], float)
preds = np.array([[[0, 10], [0, 20], [0, 30]], [[0, 9], [-3, 17], [-9, 21]]], float)
print(min_ade_fde(preds, truth))      # (1.276..., 1.414..., 1): the worked example
🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Waymo publishes the most here: MultiPath (anchors), the Waymo Open Motion Dataset and its challenge, Wayformer (attention), MotionLM (joint futures as tokens), and a 2025 technical report showing motion forecasting and planning models improve as a power law with compute, trained on 500,000 hours of driving. In a December 2025 post, Waymo described its foundation model's “World Decoder”, which predicts other road users' behaviour and generates trajectories for the car from the same encoders.

Sources: MultiPath, MotionLM, Scaling laws (2025), Waymo, Dec 2025

🚘 Tesla

At AI Day 2021, Tesla said its planner doesn't just plan for its own car: “we literally run the Autopilot planner on every single relevant object in the scene”, so other cars' likely moves come from asking “what would a sensible driver do there?”. Since FSD v12, Tesla's release notes describe city driving as a single end-to-end network trained on video, so there's no longer a separate, published prediction module to describe.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, FSD v12.3.3 release notes (copy)

🛸 Drones

Consumer and enterprise drones mostly don't predict other agents. Skydio's X10 operator manual (July 2026) warns that cars, boats, people, animals, drones, crewed aircraft and other moving objects may not be avoided. PX4's collision prevention slows and stops the drone near obstacles its distance sensors see, with no forecast of where they'll be. In open sky, keeping big margins is cheaper than predicting.

Sources: Skydio X10 Operator Manual, PX4 Collision Prevention

Why they differ: a robotaxi in a city shares the road with hundreds of agents whose choices decide whether it can go, so Waymo invested in explicit, measurable prediction with public benchmarks. Tesla folds prediction into one learned driving network, trading inspectability for end-to-end training. A drone usually has empty air around it, so it avoids rather than predicts.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you build and evaluate a motion-prediction model?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from the constant-velocity baseline and explains why it fails beyond a second or two.
  • Explains multimodality and why an L2 loss on one trajectory averages modes; proposes K trajectories with probabilities (anchors, mixture models, or token decoding).
  • Defines minADE, minFDE, miss rate and why probability-aware metrics (mAP) are needed, and fixes K for fair comparison.
  • Raises interaction (joint vs marginal futures), map and traffic-light inputs, rare-event slices (U-turns, pedestrians), and closed-loop evaluation: does the planner do better with it?
🏭 In the real world
Waymo Open Motion Dataset & challenge 🏢 Company · 2021 waymo.com/open

How: 100,000+ mined scenes with auto-labelled 3D boxes and HD maps; predict 8 s from 1 s, scored per object type at 3, 5 and 8 s.

Why this method: ordinary driving is mostly straight lines; mining for interactions makes the benchmark measure the hard part.

Anchors: MultiPath 🧪 Research · 2019 arXiv 1910.05449

How: fixed anchor trajectories from data; the network picks among them and refines them, outputting a Gaussian mixture per time step.

Why this method: one pass, compact output a planner can query; far fewer trajectories than sampling.

Joint futures as tokens: MotionLM 🧪 Research · 2023 arXiv 2309.16534

How: motion becomes a vocabulary of small moves; the model writes all agents' futures step by step, each conditioned on the others so far.

Why this method: consistent multi-agent futures (who goes first) without hand-made interaction rules.

Tesla: plan for everyone 🏢 Company · 2021 Tesla AI Day 2021

How: the planner was run for other relevant agents too, to anticipate what they'd sensibly do, and plans were optimized jointly for the scene.

Why this method: in dense traffic, your plan and theirs depend on each other, so predicting and planning become one problem (Lesson 7.7).

🤯 Fun fact · the dataset was mined with SQL

To build the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, the authors hand-wrote predicates like “agent A changed lanes at time t” and “agents A and B crossed paths with time gap t”, then queried a far larger driving log with them in a relational database, keeping the scenes with merges, unprotected turns and close encounters (Ettinger et al. 2021). Good benchmarks are curated, not just collected.

Check yourself

1. A model makes K = 3 guesses. Their ADEs against the true path are 3.2 m, 0.9 m and 5.0 m, and their final errors are 6.0 m, 2.1 m and 1.7 m. What are minADE and minFDE?
Each metric takes its own minimum over the guesses: minADE = 0.9 m (guess 2), minFDE = 1.7 m (guess 3). They can come from different guesses. Averaging over guesses would punish the model for the guesses that were supposed to cover other futures.
2. Your single-trajectory model has great average error on highway data but, at junctions, its predictions cut across corners where no lane exists. Most likely cause?
On the highway there's essentially one mode, so the average is right. At a junction the futures split, and the mean of “left” and “straight” cuts the corner. Multi-modal outputs (anchors, mixtures, or tokens) with best-of-K training fix it.
3. A team reports minADE falling from 1.3 m to 0.6 m after raising K from 6 to 64. Is the model better?
With 64 guesses you can cover almost anything and still give the planner useless, unranked noise. That's why benchmarks fix K (6 on Waymo's) and rank by probability-aware scores such as Soft mAP.
📝 TL;DR
  • Constant velocity is perfect for ~1 s and terrible at 5–8 s: 11.0 m minADE vs 1.34 m for a learned K = 6 model on Waymo's data (vehicles, 8 s).
  • The future is multimodal; one squared-error prediction averages modes into an impossible path.
  • Predict K weighted trajectories; grade with minADE, minFDE and miss rate (along/across tolerances), plus probability-aware mAP.
  • Waymo's line: MultiPath (anchors) → WOMD (benchmark) → Wayformer (attention) → MotionLM (joint tokens) → joint forecasting-and-planning models.
  • Other agents react to you; that's where prediction turns into interactive planning (7.7).
