Phase 7 · Lesson 5

Lattice & Frenet planners

On a road you don't need to search the whole plane: you need to decide how far along and how far across the lane to be, and how fast. Measure position along the road and sideways from it, generate a fan of smooth candidate maneuvers ten times a second, score them all, and drive the best. That's how most on-road planners have worked since the DARPA days.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Position, velocity, acceleration, jerk: each is the rate of change of the one before. Jerk (m/s³) is how fast acceleration changes; it's what passengers feel as a lurch. Refresher: derivatives in the Math course.
  • A polynomial like d(t) = c₀ + c₁t + c₂t² + … is a smooth curve whose derivatives are easy to compute.
  • Predicted trajectories of other cars (Lesson 7.1): where each will be at each future moment.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're at 90 km/h (25 m/s) and need to move one lane left: 3.5 m sideways. A quick, decisive move takes 2 seconds; a lazy one takes 6. How much more “lurch” (jerk) does the 2-second move put into your passengers' stomachs: 3× as much? 9×? 27×? The second playground has the answer, and it's the reason comfortable planners are patient.

In 2010, Werling, Ziegler, Kammel and Thrun published a planner that does exactly this on real streets: describe the motion in road coordinates, generate many polynomial candidates for lateral and longitudinal motion, score each for jerk, time and deviation, and keep the cheapest one that doesn't collide (Werling et al., ICRA 2010). Three years earlier, Carnegie Mellon's Urban Challenge winner Boss followed lanes by generating trajectories to goals at several lateral offsets and picking the best (Urmson et al. 2008).

Road coordinates: s along, d across

A Frenet frame (named after the 19th-century mathematician who described curves this way) measures every point by s, the distance along the road's center line, and d, the signed sideways offset from it (left positive). In these coordinates a curvy road becomes a straight corridor: “stay in the lane” is d stays near 0, “change lanes left” is d goes from 0 to 3.5 m, and “make progress” is s grows.

🧩 Predict: two cars drive side by side through a left-hand bend, one on the center line (d = 0) and one in the inside lane (d = +3.5 m). Both go from s = 0 to s = 60 m. Who drives farther?
The car on the center line. On the bend, the inside lane follows a smaller circle: with a 20 m-radius, 70° bend, the center line's arc is 24.4 m while the inside lane's (radius 16.5 m) is only 20.2 m. So equal steps in s are not equal meters for every lane, and speeds and curvatures must be converted when you go back to x, y. Check it in the playground: the stats show the real length of the d-line you choose.

Numbers first: a comfortable lane change

A lane change must start where you are (d = 0, not moving sideways, no sideways acceleration) and end in the next lane (d = 3.5 m, again no sideways velocity or acceleration). That's six conditions, and the simplest curve that can meet six conditions is a polynomial with six coefficients: a quintic (degree 5).

✏️ Worked example: the minimum-jerk lane change, by hand

Move D = 3.5 m in T seconds, starting and ending at rest sideways. With τ = t ÷ T (0 at the start, 1 at the end), the quintic that satisfies all six conditions is

d(t) = D · (10τ³ − 15τ⁴ + 6τ⁵) At τ = 0 and τ = 1 its slope and curvature are zero, so the move starts and ends gently. It is also the curve with the smallest total squared jerk for these end conditions.
  1. Peak sideways acceleration ≈ 5.77 D ÷ T². For T = 4 s: 5.77 × 3.5 ÷ 16 = 1.26 m/s².
  2. Peak jerk = 60 D ÷ T³, at the very start and end. For T = 4 s: 60 × 3.5 ÷ 64 = 3.28 m/s³.
  3. The hook's answer: jerk scales with 1/T³, so a 2-second change versus a 6-second one is (6 ÷ 2)³ = 27× the jerk (26.3 vs 0.97 m/s³). Halving the time multiplies the lurch by 8.
  4. Speed changes are planned the same way along s: a quartic (degree 4) when you care about reaching a target speed rather than a position (velocity keeping), a quintic when you must stop or follow at an exact spot.

💡 Big idea A Frenet planner doesn't search for the path; it generates a fan of candidates (several end offsets df × durations T × target speeds vf, each a pair of polynomials in d and s), throws away the ones that collide with predicted traffic or break limits, scores the rest with a cost function, and drives the cheapest. Then, a tenth of a second later, it does it all again from where it actually is: receding-horizon replanning.
cost = wcomfort · ∫ jerk² dt  +  T  +  wprogress · (vdesired − vf)²  +  wclear · risk(gap)  +  lane-center penalty(df) Comfort: total squared jerk. Time: quicker maneuvers are preferred. Progress: how far the final speed falls short of what you want. Clearance: grows as the gap to the nearest predicted car shrinks. Candidates that collide or exceed acceleration limits are discarded before scoring. Werling et al. used the same pattern (jerk, time and deviation terms with weights).
🧠 Analogy: a chess player who only thinks 3 seconds ahead, 10 times a second

Instead of planning the whole trip, the planner looks at a few dozen plausible next moves (stay, nudge left, change lanes in 3 s, in 5 s, slow down a bit…), imagines each one against where the other cars are predicted to be, picks the best, makes only the first tiny bit of that move, and then looks again. Mistakes in the prediction get corrected on the next look, 100 ms later.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. On roads, you must plan when as well as where (other cars move), at highway speed, 10+ times a second, and the result must be smooth enough for passengers. Grid and sampling planners in x, y (Lessons 7.2–7.4) ignore time, produce kinks or randomness, and waste effort exploring places a car would never be on a road.
  2. What people tried. Hand-written lane-change curves (one fixed shape, can't react). Grid search in space-time (huge). Sampling in x, y (lanes ignored). Boss, in 2007, already generated trajectories to several lateral offsets along the lane and scored them for obstacles, distance from the center line and smoothness.
  3. Why this works. Road coordinates turn the problem into a few numbers per candidate (df, T, vf); polynomials give exact jerk; ~100–200 candidates can be generated and checked in milliseconds. In this lesson's highway, 7 offsets × 5 durations × 4 speeds = 140 candidates every 0.2 s. Jerk scales as 1/T³, so the comfort weight directly buys patience: from 7.5 m/s³ down to 1.6 m/s³ in Playground 3.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It needs a good reference line: no lanes (parking lots, construction zones) means no Frenet frame (use Hybrid A*, Lesson 7.3). Sharp curves distort the conversion back to x, y. The candidate set is finite, so a clever maneuver outside it is invisible. Weights are hand-tuned and interact (Playground 3's “too comfortable” setting cuts clearance). And it trusts predictions; it doesn't model others reacting to you (Lesson 7.7).
  5. What came next. State lattices that connect a precomputed grid of states with feasible motions (Boss used one in parking zones; Nav2 ships a State Lattice planner); optimization of the chosen candidate (Baidu Apollo's EM planner: dynamic programming then spline quadratic programming in the Frenet frame); and learned scoring or end-to-end trajectory generation (Lesson 7.6, Phase 9).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The candidate generator is a page of NumPy (the Phase 7 lab builds the quintic and its jerk). PythonRobotics has a complete Frenet optimal-trajectory planner based on Werling et al. (with cost weights named like Playground 3's); MATLAB's Navigation Toolbox has a Frenet trajectory generator; Baidu Apollo's open-source planning module shows a production Frenet stack; Nav2's Smac State Lattice planner covers the lattice idea for robots.

import numpy as np

def quintic(x0, v0, a0, x1, v1, a1, T):
    """Coefficients c0..c5 of x(t) meeting position, velocity, acceleration at t = 0 and t = T."""
    A = np.array([[T**3, T**4, T**5], [3*T**2, 4*T**3, 5*T**4], [6*T, 12*T**2, 20*T**3]])
    b = np.array([x1 - (x0 + v0*T + a0*T**2/2), v1 - (v0 + a0*T), a1 - a0])
    return np.r_[x0, v0, a0 / 2, np.linalg.solve(A, b)]

c = quintic(0, 0, 0, 3.5, 0, 0, T=4.0)                 # a 3.5 m lane change in 4 s
t = np.linspace(0, 4, 401)
jerk = 6*c[3] + 24*c[4]*t + 60*c[5]*t**2
print(abs(jerk).max())                                   # 3.28 m/s³ = 60·3.5/4³

# Score a fan of candidates (sketch): keep valid ones, take the cheapest
cands = [(df, T) for df in (0, 1.75, 3.5) for T in (2, 3, 4, 5, 6)]
cost = lambda df, T, w=0.5: w * 720 * df**2 / T**5 + T + 5 * min(df, abs(df - 3.5))**2
best = min(cands, key=lambda c: cost(*c))                # 720·D²/T⁵ = ∫jerk² for a rest-to-rest quintic
🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

At AI Day 2021 Tesla described its planner as searching over many candidate maneuvers (“thousands of such searches in a very short time span”), choosing among them on “safety, comfort, and easily making the turn”, and then running a continuous optimization to produce the final trajectory: the candidates-plus-costs pattern of this lesson. Since FSD v12, Tesla's release notes say city driving is handled by a single end-to-end network, and it hasn't published what, if any, explicit candidate scoring remains.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, FSD v12.3.3 release notes (copy)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its on-road trajectory generator. Its December 2025 post says the foundation model's World Decoder generates trajectories for the vehicle and produces signals for validating them, and that structured outputs (objects, road graph) allow safety validation at inference time. The classic open references for road-aligned planning remain Boss (2007) and Werling et al. (2010), with Stanford colleagues of Waymo's founders among the authors.

Sources: Waymo, Dec 2025, Boss (2008)

🛸 Drones

Drones care about jerk too: PX4's “Jerk-limited” trajectory type, always on in autonomous modes like Mission mode, generates smooth S-curve velocity profiles that guarantee configurable jerk and acceleration limits (for filming, mapping, cargo). There's no road, so no Frenet frame: the reference is the line between waypoints.

Sources: PX4: Jerk-limited trajectory

Why they differ: cars have lanes, so road coordinates and a fan of lane-aligned candidates fit naturally; Tesla described exactly that before moving to an end-to-end network, and Waymo pairs learned trajectory generation with explicit validation. Drones have no lanes but the same physics of comfort and stress, so they limit jerk along waypoint lines instead.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design the highway lane-change planner.” A strong answer:

  • Works in Frenet coordinates (s, d) around a lane reference line, and explains the conversion back to x, y and curvature.
  • Generates candidates: lateral quintics to several df over several T, longitudinal quartics/quintics for speed keeping, following and stopping.
  • Filters by hard constraints (collision with predicted agents, acceleration and curvature limits) before scoring soft costs (jerk, time, progress, clearance, lane centering), with numbers like jerk ∝ 1/T³.
  • Replans at 10 Hz from the actual state, adds a consistency term against flip-flopping, and discusses tuning the weights on logged scenarios.
🏭 In the real world
Werling et al.'s Frenet planner 🧪 Research · 2010 ICRA 2010

How: lateral and longitudinal polynomial candidates in the Frenet frame, each scored for jerk, time and deviation; the cheapest valid one is executed, replanned continuously.

Why this method: handles velocity keeping, merging, following and stopping with one framework, plus reactive collision avoidance.

Boss on the road and in the lot 📜 History · 2007 Urmson et al., JFR 2008

How: on roads, trajectories to goals at different lateral offsets, scored for obstacle proximity, center-line distance and smoothness; in parking zones, a lattice planner over (x, y, θ, v) with Anytime D*.

Why this method: lanes give a natural reference on roads; zones have none, so it switched to a lattice search.

Baidu Apollo EM planner 🧰 Open source · 2018 arXiv 1807.08048

How: per lane, path and speed optimization in a Frenet frame using dynamic programming and spline quadratic programming; lane-level trajectories computed in parallel and compared for lane changes.

Why this method: scalable and tunable for highway and city driving; the paper reports 3,380 hours and about 68,000 km of closed-loop testing by May 2018.

PX4 jerk-limited trajectories 🧰 Open source PX4 docs

How: S-curve velocity profiles with guaranteed jerk and acceleration limits between mission waypoints.

Why this method: smooth motion keeps cameras steady and cargo secure, the drone version of passenger comfort.

🤯 Fun fact · jerk has children

Jerk is the third derivative of position. The fourth, fifth and sixth have been jokingly named snap, crackle and pop, and “minimum snap” trajectories are a real, widely used technique for planning aggressive quadrotor flights. The quintic in this lesson is the minimum-jerk curve; a degree-7 polynomial gives minimum snap.

Check yourself

1. A 3.5 m quintic lane change takes 5 s. What's its peak jerk (60 D ÷ T³)?
60 × 3.5 ÷ 125 = 1.68 m/s³. At 2.5 s it would be 8× that (13.4 m/s³), because jerk scales with 1/T³.
2. Your Frenet planner keeps switching between “change left now” and “stay” every cycle, so the car wiggles. What's the usual fix?
Two candidates with nearly equal costs swap places with tiny changes in the inputs. A cost for deviating from the last plan adds hysteresis, so the planner only switches when the new option is clearly better. Replanning less often makes it slow to react.
3. The car enters a big parking lot with no lane markings and no map lanes. Is a Frenet planner a good choice?
Frenet coordinates are only as good as the reference they measure from. Boss did exactly this in 2007: lane-based trajectories on roads, a lattice planner in zones. Lesson 7.3's Hybrid A* is the other classic choice.
📝 TL;DR
  • Frenet coordinates: s along the road, d across. Lanes become straight lines; lane change = d from 0 to 3.5 m.
  • Quintic polynomials meet position, velocity and acceleration at both ends; peak jerk = 60D/T³, so 2 s vs 6 s is 27× the jerk.
  • Generate many candidates (offsets × durations × speeds), drop collisions and limit-breakers, score the rest (jerk, time, progress, clearance), drive the cheapest, replan at ~10 Hz.
  • Weights interact: too little comfort lurches, too much squeezes past cars. Tuning is the real work.
  • Boss (2007), Werling (2010) and Apollo (2018) are the open lineage; Tesla described candidates + costs + optimization at AI Day 2021; drones limit jerk along waypoints (PX4).
