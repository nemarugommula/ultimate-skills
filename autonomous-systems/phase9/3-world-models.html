World models: a car that can imagine the next few seconds

A world model is a learned simulator: give it what the world looks like now and what you're about to do, and it predicts what happens next. Wayve, NVIDIA, Waymo and Tesla all build them, to train and test drivers on situations that are too rare or too dangerous to wait for. The catch: imagination drifts, and it drifts faster the further ahead you look.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A motion model: “from this state and this command, here's the next state”, like the kinematic bicycle model of Lesson 8.2. Model predictive control rolls one forward to plan (Lesson 8.5).
  • Compounding error: a small mistake per step, fed back into the next step, grows with the horizon (Lesson 9.1).
  • Least squares: fit a model's numbers to data; flexible models can say nonsense outside the data they were fit on (Lesson 9.1's camera pixels). Refresher: overfitting in the Math course.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Your model of the car's motion is 99% right per tenth of a second: its predicted turn rate is off by only about 1%. You ask it to imagine a gentle curve at 12 m/s. After 1 second its imagined position is off by about a centimeter. How far off will it be after 6 seconds? Six times as much? More? Less?

Hold that guess. It's the central question for a technology every AV company now invests in. In February 2026 Waymo introduced the Waymo World Model, built on Google DeepMind's Genie 3, which generates camera and lidar data and can “simulate exceedingly rare events—from a tornado to a casual encounter with an elephant” (Waymo). Wayve's GAIA models and NVIDIA's Cosmos do related things.

Numbers first: why imagination drifts

✏️ Worked example: a 1% turn-rate error, 60 steps later

The car drives at 12 m/s on a curve with a true turn rate of 0.20 rad/s. The model predicts 0.198 rad/s: 1% low, an error of 0.002 rad/s.

  1. Heading error grows linearly: after t seconds it's 0.002 × t rad. After 6 s: 0.012 rad (0.7°). Tiny.
  2. Position error grows with the square: a heading error of 0.002·t sends you sideways at speed × heading error, 12 × 0.002·t m/s. Add that up over time and you get ½ × 12 × 0.002 × t² = 0.012·t² m.
  3. After 1 s: 0.012 m. After 3 s: 0.11 m. After 6 s: 0.43 m, 36× the 1-second error for 6× the time.

A model that's off in its speed estimate too, or in how it reacts to the pedal, stacks a second error on top, and a learned model that predicts pixels can drift in a hundred subtler ways (a car slowly changing colour, a lane line wandering). That's why every world-model paper reports quality versus horizon, and why “how far ahead can I trust it?” is the first question to ask.

💡 Big idea A world model learns next state = f(current state, action) from data, and then you run it on its own output, step after step, to imagine a future. Each step's error becomes the next step's input, so errors compound: the model is trustworthy only up to a horizon, and only for actions and situations that resemble its training data.
🧠 Analogy: a weather forecast for the road

Tomorrow's forecast is good, next week's is rough, and a forecast for a month away is little better than the seasonal average. The physics is the same every day; the errors in today's measurements and in the model grow each day the forecast is stepped forward. A driving world model is a weather forecast for the next few seconds of traffic, conditioned on what you choose to do.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. You can't test a new driving model on a real road every time you change it, and replaying logs isn't enough: once the new model drives differently, the recorded camera frames no longer match where the car is (Lesson 9.4). Hand-built simulators look and behave subtly unlike reality, and every new scene (a city, a weather, an elephant) needs artists and code.
  2. What people tried. Game-engine simulators like CARLA (Lesson 9.4); log replay with small edits; physics models with hand-tuned parameters, like Lesson 8.2's bicycle model, which predict the car's own motion well but not what other road users or the camera will see.
  3. Why learned world models work. They learn what scenes look like and how they change from real fleet video, so they're realistic by construction, controllable (“make the car ahead brake”, “now it's snowing”) and scale with data rather than artists. In Playground 1, the richer model's 6-second error is far smaller than the simple one's, because it learned a real effect (understeer) from the same few minutes of logs.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Errors compound with horizon (a 1% turn-rate error becomes 0.43 m after 6 s, 36× the 1-s error). Outside the training data the model can confidently invent physics. A generated video can look perfect and still be wrong in ways that matter (an object quietly vanishing). They're expensive to train and run, and a policy trained inside one can learn to exploit its mistakes, like finding a gap the model invented.
  5. What came next. Multi-camera, multi-sensor generators (GAIA-2 makes consistent video across cameras; the Waymo World Model generates lidar too), conditioning on structured inputs (agents, road layout, weather) for control, ensembles and uncertainty to flag when imagination can't be trusted (Playground 3), and grounding generated runs in real logs so a simulation starts from a real moment and diverges only as far as needed.
🧩 Predict: in the next playground, the model is fit on 3 minutes of logs. Will 30 minutes of logs make the simple model trustworthy for 6 seconds?
No. More data shrinks the error that comes from noise (a variance problem), but the simple model's error comes from missing physics (a bias problem: it can't represent understeer at any amount of data). Its safe horizon barely moves. Switching to the richer model, which can represent the effect, is what buys the long horizon. Real world-model teams face both: more data and bigger, better-structured models.

Knowing when imagination is lying

A world model fit on gentle driving has never seen a sharp turn. Ask it to imagine one and it will happily answer, with no warning. A standard trick is an ensemble: train several copies of the model on different random resamples of the data. Where data was plentiful they agree; where data was thin, their guesses spread apart. The spread is a cheap “I'm not sure” signal that planners and simulators can check before trusting a rollout.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Small dynamics models are plain regression (NumPy, scikit-learn, PyTorch). Big video world models are mostly research or company-internal; the open ones you can try are NVIDIA's Cosmos world foundation models (open weights on GitHub and Hugging Face) and research code from papers such as DreamerV3. The core of a learned dynamics model and its rollout:

import numpy as np
# logs: speed v, steering d, pedal a, measured turn rate w and acceleration acc (arrays of length N)
Fw = np.c_[v * d, v**3 * d]                       # features for turn rate (with understeer)
Fa = np.c_[a, v**2, np.ones_like(v)]            # features for acceleration (pedal, drag, offset)
cw = np.linalg.lstsq(Fw, w, rcond=None)[0]
ca = np.linalg.lstsq(Fa, acc, rcond=None)[0]

def imagine(x, y, th, v, plan, dt=0.1):              # plan = [(pedal, steer), ...]
    path = []
    for a, d in plan:                                # feed the model its own output
        x, y = x + v * np.cos(th) * dt, y + v * np.sin(th) * dt
        th += (cw @ [v * d, v**3 * d]) * dt
        v = max(0.0, v + (ca @ [a, v**2, 1.0]) * dt)
        path.append((x, y))
    return np.array(path)

# Ensemble: refit on bootstrap resamples, and treat the spread of their rollouts as uncertainty
idx = [np.random.randint(0, len(v), len(v)) for _ in range(5)]

Video world models replace “state” with compressed image tokens or latents and f with a large transformer or diffusion model (GAIA-1 predicts the next token; GAIA-2 is a latent diffusion model), but the rollout loop, and its compounding error, is the same.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / Wayve / NVIDIA do it

🚕 Waymo

The Waymo World Model (Feb 6, 2026) is built on Genie 3 and adapted to driving; it generates camera and lidar data and offers “driving action control, scene layout control, and language control”, so engineers can replay a real event and branch into a counterfactual. Waymo says its driver navigates “billions of miles in virtual worlds”. Earlier simulation work: SimulationCity (2021), which “automatically synthesizes entire journeys”.

Sources: Waymo, Feb 2026, SimulationCity

🇬🇧 Wayve

GAIA-1 (Sept 2023) is a generative world model that takes video, text and action inputs and treats world modelling as next-token prediction. GAIA-2 (Mar 2025) is a latent diffusion model that generates consistent multi-camera video, conditioned on ego dynamics, other agents, weather and road layout, across UK, US and German scenes.

Sources: GAIA-1, GAIA-2

🟩 NVIDIA

NVIDIA launched Cosmos at CES on Jan 6, 2025: “state-of-the-art generative world foundation models, advanced tokenizers, guardrails” for robots and AVs, with open weights. The paper's opening line: “Physical AI needs to be trained digitally first.” Later releases added Cosmos Reason (Mar 2025) and Cosmos Predict-2 (Jun 2025).

Sources: NVIDIA, Jan 2025, Cosmos paper

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published a paper or a shareholder-letter description. Its AI lead Ashok Elluswamy described a learned neural world simulator in an ICCV 2025 workshop keynote and an Oct 2025 post; according to coverage of those, it generates the car's camera views, is used for closed-loop evaluation and replaying failures, and is shared with the Optimus robot. Treat details as reported, not documented.

Source (secondary): Humanoids Daily, Oct 2025

Why they differ: Waymo must prove safety for a lidar-equipped robotaxi, so it needs lidar in its generated worlds and tight control for counterfactual tests. Wayve, with less fleet data, uses generation to multiply scenarios. NVIDIA sells the platform, so it builds general, open models. Tesla's camera-only stack only needs camera views, and it has the video to learn them from.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you know whether a learned simulator is good enough to evaluate your driving policy?” A strong answer:

  • Measures error versus horizon against held-out real logs, and picks horizons where it's trustworthy; explains why errors grow faster than linearly.
  • Separates looks right (image quality) from behaves right (agents react plausibly, physics consistent across cameras and time).
  • Checks that rankings agree: policies that are better on the road should also be better in the simulator (correlation of sim and real metrics).
  • Uses uncertainty (ensembles, out-of-distribution checks) and watches for a policy exploiting the model's errors.
🏭 In the real world
Waymo World Model 🚗 AV · 2026 Waymo blog

How: a Genie 3-based generator adapted to driving produces camera and lidar data, controlled by driving actions, scene layout and language prompts, and can take over from a real logged event to explore “what if”.

Why this method: rare events like a wrong-way driver can't be collected at scale on real roads.

GAIA-2 🏢 Company · 2025 Wayve

How: latent diffusion generates multi-camera driving video conditioned on ego motion, agents, environment and road semantics.

Why this method: multi-camera consistency and fine control are what a driving simulator needs; plain video generators lack both.

DreamerV3 🧪 Research · 2023 Hafner et al.

How: learns a world model and improves its behaviour “by imagining future scenarios”; the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data.

Why this method: practicing in imagination is far cheaper than in the real environment.

NVIDIA Cosmos 🧰 Open weights · 2025 github.com/nvidia-cosmos

How: pre-trained world foundation models plus a video curation pipeline and tokenizers, meant to be fine-tuned into a specific robot's or car's world model.

Why this method: most robotics teams can't afford to train a video world model from scratch.

🤯 Fun fact · the elephant test

Waymo's world-model post lists a “casual encounter with an elephant” among the rare events it can simulate. No fleet will ever log enough elephants to train on. A generator that has learned what elephants look like from general video, and how cars move from driving video, can put the two together: that's the bet behind building driving world models on top of general-purpose ones like Genie 3 and Cosmos.

Check yourself

1. A world model's predicted turn rate is off by 0.002 rad/s at 12 m/s. Using position error ≈ ½ × v × error × t², how far off is it after 4 s?
½ × 12 × 0.002 × 4² = 0.012 × 16 = 0.19 m. Doubling the horizon from 2 s (0.048 m) to 4 s quadruples the error: that's compounding.
2. Your simple world model's 6-second predictions are off by 2 m. Ten times more training logs barely help. What's the most likely reason?
Noise (variance) shrinks with more data; missing structure (bias) doesn't. In Playground 2 the simple model's safe horizon stays near 3–4 s at any amount of data; the richer model is what moves it.
3. A planner considers a swerve sharper than anything in the world model's training data. Five ensemble members predict very different outcomes. What should the system do?
Large disagreement means thin data, so none of the five deserves trust. Penalizing uncertain plans also stops an optimizer from exploiting the model's mistakes.
📝 TL;DR
  • A world model learns next state = f(state, action) and is rolled out on its own predictions to imagine futures.
  • Errors compound: a 1% turn-rate error gives 0.43 m after 6 s, 36× the 1-s error. Every world model has a trustworthy horizon.
  • More data fixes noise, not missing physics; outside the training data, models confidently invent. Ensembles flag that.
  • Uses: closed-loop simulation and evaluation, synthetic training data, and learning or planning in imagination.
  • Waymo World Model (Genie 3, camera + lidar, 2026), Wayve GAIA-1/2, NVIDIA Cosmos (open, 2025); Tesla has described a neural world simulator in talks.
