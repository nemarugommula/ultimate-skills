Phase 9 · Lesson 1

Imitation learning: copy the expert, then fall off the road

The simplest way to teach a car to drive: record a good driver and train a model to copy every steering move. It works surprisingly well, right up to the moment the copy makes a mistake the expert never made. That moment, and the 2011 fix called DAgger, is this lesson.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Closed loop: a controller reads the state, sends a command, the car moves, and the next reading depends on that command (Lesson 0.1, and Phase 8 for PID and path tracking).
  • Lane offset and heading error: how far the car is from the lane center (meters) and how much its nose points away from the lane direction (radians). The path trackers of Lesson 8.3 steer to drive both to zero.
  • Least squares: fit the numbers w of a model so its outputs are as close as possible to the targets, on average squared. Refresher: gradient descent and overfitting in the Math course.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You record an expert driving one perfect lap. You train a model that copies the expert's steering with almost no error on that lap. You let it drive. Within one lap it's in the grass.

Nothing about the road changed, the model isn't broken, and its error on the recorded lap is tiny. So what went wrong? And here's the follow-up that trips up most people: would recording five more perfect laps fix it?

This exact problem showed up in the very first neural-network car. In 1988–89, Dean Pomerleau's ALVINN steered Carnegie Mellon's Navlab test vehicle from a tiny 30 × 32 pixel camera image (NIPS 1988). In 2016 NVIDIA trained a network on about 72 hours of human driving and reported the car steering itself about 98% of the time on a test drive in New Jersey (Bojarski et al., 2016). Both had to solve the puzzle above to get there.

🧩 Predict before you play: the expert below drives on a calm day and almost never drifts more than a few centimeters. What do you think the copy has learned about getting back to the lane center from half a meter off?
Nothing at all. The expert was never half a meter off, so there is not a single example of “here's how you recover from there”. The copy learned what to do in the states the expert visits, and the first small slip takes it into states nobody showed it. Numbers first: why a small slip becomes a crash

✏️ Worked example: one slip, then the snowball

Say the copy is very good: in the states the expert visited it makes a mistake on only 1 step in 100 (ε = 0.01). A lap is T = 100 decisions.

  1. The expert's world. If every mistake were forgiven (the car snapped back to familiar states), you'd expect ε × T = 0.01 × 100 = 1 mistake per lap.
  2. The copy's world. After its first mistake the car is somewhere the expert never was: 20 cm to the left, nose pointing out. The copy has no data there, so it keeps making mistakes until the end of the lap. The chance it has already slipped by step t is 1 − 0.99t: about 10% by step 10, 40% by step 50, 63% by step 100.
  3. Add them up. Expected mistakes = Σt=1..100 (1 − 0.99t) ≈ 37 mistakes, not 1.

That's 37 times worse from the same 1% error rate, purely because errors land the car in unfamiliar states. Stretch the lap to T = 1,000 and it's about 901 versus 10. Ross and Bagnell proved this in general: copying the expert gives errors that grow like εT², while training on the learner's own states gives errors that grow like εT (Ross, Gordon & Bagnell, AISTATS 2011).

💡 Big idea Behavior cloning is ordinary supervised learning on (observation, expert action) pairs, and it's only as good as the states those pairs cover. Driving is closed-loop: the model's own actions choose its next inputs. So the inputs at test time drift away from the training inputs, a problem called covariate shift (the inputs, the “covariates”, shift). Small errors push you off the data, and off the data errors get bigger: they compound.
🧠 Analogy: learning to drive by watching from the back seat

Imagine learning to drive only by sitting behind an excellent driver for a year. You've seen thousands of hours of “stay in the middle of the lane”. You have never once seen “the car is half on the shoulder, what now?”, because your driver never let that happen. The first time it happens to you, you're improvising. A good instructor fixes this by letting you drive and saying, in the moment, “now steer left a bit”. That is DAgger.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Writing a rule for every driving situation by hand never ends, and learning by trial and error (reinforcement learning) on a real road means crashing to learn what a crash is. Meanwhile millions of people drive well every day. Copying them is the obvious shortcut.
  2. What people tried. Behavior cloning: ALVINN (1989) on Navlab, NVIDIA's DAVE-2 (2016) mapping raw pixels from a single front camera to steering. Both hit covariate shift. NVIDIA's fix was data augmentation: it also recorded from left and right cameras and randomly shifted and rotated images, labelling them with the steering that would bring the car back, so the model saw fake “I've drifted” views (paper). Waymo's ChauffeurNet (2018) found that “30 million examples are still not enough” and added synthesized perturbations, such as going off the road, to its training (paper).
  3. Why DAgger works. Let the learner drive, record the states it visits, ask the expert “what would you do here?”, add those labels to the dataset and retrain. Repeat. The training data now covers the states the learner actually gets into, including its own mistakes. With ε = 1% and T = 100: about 37 expected mistakes for plain copying versus about 1 when trained on your own states.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need an expert who can label any state, even weird ones. Easy for a computer expert in simulation; awkward for a human, who must say “steer 4° left” for a car they aren't driving, often from a recording, and whose labels suffer from perceived lag (the HG-DAgger paper calls this out). The learner drives while data is collected, so on a real road a safety driver must be ready to take over. And if the expert relies on information the learner can't see, no amount of copying closes the gap.
  5. What came next. HG-DAgger (2018): the human takes over only when the learner gets into trouble, and those takeovers become the labels. DART (2017): inject noise while the expert demonstrates, so the demos include recoveries (the gusty-day checkbox in Playground 1). Privileged experts in simulation that know everything and label every state for free (“Learning by Cheating”, 2019). Then fine-tuning the copy with reinforcement learning (Tesla said in April 2026 that FSD v14.3 “upgraded the Reinforcement Learning stage of training”) and checking the copy in closed loop, in simulation (Lessons 9.3 and 9.4).

DAgger in four lines

DAgger stands for Dataset Aggregation. Start with expert demonstrations, then loop:

1. Train policy π on dataset D
2. Run π in the world; record the observations it actually sees
3. Ask the expert for the action at each of those observations
4. D ← D ∪ {(observation, expert action)}; go to 1 The learner chooses where the data comes from; the expert only chooses the labels. Keep all the old data too (“aggregation”), so the policy doesn't forget the expert's own states.

Two details matter in practice. First, you don't need to label every visited state: neighbouring frames look almost the same, so asking every 10th or 20th frame buys most of the coverage for a fraction of the labels. Second, the expert's answers must be about what to do from here, not “what I would have done to never get here”. Recovery is the whole point.

🧩 Predict: in the next playground, DAgger asks for an expert label every N-th state the learner visits. Will asking for every state (N = 2) beat asking for every 20th, if you're scored on labels used?
Usually not. States 0.05 s apart are nearly identical, so labelling all of them mostly repeats information. Sparse labels over a few rounds spread your budget over more different states, which is what the model needs. Real labelling budgets work the same way: Lesson 9.5's data engine is about choosing which clips are worth a label.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Behavior cloning is a supervised regression: a dozen lines of NumPy or PyTorch. For experiments, the imitation library (HumanCompatibleAI, built on Stable-Baselines3 and Gymnasium) has BC, DAgger, GAIL and more; CARLA is the usual open driving simulator for imitation research (Lesson 9.4), and “Learning by Cheating” (2019) set records on its benchmarks with a privileged expert teaching a camera-only student.

import numpy as np
# Behavior cloning with least squares: X = observations (N × d), y = expert steering (N)
X1 = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]                          # add a bias column
w = np.linalg.solve(X1.T @ X1 + 0.01 * np.eye(X1.shape[1]), X1.T @ y)   # ridge fit
policy = lambda obs: np.clip(w[0] + obs @ w[1:], -0.5, 0.5)

# DAgger with the imitation library (from its docs)
from imitation.algorithms import bc
from imitation.algorithms.dagger import SimpleDAggerTrainer
bc_trainer = bc.BC(observation_space=env.observation_space,
                   action_space=env.action_space, rng=rng)
dagger_trainer = SimpleDAggerTrainer(venv=env, scratch_dir=tmpdir,
                                     expert_policy=expert, bc_trainer=bc_trainer, rng=rng)
dagger_trainer.train(8_000)                               # environment steps, over several rounds

The ridge term (0.01 × identity) keeps the fit stable when some pixels never light up. It also means those pixels get weights near zero: the model literally has no opinion about them, which is what you saw in Playground 1.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's FSD v12 release notes (Dec 2023) described city driving as “a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code” (as reproduced by Not a Tesla App). Its Q4 2023 shareholder letter says the system “uses AI to influence vehicle controls… instead of hard-coding every driving behavior”. That is imitation of human driving at fleet scale. By April 2026 Tesla described a further reinforcement learning stage on top for v14.3. Tesla hasn't published how it handles covariate shift; its public answer is scale and fleet data (Lesson 9.5).

Sources: Tesla Q4 2023 update, Tesla Q1 2026 update, v12.1 notes (Not a Tesla App)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo's 2018 ChauffeurNet paper is a textbook covariate-shift story: standard behavior cloning was “insufficient… 30 million examples are still not enough”. Their fix was to perturb the expert's logged trajectories (pushing the car toward collisions or off the road) and add losses that penalize those outcomes, so the model learned recoveries. Waymo's current driver combines learned models with structured outputs (Lesson 9.2).

Sources: ChauffeurNet (Bansal, Krizhevsky, Ogale, 2018), Waymo, Dec 2025

🛸 Drones

Drone researchers love the “privileged expert in simulation” trick, because a simulated expert can label any state for free. UZH's Deep Drone Acrobatics (2020) trained a policy “entirely in simulation by leveraging demonstrations from an optimal controller that has access to privileged information” and flew it on a real quadrotor without fine-tuning. Learning High-Speed Flight in the Wild (2021) did the same for flying fast through forests, imitating an expert with full knowledge of the map.

Sources: Kaufmann et al., 2020, Loquercio et al., 2021

Why they differ: Tesla has millions of cars recording human driving, so it leans on sheer volume of demonstrations. Waymo has far fewer cars but a big simulator and a structured stack, so it synthesizes the hard cases. Drone labs have neither a fleet nor human experts who can fly acrobatics, but they have physics simulators where a perfect expert is cheap, so DAgger-style labelling is almost free.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your behavior-cloned driving model has 98% accuracy on held-out logs but drifts out of the lane in closed loop. Why, and what do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Names covariate shift: held-out logs are expert states; in closed loop the model visits its own states, which aren't in the data.
  • Explains compounding with a number: errors grow like εT² (1% per step over 100 steps ≈ 37 mistakes, not 1).
  • Offers fixes in order of cost: recovery augmentation (side cameras, perturbations), noise injection during demos, DAgger or intervention-based collection, closed-loop evaluation in simulation before the road.
  • Says how they'd measure: closed-loop metrics (off-road rate, interventions per mile), not open-loop accuracy (Lesson 9.4).
🏭 In the real world
ALVINN steers Navlab 📜 History · 1988–89 Pomerleau, NIPS 1988

How: a small neural network took a 30 × 32 video “retina” and an 8 × 32 range-finder image and output a steering direction for CMU's Navlab, trained on simulated road images; the paper reports driving at 1/2 m/s along a 400 m path.

Why this method: hand-coding road following for every road appearance was brittle; learning from examples was the first approach that adapted to what the camera saw.

NVIDIA DAVE-2 🏢 Company · 2016 End to End Learning for Self-Driving Cars

How: a CNN mapped raw pixels from a single front camera to steering, trained on about 72 hours of driving, with left and right cameras plus random shifts to teach recoveries; it reported about 98% autonomy on a test route and 10 miles on the Garden State Parkway with zero interventions.

Why this method: one network, no hand-designed lane detector; the recovery augmentation was there specifically to beat covariate shift.

DAgger 🧪 Research · 2011 Ross, Gordon & Bagnell

How: iteratively run the learned policy, collect expert labels on the visited states, aggregate and retrain; tested on two imitation-learning problems and a sequence-labelling benchmark.

Why this method: it comes with a guarantee that errors grow linearly with the horizon instead of quadratically.

Acrobatic and high-speed drones 🧪 Research · 2020–21 Learning High-Speed Flight in the Wild

How: a network trained only in simulation by imitating a privileged expert, with simulated sensor noise, flew a real quadrotor fast through forests and snow-covered terrain.

Why this method: the simulator's expert can label any state the learner reaches, so covariate shift is fixed DAgger-style at zero cost.

🤯 Fun fact · the 1989 network was tiny

ALVINN's input was 30 × 32 = 960 pixels, about what a single app icon on your phone holds today, and it drove a real van. Playground 1's camera has 48 pixels. The lesson both teach is the same one Tesla and Waymo still wrestle with: the hard part isn't fitting the demonstrations, it's what happens one step after you leave them.

🌉 How it connects

Behavior cloning learns the whole mapping from what the car sees to what it does, which is the seed of end-to-end driving (Lesson 9.2), where a second trap, learning the wrong cue, appears. Fixing covariate shift needs a place where the learner can make mistakes safely: learned world models (9.3) and simulators (9.4). The fleet data engine (9.5) is DAgger at company scale: the car drives, interesting moments get flagged, people or auto-labelers provide the answers, and the model retrains. The RL course covers the theory side, including GAIL and inverse RL: Imitation learning in the RL course, and its offline RL lesson for learning from logs without an expert on call.

Check yourself

1. A cloned policy errs on 2% of steps in states the expert visited (ε = 0.02). Over an episode of T = 50 steps, roughly how many mistakes should you expect if every mistake leads into unfamiliar states?
Σ (1 − 0.98t) for t = 1..50 ≈ 18.8. ε × T = 1 is what you'd get if mistakes were always recoverable (the DAgger regime); the compounding regime is about 19× worse here.
2. Your behavior-cloned car drifts out of its lane on test drives. A teammate proposes recording 10× more expert driving on the same roads in the same way. What's the most likely outcome?
More of the same distribution doesn't add the missing states (Playground 1). You need data from where the learner goes: recovery augmentation, noise injection, interventions or DAgger.
3. In DAgger, who decides which states get labelled, and who provides the labels?
That split is the whole trick: data comes from the learner's state distribution (so it covers its mistakes), and correctness comes from the expert. Aggregating with all earlier data keeps the expert's own states covered too.
📝 TL;DR
  • Behavior cloning = supervised learning on (observation, expert action). Easy, offline, and used from ALVINN (1989) to DAVE-2 (2016) to today's end-to-end stacks.
  • Driving is closed-loop, so the learner's inputs drift from the expert's: covariate shift. Errors compound: ε = 1%, T = 100 → about 37 mistakes, not 1.
  • More demos of the same kind don't help; demos that include recoveries do (side cameras, perturbations, noise injection).
  • DAgger: let the learner drive, have the expert label what it should do there, aggregate, retrain. Errors grow like εT instead of εT².
  • Tesla copies human driving at fleet scale and adds RL; Waymo's ChauffeurNet synthesized the worst cases; drone labs use privileged experts in simulation.
