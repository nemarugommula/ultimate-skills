Phase 9 · Lesson 2

End-to-end driving: photons to controls

For twenty years self-driving software was a relay race of hand-built modules: detect, track, predict, plan, control. End-to-end driving trains one big network from camera pixels to the car's path instead. Tesla bet its whole stack on it; Waymo and Wayve each took their own version. Why it's so powerful, and why it's so hard to trust.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The modular stack: perception → tracking → prediction → planning → control, each a separate piece with its own outputs (Lesson 0.3), and the modular vs end-to-end debate in outline (Lesson 0.4).
  • Behavior cloning and covariate shift: copying an expert's actions from logs, and why errors compound once the car leaves the expert's states (Lesson 9.1).
  • Logistic regression: a model that outputs a probability from a weighted sum of inputs, σ(w·x). Neural networks are stacks of these; see the Deep Learning course, Phase 1 (losses).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Two driving models are scored on a held-out log of expert driving. Model A predicts the expert's brake pedal correctly 96% of the time. Model B gets 82%. Same training data, same kind of model. You get to put one in the car.

Which one? Lock in your answer; Playground 2 will show you one of these models crashing in 15 of 20 test drives. And the stranger fact: the worse one on paper got worse because it was given more information.

Why this matters now: Tesla's FSD v12 release notes (December 2023) described city driving as “a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code” (as reproduced by Not a Tesla App), and Tesla's Q4 2023 letter says “V12 utilizes end-to-end training” (Tesla). Every big AV company now has an answer to “are you end-to-end?”.

✏️ Worked example: what a box can't say

A pedestrian stands 1.0 m from the curb, walking toward the road at 0.4 m/s. The perception module sends the planner exactly that: a box at (x, y), velocity 0.4 m/s, class “pedestrian”.

  • Case 1: she's looking at her phone, walking parallel to the curb with a slight drift.
  • Case 2: she's turned her head and is looking straight at your car, about to step out.

Both produce the same box. A planner downstream literally cannot tell them apart, so it must either brake for both (annoying, and dangerous if the car behind you doesn't expect it) or brake for neither (dangerous). The pixels held the answer, the head direction, but the interface threw it away. Engineers can add a “head direction” field, then a “holding a phone” field, then a “waving you through” field… and the list never ends. End-to-end training lets the network decide what to keep, because it's graded only on the final driving.

💡 Big idea End-to-end means the whole mapping from sensors to driving is trained on the driving objective, so nothing is lost at hand-designed interfaces and the system improves by adding data rather than code. The price: you can no longer test each piece on its own, it's hard to see why it did something, and it can learn a shortcut that predicts the expert in the logs without being the real reason the expert acted.
🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Modular stacks are made of hand-designed interfaces (boxes, tracks, lanes) and hand-written rules on top. Each interface drops information, each module's errors pass downstream unseen, and every new situation (a hand signal, a double-parked truck, a ball rolling into the road) needs new code. Tesla's own description of v12: replacing “over 300k lines of explicit C++ code”.
  2. What people tried. Better interfaces: richer outputs like occupancy grids and multi-modal predictions (Lessons 6.3 and 7.1). Learned planners on top of hand-built perception. Then planning-oriented networks that keep the modules but train them all together for planning, like UniAD (a CVPR 2023 best paper), and pure behavior cloning from pixels to steering like NVIDIA's 2016 DAVE-2 (Lesson 9.1).
  3. Why end-to-end works. The training signal is the thing you care about (did it drive like a good driver?), so the network keeps whatever helps, including cues no engineer listed. In Playground 1, a box-only model at a 0.3 threshold misses about 134 crossers per 1,000; letting the head direction through cuts that to about 61 at the same threshold, with slightly fewer needless brakes too. And it scales with data: more fleet video, not more code.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Validation: you can't unit-test a perception module that doesn't exist; you test the whole driver, mostly in closed loop (Lessons 9.4 and 9.6). Interpretability: when it brakes for nothing, there's no box list to inspect. Causal confusion: with every pixel available, the network may latch onto a cue that predicts the expert's action without causing it (Playground 2). Data hunger: rare events are rare in video too.
  5. What came next. Hybrids that keep learned embeddings flowing end to end but also output structured, checkable things (objects, lanes, trajectories) for validation, as Waymo describes its Foundation Model. Language-model backbones that bring world knowledge (Waymo's EMMA research, Wayve's LINGO work). Reinforcement learning after imitation (Tesla's v14.3). And closed-loop benchmarks and learned simulators to test the whole driver (Lessons 9.3 and 9.4).

The trap: learning the wrong cue

In 2019 de Haan, Jayaraman and Levine named a strange effect in imitation learning, causal misidentification: “access to more information can yield worse performance” (Causal Confusion in Imitation Learning). The model is trained to predict the expert's action, not to understand why the expert acted. If something in the input is a consequence of the expert's past actions, it can be the best predictor of all, and useless for driving.

Here's the classic kind of example. The camera sees part of the dashboard, where a light comes on whenever the brakes are pressed. Drivers hold the brake for a few seconds at a time, so “the light was on a moment ago” predicts “the driver is braking now” almost perfectly. The real reason the driver brakes, the car ahead getting close, is visible too, but blurrier. Guess which one an optimizer prefers.

🧩 Predict: a model that leans on the brake light. What happens when it drives itself, starting from cruising (light off)?
It has learned “brake when the light is on”. With the light off, it needs the car ahead to be very close before the distance cue alone outweighs the missing light, so it starts braking far too late. In the logs this never mattered, because the expert always started the braking (and turned the light on) on time. On the road, the model has to start the braking itself, and the shortcut can't do that. Open-loop accuracy looks great; closed-loop driving crashes.
🎮 Playground 2 · Causal confusion: the dashboard brake light

An expert follows a lead car that brakes at random, sometimes hard, holding the brake until it's at a comfortable distance. A logistic-regression “driver” is trained on the expert's logs. Its inputs: the time gap to the car ahead as estimated from the camera (a bit noisy) and, if you allow it, the dashboard brake light (on if the car braked a moment ago). Open-loop = accuracy at predicting the expert on new logs. Closed-loop = 20 test drives where the model drives.

What you just saw is the reason end-to-end teams obsess over closed-loop evaluation: the model that wins the log-replay leaderboard (96%) is the one that crashes, and the model that “only” gets 82% drives perfectly. Masking the shortcut is one fix; de Haan et al. propose targeted interventions, asking the expert or the environment which inputs really matter. Real shortcuts are subtler than a dashboard light: the car's own speed and recent path (the “ego status”), a time of day that happens to correlate with traffic, the lead car's brake lights versus its actual distance. Codevilla et al. (2019) list “the lack of a causal model” among behavior cloning's core limitations for driving (paper). Lesson 9.4 shows the ego-status shortcut fooling a whole research benchmark.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Open end-to-end research mostly runs on public datasets and simulators: nuScenes and the Waymo Open Dataset for open-loop training, nuPlan (Motional's closed-loop planning benchmark), NAVSIM (a middle ground: non-reactive simulation scored on real data), Bench2Drive (closed-loop tests in CARLA with 44 scenarios over 220 routes), and CARLA itself. Models are ordinary PyTorch. The core training loop of an imitation-trained planner is short:

import torch, torch.nn as nn

class TinyDriver(nn.Module):                       # camera frames → future path (end to end)
    def __init__(self, horizon=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, 32, 5, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Conv2d(32, 64, 5, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1), nn.Flatten())
        self.head = nn.Linear(64 + 2, horizon * 2)      # + 2 = route command (e.g. turn left / go straight)
    def forward(self, img, cmd):
        return self.head(torch.cat([self.enc(img), cmd], 1)).view(-1, 8, 2)   # 8 future (x, y) waypoints

model = TinyDriver(); opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), 3e-4)
for img, cmd, expert_path in loader:               # logged human driving
    loss = (model(img, cmd) - expert_path).abs().mean()  # L1 imitation loss on the waypoints
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
# Notice: nothing here feeds in the car's own speed or past actions. Adding them lowers
# this loss quickly... and invites the shortcut of Playground 2 (see Lesson 9.4).

Outputting a short path of waypoints (and letting a simple controller from Phase 8 follow it) is the common design in research; it's easier to check for collisions than raw steering and pedal commands.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / Wayve do it

🚘 Tesla

The most committed to end-to-end. v12 (release notes Dec 2023, wide release in 2024) replaced hand-written driving code with one network trained on video. Tesla's Q4 2024 letter credits v13 with a “4.2x increase in data, higher resolution video inputs, 2x reduction in photon-to-control latency and redesigned controller”. v14 (October 2025) brought “a large portion of the Robotaxi FSD model to consumers”, and Tesla's Jan 2026 letter calls it an “end-to-end foundation model trained on both customer and Robotaxi real-world data”. The product is still named FSD (Supervised): a human must supervise.

Sources: Tesla Q4 2024, Q3 2025, Q4 2025, tesla.com/fsd

🚕 Waymo

Explicitly hybrid. Its Dec 2025 post says the Waymo Foundation Model “provides significant benefits over the pure end-to-end or modular approaches”: learned embeddings flow between components and it “supports full end-to-end signal backpropagation during training”, while “compact, materialized structured representations like objects, semantic attributes, and roadgraph elements” allow safety validation. It pairs a fast sensor-fusion encoder (camera, lidar, radar) with a Gemini-based driving VLM. Its Oct 2024 EMMA research mapped camera pixels straight to trajectories with a multimodal language model, and listed limits: up to 4 frames, no lidar or radar.

Sources: Waymo, Dec 2025, EMMA blog, EMMA paper

🇬🇧 Wayve

The London startup built around end-to-end “embodied AI” from the start, and the source of the GAIA world models (Lesson 9.3). Nissan announced in Dec 2025 that its next-generation ProPILOT will integrate “Wayve AI technology”, with the first model planned in Japan in fiscal year 2027; Uber and Wayve announced plans in June 2025 for public-road trials of Level 4 vehicles in London. (For drones, the end-to-end story is research-stage: Lesson 9.1's high-speed forest flight maps sensor data straight to trajectories.)

Sources: Nissan, Dec 2025, GAIA-1, high-speed flight

Why they differ: Tesla sells millions of camera-only cars, so its edge is video volume, and an end-to-end network is the design that turns volume into skill. Waymo runs driverless robotaxis today and must convince regulators and itself that each release is safe, so it keeps structured outputs it can check. Wayve has neither a huge fleet nor HD maps everywhere, so it bets on a network that generalizes to new cities and carmakers' hardware.

⚖️ Trade-offs
Choice✅ Gains❌ Costs
Modular, hand-designed interfacesEach module testable alone; inspectable; easy to add rules (“never enter a red-light intersection”)Information lost at every interface; errors cascade; endless special cases in code
Pure end-to-end (pixels → path or controls)Keeps every useful cue; improves with data; one model to optimizeHard to validate and explain; shortcuts and causal confusion; needs huge, well-curated data
Hybrid (end-to-end training + structured outputs)Gradient flows through everything, yet objects, lanes and paths can be checkedMore complex training; the structured outputs constrain what the model can express
Language-model backbone (EMMA, LINGO)World knowledge (“a school bus with flashing lights”); can explain itself in wordsBig and slow for real-time control; explanations aren't guaranteed to be the real reason
🎤 Interview angle

“Would you build our driving stack end-to-end or modular?” Interviewers want trade-offs, not a side:

  • Explain the lossy interface argument with an example (head direction, hand signals) and the scaling argument (data instead of code).
  • Explain the costs: validation without module-level tests, interpretability, and causal confusion, with a concrete shortcut (brake light, ego speed).
  • Propose a hybrid: end-to-end training with structured intermediate outputs for monitoring, a rule-based safety checker on the final trajectory, and closed-loop simulation as the main metric.
  • Mention where it would run: a camera-only consumer car under human supervision is a different risk budget from a driverless robotaxi.
🏭 In the real world
Tesla FSD v12 → v14 🏢 Company · 2023–2026 Tesla Q1 2026 update

How: one network from the car's cameras to driving, trained on fleet video (v12), scaled up with more data, higher-resolution video and lower photon-to-control latency (v13), merged with the Robotaxi model (v14), with an upgraded reinforcement-learning stage in v14.3 (April 2026).

Why this method: camera-only hardware plus millions of cars means data is plentiful and code is the bottleneck.

Waymo Foundation Model 🚗 AV · 2025 Demonstrably Safe AI

How: a sensor-fusion encoder and a driving VLM feed a World Decoder that predicts other road users, produces maps and generates trajectories, trained end to end but with structured outputs.

Why this method: keep the learning benefits while keeping things a safety case can check.

UniAD 🧪 Research · 2023 Planning-oriented Autonomous Driving

How: perception, prediction and planning tasks in one network, connected by learned queries and all trained toward planning; a CVPR 2023 best paper.

Why this method: separate modules “might suffer from accumulative errors or deficient task coordination”, as the paper puts it.

Closed-loop benchmarks 🧰 Open source Bench2Drive

How: nuPlan (2021) scores planners in closed loop with reactive agents on real logs; NAVSIM (2024) uses short non-reactive simulation; Bench2Drive (2024) runs end-to-end models through 220 routes in CARLA.

Why this method: open-loop scores rewarded shortcuts (Lesson 9.4); driving the model is the honest test.

🤯 Fun fact · “photon-to-control”

Tesla's own shareholder letter uses the phrase this lesson is named after: v13 halved the photon-to-control latency, the time from light hitting the camera sensor to the car acting on it. At 30 m/s (about 108 km/h), every 100 ms saved is 3 meters of road the car reacts sooner. In an end-to-end design there's no pipeline of modules to wait on, just one network to make faster.

🌉 How it connects

End-to-end driving is behavior cloning at scale (Lesson 9.1), so it inherits covariate shift and adds causal confusion. It can't be judged on logs, which is why learned world models (9.3) and closed-loop simulation (9.4) matter so much, and why the fleet data engine (9.5) and safety validation (9.6) are the real bottlenecks. The modules it replaces are Phases 6–8: BEV perception and occupancy (6.2, 6.3), prediction (7.1), planning (7.6) and control (8.3). For the networks themselves, see the Deep Learning course, Phase 4 (vision transformers) and Phase 6 (the transformer block).

Check yourself

1. A car at 25 m/s cuts its photon-to-control latency from 200 ms to 100 ms. How much sooner (in road distance) does it start reacting?
Distance = speed × time saved = 25 m/s × 0.1 s = 2.5 m. That's half a car length of extra braking room, from software alone.
2. A driving model scores 96% at predicting the expert's braking on held-out logs, but crashes in closed loop. Removing one input drops it to 82% offline and stops the crashes. What happened?
Causal confusion: a cue like “the brake light was on a moment ago” predicts the next action in logs but can't start a braking event on its own. Offline accuracy rewards it; driving punishes it (Playground 2).
3. Which design best matches Waymo's December 2025 description of its Foundation Model?
Waymo says it “provides significant benefits over the pure end-to-end or modular approaches”: full backpropagation in training, plus compact structured representations for safety validation, simulation and evaluation.
📝 TL;DR
  • Modular stacks pass hand-designed summaries (boxes, tracks) between modules; each interface can drop the cue you needed (head direction, hand signals).
  • End-to-end trains sensors → driving on the driving objective: nothing lost at interfaces, and it improves with data instead of code.
  • Costs: hard to validate module by module, hard to explain, and prone to causal confusion: a 96%-accurate model on logs can crash 15 drives in 20.
  • Tesla is all-in (v12 → v14, “photon-to-control”); Waymo is hybrid with structured, checkable outputs; Wayve built end-to-end from day one.
  • Judge end-to-end models by driving them in closed loop (Lesson 9.4), not by log accuracy.
