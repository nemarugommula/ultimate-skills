Phase 1 · Lesson 1

Coordinate frames: world, body, sensor

“2 m ahead” is not a place on the map. Every number a robot measures is written in some frame: the world's, its own body's, or one sensor's. Learn to ask “in which frame?” and half of all robotics bugs disappear.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector is a list of numbers like (2, 1): “2 along the first axis, 1 along the second”. Refresher: vectors and adding vectors in the Math course.
  • Cos and sin of an angle: walk 1 m in direction θ and you move cos θ along x and sin θ along y. cos 90° = 0, sin 90° = 1.
  • The autonomy loop: sense → perceive → localize → plan → act (Lesson 0.1). Today is about the language every box in that loop uses to say where.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A delivery robot sits at (10, 5) on the warehouse map, facing “up” the map (heading 90°). Its lidar reports an obstacle 2 m ahead and 1 m to the left.

Where is the obstacle on the map? Many people answer (12, 6): add 2 to x and 1 to y. Is that right? Lock in your guess on the map below before reading on.

This isn't a toy problem. The dataset Waymo released for researchers in 2019 records five lidars and five cameras, and every one of them has its own frame, plus a frame for the car and one for the world. The paper's first technical section is simply called “Coordinate Systems” (Sun et al., Waymo Open Dataset).

What a frame is

A coordinate frame is three things: an origin (where 0 is), a set of axes (which way x, y and z point) and a unit (meters, always, in robotics). Numbers mean nothing until you say which frame they're in. The same obstacle from the puzzle has different coordinates in every frame:

FrameAttached toSame obstacle, in this frameWho thinks in it
map (world)The building, fixed forever(9, 7)Planner, map, fleet dispatch
base_link (body)The robot's chassis, moves with it(2, 1): 2 ahead, 1 leftObstacle avoidance, controller
laser (sensor)The lidar's spinning head(1.7, 1) if the lidar sits 0.3 m ahead of the robot's centerThe lidar driver

Three families show up in every robot, car and drone:

  • World frames don't move with the robot: the map, a GPS-anchored local frame, the drone's take-off point.
  • The body frame is glued to the vehicle. “Ahead”, “left” and “up” are its axes. For a car it's usually the center of the rear axle; for a drone, its center.
  • Sensor frames are glued to each sensor. Every lidar, camera, radar and IMU (inertial measurement unit: the gyro and accelerometer chip) reports in its own frame, because that's all it can know.

Numbers first: solve the puzzle by hand

✏️ Worked example: from “2 ahead, 1 left” to the map

The robot faces heading θ = 90°, so its forward axis points along the map's +y, and its left axis points along the map's −x. Walk the obstacle out step by step:

  1. Start at the robot: (10, 5).
  2. Go 2 m forward. Forward is map +y, so add (0, 2) → (10, 7).
  3. Go 1 m left. Left is map −x, so add (−1, 0) → (9, 7).

The general recipe, for any heading θ, is the same two moves written with cos and sin (Lesson 1.2 shows where it comes from):

xmap = xrobot + cosθ · xbody − sinθ · ybody
ymap = yrobot + sinθ · xbody + cosθ · ybody With θ = 90°: x = 10 + 0·2 − 1·1 = 9 and y = 5 + 1·2 + 0·1 = 7. The answer (12, 6) would only be right if the robot faced along the map's x axis (θ = 0°).

And the sensor frame? If the lidar sits 0.3 m ahead of the robot's center (and isn't rotated), a point the lidar calls (1.7, 1) is (1.7 + 0.3, 1) = (2, 1) in the body frame. Two hops, sensor → body → world: that chain is the whole of Lesson 1.5.

💡 Big idea A position is a vector plus the name of its frame. Never pass “(2, 1)” around a robot; pass “(2, 1) in base_link”. Converting between frames is just two moves: rotate the vector into the other frame's directions, then shift it by where that frame's origin sits.
🧠 Analogy: directions vs addresses

“The café is two blocks ahead, on your left” is a body-frame description: perfect for you, useless to a friend across town. “12 Elm Street” is a world-frame description: anyone can use it, but you have to know where you are and which way you face to walk there. Your phone's map app converts between the two all day long, by knowing your position and your heading. So does every robot.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Every sensor reports in its own frame: the lidar from its spinning head, each camera from its lens, the IMU from its chip. A planner needs everything in one shared frame to decide where to drive. Without an explicit system, every team hard-codes its own “add 0.3 to x” somewhere, and nobody knows which numbers are in which frame.
  2. What people tried. Put everything straight into one global frame (but sensors physically measure relative to themselves, and a robot's own world position is uncertain and jumps). Use latitude and longitude for everything (not Cartesian: one degree of longitude is a different number of meters at different latitudes, and big numbers lose precision in 32-bit floats). Hand-written conversions in each program (sign bugs, copy-paste drift, silent breakage when a sensor is remounted).
  3. Why named frames work. Give every rigid thing its own frame, agree on conventions (ROS: x forward, y left, z up), and store one transform per pair of connected frames. Then any number can be moved to any frame mechanically: “2 m ahead, 1 m left” at (10, 5) facing 90° → (9, 7) on the map, every time, by the same formula. Sensor positions live in one place, so remounting a sensor is a one-line change.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Bookkeeping: every message must carry its frame's name, and conventions differ between communities (robots use ENU, aircraft and PX4 drones use NED, cameras point z forward). Mixing two conventions gives bugs that look like “the drone flies into the ground” or “obstacles appear behind the car”. If the stored sensor position is wrong (a bumped camera), every converted point is wrong by the same amount, silently.
  5. What came next. Rotation matrices make the cos/sin recipe clean and 3D (Lessons 1.2–1.3), quaternions store 3D orientation safely (1.4), 4×4 homogeneous transforms chain frames by plain matrix multiplication (1.5), and ROS's tf2 library keeps the whole tree of frames, through time, for you (1.6).

The conventions everyone agrees on (mostly)

The robotics community wrote its conventions down in two short documents, REP 103 and REP 105 (REP: ROS Enhancement Proposal; ROS is the Robot Operating System, the most common robotics software framework). Learn these once and you can read almost any robot's data.

FramexyzWhere it's used
Body (REP 103)forwardleftupbase_link of every ROS robot; Waymo's vehicle frame

Camera “_optical” (REP 103)rightdownforward (out of the lens)Images: pixel u grows right, v grows down (Lesson 2.1)

Body, FRDforwardrightdownPX4 drones, aircraft

All of these are right-handed: point your right hand's fingers along x, curl them toward y, and your thumb gives z. REP 103 also fixes the units: meters, seconds and radians.

map, odom and base_link: two kinds of “world”

REP 105 names the frames of a mobile robot. The surprising part is that there are two world frames, because a robot's sense of its own position comes in two flavors:

FrameGuaranteeWeaknessPublished by
mapDoesn't drift: good for “where am I in the building?”Can jump when localization corrects itselfLocalization (it publishes map → odom)
odomContinuous: the robot's pose changes smoothly, never jumpsDrifts without bound (wheel slip adds up)Odometry (odom → base_link)
base_linkRigidly attached to the robotMoves with it(the robot itself)

They're stacked as a chain: map → odom → base_link → sensors. Lesson 1.6 explains why it's a chain and not two separate links.

🧩 Predict: your robot's localization recognizes a pillar and its map position snaps 20 cm sideways. Your obstacle-avoidance code tracks a box 1 m ahead. Should that code work in the map frame or the odom frame?
odom (or base_link). In the map frame, the 20 cm snap would make the box appear to jump 20 cm, and the avoider might swerve at nothing. In odom, motion is always smooth; the small drift doesn't matter over the next few meters. REP 105 says exactly this: odom is accurate in the short term, map in the long term. Planning a route across the building uses map; dodging the box uses odom.

ENU vs NED: why drones fly “upside down”

Robots and maps use ENU (x East, y North, z Up). Aviation has used NED (x North, y East, z Down) for decades: z down means a positive altitude rate is a descent, and a compass heading of 0° is north. PX4, the open-source autopilot inside many drones, uses NED for the world and FRD (forward, right, down) for the body. Its ROS 2 guide warns that “the local/world and body frames used by ROS and PX4 are different” and that ROS 2 nodes talking to PX4 must convert.

✏️ Worked example: one drone, two descriptions

A drone hovers 30 m east and 40 m north of its take-off point, 10 m up, with its nose pointing north-east.

ENU (ROS)NED (PX4)Rule
Position(30, 40, 10)(40, 30, −10)swap x and y, negate z
Heading (yaw)45° (counter-clockwise from East)45° (clockwise from North)yawNED = 90° − yawENU
Nose pointing Northyaw 90°yaw 0°90° − 90° = 0°

North-east happens to be 45° in both. Point the nose East and ENU says 0° while NED says 90°. The body frames differ too: ROS's left (y) and up (z) are PX4's −right and −down, a 180° turn about the forward axis.

🧩 Predict: a ROS 2 program wants the drone to climb to 5 m and sends PX4 a position setpoint with z = 5, copied straight from its ENU code. What does the drone try to do?
In NED, z = +5 means 5 m below the origin. The drone tries to descend into the ground (in practice it lands or hits it). The correct NED setpoint is z = −5. This is why PX4 ships a frame_transforms library with its ROS 2 tools, and why the first thing to check in any drone bug is “which frame is this number in?”.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The body → world conversion is three lines of NumPy; write it once and you'll recognize it inside every library. On a ROS 2 robot you never write it by hand: tf2 (Lesson 1.6) stores the frames and does it for you, and its command-line tools show what's going on.

import numpy as np

def body_to_world(pose, p_body):
    """pose = (x, y, heading in radians) of the robot in the world; p_body = (forward, left)."""
    x, y, th = pose
    c, s = np.cos(th), np.sin(th)
    return np.array([x + c * p_body[0] - s * p_body[1],
                     y + s * p_body[0] + c * p_body[1]])

body_to_world((10, 5, np.pi / 2), (2, 1))    # → array([9., 7.])  the puzzle
# On a running ROS 2 robot:
ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames            # draws the frame tree (map → odom → base_link → ...) to a PDF
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo map base_link   # prints the transform between two frames, live

For drones, PX4's px4_ros_com package provides a frame_transforms library for the ENU ↔ NED and FLU ↔ FRD conversions, so you don't hand-roll them.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

At Tesla AI Day 2021, Andrej Karpathy (then head of AI) showed how Tesla's networks stopped predicting things separately in each of the eight camera images and instead fuse all cameras into one “vector space” around the car: a single, vehicle-centered, top-down (bird's-eye view) frame where lanes, cars and curbs live. That's a frame choice: predictions come out in the car's frame, ready for planning, instead of in eight image frames that would each need converting. Tesla hasn't published its exact frame conventions.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021 (Tesla's video)

🚕 Waymo

The Waymo Open Dataset paper spells out the frames of a Waymo car: a global frame that is East-North-Up, a vehicle frame with x forward, y left, z up (exactly REP 103's body frame), a frame per lidar and camera, and image frames. Each vehicle pose is stored as a 4×4 transform from vehicle to global, and each sensor's “extrinsics” as a 4×4 transform from sensor to vehicle (Lesson 1.5). Fun twist: in that dataset a camera's x axis points out of the lens, not z as in ROS optical frames. Always read the conventions.

Sources: Waymo Open Dataset paper (Sec. 3.2)

🛸 Drones (PX4)

PX4 does everything in NED for the world and FRD for the body. Its docs say these conventions “are adopted on all PX4 topics” unless a message says otherwise, and give the conversions: ENU → NED is a 90° turn about z plus a 180° turn about x; FLU → FRD is a 180° turn about the forward axis. ROS 2 users must convert vectors and attitudes on the way in and out.

Sources: PX4 ROS 2 user guide, PX4 notation

Why they differ: drones inherit aviation, where “down” is the natural direction for altitude and compasses start at north, so NED won. Ground robots and maps grew up with math and ROS, so ENU and x-forward-y-left won. Tesla's choice is about networks: predicting directly in one car-centered frame lets a network combine eight cameras itself, instead of leaving the fusion to hand-written geometry afterwards.

🎤 Interview angle

“A detection arrives from the lidar. Walk me through getting it onto the map.” A strong answer:

  • Names the frames: sensor → base_link → odom → map, and says which component publishes each link (calibration, odometry, localization).
  • Explains the two moves per hop (rotate, then shift) and does a quick number: (2, 1) at (10, 5) facing 90° is (9, 7).
  • Knows REP 103 (x forward, y left, z up, ENU, radians) and the camera optical frame; mentions the NED/FRD difference when drones or PX4 come up.
  • Uses odom for short-term reactions and map for long-term goals, and explains why (continuity vs drift).
🏭 In the real world
REP 103 and REP 105 🧰 Open source REP 105: Coordinate Frames for Mobile Platforms

How: two short documents fix units, axis directions and the names map, odom, base_link and earth, plus who publishes each transform.

Why this method: a shared convention means a lidar driver, a localization package and a planner written by three different teams fit together without a meeting.

Waymo Open Dataset 🧪 Research · 2019 Scalability in Perception for Autonomous Driving

How: every lidar point and camera image comes with its frame and the transforms needed to move it to the vehicle and global frames.

Why this method: researchers fuse five lidars and five cameras recorded at different instants and positions; without explicit frames, that fusion is impossible.

PX4 ↔ ROS 2 bridge 🧰 Open source PX4 ROS 2 user guide

How: PX4 publishes NED/FRD; ROS 2 code expects ENU/FLU; a small conversion library sits at the boundary.

Why this method: each side keeps the convention its own tools assume, and the conversion lives in exactly one tested place.

🤯 Fun fact · ROS's “zero heading” points East

For geographic poses, REP 103 says yaw is zero when pointing east and grows counter-clockwise, and adds that this “differs from a traditional compass bearing”, which is zero at north and grows clockwise. Hardware drivers (like a GPS compass) must convert before publishing. A compass bearing of 90° (East) is a ROS yaw of 0°; a compass bearing of 0° (North) is a ROS yaw of 90° (REP 103).

Check yourself

1. A robot at map position (4, 2) faces heading −90° (straight “down” the map). Its lidar sees a box 3 m straight ahead. Where is the box on the map?
Forward is the map's −y direction (cos(−90°) = 0, sin(−90°) = −1), so 3 m ahead adds (0, −3): (4, 2 − 3) = (4, −1). (7, 2) is the “just add to x” mistake again.
2. Your ROS 2 node sends a drone running PX4 a position setpoint of z = +20 m, meaning “20 m up”. What happens?
PX4 uses NED on its topics unless a message says otherwise, so z = +20 means 20 m down. Convert at the boundary (ENU → NED: swap x and y, negate z), ideally with a tested library such as PX4's frame_transforms.
3. Localization makes your robot's map pose jump 30 cm when it sees a landmark. Which frame should the local obstacle-avoidance code use?
REP 105: odom is continuous but drifts; map doesn't drift but can jump. Short-term reactions want continuity, so they use odom or base_link. Staying only in the lidar frame breaks as soon as you combine two sensors or remember an obstacle while turning.
📝 TL;DR
  • A frame is an origin + axes + units. A coordinate without its frame's name is a bug waiting to happen.
  • Three families: world (map, odom), body (base_link), sensor (laser, camera…). Converting = rotate into the new axes, then shift by the origin: (2, 1) at (10, 5) facing 90° → (9, 7).
  • REP 103: x forward, y left, z up, ENU for the world, meters and radians; camera optical frames are z forward, x right, y down.
  • REP 105: map (no drift, may jump) → odom (smooth, drifts) → base_link.
  • PX4 drones use NED + FRD: swap x/y and negate z for positions; yawNED = 90° − yawENU. Tesla predicts in one car-centered vector space; Waymo stores every sensor's frame explicitly.
