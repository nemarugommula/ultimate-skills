Phase 1 · Lesson 5

Homogeneous transforms: chaining frames

Rotate, then shift, then do it again for the next frame… Add a single “1” to every point and the whole chain collapses into multiplying matrices. That's how a lidar point travels from the sensor to the map, and why one degree of calibration error can cost you a pedestrian's width.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Frames: a point's coordinates depend on the frame; sensor → body → world (Lesson 1.1).
  • Rotate, then shift: p′ = R p + t, and R⁻¹ = Rᵀ (Lesson 1.2); 3D rotations as 3×3 matrices or quaternions (Lessons 1.3, 1.4).
  • Matrix multiplication chains transformations; the order is the order of application read right to left. Refresher: matrix multiplication.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A car wash brush knocks the roof lidar of your test car. It's now turned 1° from where the calibration file says it is, and shifted by 1 cm. A pedestrian stands 50 m ahead. How far off will the car place the pedestrian on its map? About 1 cm? About 9 cm? About 87 cm?

Car makers take this seriously. Tesla's Model 3 owner's manual says the cameras calibrate themselves over the first 20–25 miles of driving, and that if “a camera has shifted from its calibrated position (for example, the camera or windshield was replaced)” you must clear the calibration and let it redo it (Tesla). Waymo's open dataset ships a 4×4 calibration matrix for every one of its ten sensors. By the end of this lesson you'll know why.

🧩 Predict: 1° of rotation error and 1 cm of shift error, pedestrian 50 m away. Which error matters more, and what's the total?
The rotation, by far. A 1° turn moves a point 50 m away sideways by 50 × sin 1° = 0.87 m. The 1 cm shift moves every point by 1 cm, no matter how far. Total: about 87 cm, half a lane width. Translation errors stay the same size; rotation errors grow with distance. You'll find the misplaced lidar yourself in Playground 2.

The trick: one extra number

Chaining “rotate, then shift” by hand gets messy: pmap = R1(R2 p + t2) + t1, and it gets worse with every frame. The fix is to write every 2D point as (x, y, 1) and every rotate-and-shift as one 3×3 matrix:

T = R₁₁R₁₂txR₂₁R₂₂ty001   × xy1 = R₁₁x + R₁₂y + txR₂₁x + R₂₂y + ty1 The extra 1 picks up the shift column, so “R p + t” becomes a single multiplication. In 3D the same trick gives a 4×4 matrix: the 3×3 rotation in the corner, the translation in the last column, and (0, 0, 0, 1) at the bottom. These are homogeneous transforms.

Now chaining is just multiplying. Use the naming convention Ta_b = “the pose of frame b in frame a”, which turns coordinates in b into coordinates in a. Then the inner names cancel, like units:

Tmap_lidar = Tmap_base · Tbase_lidar     pmap = Tmap_lidar · plidar “base” appears on the inside of both, so it cancels and leaves map_lidar. If your names don't cancel, the product is wrong: a free bug detector.
✏️ Worked example: a lidar point onto the map

The car is at map (10, 5) with heading 90°. Its lidar sits 1.5 m ahead of the car's origin, facing forward. The lidar sees a point 20 m straight ahead: (20, 0).

TransformMatrixMeaning
Tmap_base[[0, −1, 10], [1, 0, 5], [0, 0, 1]]turn 90° (cos 0, sin 1), car origin at (10, 5)
Tbase_lidar[[1, 0, 1.5], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1]]no turn, 1.5 m forward
Tmap_lidar = product[[0, −1, 10], [1, 0, 6.5], [0, 0, 1]]the lidar is at map (10, 6.5), facing 90°

Apply it to (20, 0, 1): x = 0 × 20 − 1 × 0 + 10 = 10, y = 1 × 20 + 0 × 0 + 6.5 = 26.5. The point is at map (10, 26.5): 21.5 m “up” the map from the car, as it should be (20 m from the lidar, which is 1.5 m ahead of the car).

Going backwards: the inverse

To move a map point into the car's frame you need Tbase_map = Tmap_base−1. No general inverse needed; undo the shift, then the turn:

T−1 = Rᵀ−Rᵀ t01 Check with Lesson 1.1's puzzle: Tmap_base has R = 90°, t = (10, 5). Rᵀ t = (5, −10), so the inverse's shift is (−5, 10). The map point (9, 7) → Rᵀ(9, 7) + (−5, 10) = (7, −9) + (−5, 10) = (2, 1): “2 m ahead, 1 m left”. ✅

One more subtlety: points get the 1 (x, y, z, 1), so they are shifted. Directions such as velocities or surface normals get a 0 (x, y, z, 0), so they're only rotated: a car's velocity doesn't change because you measured it from the roof instead of the bumper.

💡 Big idea A homogeneous transform packs a rotation and a translation into one matrix, so moving between frames is multiplying, chaining is multiplying, undoing is a cheap inverse. Name them Ta_b and check that the inner names cancel. And remember the asymmetry: translation errors stay small, rotation errors grow with distance (about d × δ).
🧠 Analogy: currency conversion

Converting dollars to euros to yen: (yen per euro) × (euros per dollar) × dollars. The “euros” cancel, and if you multiply in the wrong order the units don't cancel and you know you've slipped. Tmap_base · Tbase_lidar is exactly that, with frames instead of currencies. The extra “1” is the trick that lets a fixed fee (the shift) ride along inside the exchange rate.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. Real robots have long chains: map → odom → base_link → roof rack → lidar, or map → drone → gimbal yaw → gimbal pitch → camera. Applying R and t one hop at a time, by hand, in every program, is slow to write and easy to get wrong (forget one shift, use one R instead of Rᵀ).
  2. What people tried. Separate rotation and translation everywhere (correct, but each composition needs the formula R₁R₂ and R₁t₂ + t₁, and inverses need −Rᵀt: easy to botch). Euler angles plus offsets in config files (convention mix-ups, Lesson 1.3). Pre-computing one fixed sensor → map offset (breaks as soon as the robot moves).
  3. Why homogeneous transforms work. One matrix per hop, multiplication for chains, a closed-form inverse, and the same code for every frame pair. Worked example: [[0, −1, 10], [1, 0, 5]] · [[1, 0, 1.5], [0, 1, 0]] → lidar at (10, 6.5), point (20, 0) → map (10, 26.5). With 4×4 matrices, transforming a whole point cloud is one matrix product, which GPUs and vector units do extremely fast; graphics engines have used 4×4 matrices for this for decades.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. 16 numbers to store 6 degrees of freedom, and the rotation block can drift from a true rotation after many products (re-orthonormalize, or store a quaternion and translation). The math is only as good as the numbers in it: a stale or wrong extrinsic silently moves everything, with errors growing with distance (1° → 87 cm at 50 m). And it can't represent motion over time by itself; that needs timestamps (Lesson 1.6).
  5. What came next. Transform libraries like ROS's tf2 store the whole tree with timestamps and chain automatically (Lesson 1.6); calibration methods estimate extrinsics from data (Lesson 2.2 and 2.7); Lie-group tools (SE(3)) let optimizers adjust poses smoothly in SLAM and bundle adjustment (Phases 4 and 5).

Why extrinsics have to be so precise

The fixed transform from the car's body to a sensor is called the sensor's extrinsic calibration (“extrinsics”). It never changes while driving, so it's measured once, carefully. How carefully? A rotation error δ moves a point at distance d by about d · δ:

Distance1 cm shift error0.1° rotation error1° rotation error
10 m1 cm1.7 cm17 cm
50 m1 cm8.7 cm87 cm
100 m1 cm17 cm1.75 m (half a lane)

Long-range sensors make angle errors expensive. At highway speed, a car needs to know which lane a stopped vehicle 100 m ahead is in; a 1° error puts it half a lane off.

🧩 Predict: in the circle drive, the car always faces along the circle with the pole on its left at the same distance. Why does that make the extrinsic impossible to recover?
The lidar sees the pole from the same body-relative direction and distance at every stop, so all 16 measurements are identical in the car's frame. Any extrinsic error then moves all 16 estimates the same way relative to the car, and the circle's symmetry hides it: some wrong combinations of x, y and yaw stack them perfectly. Calibration needs excitation: varied views, turns, distances and directions. Real calibration routines (and Tesla's “drive 20–25 miles”) rely on varied driving for the same reason.

In 3D: a drone's gimbal camera

The same chain works with 4×4 matrices. A drone carries a camera on a tilting gimbal. To know which pixel a point on the ground lands on, chain map → drone → gimbal → camera, then switch to the camera's optical frame (z out of the lens, x right, y down, REP 103) so the pinhole projection of Lesson 2.1 works: pixel u = fx · x / z + cx, v = fy · y / z + cy.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Build 4×4 matrices with NumPy, invert them with the Rᵀ trick, and transform whole point clouds in one product. Open3D's point clouds take a 4×4 directly, and in ROS 2 tf2_geometry_msgs applies a looked-up transform to a message (Lesson 1.6).

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation as R

def make_T(rot, t):
    T = np.eye(4); T[:3, :3] = rot; T[:3, 3] = t
    return T

def invert_T(T):
    Rt = T[:3, :3].T
    return make_T(Rt, -Rt @ T[:3, 3])      # [Rᵀ, −Rᵀt]: no np.linalg.inv needed

T_map_base  = make_T(R.from_euler("z", 90, degrees=True).as_matrix(), [10, 5, 0])
T_base_lidar = make_T(np.eye(3), [1.5, 0, 1.8])                  # roof lidar, 1.8 m up
T_map_lidar = T_map_base @ T_base_lidar                                 # inner names cancel

cloud = np.random.rand(100_000, 3) * 50                              # N × 3 points in the lidar frame
hom = np.hstack([cloud, np.ones((len(cloud), 1))])                     # add the 1
cloud_map = (hom @ T_map_lidar.T)[:, :3]                                 # rows: p @ Tᵀ

import open3d as o3d
pcd = o3d.geometry.PointCloud(o3d.utility.Vector3dVector(cloud))
pcd.transform(T_map_lidar)                                              # same thing, in place

Unit test your chain with one hand-checked point (like the worked example) before trusting it on a million. And keep a test that the inner names cancel: write Ta_b as T_a_b in code and review products by eye.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

The Waymo Open Dataset is this lesson in data form: each frame stores the vehicle pose as a 4×4 transform from the vehicle frame to the global (East-North-Up) frame, and each sensor's extrinsics as a 4×4 transform from sensor to vehicle. A lidar point reaches the world as Tglobal_vehicle · Tvehicle_lidar · p. The paper notes the dataset “contains all information needed to transform data between any two frames”.

Sources: Waymo Open Dataset paper (Sec. 3.2)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's manuals describe the user-visible side of extrinsic calibration: cameras calibrate during the first 20–25 miles (32–40 km) of driving (contact Tesla if it isn't done after 100 miles), and after a camera or windshield replacement you clear the calibration so it can be redone. Tesla hasn't published the algorithm; driving is what supplies the varied views (the “excitation” of Playground 2).

Sources: Tesla Model 3 manual: Cameras

🛸 Drones (PX4)

Small offsets matter on drones too. PX4's EKF2 has EKF2_IMU_POS_X/Y/Z: the IMU's position relative to the vehicle's center of gravity, in the body frame. The docs say the position and velocity states are “adjusted to account for the offset between the IMU and the body frame” before reaching the controllers: a lever-arm correction, exactly the translation part of a transform. A spinning drone with an off-center IMU would otherwise feel fake accelerations.

Sources: PX4 EKF2 docs, parameter reference

Why they differ: Waymo's many long-range sensors make extrinsics a precision-engineering problem, measured and stored per sensor. Tesla's fleet of millions can't be calibrated in a lab one by one, so each car calibrates itself while driving. A drone's sensors are centimeters apart, so the translation offsets are small but still corrected, because rotation rates are high and lever arms turn spin into apparent acceleration.

🏭 In the real world
Multi-sensor datasets 🧪 Research · 2019 Waymo Open Dataset

How: per-frame vehicle poses and per-sensor extrinsics as 4×4 matrices; users chain them to fuse lidar, cameras and time.

Why this method: any two frames can be related by multiplying stored matrices, with no convention guesswork.

Self-calibrating cameras 🏢 Company · current Tesla Model 3 manual

How: the car estimates its cameras' calibration while driving, and redoes it after a camera or windshield replacement.

Why this method: lab calibration doesn't scale to millions of cars and doesn't survive repairs.

IMU lever arm 🧰 Open source PX4 EKF2

How: the IMU's offset from the center of gravity is a fixed translation the estimator removes from its outputs.

Why this method: the controller wants the motion of the vehicle's center, not of a chip a few centimeters away.

Point cloud processing 🧰 Open source Open3D

How: clouds are moved between frames with a 4×4 matrix (transform); registration algorithms (Lesson 5.3) output one.

Why this method: one representation for “where is this cloud?”, shared by every algorithm in the library.

🤯 Fun fact · the Möbius strip man invented the extra 1

Homogeneous coordinates were introduced by August Ferdinand Möbius, the same Möbius as the one-sided strip, in his 1827 book Der barycentrische Calcul, which also discussed geometric and projective transformations (MacTutor). Almost two centuries later, the same trick puts every lidar point on a robotaxi's map and every triangle in a video game on your screen.

Check yourself

1. A lidar's yaw calibration is off by 0.5°. Roughly how far off is an object detected 100 m away?
d × δ = 100 m × 0.5° × 0.01745 rad/° ≈ 0.87 m. The same 0.5° would be under 9 cm at 10 m. Rotation errors grow with distance; translation errors don't.
2. You have T_map_base and T_lidar_base. Which product gives T_map_lidar?
You need T_base_lidar = (T_lidar_base)⁻¹, so that the inner names cancel: T_map_base · T_base_lidar. Option 1's inner names (base, lidar) don't match; option 3 is in the wrong order.
3. You transform a car's velocity vector from the lidar frame to the base frame with a 4×4 transform, writing it as (vx, vy, vz, 1). What's wrong?
Points get w = 1 (shifted and rotated); directions such as velocities and normals get w = 0 (rotated only). With w = 1, a lidar mounted 1.5 m forward would add 1.5 “m/s” to every velocity. (For a rotating vehicle, velocities at different points also differ by ω × r, a Phase 3 subtlety.)
📝 TL;DR
  • Homogeneous transform: [[R, t], [0, 1]]; points get a 1, directions a 0. Rotate-and-shift becomes one multiplication.
  • Name them T_a_b (b's pose in a); chain with inner names cancelling: T_map_lidar = T_map_base · T_base_lidar.
  • Inverse = [Rᵀ, −Rᵀt]: exact and cheap. (9, 7) on the map → (2, 1) in the robot.
  • Extrinsic errors: translation stays its size, rotation grows with range: 1° → 87 cm at 50 m, 1.75 m at 100 m.
  • Calibration needs varied motion (a circle hides errors). Waymo stores 4×4 extrinsics per sensor; Tesla self-calibrates while driving; PX4 corrects the IMU lever arm.
