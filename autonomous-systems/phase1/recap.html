Recap: Spatial math

Frames, 2D and 3D rotations, Euler angles and gimbal lock, quaternions, homogeneous transforms and the tf2 tree. Here's the phase on one page, a game of “which frame is this number in?”, a drone to design, and the Python lab.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🧭

1.1 Frames

A coordinate is a vector + a frame name. REP 103: x forward, y left, z up, ENU, meters, radians. PX4: NED + FRD.

World (map: no drift, may jump; odom: smooth, drifts), body (base_link), sensor frames. (2, 1) at (10, 5) facing 90° → (9, 7). ENU → NED: swap x/y, negate z.

🧰 tf2_echo, view_frames, PX4 frame_transforms

🔄

1.2 2D rotations

R(θ) = [[cos, −sin], [sin, cos]]: columns = rotated axes. R⁻¹ = Rᵀ. Pose change = R p + t.

Rotations keep lengths. Turning the frame by θ = turning points by −θ. A heading error δ moves a point at distance d by ≈ d·δ: 1° → 87 cm at 50 m.

🧰 NumPy, scipy Rotation, cv2.getRotationMatrix2D

✈️

1.3 Euler & gimbal lock

ZYX: R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll). Order matters. Singular at pitch ±90°.

At the lock only yaw ∓ roll is defined; rates scale as 1/cos θ (5.8× at 80°). ROS +pitch = nose down; FRD +pitch = nose up. 24 conventions: always ask which.

🧰 scipy as_euler (warns at the lock)

🧮

1.4 Quaternions

q = (cos θ/2, sin θ/2 · n), |q| = 1. Chain with ⊗, undo with q*, blend with slerp, repair by normalizing.

Yaw 90° = (0.707, 0, 0, 0.707). q ≡ −q (compare |q₁·q₂|). Slerp 170° → −170° passes 180°. No gimbal lock: PX4 EKF2 and ROS messages use them. ROS: w last.

🧰 scipy Rotation / Slerp, geometry_msgs/Quaternion

🔗

1.5 Homogeneous transforms

T = [[R, t], [0, 1]]. T_map_lidar = T_map_base · T_base_lidar (inner names cancel). T⁻¹ = [Rᵀ, −Rᵀt].

Points get 1, directions 0. Translation errors stay; rotation errors grow with range (1° → 1.75 m at 100 m). Calibration needs varied motion.

🧰 NumPy 4×4, Open3D transform, Waymo extrinsics

🌳

1.6 tf2

One parent per frame. Static → /tf_static once; dynamic → /tf, buffered 10 s, interpolated. Look up at the data's stamp.

lookup_transform(target, source, time, timeout). Errors: does not exist; not part of the same tree; extrapolation into the future / past. Latest at 2 m/s, 200 ms old → ~40 cm off.

🧰 tf2_ros Buffer, TransformListener, static_transform_publisher

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “A roof lidar on our robotaxi detects a cyclist. Walk me through every transform that gets that detection onto the map, how each one is represented and who publishes it. Then: the cyclist shows up half a lane to the left at long range but is fine up close. What's your first suspect, and how do you prove it?”

A strong answer: the point is in the lidar frame; T_base_lidar (a static extrinsic from calibration, stored as translation + quaternion or a 4×4) takes it to base_link; odom → base_link (odometry, dynamic) and map → odom (localization) take it to the map; each hop is rotate-then-shift, chained by multiplying 4×4 matrices with inner names cancelling, looked up in tf2 at the lidar scan's timestamp. Orientation is stored as a quaternion (no gimbal lock, cheap to renormalize), shown as yaw/pitch/roll only for people. Error growing with range points to a rotation error, most likely the lidar's yaw extrinsic: half a lane (1.75 m) at 100 m is 1°. Prove it by driving varied routes past a known landmark and checking that its map estimates smear in proportion to range (or by checking lidar-camera alignment at long range), then recalibrate; also rule out time (a timestamp error grows with speed, not range).

🏗️ Build it yourself: the frame tree for an inspection drone

✏️ The problem

You're building a bridge-inspection drone. A PX4 flight controller flies it; a ROS 2 companion computer runs the camera, detection and mapping. The camera sits on a two-axis gimbal (yaw and pitch motors with encoders) under the drone. The drone inspects cracks from about 50 m away; the inspectors want each crack's position on a site map to within 0.5 m. The gimbal can slew at 60°/s; images reach the detector about 30 ms after capture.

Design the frame tree: every frame, its parent, static or dynamic, who publishes it, which conventions apply where, and the numbers that tell you what has to be precise. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

The tree (ROS side, REP 103 / 105 conventions)

map            (ENU, site map; root)
 └─ odom       ← published by localization (map → odom), dynamic
     └─ base_link (FLU, drone center)  ← odometry from PX4 state, converted NED/FRD → ENU/FLU, dynamic
         ├─ imu_link          static  (PX4 board orientation, Lesson 1.2)
         ├─ gps_antenna       static  (lever arm from the center, Lesson 1.5)
         └─ gimbal_yaw        dynamic ← gimbal driver, from the yaw encoder (joint)
             └─ gimbal_pitch  dynamic ← gimbal driver, from the pitch encoder (joint)
                 └─ camera_link     static  (mount on the pitch arm)
                     └─ camera_optical static (z forward, x right, y down)

Conventions and where they change

  • PX4 speaks NED/FRD; ROS speaks ENU/FLU. Convert in exactly one node at the boundary (PX4's frame_transforms), never in the detector. Position: swap x/y, negate z; body: 180° about x.
  • Attitudes travel as quaternions (ROS: x, y, z, w; PX4: w first). Euler angles only on the operator's screen. The gimbal points almost straight down at times: pitch 90° is the Euler singularity, another reason not to store its orientation as angles.
  • The gimbal links are dynamic (they move), published from encoders, typically with robot_state_publisher from a URDF with two revolute joints plus fixed ones.

Numbers

  • Accuracy budget at 50 m: 0.5 m ÷ 50 m = 0.01 rad ≈ 0.57° of total pointing error, for everything: drone attitude, gimbal encoders, camera mount, time.
  • A 0.5° gimbal encoder error alone → 50 × sin 0.5° = 0.44 m: nearly the whole budget. Calibrate the camera mount and gimbal zero carefully; a 1 cm mount offset barely matters.
  • Time: slewing at 60°/s, a 30 ms-old image seen with the latest gimbal angle is off by 60 × 0.03 = 1.8° → 50 × sin 1.8° ≈ 1.6 m. So look up map ← camera_optical at the image's timestamp, and make sure the camera driver stamps with capture time.

Classic mistakes

  • Publishing base_link from both PX4 odometry and localization (two parents). Localization must publish map → odom.
  • Forgetting camera_link → camera_optical, so the projected cracks are rotated 90° in the image.
  • Feeding ENU setpoints to PX4 (the drone tries to descend when told to climb, Lesson 1.1).
  • Static transform angles typed in degrees (static_transform_publisher takes radians).
  • Clocks: the companion computer and PX4 must share time, or every stamped lookup is wrong by the clock offset.

What this design doesn't cover yet: how the camera's own intrinsics map rays to pixels (Lesson 2.1–2.2), how PX4 estimates the drone's attitude (Phase 3), and how localization builds the map → odom link (Phase 5).

💻 Lab 1: frames, rotations and transforms in NumPy

🧰 What you'll build

File: labs/phase1_frames.py. NumPy only, runs in a second or two. From the autonomous-systems folder: cd labs && python3 phase1_frames.py (or run it cell by cell in VS Code). Fill each TODO until its ✅ checks pass:

  1. rot2d: the 2D rotation matrix, and check R⁻¹ = Rᵀ (Lesson 1.2).
  2. body_to_world: solve Lesson 1.1's puzzle, (2, 1) at (10, 5, 90°) → (9, 7).
  3. euler_zyx: build R = Rz Ry Rx and check the nose direction of Lesson 1.3's worked example.
  4. rot_to_euler: convert back, and detect gimbal lock at pitch ±90°.
  5. quat_mul: the Hamilton product; two quarter-turns make a half-turn (Lesson 1.4).
  6. quat_rotate: rotate a vector with q ⊗ (0, v) ⊗ q*, and match the matrix.
  7. slerp: shortest-arc blending; halfway from 170° to −170° is 180°.
  8. make_T / invert_T: 4×4 transforms, the [Rᵀ, −Rᵀt] inverse and a chain (Lesson 1.5).
  9. cloud_to_map: move a whole lidar point cloud to the map in one product, and measure what a 1° extrinsic error does at 50 m.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A robot at map (2, 3) faces heading 180°. A box is 4 m ahead and 1 m to its left. Where is the box on the map?
cos 180° = −1, sin 180° = 0: x = 2 + (−1)(4) − 0 = −2; y = 3 + 0 + (−1)(1) = 2. Facing “left” on the map, its own left points down the map, so (−2, 2). (Lessons 1.1, 1.2.)
2. Which statement about the ZYX Euler angles (yaw 40°, pitch 90°, roll 10°) is true?
At pitch +90° only yaw − roll matters: 40 − 10 = 30 = 30 − 0. That's gimbal lock: the orientation is fine, the angles aren't unique. (Lesson 1.3.)
3. Two estimators report q₁ = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5) and q₂ = (−0.5, −0.5, −0.5, −0.5) (w first). How far apart are the two orientations?
q₂ = −q₁, and q and −q are the same rotation (double cover). The angle is 2·acos(|q₁·q₂|) = 2·acos(1) = 0°. (Lesson 1.4.)
4. T_base_cam has R = identity and t = (2, 0, 1). What is T_cam_base?
T⁻¹ = [Rᵀ, −Rᵀt]; with R = I that's just −t. The camera sees the base's origin 2 m behind and 1 m below it. (Lesson 1.5.)
5. A detection node places obstacles correctly when the robot is still, but 30 cm behind their true place when it drives at 1.5 m/s. Most likely cause?
An error that grows with speed (0.3 m ÷ 1.5 m/s = 0.2 s) is a time problem; an error that grows with range is a rotation problem. Look up at the data's stamp and check the driver's timestamps. (Lesson 1.6.)
