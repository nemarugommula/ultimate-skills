3D rotations: Euler angles & gimbal lock

Yaw, pitch and roll: three friendly angles that pilots, drone apps and robot configs all use. They're perfect for humans and treacherous for computers. Watch the order flip your answer, then watch a whole axis vanish at 90° of pitch.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • 2D rotation matrix: R(θ) = [[cos, −sin], [sin, cos]], whose columns are the rotated axes; undo it with the transpose (Lesson 1.2).
  • ROS body axes: x forward, y left, z up, right-handed (Lesson 1.1).
  • Multiplying matrices chains transformations, and order matters in general: AB ≠ BA. Refresher: matrix multiplication.

On 20 July 1969, a couple of hours after Eagle landed on the Moon, Mission Control radioed Michael Collins, alone in orbit in Columbia: “We noticed you are maneuvering very close to gimbal lock. I suggest you move back away.” Collins steered around it and joked, “How about sending me a fourth gimbal for Christmas.” Apollo's guidance platform hung on three nested gimbals, and one particular orientation would freeze it. Today you'll find that orientation yourself (Apollo Lunar Surface Journal, 104:59:35 to 105:00:30).

Three angles, one after another

In 3D, an orientation needs three numbers. The most human-friendly choice is to describe it as three turns about the vehicle's own axes, in a fixed order. The order robotics and aerospace use is called ZYX:

  1. Yaw ψ: turn about the vertical z axis (where the nose points on the compass).
  2. Pitch θ: tip about the new sideways y axis (nose up or down).
  3. Roll φ: bank about the new forward x axis (wings tilt).

Each turn is a 2D rotation of Lesson 1.2 sitting inside a 3×3 matrix, with one axis held still:

Rz(ψ) = cos−sin0sincos0001   Ry(θ) = cos0sin010−sin0cos   Rx(φ) = 1000cos−sin0sincos

R = Rz(ψ) · Ry(θ) · Rx(φ) Yaw first, then pitch about the new y, then roll about the newest x. Each turn about the body's current axes multiplies on the right. The same matrix also reads as “roll about the fixed world x, then pitch about fixed y, then yaw about fixed z”, which is how REP 103 describes it: one matrix, two ways to say it.
✏️ Worked example: where does the nose point?

A drone has yaw 30°, pitch −20°, roll 0°. Where does its nose (body x) point in the world? Apply the turns to (1, 0, 0) from the right:

  1. Roll 0°: nothing happens, still (1, 0, 0).
  2. Pitch −20° (Ry): (cos 20°, 0, sin 20°) = (0.940, 0, 0.342). The nose tips up, because of the sign rule below.
  3. Yaw 30° (Rz): (0.940 × 0.866, 0.940 × 0.5, 0.342) = (0.814, 0.470, 0.342).

The nose points 30° left of world x and 20° above the horizon: sin 20° = 0.342 is exactly its height. Length check: 0.814² + 0.470² + 0.342² = 1.000. ✅

⚠️ Sign gotcha: is positive pitch nose up or nose down?

Use the right-hand rule: thumb along the axis, fingers curl in the positive direction. In ROS, y points left, so a positive pitch about y tips the nose down (check: Ry(+90°) sends (1, 0, 0) to (0, 0, −1)). In aircraft and PX4's FRD body frame, y points right, so positive pitch is nose up. Same physics, opposite sign. The sliders in this lesson follow ROS.

Order matters in 3D because rotations about different axes don't commute: RzRy ≠ RyRz. (In 2D every turn is about the same axis, which is why Lesson 1.2's R(30°)R(60°) = R(60°)R(30°).) That's why “yaw 90, pitch −90” is meaningless until you also say the order and whether the axes move with the body. REP 103 notes that Euler angles come in 24 “valid” conventions and discourages them for exactly this reason.

Gimbal lock: the axis that disappears

Picture the three angles as three nested rings, like Apollo's platform: the outer ring yaws about the vertical, the middle ring pitches, the inner ring rolls the vehicle. Normally the three ring axes point in three different directions, so you can turn any way. But pitch to exactly 90° and the roll axis (the nose) points straight down, along the yaw axis. Yaw and roll now spin about the same line. You've lost a direction: no combination of the three angles can make a small turn about the missing axis.

At θ = +90°:   R = Rz(ψ) Ry(90°) Rx(φ) depends only on ψ − φ Yaw 30° with roll 0°, yaw 50° with roll 20°, and yaw 0° with roll −30° are the same orientation. Three sliders, but only two degrees of freedom left, so converting the matrix back to angles has infinitely many answers.

🧩 Predict: set pitch to 89° (not quite 90°). Will toEuler still give you back your exact yaw and roll?
Yes: at 89° the answer is still unique, so (30°, 89°, 20°) comes back as (30°, 89°, 20°). Only at exactly ±90° does it give up and return (10°, 90°, 0°): it lumps everything into yaw (yaw − roll = 10°) and sets roll to zero, just as SciPy does with a “Gimbal lock detected” warning. The real trouble near 89° is different: tiny turns need huge angle changes, which Playground 3 measures.
💡 Big idea Three angles are a fine way to describe an orientation to a human, but a poor way to store and compute with one. Every three-angle convention has a singularity somewhere (for ZYX, at pitch ±90°) where two angles merge, the inverse isn't unique, and the angle rates blow up. Computers store orientation as a rotation matrix or a quaternion instead (Lesson 1.4), and convert to angles only for display.
🧠 Analogy: the North Pole

Latitude and longitude describe any place on Earth with two angles, until you stand on the North Pole. There, every longitude is the same spot, and “walk 1 m east” has no meaning. Nothing is wrong with the Earth; the coordinates break. Gimbal lock is the North Pole of Euler angles: the drone can still turn any way it likes, but the three numbers describing it can't follow smoothly.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A 3D orientation needs three numbers, and humans need to read, type and command them: “hold pitch at 5°”, “the camera is tilted 30° down”, “roll limit 35°”. A 3×3 matrix of nine numbers is unreadable for that.
  2. What people tried. Store the 3×3 matrix (exact and singularity-free, but nine numbers for three degrees of freedom and impossible to read at a glance). Use a rotation axis and angle (readable for one turn, awkward to compose). Euler angles: three turns about named axes in a fixed order, which map directly onto how pilots, gimbals and joysticks work.
  3. Why Euler angles work (for people). Each angle has a physical meaning: yaw 30°, pitch −20°, roll 0° → nose at (0.814, 0.470, 0.342), 30° left and 20° up, readable at a glance. Small attitude changes near level flight map one-to-one onto the three angles, which is why attitude controllers, flight logs and configs speak yaw/pitch/roll.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. The order and axis conventions must be agreed (24 variants; ZYX vs XYZ, ROS vs FRD signs). At pitch ±90° the yaw and roll axes line up: only yaw − roll is defined, and near it the angle rates scale like 1/cos θ, so a gentle 30°/s turn at 80° pitch looks like 173°/s of yaw. Averaging or interpolating angles also goes wrong (170° and −170° average to 0°). Apollo's three-gimbal platform physically froze at this singularity.
  5. What came next. Store orientation as a quaternion (four numbers, no singularity, cheap to keep valid, smooth to interpolate: Lesson 1.4) or a rotation matrix, and compute with those. Engineers also pick conventions so the singularity is where the vehicle never goes (cars never pitch 90°), add a fourth gimbal (Gemini's platform had four), or use strapdown IMUs with no physical gimbals at all, which is what drones and phones do.

Near the lock, the numbers explode

Even before exactly 90°, Euler angles behave badly. If the vehicle spins about its own z axis at rate r while pitched at θ, the Euler yaw angle must change at r / cos θ and the roll angle at about r · tan θ, to describe that one gentle spin. At 0° pitch that's 1× and 0×. At 80° it's 5.8× and 5.7×. At 89°, 57×.

🧩 Predict: a racing drone tilted 80° nose-down spins about its own vertical axis at a gentle 30°/s. How fast does its Euler yaw angle change?
About 173°/s: 30 / cos 80° = 30 / 0.174. Its roll angle changes at about 170°/s at the same time, in a way that mostly cancels the yaw. A controller that commanded Euler-angle rates directly would see wild numbers for a calm motion. Try it in the next playground.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

SciPy's Rotation handles every convention; the uppercase sequence "ZYX" means intrinsic (about the moving body axes), lowercase "xyz" means extrinsic (about fixed axes), and these two give the same matrix. It warns you at the lock. In ROS 2, tf_transformations or your own helper converts roll/pitch/yaw to the quaternions that messages actually carry (Lesson 1.4).

from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation as R

r = R.from_euler("ZYX", [30, -20, 0], degrees=True)   # yaw, pitch, roll about the body axes
r.apply([1, 0, 0])          # → [0.814, 0.470, 0.342]: the nose, as in the worked example
r.as_matrix()                # the 3×3 rotation matrix

lock = R.from_euler("ZYX", [30, 90, 20], degrees=True)
lock.as_euler("ZYX", degrees=True)
# UserWarning: Gimbal lock detected. Setting third angle to zero since it is not
# possible to uniquely determine all angles.   → array([10., 90.,  0.])

Before using any Euler angles from a file, a log or another team, find out: which order, intrinsic or extrinsic, degrees or radians, and which way the axes point. Four questions, every time.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4)

Drones are where gimbal lock actually bites: a racing drone or a flip passes straight through 90° of pitch. PX4 sidesteps it by representing attitude internally as a quaternion: its EKF2 estimator's state includes an attitude quaternion, and its docs state that the multicopter attitude controller “makes use of quaternions”. Euler angles appear where humans look: setpoints in the user interface, flight logs, tuning.

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, PX4 EKF2 docs

🚕 Waymo

A car never pitches anywhere near 90°, so a car's yaw/pitch/roll never hits the ZYX singularity, and heading (yaw) is the number that matters most for driving. Still, the Waymo Open Dataset stores vehicle and sensor poses as 4×4 matrices, not angles, so every user gets the exact rotation with no convention to misread. Waymo hasn't published which representation its onboard software uses internally.

Sources: Waymo Open Dataset paper (Sec. 3.2)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published how it represents orientation inside its software. What it has shown publicly (AI Day 2021) is that its networks predict the scene in a car-centered top-down frame, where the most important orientation of each object is its heading on the road: a single angle, where Euler-angle troubles can't arise. Ask “which convention?” whenever you see a heading number from any stack.

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021

🚀 Apollo

Apollo's inertial platform sat inside three nested gimbals. Mission planners chose platform alignments (REFSMMATs) so that the spacecraft's planned attitudes kept away from lock, and controllers warned crews who drifted near it, as Collins did in lunar orbit. Gemini's platform had four gimbals, so it couldn't lock.

Sources: ALSJ: Apollo 11 post-landing

Why they differ: it's about how far each vehicle can tilt. Spacecraft and drones point anywhere, so they store orientation without angles (or, in the 1960s, added a gimbal). Cars stay nearly level, so yaw/pitch/roll never meet their singularity, and the hard question becomes getting the heading right, not the representation.

🎤 Interview angle

“What is gimbal lock, and how do you avoid it?” Interviewers want more than “use quaternions”:

  • The geometric reason: at pitch ±90° the first and third rotation axes align, so one degree of freedom is lost and the inverse (matrix → angles) isn't unique: only ψ − φ (or ψ + φ) is defined.
  • That it's a property of the representation, not the vehicle: the drone can still rotate, the three numbers just can't describe it smoothly. Near the lock, angle rates scale with 1/cos θ.
  • The fix: store and integrate attitude as a quaternion or matrix; convert to Euler only at the edges (display, logs, human setpoints); pick the convention so the singularity lies outside the vehicle's envelope.
  • Bonus: the Apollo story, and the fact that order matters (non-commuting rotations), with a two-turn example.
🏭 In the real world
Apollo guidance platform 📜 History · 1969 Apollo Lunar Surface Journal

How: a stabilized platform inside three gimbals held a fixed orientation in space; the gimbal angles told the computer the spacecraft's attitude.

Why this method: three gimbals were lighter and simpler than four; the price was a forbidden attitude that crews and planners had to steer around.

PX4 attitude control 🧰 Open source PX4 controller diagrams

How: the attitude controller works with quaternions; pilots and logs see roll/pitch/yaw.

Why this method: multicopters can flip through any attitude, so the internal math must have no singularity anywhere.

ROS conventions 🧰 Open source REP 103

How: REP 103 ranks rotation representations: quaternion first, then rotation matrix, then fixed-axis roll-pitch-yaw, with Euler angles “generally discouraged”.

Why this method: quaternions and matrices have no singularities and no order ambiguity; ROS messages carry quaternions.

SciPy Rotation 🧰 Open source SciPy docs

How: one object, many views: from_euler / as_euler in any of the conventions, plus matrices and quaternions, and a warning at the lock.

Why this method: stores the rotation once (as a quaternion) and converts at the edges, the pattern every robotics stack follows.

🤯 Fun fact · “This is Mike at his best”

Neil Armstrong's later comment on Collins's Christmas joke, recorded in the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal, explains why it was funny to the crew: “We had a four-gimbal platform on Gemini.” Collins had flown on Gemini 10, so he knew a fourth, redundant gimbal would have made the lock impossible. The journal's own explainer notes that, in lock, the platform starts rotating “in lockstep with the spacecraft”, hence the name (ALSJ).

Check yourself

1. A drone (ROS axes) has yaw 90°, pitch 0°, roll 0°. Where does its nose point in the world frame?
Only Rz(90°) acts: (1, 0, 0) → (cos 90°, sin 90°, 0) = (0, 1, 0), world +y (90° to the left of x). Pitch and roll are zero, so the nose stays horizontal.
2. Your drone's logged attitude shows yaw and roll suddenly jumping by about 180° while it does a vertical loop, though the flight was smooth. What happened?
Passing through pitch ±90° forces the Euler representation to jump (yaw and roll each swing by about 180° while pitch turns back), even though the real rotation is continuous. The internal quaternion was fine; only the human-readable conversion jumped.
3. At pitch exactly +90° (ZYX), which pair of settings gives the same orientation as (yaw 40°, roll 10°)?
At pitch +90° only yaw − roll matters: 40 − 10 = 30, and 30 − 0 = 30. (50 − 0 = 50 and 40 − (−10) = 50 are a different orientation.) At pitch −90° it's yaw + roll instead.
📝 TL;DR
  • ZYX Euler angles: R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll), turns about the body's own axes in that order (= roll, pitch, yaw about fixed axes).
  • Order matters: yaw-then-pitch ≠ pitch-then-yaw, because 3D rotations don't commute. Say the order, the axes and the sign convention.
  • ROS (y left): positive pitch is nose down. FRD (y right): nose up.
  • Gimbal lock at pitch ±90°: yaw and roll share an axis, only yaw ∓ roll is defined, and angle rates grow as 1/cos θ (5.8× at 80°).
  • Store attitude as a quaternion or matrix (PX4 does); use Euler angles for people. Apollo's three-gimbal platform had to steer around the lock.
