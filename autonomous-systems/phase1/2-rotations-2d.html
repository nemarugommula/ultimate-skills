Phase 1 · Lesson 2

2D rotations & rotation matrices

One 2×2 grid of four numbers turns any vector by any angle, undoes itself for free, and stacks with other turns by plain multiplication. Every lidar point a robot sees gets pushed through one of these, so let's make it feel obvious.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Body vs world frame: “ahead, left” vs map coordinates, and the cos/sin recipe that converts between them (Lesson 1.1).
  • Matrix × vector: each output number is a row of the matrix dotted with the vector. Refresher: matrices and matrix multiplication.
  • Dot product: a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂; zero means perpendicular, and a · a is the squared length. Refresher: dot product.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

Your robot's lidar sees a wall corner 4 m dead ahead: (4, 0) in the robot's body frame. The robot now turns 30° to the left on the spot. The corner hasn't moved.

Where does the lidar see the corner now? At (3.46, +2.0), turned 30° left? Or at (3.46, −2.0)? Commit to one, then check the predict box below.

Get this sign wrong and a robot builds its map in a mirror world, or steers away from the gap it wanted. A typical spinning lidar returns hundreds of thousands of points every second, and every single one is rotated by the matrix you're about to meet.

🧩 Predict: the robot turned 30° left. Does the corner appear at (3.46, +2.0) or at (3.46, −2.0) in the robot's frame?
(3.46, −2.0): now 2 m to the robot's right. When you turn left, the world seems to turn right. Sit in a swivel chair and look at a door: swivel left, and the door slides to your right. Rotating the frame by +30° is the same as rotating the point by −30°. Keep this in mind; it's the single most common sign bug in robotics, and by the end of the lesson you'll have a one-word fix for it: transpose.

Where cos and sin come from

Forget formulas for a moment. Ask a simpler question: where do the two axes go when you turn by an angle θ?

  • The x axis, the arrow (1, 0), swings up to (cos θ, sin θ): that's what cos and sin mean, a point on the unit circle.
  • The y axis, the arrow (0, 1), is always 90° further round, so it lands on (−sin θ, cos θ).

Any vector is “a bit of x plus a bit of y”: (2, 1) = 2·(1, 0) + 1·(0, 1). Rotating it just means using the rotated axes instead: 2·(cos θ, sin θ) + 1·(−sin θ, cos θ). Write the two rotated axes side by side as columns, and you've built the rotation matrix:

R(θ) = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ     R(θ) v = vx · (column 1) + vy · (column 2) The first column is where the x axis lands; the second is where the y axis lands. Multiplying by a vector mixes the two columns in the vector's proportions. That's the whole trick, and it's the same “rotate” step as Lesson 1.1's recipe.
✏️ Worked example: rotate (2, 1) by 30°

cos 30° = 0.866, sin 30° = 0.5, so R(30°) = [[0.866, −0.5], [0.5, 0.866]].

  1. Row 1 · v: 0.866 × 2 + (−0.5) × 1 = 1.732 − 0.5 = 1.232
  2. Row 2 · v: 0.5 × 2 + 0.866 × 1 = 1 + 0.866 = 1.866

So (2, 1) → (1.232, 1.866). Check the length: √(2² + 1²) = √5 ≈ 2.236 before, and √(1.232² + 1.866²) = √(1.518 + 3.482) = √5.000 ≈ 2.236 after. Rotations never change lengths (or the angles between vectors). That's how you can spot a corrupted rotation matrix: it stretches things.

Three superpowers of rotation matrices

PropertyIn numbers (θ = 30°)Why a robot cares
Columns are unit length and perpendicular0.866² + 0.5² = 1; column 1 · column 2 = 0.866 × (−0.5) + 0.5 × 0.866 = 0A quick health check: if these drift away from 1 and 0, the matrix has been corrupted (Lesson 1.4).
Undo = transpose: R⁻¹ = RᵀR(30°)ᵀ = [[0.866, 0.5], [−0.5, 0.866]] = R(−30°)Going back the other way (world → body) costs nothing: no matrix inverse to compute, just read the numbers in a different order.
Composing = multiplyingR(30°) R(60°) = R(90°)A chain of turns (car on a map, lidar on the car) collapses into one matrix.

Why does the transpose undo it? Rᵀ R is a table of dot products between the columns of R. Each column dotted with itself gives 1, and the two different columns dotted give 0. That table is the identity matrix, so RᵀR = I. (Matrices with this property are called orthogonal; rotations are the orthogonal ones with determinant +1, so no mirror flip.)

And now the hook, solved: when the frame turns by +30°, the coordinates of a fixed point in the new frame are Rᵀ v = R(−30°)(4, 0) = (4 × 0.866, −4 × 0.5) = (3.46, −2.0). Rotating a point uses R; re-expressing a point in a rotated frame uses Rᵀ.

💡 Big idea A rotation matrix is just the rotated axes, written as columns. Multiply a vector by it to turn the vector; multiply by its transpose to turn it back, or to ask “what does this look like from the turned frame?”. Chains of turns are products of matrices.
🧠 Analogy: the recipe card

A recipe says “2 cups of flour, 1 cup of sugar”. The matrix's columns say what one “cup of x” and one “cup of y” become after the turn. The vector (2, 1) is the recipe; the rotated vector is what you get when you cook it with the new ingredients. Change the angle, and you change the ingredients, never the recipe.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A robot needs to turn vectors constantly: lidar points into the body frame, body velocities into the world frame, a planned path into the robot's view. Doing it with ad-hoc trigonometry everywhere (“x' = x cos θ − y sin θ, and was that a plus or a minus?”) breeds sign errors and makes chaining several turns a mess.
  2. What people tried. Store just the angle θ and recompute sin and cos at every use (fine in 2D, but angles wrap at ±180°, and in 3D a few angles stop being enough: Lesson 1.3). Rotate points one at a time with hand-written formulas (no clean way to undo or chain them). Complex numbers (multiplying by cos θ + i sin θ rotates a 2D point beautifully, but there's no direct 3D version; quaternions are its 3D cousin, Lesson 1.4).
  3. Why the rotation matrix works. It packages the turn as data you can multiply. (2, 1) by 30° → (1.232, 1.866), length still √5. Undo is a free transpose, chaining is a product (R(30°)R(60°) = R(90°)), and the same idea scales to 3D (3×3) and to turns plus shifts (Lesson 1.5). Hardware loves it: one matrix times a million points is exactly what CPUs' vector units and GPUs are built for.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Four numbers to store one angle in 2D (nine for three angles in 3D). After many multiplications, rounding errors make the matrix slightly non-orthogonal, so it starts to stretch or shear; you have to re-orthonormalize it. And the sign trap remains: R vs Rᵀ, “rotate the point” vs “rotate the frame”, is still a choice you must make explicitly.
  5. What came next. 3D rotation matrices built from three angles (Euler angles, Lesson 1.3), quaternions for storing and interpolating 3D orientation compactly (1.4), and 3×3 / 4×4 homogeneous matrices that fold the shift into the same multiplication (1.5).

Turn and shift: does the order matter?

Moving a robot (or a point) to a new pose takes a rotation R and a translation t (a shift). There are two orders:

rotate, then shift:   p′ = R p + t      shift, then rotate:   p′ = R (p + t) = R p + R t They differ by where the shift ends up. In the second version, the shift itself gets turned by R before it's applied.
🧩 Predict: a toy car at the origin, nose along +x. Recipe A: turn 90° left, then drive the shift (3, 0). Recipe B: shift by (3, 0), then turn 90° left about the origin. Where does the car end up in each case?
A: the car turns in place, then the shift moves it to (3, 0), nose pointing up. B: the car first moves to (3, 0), then the turn about the origin swings it round like the hand of a clock, to (0, 3), also nose up. Same final heading, different place. In robotics the convention is almost always A, p′ = R p + t: “turn into the parent frame's directions, then add where the child's origin sits”, exactly Lesson 1.1's recipe.

Small angles, big misses

Rotations preserve lengths, but a wrong rotation moves points sideways in proportion to their distance. A heading error of δ moves a point at distance d by about d × δ (δ in radians; 1° ≈ 0.01745 rad). A 1° error is 5 cm for a point 3 m away, but 87 cm at 50 m: the width of a pedestrian. That's why heading matters so much more than you'd guess, and why Lesson 1.5 obsesses over calibration angles.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In NumPy a rotation matrix is two lines, and rotating a whole scan is one matrix product. SciPy's Rotation class builds the same matrices and also speaks the 3D languages of the next two lessons. OpenCV's version is for rotating images, where y points down.

import numpy as np

def rot2d(theta):
    c, s = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    return np.array([[c, -s], [s, c]])

R = rot2d(np.radians(30))
R @ np.array([2.0, 1.0])                 # → [1.232, 1.866]
np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(2))            # True: the transpose undoes it

scan_body = np.random.rand(100_000, 2)        # N points, one per row
scan_world = scan_body @ R.T + np.array([4.0, 3.0])   # R p + t for every row at once

from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation
Rotation.from_euler("z", 30, degrees=True).as_matrix()[:2, :2]   # same 2×2

import cv2   # images: angle in degrees, positive = counter-clockwise on screen (origin top-left)
M = cv2.getRotationMatrix2D(center=(320, 240), angle=30, scale=1.0)   # 2×3: rotation + shift

Note the trick in scan_body @ R.T: with points stored as rows, “R times each point” becomes “points times Rᵀ”. Forgetting that transpose silently rotates your scan the wrong way, which is the hook's bug again.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published its camera-geometry pipeline. Its public talks show networks that output lanes and objects directly in a top-down frame around the car (the “vector space” of AI Day 2021), so the network has to account for how each camera is turned relative to the car. Each car also calibrates its own cameras: the Model 3 owner's manual says calibration typically completes after 20–25 miles of driving, and must be cleared and redone if a camera or windshield is replaced, because a slightly turned camera means slightly turned everything (Lesson 1.5).

Sources: Tesla AI Day 2021, Model 3 manual: Cameras

🚕 Waymo

In the Waymo Open Dataset, every lidar return is stored in a range image tied to its sensor, and the per-sensor 4×4 extrinsic matrix (a rotation plus a shift, Lesson 1.5) brings it into the vehicle frame. Its top-left 3×3 block is exactly the rotation matrix of this lesson, in 3D. Waymo's rotation math is classical; what it guards is the accuracy of those matrices.

Sources: Waymo Open Dataset paper (Sec. 3.2)

🛸 Drones (PX4)

The IMU chip is rarely mounted perfectly aligned with the drone. PX4's SENS_BOARD_ROT parameter says how the flight controller board is turned relative to the airframe (a list of fixed rotations), so every gyro and accelerometer sample is rotated into the body frame before the estimator sees it. Get it wrong and the estimator sees every tilt on the wrong axis, so the controller corrects in the wrong direction.

Sources: PX4: flight controller orientation, PX4 parameter reference

Why they differ: nobody differs on the math; everyone multiplies by rotation matrices. They differ in where the matrices come from: Waymo measures and stores each sensor's rotation explicitly, PX4 lets the builder pick the board's orientation from a list, and Tesla's cameras self-calibrate while driving and its networks learn the camera-to-car geometry as part of their job.

🎤 Interview angle

“Write a function that rotates a point cloud, then tell me how you'd transform it back.” Interviewers listen for:

  • The matrix from first principles: columns are the rotated axes (cos θ, sin θ) and (−sin θ, cos θ).
  • Rᵀ as the inverse, with the reason (orthonormal columns), and no call to a general matrix inverse.
  • The rows vs columns detail with NumPy: points @ R.T.
  • Active vs passive: rotating a point by θ vs expressing it in a frame rotated by θ (R vs Rᵀ), and the order of rotate-then-shift.
🏭 In the real world
Lidar point clouds into the car frame 🚗 AV Waymo Open Dataset

How: each sensor's points are multiplied by its rotation (and shifted by its position) to land in the shared vehicle frame.

Why this method: one matrix product handles a whole sweep at once, which matters at hundreds of thousands of points per second.

Flight controller board orientation 🧰 Open source PX4 docs

How: a fixed rotation turns every IMU sample from the board's frame into the drone's body frame.

Why this method: builders can mount the board sideways or backwards to fit the frame, and fix it in software with one parameter.

Rotating images 🧰 Open source OpenCV geometric transforms

How: getRotationMatrix2D builds a 2×3 matrix (a rotation plus a shift so the image turns about its center), and warpAffine applies it to every pixel.

Why this method: the same rotate-then-shift idea, one small matrix for millions of pixels; data augmentation for training vision networks uses it constantly.

🤯 Fun fact · complex numbers are secretly rotations

Multiply the complex number 2 + i (the point (2, 1)) by cos 30° + i sin 30°, expand, and you get (2 cos 30° − sin 30°) + i (2 sin 30° + cos 30°): exactly the worked example's (1.232, 1.866). Multiplying by i is a 90° turn, and i × i = −1 is a 180° turn. In 1843 William Rowan Hamilton went looking for the 3D version of this trick, and found that it needs four numbers: the quaternions of Lesson 1.4.

🌉 How it connects

Lesson 1.1's cos/sin recipe was R(θ) p + t all along. Next, 1.3 builds 3×3 rotation matrices from three angles (and finds out why order suddenly matters), 1.4 swaps them for quaternions when storing and blending orientations, and 1.5 adds a row and a column so the shift t joins the multiplication. Later, the heading error effect (d × δ) comes back in calibration (2.2), scan matching (5.3) and the motion models of Phase 3. The matrix product itself is matrix multiplication; orthogonality is a dot-product fact.

Check yourself

1. Rotate the vector (0, 2) by +90°. What do you get?
R(90°) = [[0, −1], [1, 0]]. Row 1 · (0, 2) = −2; row 2 · (0, 2) = 0. A vector pointing up, turned a quarter-turn counter-clockwise, points left: (−2, 0).
2. You have R, the rotation that takes body-frame vectors into the world frame. You need the reverse, world → body. What's the cheapest correct way?
Rotation matrices have orthonormal columns, so RᵀR = I and R⁻¹ = Rᵀ: no arithmetic at all, just read the entries in a different order. Negating every entry gives a 180° turn composed with R, not the inverse.
3. Your new mapping code makes every wall look curved while the robot turns, and straight when it drives straight. Which bug is most likely?
A sign or transpose error only shows up when the heading changes, so straight driving looks fine. While turning, each new scan is rotated the wrong way relative to the last, so walls smear into arcs. Check with a test: a point dead ahead of a robot facing 90° must land at +y.
📝 TL;DR
  • R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]: its columns are where the x and y axes land.
  • Rotations keep lengths and angles: (2, 1) → (1.232, 1.866), length √5 both times.
  • R⁻¹ = Rᵀ (orthonormal columns). Rotating a point uses R; viewing it from a rotated frame uses Rᵀ, which is why a robot turning left sees the world turn right.
  • Chain turns by multiplying. Pose changes are R p + t (rotate, then shift); R(p + t) is a different place.
  • A heading error δ moves a point at distance d by about d·δ: 1° → 87 cm at 50 m.
