Phase 8 · Lesson 5

Model predictive control: plan ahead, act once, repeat

A PID controller (proportional–integral–derivative, Lesson 8.1) reacts to the error you have now. MPC asks a better question: “if I keep doing this, what happens in the next two seconds?” It plans a whole sequence of moves that respects the car's limits, uses only the first one, and plans again. That one trick is why it can brake before a corner.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The kinematic bicycle model: state x, y, heading θ, speed v; inputs steering δ and acceleration a; turning curvature tan(δ)/L (Lesson 8.2).
  • A cost: one number that says how bad a behaviour is, like Q (care about errors) vs R (care about effort) in LQR, the linear–quadratic regulator (Lesson 8.4).
  • Saturation: a real actuator has limits; a PID that asks for more just gets clipped (Lesson 8.1).
  • Constrained optimization: minimize a cost while some inequalities must hold. Refresher: constraints in the Math course.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're driving at 20 m/s (72 km/h) toward a red light 100 m ahead. Your brakes give at most 6 m/s². Your “driver” is a computer that, every 0.1 s, imagines the next H seconds and picks the best plan: stay near 20 m/s, never cross the line.

How far ahead must it look? Half a second? The 3.3 seconds it takes to stop? Ten seconds, to be safe? Hold your guess; the first playground lets you lock it in.

This “look ahead, then act” controller is called model predictive control (MPC). It didn't start in cars: engineers at Shell Oil built their own version in the early 1970s, with a first application in 1973, and a 2003 survey counted more than 4,600 industrial MPC applications, mostly in refineries and chemical plants (Qin & Badgwell, 2003). Today it steers cars in Autoware and flies research drones at 20 m/s.

🧩 Predict: after you've found the shortest safe horizon at 20 m/s, drag the speed to 30 m/s. Does the safe horizon grow 1.5× (like the speed) or 2.25× (like the stopping distance)?
About 1.5×. The stopping distance grows with v² (33 m → 75 m), but so does the distance the plan reaches, v × H. Setting v × H ≥ v²/(2a) gives H ≥ v/(2a): linear in speed. At 20 m/s that's 1.7 s, at 30 m/s 2.5 s; the playground needs a few tenths more because it only re-plans every 0.1 s and its plans are coarse (two blocks of constant acceleration). And tick the terminal rule: then even a 0.5 s horizon stops safely. That's a real MPC trick you'll meet again below.

Numbers first: the red light, by hand

✏️ Worked example: why 1 second crashes and 2 seconds doesn't
  1. Stopping distance at 20 m/s with 6 m/s² of braking: v² ÷ (2a) = 400 ÷ 12 = 33.3 m, taking 20 ÷ 6 = 3.3 s.
  2. What a 1 s horizon sees. The “keep cruising” plan covers 20 × 1 = 20 m. The controller only notices the line when even that plan would cross it, so it starts braking with 20 m to go. Too late: it needs 33 m. It hits the line still doing about √(400 − 2·6·20) = √160 ≈ 13 m/s.
  3. What a 2 s horizon sees. The cruising plan reaches 40 m. Trouble shows up 40 m out, braking needs 33 m: it stops with room to spare.
  4. The rule. The plan must reach at least the stopping distance: v·H ≥ v²/(2a) → H ≥ v/(2a) = 1.7 s. Not the 3.3 s stopping time: what matters is how far ahead the plan reaches.

So a horizon is not a detail: too short and the controller is myopic, it can't see a problem until it's already unavoidable.

The MPC loop, in four steps

1 · PredictStart from the current state estimate. Use the model (here, the bicycle) to simulate what a candidate input sequence would do over the next N steps.
2 · OptimizeScore each sequence with a cost (tracking error, comfort, progress) and keep only sequences that obey the constraints. Find the best one.
3 · Apply the first inputSend only u₀, the first steering and throttle command, to the car. Throw the rest away.
4 · Repeat0.1 s later, measure again and re-plan from scratch (warm-started from the old plan). The horizon slides forward: a receding horizon.
minimize over u₀ … uN−1   Σk ( ekᵀ Q ek + ukᵀ R uk ) + terminal cost

subject to   xk+1 = f(xk, uk)  ·  |δ| ≤ δmax  ·  amin ≤ a ≤ amax  ·  v ≤ vlimit  ·  stay inside the lane Find the input sequence with the lowest total cost (errors e weighted by Q, effort weighted by R, just as in LQR), using the model f to predict each next state, while every limit holds at every step of the plan.

Notice how close this is to LQR (Lesson 8.4): same Q and R, same model. The two differences are the whole point. LQR solves the problem once, for an infinite horizon and no limits, and gets a fixed gain K. MPC solves it again every tick, over a finite horizon, with limits. That costs a lot more compute, and buys you constraints.

💡 Big idea MPC turns control into repeated planning: plan a short future that obeys every limit, commit to only the first move, and re-plan as soon as you know more. Re-planning is what makes it robust to model errors and surprises; the horizon is what lets it act early.
🧠 Analogy: a driver on a mountain road

A good driver doesn't steer by the white line under the bonnet. They look down the road, see the hairpin coming, and think “brake now, turn in late, accelerate out.” They don't execute that whole plan blindly though: a cyclist appears, and they re-plan instantly. And their plan respects what the car can do: they never plan a turn sharper than the tyres allow. That's MPC: a short look ahead, a plan inside the limits, the first move, and again.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. PID and LQR compute the command from the error right now. They can't see a corner coming, and they know nothing about limits: when they ask for 0.6 rad of steering on a car that has 0.5, the command is simply clipped and the car runs wide. On a car that must brake for a bend, respect a speed limit, and never exceed its tyres' grip, “react to now” isn't enough.
  2. What people tried. Feedforward from the path curvature (good, but no limits). Gain scheduling: a different PID per speed. Anti-windup and hand-made rules (“if steering is saturated, slow down”), which pile up fast. Planning a trajectory once and then tracking it: fine until the world changes mid-plan.
  3. Why this works. Put the limits inside the optimization, and plan far enough ahead to act early. The red light: at 20 m/s with 6 m/s² of brakes, a plan that reaches v·H ≥ 33 m (H ≥ 1.7 s) brakes in time; a 1 s plan sees the line at 20 m and hits it at 13 m/s. Re-planning every 0.1 s fixes small model errors before they grow.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You solve an optimization every control tick (10–100 times a second), and compute grows with the horizon. The solver must finish on time, every time, or you have no command. It's only as good as its model: if the road is wetter than the model thinks, it plans to a limit that isn't there (Playground 3). Nonlinear models give non-convex problems that can converge to a poor answer, and too-short horizons give myopic, even unstable, behaviour.
  5. What came next. Fast structured solvers (OSQP for quadratic programs, acados for nonlinear MPC) and tools like CasADi to build the problem; terminal constraints and costs that make short horizons safe; robust and tube MPC that tighten limits for model error; sampling-based MPC (MPPI, the cross-entropy method) that runs on GPUs; and learned planners whose output trajectories an MPC or simpler controller then tracks.

Constraints are where MPC shines

Here's a limit every car has. The tyres can only push sideways so hard: call it 4 m/s² of lateral acceleration for comfortable, safe driving (dry asphalt can do more, wet less). Sideways acceleration on a curve is v² × curvature, so the steering you can actually use shrinks with speed:

alat = v² · tan(δ) / L ≤ 4 m/s²   ⇒   δusable = min( δmax, atan( L · 4 / v² ) ) At 14 m/s the usable steering is only atan(2.7 · 4 / 196) ≈ 0.055 rad (3°), a 49 m circle. At 6 m/s it's 0.29 rad, a 9 m circle. To take a 10 m-radius bend you must be at or below √(4 × 10) ≈ 6.3 m/s.

🧩 Predict before Playground 2: braking needs about 16 m and the car starts at 14 m/s. Roughly what's the shortest horizon that can make this corner: 0.5 s, 1.2 s or 3 s?
Around 1.2–1.4 s: 14 m/s × 1.2 s ≈ 17 m, just over the braking distance. Shorter horizons notice the bend too late; the car can't slow down enough and slides off. The playground's MPC also uses the lane's width (swinging out, cutting the apex), which buys a little extra radius. Test it: start at 3 s (comfortable), then shorten until it breaks.

What you should see in the sweep: below about 1.2 s the worst error shoots past the road edge (myopic); above about 1.4 s it stays inside the road, and longer horizons mostly buy speed: the lap drops from about 10 s to under 9 s because the plan can brake later and more precisely, with diminishing returns past 2 s. Meanwhile the compute per decision climbs linearly with the horizon, because every candidate plan is simulated step by step. That is the horizon trade-off in one picture: too short is blind, too long is expensive, and the sweet spot is “a bit more than the distance you need to react.”

Inside the tiny MPC on this page

Real automotive MPCs solve the problem with gradient-based optimizers. To keep this page light, the playgrounds use a sampling-based optimizer, the cross-entropy method: about 150 plan simulations per decision.

  • A plan is 8 numbers: steering and acceleration at 4 knots spread over the horizon, joined by straight lines. Longer horizons don't add numbers, just simulation steps.
  • Round 1: sample 48 plans around last tick's best plan (the warm start), simulate each with the bicycle model, score them.
  • Rounds 2–3: keep the 8 cheapest, re-centre and narrow the sampling around them, try again.
  • Constraints: each sampled steering value is clipped to the usable limit for the predicted speed at that step, so every plan obeys the grip limit; leaving the lane costs a big penalty.

This is the same family as MPPI (model predictive path integral control), which ROS 2's Nav2 ships for mobile robots and measures at 50+ Hz on a modest 4th-generation Intel i5. Gradient methods (next section's tools) need far fewer evaluations for smooth problems; sampling shines when the cost is jagged, like collisions.

🧩 Predict before Playground 3: it starts raining. The road now grips only 3 m/s², but the MPC's model still says 4. The MPC obeys its constraint perfectly. Does the car make the corner?
No. MPC obeys the limit in its model. It brakes to the speed that 4 m/s² allows (about 6.3 m/s), turns in, and the real tyres, which only give 3, slide. Every “optimal” plan was optimal for the wrong world. The fix is honest models plus a safety margin: plan with 3 (or a bit less) and the car makes it, slightly slower. Too much margin and you crawl around the bend.

This is the most important practical lesson about MPC: it's a model-based controller. Real systems estimate friction online, leave margins (“constraint tightening”), and re-plan fast so that small surprises are corrected before they matter. Robust MPC makes the margin systematic.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You almost never write the solver. You describe the problem and hand it to one: CasADi (Python/MATLAB/C++; builds the problem and computes exact derivatives by algorithmic differentiation, then calls a solver such as IPOPT), acados (C with Python/MATLAB interfaces, “fast and embedded solvers for nonlinear optimal control”, made for real-time MPC) and OSQP (an operator-splitting solver for the quadratic programs, QPs, you get from linear models; its docs include a full MPC example with warm starting). Autoware's MPC lateral controller and Nav2's MPPI controller are open-source MPCs you can read.

import casadi as ca              # pip install casadi

N, dt, L = 20, 0.1, 2.7                    # 2 s horizon
opti = ca.Opti()
X = opti.variable(4, N + 1)                  # x, y, heading, speed at each step
U = opti.variable(2, N)                      # steering, acceleration
x0 = opti.parameter(4)                       # current state estimate
ref = opti.parameter(2, N + 1)                # reference points along the path

cost = 0
for k in range(N):
    x, y, th, v = X[0, k], X[1, k], X[2, k], X[3, k]
    d, a = U[0, k], U[1, k]
    opti.subject_to(X[:, k + 1] == ca.vertcat(      # the bicycle model
        x + v * ca.cos(th) * dt, y + v * ca.sin(th) * dt,
        th + v / L * ca.tan(d) * dt, v + a * dt))
    cost += (x - ref[0, k])**2 + (y - ref[1, k])**2 + 0.5 * d**2 + 0.1 * a**2
    opti.subject_to(v**2 * ca.tan(d) / L <= 4)      # grip limit (left turns)
    opti.subject_to(v**2 * ca.tan(d) / L >= -4)     # and right turns

opti.minimize(cost)
opti.subject_to(X[:, 0] == x0)
opti.subject_to(opti.bounded(-0.5, U[0, :], 0.5))     # steering rack limit (rad)
opti.subject_to(opti.bounded(-5, U[1, :], 2))         # brake / throttle (m/s²)
opti.subject_to(opti.bounded(0, X[3, :], 14))         # speed limit (m/s)
opti.solver("ipopt")

# every control tick: set the new state and reference, solve, apply only the first input
opti.set_value(x0, [0, 0, 0, 14]); opti.set_value(ref, ref_now)   # ref_now: 2 × 21 array you compute
sol = opti.solve()
steer, accel = sol.value(U[:, 0])

In a real loop you'd warm-start each solve from the previous solution (opti.set_initial), and for speed switch to acados, which generates tailored C code. With a linearized model, the whole problem becomes a QP that OSQP solves in a loop: prob.setup(P, q, A, l, u, warm_starting=True), then per tick res = prob.solve() and prob.update(l=l, u=u) with the new initial state.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published a controller design. What it has said publicly is in its FSD v12 release notes (early 2024): v12 “upgrades the city-streets driving stack to a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code.” Whether any optimization-based control remains between that network and the steering rack isn't public. The MPC idea still matters for reading Tesla's direction: it's the hand-written “plan, check limits, act” logic that an end-to-end network is trained to imitate from data.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (third-party record of Tesla's notes), Tesla FSD (Supervised)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published how its production vehicle controller works. Its published research shows the layer above it: EMMA, a research model built on a multimodal language model, maps camera images directly to planner trajectories (plus detected objects and road graph). Waymo lists its limits frankly: few image frames, no lidar or radar, computationally expensive. A trajectory like that still has to be turned into steering and throttle within the car's limits, which is the job this lesson's tracking controllers and MPC do in open stacks.

Sources: Waymo Research: EMMA, arXiv 2410.23262

🛸 Drones

PX4's default multicopter controller is not an MPC: it's a cascade of P and PID loops (position → velocity → attitude → rate; Lesson 8.7), because it's cheap and runs at hundreds of hertz on a microcontroller. MPC appears in research and on companion computers: UZH's study of agile quadrotors at up to 20 m/s found nonlinear MPC tracks dynamically infeasible trajectories better than a flatness-based controller, “at the cost of higher computation time and risk of numerical convergence issues.” Their open Agilicious platform ships an MPC controller.

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, Sun et al., IEEE T-RO 2022, Agilicious

🤖 Open self-driving stacks

Autoware, the open-source self-driving stack, ships an MPC lateral controller: a linear MPC, posed as a QP, that simulates a bicycle kinematics model (with a first-order steering delay) and outputs steering angle and steering rate, next to a pure-pursuit option (Lesson 8.3). Nav2 for ROS 2 robots ships MPPI, a sampling-based MPC much like this page's.

Sources: Autoware MPC lateral controller, Nav2 MPPI controller

Why they differ: a flight controller must run on a small chip hundreds of times a second, so drones keep the cheap PID cascade inside and put MPC (if any) on a companion computer for aggressive flight. Open modular car stacks use MPC where limits and comfort matter. Tesla publicly moved its driving logic into one learned network, trading explicit constraints for data; Waymo publishes research on learned planners but not its production controller.

🎤 Interview angle

“When would you use MPC instead of PID, and what can go wrong?” A strong answer:

  • Explains the receding horizon: optimize N steps, apply the first, re-plan; and links it to LQR (same Q, R; MPC adds limits and a finite horizon).
  • Picks MPC for constraints (steering, rates, speed, grip, keep-out zones) and for preview of the road ahead; keeps PID for fast, simple inner loops.
  • Sizes the horizon from physics: “it must see past the stopping or reaction distance: v/(2a) for braking”, then checks the compute budget at the loop rate.
  • Knows the failure modes: solver timeouts (need a fallback command), infeasibility (soft constraints with slack), model mismatch (estimate friction, tighten constraints), and non-convexity (warm starts).
🏭 In the real world
Oil refineries, since 1973 📜 History · 1970s Qin & Badgwell survey (2003)

How: Shell's dynamic matrix control (DMC) and Richalet's IDCOM predicted plant outputs over a horizon from step or impulse responses; Shell later posed it as a quadratic program (QDMC) so constraints appeared explicitly.

Why this method: plants with many coupled valves and hard limits (temperatures, pressures) where operating right at the limit earns money; slow processes left minutes for each solve.

Autoware's lateral controller 🧰 Open source Autoware docs

How: a linear MPC posed as a quadratic program: a bicycle model with steering delay predicts the car along the reference path, and the optimized steering sequence's first command is applied.

Why this method: it uses the upcoming curvature (preview) and respects steering limits, which pure pursuit can't.

Agile quadrotors at 20 m/s 🧪 Research · 2022 Sun et al., NMPC vs flatness-based control

How: a nonlinear MPC over the full quadrotor model tracks racing trajectories, compared head-to-head with a differential-flatness controller in simulation and a large motion-capture arena.

Why this method: when the requested trajectory is beyond what the motors can do, MPC finds the best feasible compromise, at a higher compute price.

ROS 2 robots with MPPI 🧰 Open source Nav2 MPPI controller

How: samples many control sequences, scores them with costs for the path, obstacles and smoothness, and blends the best: sampling-based MPC, like Playground 2.

Why this method: obstacle costs are jagged and non-convex, which samples handle and gradients don't.

🤯 Fun fact · MPC was born in no hurry

The first industrial MPCs ran on chemical plants where nothing changes faster than minutes, so a 1970s computer had plenty of time to solve an optimization each step. The same idea now has to finish in a few milliseconds on a car. The 2003 industry survey already counted over 4,600 applications, “over twice the number” of five years earlier (Qin & Badgwell).

🌉 How it connects

MPC is LQR (8.4) with a finite horizon and constraints, solved again every tick on the bicycle model (8.2); it can replace pure pursuit or Stanley (8.3) for path tracking, and it's how aggressive drones use the quadrotor model (8.6). Its input is the state estimate from Phase 3 and the trajectory from Phase 7's planners; with the planner's trajectory, MPC is the last step before the actuators. Planning by simulating the future also powers model-based RL: see model-based RL and MCTS in the RL course. The math is constrained optimization and gradient descent in the Math course.

Check yourself

1. A car at 20 m/s can brake at 5 m/s². Using “the plan must reach the stopping distance”, what's the shortest MPC horizon that can still stop for an obstacle?
Stopping distance v²/(2a) = 400/10 = 40 m. The cruising plan reaches v·H = 20H m, so 20H ≥ 40 → H ≥ 2 s (in practice add a margin for re-planning delays). 4 s is the stopping time; you don't need to plan that far, because braking starts as soon as the plan sees the problem.
2. Your car's MPC runs at 20 Hz (a 50 ms budget), but the solver now takes 120 ms after you doubled the horizon. What's the best fix?
A late answer is an answer for a state that's already gone. Fit the budget: fewer or coarser steps (a terminal cost or constraint keeps short horizons safe), warm starts, a faster structured solver (acados, OSQP); and always have a safe fallback. Dropping constraints throws away the reason you chose MPC.
3. The MPC computes an optimal 2-second plan of 20 steering commands. What does it do with them?
That's the receding horizon. Re-planning every tick from the latest state estimate turns an open-loop plan into feedback: model errors, gusts and surprises get corrected within one tick instead of piling up for two seconds.
📝 TL;DR
  • MPC: predict with a model over a horizon, optimize an input sequence under constraints, apply the first input, repeat (receding horizon).
  • The horizon must reach past what you need to react to: H ≥ v/(2a) for braking (1.7 s at 20 m/s and 6 m/s²). Too short → myopic; too long → compute grows.
  • Constraints are its superpower: steering, speed and grip limits are part of the plan, so it brakes before the corner instead of sliding wide.
  • It's only as good as its model and its solver's deadline: wet roads need honest grip estimates and margins; solves need warm starts and a fallback.
  • Tools: CasADi, acados (nonlinear, real-time), OSQP (QPs); Autoware and Nav2 ship open MPCs; PX4 flies a PID cascade; Tesla's public statements describe an end-to-end network, Waymo publishes learned planners but not its controller.
