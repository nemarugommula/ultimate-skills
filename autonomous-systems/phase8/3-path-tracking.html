Phase 8 · Lesson 3

Path tracking: pure pursuit & Stanley

The planner hands you a line on the map. Now turn the steering wheel so the car stays on it, at 10 m/s, with steering that takes time to move. Two tiny geometric rules do this job in robots, rovers, farm tractors and the car that won the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The kinematic bicycle model (Lesson 8.2): a car with wheelbase L and steering angle δ drives a circle of curvature κ = tan δ / L, i.e. radius R = 1/κ. Curvature is “how much you turn per meter”: κ = 0.1 means a 10 m circle. A refresher on curvature: curvature in the Math course.
  • Feedback (Lesson 8.1): measure an error, correct in proportion to it. Too much correction, too late, and you oscillate.
  • The path is a list of points from the planner (Phase 7), in the teal of plans; the car's own pose (x, y, heading θ) comes from the state estimator (Phase 3), in purple.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're driving a winding road at 36 km/h (10 m/s). You can fix your eyes on the road 3 m in front of the bumper, or 30 m ahead. Which gives you the cleanest line through the bends? And here's the odd part: one of them makes the car weave even on a straight road. Which one, and why?

Hold your guess. On October 8, 2005, Stanford's robot Stanley, a modified VW Touareg, finished the DARPA Grand Challenge desert course first in 6 h 53 min 58 s, ahead of every other robot. Its steering controller sent a command 20 times a second, and at its core was one line: steer toward the path's direction, plus an arctangent of how far the front wheels have drifted from it (Thrun et al., 2006). By the end of this lesson you'll have tuned that line yourself.

🧩 Predict: a path-tracking controller decides only one thing: the steering angle. What's the simplest information it could use? (a) the whole path, (b) one point on the path some distance ahead, (c) the distance from the car to the path right now.
Both (b) and (c) work, and they are this lesson's two controllers. Pure pursuit uses (b): pick one point a fixed distance ahead on the path and steer the arc that hits it. Stanley uses (c) plus the path's direction: how far the front wheels are off the line, and how badly the car is pointing. Neither needs the whole path or an optimizer; that's what LQR and MPC add (Lessons 8.4 and 8.5).

Numbers first: aim at a point, drive the arc

Stand at the car's rear axle and look at a point on the path a distance Ld away (the lookahead distance). There's exactly one circle that starts where you are, heads the way you're pointing, and passes through that point. Pure pursuit just drives that circle, then picks a new point a moment later, like a dog chasing a ball that keeps rolling away.

✏️ Worked example: the goal is 5 m ahead and 1 m to the left

Car frame: x forward, y left. The goal point is at (5, 1) m; wheelbase L = 2.7 m.

  1. Lookahead distance: Ld = √(5² + 1²) = √26 = 5.10 m.
  2. Angle to the goal from the car's heading: sin α = 1 / 5.10 = 0.196 (so α ≈ 11.3°).
  3. Curvature of the arc through it: κ = 2 sin α / Ld = 2 × 0.196 / 5.10 = 0.0769 per m, a circle of radius 13 m. (Same thing written as κ = 2y / Ld² = 2 × 1 / 26.)
  4. Steering angle from the bicycle model: δ = arctan(L κ) = arctan(2.7 × 0.0769) = 11.7°.

Look at step 3 as a controller: κ = (2 / Ld²) × y. It's a proportional controller (Lesson 8.1) on the sideways offset of a point ahead, with gain 2/Ld². A small Ld is a huge gain; a big Ld is a gentle one. Hold that thought.

💡 Big idea Pure pursuit turns path following into geometry: “the arc to a point Ld ahead”. It always produces a feasible curvature, needs no model beyond the wheelbase, and has one knob, the lookahead. A short lookahead reacts hard (high gain); a long one is smooth but lazy. Every pure-pursuit tuning conversation is about that one number.
🧠 Analogy: learning to ride a bike

Beginners stare at the ground just in front of the wheel, over-correct every wobble and zig-zag down the path. Experienced riders look further ahead and the wobble disappears, but if they look too far ahead on a twisty trail they cut the corners. That's the lookahead trade-off exactly, and why driving instructors tell you to look where you want to go, not at the bonnet.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. The planner gives a path, but the car can only command a steering angle, and it's never exactly on the line: GPS jitters, tyres slip, the road is crowned. Something must turn “I'm 0.4 m right of the path and pointing 3° off” into a wheel angle, 20 to 100 times a second, without making the ride wobble.
  2. What people tried. Steer proportional to the sideways error right now (a P controller on cross-track error): it ignores which way the car is pointing, so it overshoots and weaves. Steer toward the nearest path point: at speed it reacts too late. Early robot vans such as CMU's Navlab used arc-commanding trackers, the ancestors of pure pursuit (Coulter, 1992).
  3. Why this works. Looking ahead builds in anticipation: the goal point is already around the bend before the car is. And the geometry gives you a curvature the car can actually drive. Goal 1 m left at 5 m → κ = 0.077 → 13 m circle → 11.7° of steering. Stanley adds what pure pursuit lacks: an explicit heading term, so it straightens the car first and closes the sideways gap on an exponential curve.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Pure pursuit's lookahead is a compromise: short → high gain → weaving (worse with steering delay); long → corners are cut, because the arc to a far point ignores the curve in between. Stanley needs the path's heading (a smooth path) and goes twitchy at low speed, where e/v blows up. Both assume the car goes where the wheels point: no tyre slip. On ice or at the limit of grip, the kinematic model is wrong and so are they.
  5. What came next. Speed-scaled lookahead (Ld = lookahead time × speed) and slowing in tight curves (Nav2's Regulated Pure Pursuit); Stanley's own racing version added steering damping, yaw-rate damping and slip compensation; LQR (Lesson 8.4) weighs every error optimally; MPC (Lesson 8.5) looks along the whole path and respects steering limits explicitly.

One knob: the lookahead

Now let the car actually drive a winding loop with pure pursuit. To be honest about real cars, this simulation includes two things that make control hard: the controller runs at 20 Hz (like Stanley) and the steering motor lags (0.1 s of delay plus a 0.25 s time constant, typical for a power-steering actuator). The number to watch is the cross-track error: how far the middle of the car is from the path, sideways.

🧩 Predict before you play: a lookahead of 4 m tracks this loop to within a few centimeters at 5 m/s. What happens at 10 m/s with the same 4 m?
It weaves itself off the road: RMS error goes from about 0.04 m to about 3 m. The gain 2/Ld² is the same, but at double speed the steering lag eats twice as much road, so each correction arrives late and overshoots: a classic delayed high-gain loop. The fix is a longer lookahead at speed. That's why real implementations make Ld grow with speed.

That's corner cutting, and the chart shows it as a bump at each curve instead of a wiggle everywhere. Coulter's 1992 report describes the same two regimes: a long lookahead behaves like heavy damping when regaining a path, and limits how curvy a path you can follow.

Stanley: fix the heading, then the gap

Stanford's team took a different view. Measure two errors at the front axle: the heading error ψ (path direction minus car direction) and the cross-track error e (sideways distance of the front wheels from the path). Then:

δ = ψ + arctan( k · e / v ) First, turn the wheels parallel to the path (the ψ term). Then add a correction toward the path that grows with the sideways error, shrinks with speed, and is capped by the arctangent so a huge error just means “point straight at the path”.
✏️ Worked example: 1 m off, pointing the right way

Heading error ψ = 0, front axle 1 m right of the path, gain k = 2.5.

  • At 10 m/s: arctan(2.5 × 1 / 10) = arctan(0.25) = 14° toward the path.
  • At 2 m/s: arctan(2.5 × 1 / 2) = arctan(1.25) = 51°, beyond a 34° steering limit, so it saturates. At walking pace a 1 m error makes Stanley steer as hard as it can.
  • For small errors the Stanley paper shows the error decays like e(t) ≈ e(0) · exp(−k t): with k = 2.5 per second, a 1 m error is down to 8 cm after one second, at any speed.

That's why most implementations write the denominator as (v + ks), a small softening constant so it doesn't blow up when parking. This playground uses ks = 1 m/s.

🧩 Predict: if you crank Stanley's gain k up to 5, the error should shrink 5× faster (e ≈ e(0)·exp(−kt)). Will the car track better?
No: at 10 m/s it starts to weave. The exponential decay assumes the wheels turn instantly. With a 0.1 s delay and a lagging steering motor, a high gain asks for corrections faster than the steering can deliver, the same trap as a too-short lookahead. Stanley's race car added damping on the steering command and on the yaw rate for exactly this reason. In this playground k ≈ 1 to 2 works at 10 m/s; 3 and above weaves.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Both controllers are ten lines of NumPy; write them yourself once (the Phase 8 lab does pure pursuit). For robots on ROS 2, Nav2 ships a Regulated Pure Pursuit controller with speed-scaled lookahead and automatic slowing in tight curves; Autoware has pure pursuit and MPC lateral controllers for cars; PythonRobotics has readable versions of both, plus LQR and MPC trackers.

import numpy as np

def pure_pursuit(pose, path, Ld, L=2.7):
    """pose = (x, y, th) of the rear axle; path = (N, 2) array. Returns steering (rad)."""
    x, y, th = pose
    d = np.hypot(path[:, 0] - x, path[:, 1] - y)
    i = np.argmin(d)                                   # nearest point...
    ahead = np.nonzero(d[i:] >= Ld)[0]
    gx, gy = path[i + ahead[0]] if len(ahead) else path[-1]   # ...then the first one Ld away
    alpha = np.arctan2(gy - y, gx - x) - th
    return np.arctan2(2 * L * np.sin(alpha), Ld)  # δ = atan(2 L sin α / Ld)

def stanley(pose, path, v, k=1.5, ks=1.0, L=2.7):
    x, y, th = pose
    fx, fy = x + L * np.cos(th), y + L * np.sin(th)    # front axle
    i = np.argmin(np.hypot(path[:, 0] - fx, path[:, 1] - fy))
    j = min(i + 1, len(path) - 1)
    path_th = np.arctan2(path[j, 1] - path[i - 1, 1], path[j, 0] - path[i - 1, 0])
    e = -np.sin(path_th) * (fx - path[i, 0]) + np.cos(path_th) * (fy - path[i, 1])  # + = left of path
    psi = np.arctan2(np.sin(path_th - th), np.cos(path_th - th))                     # heading error
    return psi + np.arctan2(-k * e, ks + v)

Real paths arrive as sparse waypoints: interpolate them (every 0.1–0.5 m) before searching for the lookahead point, and search forward from last cycle's index so the car never “jumps back” to an earlier part of a path that crosses itself.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla has not published its steering controller. What it has said publicly is that FSD v12 moved the city-streets driving stack to “a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code” (v12 release notes, early 2024). How much of the final step, turning a planned motion into a steering angle, is learned versus a classic tracker underneath is not public.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (third-party archive), tesla.com/fsd

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its production controller either. Its research model EMMA maps camera images straight to planner trajectories (plus detected objects and road graph), which still have to be tracked by a controller like the ones on this page; Waymo lists its limits as few frames, no lidar or radar, and high compute. Open AV stacks show what such a tracker looks like: Autoware ships pure pursuit and an MPC lateral controller.

Sources: Waymo Research: EMMA, Autoware pure pursuit

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

Fixed-wing drones can't stop, so they track lines with a lookahead law. ArduPlane uses the L1 controller (from Park, Deyst and How, 2004): smaller NAVL1_PERIOD means sharper corners, and the docs say to raise it if the plane weaves after a turn. PX4 fixed-wing uses NPFG, whose NPFG_PERIOD is “previously called L1 distance” and “defines the tracking point ahead of the aircraft”. Same knob, same trade-off as your lookahead slider.

Sources: ArduPlane navigation tuning, PX4 fixed-wing position tuning

🤖 Robots

ROS 2's Nav2 Regulated Pure Pursuit scales the lookahead with speed and slows the robot in sharp curves and near obstacles; its README says this removes much of the sensitive lookahead tuning that plain pure pursuit needs.

Sources: Nav2 RPP

Why they differ: a geometric tracker is cheap, explainable and certifiable, so robots, rovers and fixed-wing drones use one directly. Robotaxi developers have the compute for MPC-style tracking and keep the details private. Tesla's public bet is that more and more of the driving, possibly including this last step, is learned from data rather than written as rules (Lessons 0.4 and 9.2).

🎤 Interview angle

“Your robot weaves on straight corridors but tracks curves fine. What do you check?” A strong answer:

  • Names the lookahead or gain as the first suspect: pure pursuit is a P controller with gain 2/Ld², so a short Ld means high gain; scale it with speed.
  • Asks about delay: localization latency, controller rate, steering actuator lag. Delay plus high gain is the classic oscillation recipe (Lesson 8.1).
  • Checks the inputs: a jittery pose estimate or a jagged path (no interpolation) makes the goal point jump every cycle.
  • Knows the alternatives and when to switch: Stanley for heading-aware tracking, LQR or MPC when you need constraints, preview of curvature, or high speed (Lessons 8.4, 8.5), and a dynamic tyre model when slip matters.
🏭 In the real world
Stanley wins the desert race 📜 History · 2005 Thrun et al., Journal of Field Robotics (2006)

How: steering commands at 20 Hz from δ = ψ + arctan(k·x/u), with the cross-track error x measured at the front wheels, plus damping and a slip-compensating yaw offset for the real Touareg.

Why this method: simple enough to analyse (exponential convergence for small errors) and robust from pavement to mud; the paper reports tracking error on the order of the robot's own position-estimation error.

Navlab's pure pursuit tracker 🧪 Research · 1992 Coulter, CMU-RI-TR-92-01

How: find the path point one lookahead away, transform it to the vehicle frame, command the curvature 2x/l² of the arc through it.

Why this method: geometric, cheap and forgiving, and it had already worked on CMU's Terragator and Navlab vans before being written up.

Fixed-wing drones following lines 🧰 Open source PX4 NPFG · ArduPlane L1

How: a tracking point ahead of the aircraft sets the commanded lateral acceleration (and so the bank angle), with a period or distance parameter as the lookahead knob.

Why this method: a plane can't hover to correct, so anticipation is everything; one well-understood parameter makes field tuning practical.

Warehouse and service robots 🧰 Open source Nav2 Regulated Pure Pursuit

How: pure pursuit with velocity-scaled lookahead, plus speed regulation by path curvature and by proximity to obstacles.

Why this method: plain pure pursuit overshoots on sharp or rapidly changing curvature; slowing down there stabilizes it over a wide range of lookaheads.

🤯 Fun fact · the tracker that worked with two lookaheads

When Craig Coulter rewrote the Navlab II tracker, he found the old code had been running with two different lookahead distances at once: 18 m in some parts and 4.5 m in others. The team was amazed it had tracked as well as it did, and gained “additional respect for an algorithm that was robust enough to work” when accidentally maimed (Coulter, 1992).

🌉 How it connects

Path tracking sits between the planner (Phase 7 hands it the line) and the actuators. It uses the bicycle model of Lesson 8.2 and the feedback ideas of Lesson 8.1 (pure pursuit is a P controller on a point ahead; delay plus high gain oscillates). Lesson 8.4's LQR replaces the hand-picked gain with an optimal one for the same lateral-error states, and Lesson 8.5's MPC replaces the single lookahead point with a whole predicted horizon and hard steering limits. Its inputs come from the state estimator of Phase 3, so a jumpy pose estimate becomes jumpy steering. The geometry (arcs, curvature) is in the Math course: curvature.

Check yourself

1. A car with wheelbase 2.5 m uses pure pursuit. The goal point is 10 m ahead and 2 m to the left (car frame). What circle does it drive?
Ld² = 10² + 2² = 104, so κ = 2y / Ld² = 4 / 104 = 0.0385 per m: a 26 m radius. Steering δ = arctan(2.5 × 0.0385) = arctan(0.096) ≈ 5.5°.
2. A delivery robot tracks its path perfectly at 1 m/s, but at 3 m/s it zig-zags down every straight corridor. Best first fix?
Weaving at speed is the delayed high-gain loop: the same short lookahead (gain 2/Ld²) is fine slowly, but at 3× speed the steering delay covers 3× more ground. Shortening it makes things worse. A speed-scaled lookahead, as in Nav2's Regulated Pure Pursuit, keeps the anticipation time constant.
3. A Stanley controller is pointing exactly along the path, with the front axle 1 m off, gain k = 2. Why does it steer much harder at 1 m/s than at 10 m/s?
Dividing by speed makes the sideways speed of the correction roughly constant, so the error decays like exp(−kt) whatever the speed. At very low speed the angle blows up, which is why implementations add a softening constant: arctan(k·e / (ks + v)).
📝 TL;DR
  • Pure pursuit: aim at the path point Ld ahead and drive the arc: κ = 2 sin α / Ld, δ = arctan(Lκ). Goal (5, 1) m → 13 m circle → 11.7°.
  • It's a P controller with gain 2/Ld²: short lookahead weaves (worse with delay and speed), long lookahead cuts corners. Scale Ld with speed.
  • Stanley (2005 DARPA winner): δ = ψ + arctan(k·e/v) at the front axle; heading first, then the gap, decaying like exp(−kt); soften at low speed.
  • Both assume no tyre slip and a decent pose estimate; LQR (8.4) and MPC (8.5) add optimality, preview and constraints.
  • Fixed-wing drones use the same lookahead idea (ArduPlane L1, PX4 NPFG); Tesla and Waymo don't publish their trackers.
