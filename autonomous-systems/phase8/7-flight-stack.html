Phase 8 · Lesson 7

Cascaded flight control: loops inside loops, and the PX4 / ArduPilot stack

A drone autopilot isn't one controller. It's four, nested like Russian dolls, each one faster than the one outside it. Slow the innermost one down and a perfectly tuned drone shakes itself out of the sky. Here's why the nesting works, how PX4 and ArduPilot build it, and how your own code plugs in from a companion computer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • PID: push in proportion to the error (P), its pile-up over time (I) and its rate of change (D); overshoot and settling time grade a step response (Lesson 8.1).
  • A quadrotor moves sideways by tilting: horizontal acceleration ≈ g·tan(tilt), and it changes tilt by spinning one side's rotors faster (Lesson 8.6).
  • The controller reads a state estimate (attitude, velocity, position) from a filter such as PX4's EKF2, an extended Kalman filter (Lessons 3.3 and 3.4).
  • Delay is poison for feedback: if you react to old news, you push at the wrong moment. You saw it with the D filter in 8.1.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You already know how to write a PID. So why not one PID from “I want to be 5 m to the left” straight to the four motor speeds? Why does every serious autopilot instead stack four controllers, and run them at wildly different speeds?

Look at the numbers PX4 actually uses. Its gyros are read and filtered at their full raw rate, commonly 8 kHz. The innermost loop, the one that controls how fast the drone is rotating, runs at the rate set by IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX: 400 Hz by default, configurable from 100 Hz to 2 kHz. The EKF2 state estimator updates every 10 ms (100 Hz). And a companion computer flying the drone in Offboard mode only has to prove it's alive 2 times a second (PX4 parameter reference, PX4 Offboard mode). A 4000× spread in speed, inside one small flight controller. By the end of this lesson you'll know why each number is what it is, and you'll have broken a drone by changing just one of them.

🎮 Playground 1 · Slow the inner loop (predict first)

One axis of a drone: roll. The outer attitude loop turns “roll to 20°” into a desired rotation speed; the inner rate loop turns that into a torque command for the motors (which respond with a 20 ms lag). The rate loop works on samples: it reads the gyro, computes, and its answer reaches the motors one sample later. 🧩 Predict: at 50 Hz you lower the rate gain from 40 to 15 and the overshoot barely improves. Why doesn't taming the inner loop fix it?
Because now the inner loop is slow, and the outer attitude loop is still asking for rotation as eagerly as before. The outer loop assumes “when I ask for a rotation speed, I get it almost instantly”. A sluggish inner loop breaks that promise, so the outer loop keeps pushing after it should have eased off, and overshoots. The fix is to lower both: rate gain around 15–20 and attitude gain around 5–6. Each loop must stay several times slower than the one inside it.

Numbers first: why delay eats stability

✏️ Worked example: what a slow sample costs

A sampled loop reacts, on average, about 1.5 samples late: half a sample because the command is held between updates, plus one sample to compute and send it.

Rate loopOne sampleAverage delay (1.5 samples)Phase lost at 40 rad/s (≈ 6.4 Hz)
400 Hz (PX4 default)2.5 ms3.75 ms40 × 0.00375 = 0.15 rad ≈ 9°
50 Hz20 ms30 ms40 × 0.030 = 1.2 rad ≈ 69°

What's “phase”? Think of pushing a child on a swing. Push at the right moment and the swing settles where you want; push late and you're adding energy at the wrong time. A feedback loop has a budget of lateness before it turns from damping into pumping. The 20 ms motor lag already eats part of that budget, and the drone's inertia (torque makes rotation speed build up gradually) eats more. At 400 Hz the sample delay costs 9°, small change. At 50 Hz it costs 69°, and the budget is gone: the drone pumps energy into its own wobble. That's what you watched in Playground 1.

The only cure for a slow loop is to ask less of it: lower its gain until the frequencies where it acts are slow enough that 30 ms doesn't matter. A slower inner loop means a slower drone.

💡 Big idea A cascade splits one hard problem into a ladder of easy ones. Each loop controls one quantity and hands a setpoint to the loop inside it: position → velocity → attitude → rotation rate → motors. It works because each inner loop is much faster than the loop outside it, so from the outside it looks like a perfect, instant actuator. The rule of thumb: keep each loop roughly 3 to 10 times faster than the one it serves. The innermost loop must run fastest because rotation is the fastest thing a drone does.
🧠 Analogy: a restaurant kitchen

The owner decides the menu for the month (position: slow, big picture). The head chef calls out orders every minute (velocity). The line cook adjusts the heat every few seconds (attitude). The cook's hand keeps the pan level every fraction of a second (rate). The owner never tells the hand what to do; she trusts the chef, who trusts the cook, who trusts the hand. If the hand were slow, no menu could save dinner. And if the owner changed the menu every minute, the kitchen would thrash.

🤔 Why this? (and not one big controller)
  1. The problem before. A drone's position depends on its velocity, which depends on its tilt, which depends on its rotation rate, which depends on four motor speeds: four integrations deep, in three dimensions, with a fast unstable rotation at the bottom. One PID from position error to motors would need a single gain that is both gentle enough for position and aggressive enough for rotation. No such gain exists.
  2. What people tried. A single loop on angle (early hobby stabilizers): fine for hovering, but no position or velocity control. Hand-flying everything with a pilot as the outer loops (classic RC helicopters: hard to learn). One big state-feedback controller such as LQR (Lesson 8.4) on the full model: elegant, but hard to tune per axis, hard to limit (tilt, climb rate) and hard to debug in the field.
  3. Why the cascade works. Each loop sees a simple, nearly first-order system, because the loop inside it makes its setpoint happen quickly. You tune from the inside out: rate first (it should snap to commands), then attitude, then velocity, then position. Limits drop in naturally between loops: cap the velocity setpoint, cap the tilt (PX4's MPC_TILTMAX_AIR, 45° by default). Tiny example: rate loop crossover ~40 rad/s, attitude gain 8 /s (5× slower), velocity gain 3 /s, position gain 1.25 /s. That chain moves 5 m in under 2 s with about 1% overshoot (Playground 2).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Every outer loop must be slower than the one inside, so the outermost loop ends up slow: performance is capped by the inner loops and their delays. The cascade ignores couplings between loops (aggressive position moves can saturate the motors, then every loop is wrong at once). It needs good feedback at every level: a bad velocity estimate (Lesson 3.6) wrecks position hold no matter how well the rate loop is tuned.
  5. What came next. Feedforward between loops (PX4 passes the trajectory's acceleration and rate setpoints down, not just errors), reference models that smooth setpoints, control allocation that handles motor saturation, auto-tuning (PX4 and ArduPilot both have it), and in research, MPC (Lesson 8.5) that replaces the outer loops with an optimizer while a fast rate loop stays underneath.

The ladder, rung by rung

PX4 describes its multicopter controller as “a standard cascaded control architecture” and says the controllers are “a mix of P and PID controllers” (PX4 controller diagrams). ArduPilot's copter has the same shape. Here are the real parameter names you'd tune:

And the speeds: the PX4 rate loop and motor outputs run at IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX (400 Hz default). The estimator updates at 100 Hz, so the outer loops get fresh position and velocity about that often; PX4's docs note that higher-level modules such as the navigator “run considerably slower” (PX4 architecture). ArduPilot runs its main loop at SCHED_LOOP_RATE, 50 to 400 Hz, and calls values above 400 “highly experimental” (ArduPilot parameters).

Two more PX4 details worth knowing: the rate PID's MC_ROLLRATE_K multiplies all three terms, so P = 1 gives the “ideal” form and K = 1 the “parallel” form; and both the rate and velocity loops limit their integrators to prevent windup (the velocity loop by clamping), exactly the fix from Lesson 8.1.

🏆 Challenge: with τ = 0.15 s, move 5 m with under 5% overshoot, settled within 10 cm in under 3 s. The defaults overshoot by 24%: the position loop is faster than the velocity loop under it. Then try τ = 0.6 s (a big, sluggish drone): how fast can you go now?

What you should find: the default position gain (3 /s) is bigger than the velocity gain (2 /s), an upside-down ladder, so the drone overshoots by a quarter. Drop position to about 1.25 and raise velocity to about 3 and it glides in under 2 s. With a sluggish attitude loop (τ = 0.6 s) the same gains overshoot again, and the best you can do is roughly 6 to 7 seconds: the inner loop's speed caps the whole drone. That is why racing drones push their rate loops to kilohertz and use stiff frames and fast motors.

Mixer, flight modes and uORB

At the bottom of the ladder, the mixer, which PX4 now calls control allocation, turns “this much total thrust plus these three torques” into four motor commands using a matrix built from the frame's geometry (Lesson 8.6 built one by hand). PX4's control_allocator replaced the old mixer files after v1.13 and also handles motor failures (PX4 control allocation). When the demand is impossible, say full climb and a hard roll, some motor hits 100% and the allocator has to decide what to give up.

A flight mode just decides which rung the pilot or computer grabs:

Inside PX4, every module talks over uORB, a publish-subscribe message bus: the gyro driver publishes vehicle_angular_velocity, EKF2 publishes the attitude and local position, each controller subscribes to what it needs and publishes the next setpoint. PX4's docs describe the system as reactive: modules run when new data arrives, so “the drivers define how fast a module updates”, and uorb top shows every topic's live rate (PX4 architecture, uORB).

Plugging in your own code: Offboard

Your autonomy software (planning from Phase 7, avoidance from Lesson 8.8) usually runs on a companion computer, such as a Jetson or a Raspberry Pi, next to the flight controller. It sends setpoints for one rung of the ladder and lets PX4 run everything below. Two routes:

  • ROS 2 via uXRCE-DDS: a client inside PX4 and an agent on the companion bridge uORB topics into ROS 2 topics such as /fmu/in/offboard_control_mode and /fmu/in/trajectory_setpoint. The message types come from the px4_msgs package, whose branches match PX4 releases. PX4 recommends ROS 2 Jazzy on Ubuntu 24.04, with Humble as an alternative (PX4 ROS 2 guide).
  • MAVLink via MAVSDK: a library that speaks MAVLink to PX4 or ArduPilot. Heads-up for October 2026: the gRPC-based Python wrapper was renamed from mavsdk to mavsdk-grpc (imported as mavsdk_grpc); the name mavsdk on PyPI now refers to a native binding with a different API (MAVSDK-Python README).

Either way, PX4 insists on a proof of life: a continuous stream of at least 2 Hz. If it stops for longer than COM_OF_LOSS_T (1.0 s by default), PX4 leaves Offboard and runs the failsafe set in COM_OBL_RC_ACT. With ROS 2, the keep-alive is the OffboardControlMode message; PX4's docs warn that it does not fail over if only the setpoints stop, so keep sending those too.

🧩 Predict: your Wi-Fi link to the drone drops 30% of messages. You stream at the minimum 2 Hz with the default 1 s timeout. Over a 40-second flight, how often will PX4 wrongly decide your computer is dead?
Often. At 2 Hz a gap longer than 1 s needs only two lost messages in a row, and with 30% loss that happens 9% of the time at each message: about 5 or 6 false alarms in 80 messages. The fixes pull in opposite directions: a longer timeout means fewer false alarms but slower reaction to a real crash of your computer; a faster stream gives many more chances for a message to get through. 🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Tuning happens in QGroundControl (PX4) or Mission Planner (ArduPilot), with logs in Flight Review or UAV Log Viewer; both stacks have auto-tune (PX4, ArduPilot). For your own code, the PX4 ROS 2 example looks like this (trimmed from px4_ros_com's offboard_control.py):

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from rclpy.qos import QoSProfile, ReliabilityPolicy, HistoryPolicy, DurabilityPolicy
from px4_msgs.msg import OffboardControlMode, TrajectorySetpoint

class Hover(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('hover')
        qos = QoSProfile(reliability=ReliabilityPolicy.BEST_EFFORT, durability=DurabilityPolicy.TRANSIENT_LOCAL,
                         history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=1)
        self.mode_pub = self.create_publisher(OffboardControlMode, '/fmu/in/offboard_control_mode', qos)
        self.sp_pub = self.create_publisher(TrajectorySetpoint, '/fmu/in/trajectory_setpoint', qos)
        self.create_timer(0.1, self.tick)                 # 10 Hz: well above the 2 Hz minimum

    def tick(self):
        now = int(self.get_clock().now().nanoseconds / 1000)  # PX4 timestamps are in microseconds
        m = OffboardControlMode(); m.position = True; m.timestamp = now
        self.mode_pub.publish(m)                         # the proof of life: which rung we command
        sp = TrajectorySetpoint(); sp.position = [0.0, 0.0, -5.0]   # NED: z = −5 means 5 m up
        sp.yaw = 0.0; sp.timestamp = now
        self.sp_pub.publish(sp)                          # the setpoint itself

rclpy.init(); rclpy.spin(Hover())
# Run the bridge on the companion first:  MicroXRCEAgent udp4 -p 8888
# Then arm and switch to Offboard (VehicleCommand on /fmu/in/vehicle_command, or from QGroundControl).
# The same idea over MAVLink with MAVSDK (gRPC wrapper, renamed Oct 2026):  pip install mavsdk-grpc
from mavsdk_grpc import System
from mavsdk_grpc.offboard import VelocityNedYaw

async def fly():
    drone = System(); await drone.connect(system_address="udpin://0.0.0.0:14540")
    await drone.action.arm()
    await drone.offboard.set_velocity_ned(VelocityNedYaw(0, 0, 0, 0))   # a setpoint must exist first
    await drone.offboard.start()
    await drone.offboard.set_velocity_ned(VelocityNedYaw(2.0, 0, -1.0, 90))  # north 2 m/s, up 1 m/s, face east

Notice which rung each snippet grabs: the ROS 2 one sends positions (PX4 runs all four loops), the MAVSDK one sends velocities (PX4 runs velocity, attitude and rate). Sending rates yourself is possible but means your companion computer, over a USB or Wi-Fi link, becomes part of a fast loop: rarely a good idea, as Playground 1 showed.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

Exactly this lesson, in the open: position P → velocity PID → attitude P → rate PID → control allocation, with the rate loop at the gyro rate (PX4: 400 Hz default, up to 2 kHz) and integrators limited against windup. Offboard or Guided mode lets a companion computer, running ROS 2 or MAVSDK, command any rung while the autopilot keeps the fast loops and the failsafes. Commercial drones such as Skydio's build their own autonomy on top of the same layering, with obstacle avoidance feeding the outer loops (Lesson 8.8).

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, ArduPilot parameters, PX4 Offboard

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its control loops, but the layering principle shows up in its hardware. Its Waymo Driver page lists a redundant steering system with “a redundant secondary drive motor system with independent controllers and separate power supplies”, a full secondary braking system and backup power for each critical system. The fast inner loops (steering motor, brake pressure) live in dedicated controllers, doubled for safety, while the planner above them sends trajectories.

Sources: Waymo Driver

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's FSD v12 release notes (early 2024) said it “upgrades the city-streets driving stack to a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code”. Tesla hasn't published how the network's output reaches the actuators, so whether a classic inner loop still turns its commands into steering torque and brake pressure isn't public. Any car still has fast, dedicated steering and brake controllers underneath.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (third-party archive of Tesla's notes), Tesla FSD (Supervised)

Why they differ: a multicopter is unstable: let go of the rate loop for a fraction of a second and it flips, so the cascade and its timing are non-negotiable and fully exposed for tuning. A car is stable on its own (let go of the wheel and it keeps rolling), so its fast loops can hide inside steering and brake units, and the interesting fight is over the slow outer layers: modular planners (Waymo) versus one learned network (Tesla).

🎤 Interview angle

“A drone holds position fine in calm air but oscillates slowly in a gentle wind. Walk me through how you'd debug it.” A strong answer:

  • Names the cascade and debugs from the inside out: does the rate loop track its setpoint in the log? Then attitude, then velocity, then position.
  • Knows the bandwidth rule: a slow oscillation in position with good inner tracking means the position or velocity gains are too high for the inner loops, or the estimate is lagging (check EKF2 delays and the GPS rate).
  • Checks integrators and limits: wind needs the velocity I term; a too-small integrator limit or a tilt cap can't hold the drone against the wind.
  • Mentions delay: loop rate, filtering (the D-term and gyro low-pass filters add lag), ESC protocol. And for offboard setups, the companion link's rate and loss.
🏭 In the real world
PX4 multicopter controller 🧰 Open source PX4 controller diagrams

How: P position → PID velocity (with clamped integrator) → P attitude on a smoothed reference → K-PID rate at the gyro rate → control allocation, all talking over uORB.

Why this method: every rung can be tuned, limited and logged on its own, which matters across thousands of different airframes built by different people.

ArduPilot AutoTune 🧰 Open source ArduPilot AutoTune

How: the copter twitches each axis in flight and measures the response to set the rate and angle gains (ATC_RAT_*, ATC_ANG_*), inner loop first.

Why this method: the cascade makes inside-out tuning possible one axis and one loop at a time, so a machine can do what a skilled tuner does.

ROS 2 offboard control 🧰 Open source PX4 ROS 2 user guide

How: a companion computer runs perception and planning in ROS 2 and sends trajectory setpoints through uXRCE-DDS; PX4 runs the loops below.

Why this method: heavy, slow, crash-prone autonomy code stays off the flight controller, and PX4's failsafes catch it when it dies.

Redundant steering and braking 🚗 AV Waymo Driver

How: secondary steering motors with independent controllers and power, a full second braking system and backup power for each critical system.

Why this method: with no human driver, the innermost actuator loops must survive a single failure, just as a drone's allocator must survive a weak motor.

🤯 Fun fact · the 8 kHz gyro

PX4 reads and filters gyro data at the sensor's full raw rate, “commonly 8 kHz”, even though the rate controller runs slower (PX4 IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX). Why oversample? So the filters can remove motor vibration, hundreds of hertz on a small drone, before it gets aliased into fake slow wobbles the controller would chase. The innermost loop of the innermost loop is a filter.

🌉 How it connects

Each rung is a controller from this phase: PID (8.1) at every level, with the quadrotor physics of 8.6 at the bottom and the mixer you built there. LQR (8.4) and MPC (8.5) can replace the outer rungs; the planners of Phase 7 and the avoidance of Lesson 8.8 feed the top through Offboard. The state estimate every loop consumes comes from EKF2 (Lessons 3.3 to 3.6), and its delay is part of the delay budget you fought in Playground 1. The math of “phase” behind it is the derivative of a sine: see derivatives in the Math course.

Check yourself

1. A rate loop runs at 100 Hz and reacts about 1.5 samples late. How much phase does that cost at 40 rad/s?
One sample is 10 ms, so the delay is 15 ms; 40 × 0.015 = 0.6 rad ≈ 34°. That's a big bite out of the stability budget, which is why PX4 defaults to 400 Hz (3.75 ms, about 9°).
2. You raise the position gain until the drone reaches its waypoints fast, and it starts overshooting every one. The rate and attitude logs look perfect. What's wrong?
A cascade only works if each loop is several times slower than the one inside it. The inner loops are healthy, so the problem is the ladder's top rung outrunning the one below, as in Playground 2's default settings.
3. Your ROS 2 node sends PX4 position setpoints over a lossy Wi-Fi link and PX4 keeps dropping out of Offboard mode, although your node is running fine. What's the best first fix?
False failsafes come from gaps longer than the timeout. A faster stream makes long gaps very unlikely without slowing down detection of a real failure. A 60 s timeout would leave a drone flying blind for a minute after your computer really dies.
📝 TL;DR
  • Autopilots cascade loops: position (P) → velocity (PID) → attitude (P) → rate (PID) → control allocation → motors.
  • It works because each inner loop is several times faster, so it looks instant from outside. Tune inside-out.
  • Delay eats stability: 1.5 samples at 50 Hz cost 69° at 40 rad/s versus 9° at 400 Hz (PX4's default IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX). A slow inner loop forces lower gains everywhere.
  • Real names: PX4 MPC_XY_P, MPC_*_VEL_*_ACC, MC_ROLL_P, MC_ROLLRATE_P/I/D/K; ArduPilot PSC_*, ATC_ANG_*, ATC_RAT_*.
  • Offboard: ROS 2 over uXRCE-DDS with px4_msgs, or MAVSDK (Python now mavsdk-grpc); keep a ≥ 2 Hz proof of life; COM_OF_LOSS_T (1 s) triggers the failsafe.
