Phase 8 · Lesson 1

PID control: three knobs that fly every drone

You know where you are (Phase 3) and where you want to be (Phase 7). Now push the motors so the gap closes, quickly, without wobbling, and without being fooled by a noisy sensor. The 100-year-old answer is three numbers: P, I and D.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Error = where you want to be − where you are. A drone aiming for 3 m that's at 1 m has an error of +2 m.
  • Derivative = how fast something changes (m/s for a height); integral = the area that piles up over time (m·s). Refreshers: derivatives and integrals in the Math course.
  • Newton: force = mass × acceleration. A 2 kg drone needs 2 × 9.81 = 19.6 N of thrust just to hang still.
  • The controller reads a state estimate (height, speed) from a filter like the Kalman filter of Lesson 3.3; here we feed it a noisy height sensor directly.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A delivery drone is hovering at 1 m and must climb to 3 m. The simplest rule you could write: full thrust when below the target, motors off when above. It can't possibly fail to get there, right? Where will the drone be 5 seconds later? Hold your guess; Playground 1 flies it.

Sailors knew a good helmsman doesn't just react to how far off course the ship is: he “meets and eases” the rudder as the ship swings, and holds a steady extra correction against the wind. In 1922 Nicolas Minorsky, working on automatic steering for the US Navy, turned that into math, the three-term controller, and tested it on the battleship New Mexico in 1923 (Bennett, IEEE Control Systems, 1984). Today the same three terms sit at the core of PX4 and ArduPilot, the two big open-source drone autopilots, running hundreds of times a second.

🧩 Predict: the bang-bang rule (full thrust below 3 m, off above). Will the drone settle at 3 m, settle somewhere else, or never settle?
It never settles. Below 3 m it accelerates the whole way up, so it arrives with speed and shoots past; above 3 m the motors cut and it falls, arriving fast from above. With nothing that “sees” speed, every crossing is a full-speed crossing. Fly it below: it swings between about 1.5 and 3.9 m forever, slamming the motors between 0% and 100%.

P: push in proportion to the error

The bang-bang rule has one setting: everything or nothing. A smoother idea is to push harder the further you are from the goal: extra thrust = Kp × error. With Kp = 10 N per meter, an error of 2 m adds 20 N; an error of 10 cm adds just 1 N. That's the P term (proportional).

✏️ Worked example: why P alone sags

The drone really weighs 1.5 + 0.5 = 2.0 kg, so it needs 2.0 × 9.81 = 19.6 N to hover. It supplies 14.7 N from its (wrong) hover guess, so 4.9 N is missing. The only thing that can add thrust is the P term, and it only adds thrust when there's an error:

Kp × error = 4.9 N  ⟹  error = 4.9 ÷ Kp The drone has to hang low by exactly enough that the P term makes up for the missing weight.

That permanent gap is the steady-state error. Raising Kp shrinks it, but never to zero, and a big Kp makes the second problem worse: nothing in P cares about speed, so the drone overshoots and swings like a mass on a spring. A spring with stiffness Kp and almost no friction just keeps bouncing.

I: remember the past

A good helmsman notices “I've been a little off to the left for a whole minute” and adds a steady correction. The I term (integral) does that: it adds up the error over time, and pushes in proportion to the pile. Hanging 0.49 m low for 2.5 s piles up 0.49 × 2.5 ≈ 1.2 m·s; with Ki = 4 that's 4 × 1.2 = 4.9 N, exactly the missing weight. Once the integral holds those 4.9 N, the error can be zero and the drone still hovers. I removes steady-state error, and it learns the unknown parcel without being told.

The price: the integral only reacts to error that has already happened, so it's slow, and it keeps pushing after the drone has arrived, which adds overshoot. Too much Ki and the drone swings slowly around the target.

D: look at where you're heading

The D term (derivative) reacts to speed: brake force = Kd × how fast the height is changing. Climbing at 2 m/s with Kd = 8 gives 8 × 2 = 16 N of braking, before the drone reaches the target. That's the “meeting” rudder of Minorsky's helmsman, and it's what kills the swinging. (Most autopilots, and the code below, take the derivative of the measurement rather than of the error, so a sudden new target doesn't cause a huge kick.)

💡 Big idea
thrust = hover guess + Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt − Kd·dz/dt P reacts to the error now, I to the error that has piled up (the past), D to how fast things are changing (a glance at the future). e = target − measured height.
Each term fixes one failure of the others: P alone sags and swings, I removes the sag, D removes the swing. Tuning is choosing three numbers so the fixes don't cause new problems.
🧠 Analogy: stopping a car at a line

P is “the further from the line, the more gas”. D is “I'm coming in fast, ease off now”. I is “I keep stopping short on this hill, so I'll hold a little extra gas”. A learner driver with only P lurches past the line and reverses back; add D and they glide in; add I and the hill stops mattering.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A drone, a cruise control or a steering motor never does exactly what it's told: unknown payloads, wind, hills, battery voltage. Running the motors “open loop” from a calculation drifts off target; you have to measure and correct, continuously.
  2. What people tried. On/off (bang-bang) control, like a home thermostat: simple and fine for slow, forgiving systems, but a drone with momentum overshoots every time. Proportional only: smooth, but it sags under any unknown load and swings when stiff. Hand-crafted “dodges” like the early Sperry ship autopilots worked but defied analysis.
  3. Why PID works. Three terms cover the three time scales: now (P), the past (I), the near future (D). Numbers: P with Kp = 10 leaves a 4.9 ÷ 10 = 0.49 m sag; I builds 4.9 N in about 2.5 s and erases it; D with Kd = 8 brakes 16 N at 2 m/s. It needs no model of the drone, just three numbers you can tune on the real thing, and it costs a few multiplications per tick.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. D amplifies sensor noise (you'll see it shortly). I winds up when the motors saturate. One set of gains is tuned for one operating point; heavy payloads, different speeds or a nonlinear system need retuning or gain schedules. It handles one input and one output; it knows nothing about limits ahead of time or about trade-offs between several goals.
  5. What came next. Cascades of PIDs (Lesson 8.7), feedforward (the hover-thrust guess here is one), LQR, which computes all the gains at once from a model and a cost (Lesson 8.4), and MPC, which plans ahead and respects limits (Lesson 8.5). PID is still the innermost loop in almost all of them.

✏️ Worked example: Ziegler–Nichols, the classic recipe

In 1942 John Ziegler and Nathaniel Nichols, engineers at Taylor Instruments, published the first famous tuning recipe. Turn I and D off and raise Kp until the system oscillates steadily: that's the ultimate gain Ku, and the time per swing is Tu. Then read the gains off a table. Say a test gives Ku = 20 N/m and Tu = 2 s:

GainZiegler–Nichols PID ruleHere
Kp0.6 Ku12 N/m
Ki1.2 Ku ÷ Tu12 N/(m·s)
Kd0.075 Ku Tu3 N·s/m

The recipe aims for each swing to be about a quarter of the one before: lively, with lots of overshoot. It was built for 1940s process plants, and on a drone it's a starting point, not an answer. Press Ziegler–Nichols in the next playground and see how far off it is. Modern autopilots tune with step tests and logs (PX4's tuning guide) or with automatic tuners (ArduPilot's AutoTune).

🧩 Predict: the height sensor is a barometer with about 5 cm of random noise, read 100 times a second. The D term takes the change between two readings and divides by 0.01 s. With Kd = 8, how big are the random thrust kicks from noise alone?
The difference of two noisy readings has a spread of about √2 × 5 cm ≈ 7 cm. Divided by 0.01 s, that looks like a speed of 7 m/s, up or down, every tick. Times Kd = 8: about ±57 N of thrust kicks, three times the drone's weight. The motors would slam between 0 and maximum. The fix is to low-pass filter the D term (average it over a short window). PX4 calls it the D-term filter (IMU_DGYRO_CUTOFF), ArduPilot ATC_RAT_RLL_FLTD.

🏆 Challenge: with sensor noise on, get overshoot under 5% and settling under 2 s. The defaults overshoot by about 50%. A path that works: raise Kd to kill the swing, then Kp for speed, keep Ki small but not zero (watch the steady-state error), then turn noise on and find a D-term filter that calms the motors without making the drone sluggish.

What you should find: D is the hero and the villain. Without noise, a big Kd gives a crisp, overshoot-free climb. With noise and no filter, the same Kd turns the thrust trace into a solid orange bar and the drone never settles. A filter at a few hertz fixes it, but filter too hard (1 Hz) and the D term reacts late, so the swing comes back. Every real autopilot lives on that trade-off.

When the motors max out: integral windup

Motors have limits. Our drone's maximum thrust is 1.6 × its believed weight: 1.6 × 14.7 = 23.5 N, against a real weight of 19.6 N. That leaves only 3.9 N for climbing, so asking for a 10 m climb means the controller requests far more than the motors can give. The output saturates: it's clipped at 23.5 N.

Meanwhile the I term keeps adding up the error, which stays large for seconds because the drone climbs slowly. By the time it arrives, the integral is a huge stored push (80 N in Playground 3) that has to be “unwound” by sitting above the target for a long time. That's integral windup. The fixes are simple: stop integrating while the output is saturated (clamping, what PX4's velocity loop does), or cap how big the I term may get (integrator limit: PX4 MC_RR_INT_LIM, ArduPilot ATC_RAT_RLL_IMAX).

Real autopilots: PIDs inside PIDs

A real multicopter doesn't run one PID from height to motors. It runs a cascade: an outer loop turns a position error into a speed target, a middle loop turns the speed error into a tilt or thrust target, and the innermost loop turns a rotation-rate error into motor commands, hundreds of times a second. Each loop is a small P or PID like the one you just tuned. Why nesting works so well is Lesson 8.7; here are the knobs you'd actually touch:

PX4's rate PID has an extra global gain: output = MC_ROLLRATE_K × (P·e + I·∫e + D·de/dt). Setting P = 1 gives the “ideal” form, K = 1 the “parallel” form you used above. Same controller, two ways of writing the knobs.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A PID is 15 lines; write one yourself (the Phase 8 lab does, with anti-windup). simple-pid is a tidy Python version for experiments; on a real drone you tune the autopilot's parameters, from the PX4 console or QGroundControl, or ArduPilot's Mission Planner.

class PID:
    def __init__(self, kp, ki, kd, out_min, out_max):
        self.kp, self.ki, self.kd = kp, ki, kd
        self.lo, self.hi = out_min, out_max
        self.i, self.prev = 0.0, None

    def __call__(self, target, measured, dt):
        e = target - measured
        d = 0.0 if self.prev is None else (measured - self.prev) / dt   # derivative on measurement
        self.prev = measured
        i_new = self.i + e * dt
        u = self.kp * e + self.ki * i_new - self.kd * d
        if self.lo < u < self.hi:          # anti-windup: only integrate when not saturated
            self.i = i_new
        return min(max(u, self.lo), self.hi)

# simple-pid (pip install simple-pid): derivative on measurement by default,
# and it clamps its integral to output_limits to avoid windup

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

PID is front and centre. PX4's multicopter controller is a cascade: a P loop on position gives a velocity target, a PID on velocity gives an acceleration (thrust and tilt), a P loop on attitude gives rotation rates, and a PID on rates drives the motors. The rate loop runs at IMU_GYRO_RATEMAX (default 400 Hz, up to 2 kHz). The docs note the rate integrator's authority is limited to prevent windup and the velocity loop uses clamping anti-windup, exactly Playground 3. ArduPilot names the same gains ATC_RAT_* and PSC_* and offers AutoTune.

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, PX4 parameters, ArduPilot parameters

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its low-level controllers. What it does say publicly: its software “determines the exact trajectory, speed, lane, and steering maneuvers”, and the vehicle has redundant steering (a secondary drive motor with independent controllers and separate power) and a full secondary braking system. Following a planned trajectory with steering and braking actuators is a classic feedback-control job (Lessons 8.3–8.5); which mix of PID, LQR or MPC Waymo uses isn't public.

Sources: Waymo Driver

🚘 Tesla

Tesla's FSD v12 release notes (early 2024) say v12 “upgrades the city-streets driving stack to a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code.” Hand-written code like that typically includes controllers. Tesla hasn't published what form the network's outputs take or what low-level loops remain between the network and the steering and brake actuators.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (mirror), Tesla FSD

Why they differ: a drone is unstable without fast feedback, so its autopilot must expose tunable loops that run hundreds of times a second, and open-source stacks publish every gain. Car makers sit on top of steering and brake units that already contain their own closed loops, and treat the controller layer as proprietary. Tesla's bet is that a learned network can replace much of the hand-written decision and control code; drones still rely on PIDs because they're cheap, analyzable and easy to tune per airframe.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your drone oscillates in altitude hold. Walk me through it.” A strong answer:

  • Names what each term does in one line (now, past, future), and that P alone leaves a steady-state error under load: error = missing force ÷ Kp.
  • Separates fast oscillation (too much P or D, or noise through D: check the D filter, vibration, logs of the motor outputs) from slow wallowing (too much I).
  • Mentions saturation and windup (clamping or integrator limits), derivative on measurement, and feedforward (hover thrust).
  • Tunes inner loop first, from low gains, with step tests and logs; knows Ziegler–Nichols as a historical starting point and autotuners as the practical one.

Why this method: one controller structure works for thousands of different airframes; a handful of gains adapts it to each.

Why this method: hand-tuning dozens of gains is slow and risky; the airframe measures its own response.

Why this method: those caps are anti-windup and anti-noise, the two failures you broke in Playgrounds 2 and 3.

The first tuning recipe 📜 History · 1942 Ziegler & Nichols, “Optimum Settings for Automatic Controllers” (1993 reprint)

How: measure the ultimate gain and period (or the response to a step), then read gains from a table.

Why this method: plant engineers needed a quick, repeatable way to set the dials on pneumatic controllers without any math model.

🤯 Fun fact · the battleship that steered itself, then didn't

Minorsky's automatic steering worked on the USS New Mexico in 1923; adding the rate-of-change (“acceleration”) control had a dramatic effect at sea. Yet the Navy removed it and stopped the work, partly because naval officers distrusted automatic steering at the time; the commercial winner was Sperry's simpler autopilot, which a Sperry director himself called “an unscientific dodge” (Bennett, 1984). The math was right a decade before anyone used it.

Check yourself

1. A drone's controller assumes it weighs 2 kg, but a 1 kg camera was added. With P only and Kp = 20 N/m, how far below the target does it hover?
The missing force is 1 kg × 9.81 = 9.81 N. P only supplies it when Kp × error = 9.81 N, so error = 9.81 ÷ 20 ≈ 0.49 m. Adding an I term would build up those 9.81 N and remove the sag.
2. A drone sits on the ground for 10 s with its altitude controller already active and the target at 5 m. When it finally lifts off, it rockets to 9 m. What's the most likely cause?
While stuck, the error stayed at 5 m and the integral kept growing. That stored push has to be unwound by overshooting. Fixes: don't integrate when saturated or landed (clamping), cap the I term, or reset it at takeoff; Autoware, for example, stops integrating while the vehicle is stationary.
3. After you raise Kd, the motors get hot and the drone buzzes, although the step response looks better in simulation without noise. What should you change first?
D differentiates the measurement, and differentiating noise makes it huge: 5 cm of noise at 100 Hz looks like ±7 m/s. A D-term filter (PX4 IMU_DGYRO_CUTOFF, ArduPilot ATC_RAT_RLL_FLTD) trades a little delay for calm motors. Too strong a filter brings the oscillation back, as Playground 2 shows.
📝 TL;DR
  • Bang-bang overshoots forever. P pushes in proportion to the error but sags under unknown loads (sag = missing force ÷ Kp) and swings.
  • I piles up the past and removes steady-state error; D reacts to speed and adds damping. Thrust = feedforward + Kpe + Ki∫e − Kdż.
  • Grade with overshoot, settling time, steady-state error and effort. Ziegler–Nichols (1942) is a lively starting point, not an answer.
  • D amplifies noise → filter it. Saturation winds up I → clamp or limit it.
  • PX4 and ArduPilot run cascades of P and PID loops (MC_ROLLRATE_*, ATC_RAT_*), the rate loop at hundreds of Hz; car companies keep their controllers private, and Tesla says v12 replaced its hand-written driving code with one network.
