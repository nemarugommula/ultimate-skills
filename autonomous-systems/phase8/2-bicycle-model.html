The kinematic bicycle model: a whole car in four numbers

To steer a car toward a path you need to predict how it moves when you turn the wheel. A car has four wheels, a body, tyres and suspension; for most driving, you can pretend it's a bicycle with two wheels and get a model you can write in three lines. Here's why that works, and where it lies.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Pose: position x, y and heading θ (the direction the car points, in radians, measured from the x-axis), from Lessons 1.1 and 1.2.
  • Sine, cosine and tangent: moving a distance d along heading θ changes x by d·cos θ and y by d·sin θ. tan of a small angle ≈ the angle itself in radians. Refresher: vectors in the Math course.
  • Curvature κ = 1 ÷ radius: a gentle bend has small curvature, a hairpin a big one. A straight road has κ = 0.
  • A controller (Lesson 8.1) needs a model to predict what its command will do; this lesson builds the one most car controllers use.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

You're driving a big SUV (2.9 m between the axles, 1.9 m wide) down a street that's 12 m from curb to curb. You've missed your turn. With the steering wheel at full lock (the front wheels at 30°), can you make the U-turn in one sweep, or will you be doing the embarrassing back-and-forth? And roughly how wide would the street need to be?

You'll answer it with one line of trigonometry. The same line sits inside almost every car controller you'll meet in this phase: pure pursuit and Stanley (Lesson 8.3), the default vehicle model of the open-source Autoware MPC controller (Lesson 8.5), PX4's controller for steered rovers, and the simulator Waymo Research released for driving research, which moves every car with a bicycle model.

Why a bicycle?

Start with what a wheel can't do: slide sideways. At parking-lot speeds a rolling tyre moves (almost) only in the direction it points. Now look at a car from above while it turns:

  • The rear wheels point straight ahead, so the rear axle moves straight forward, along the car's body.
  • The front wheels are turned by the steering angle δ (delta), so the front axle moves at angle δ.
  • Draw a line through each wheel, perpendicular to it. Where the lines meet is the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR): the whole car is, at this moment, rotating around that point.

The two front wheels are actually turned by slightly different amounts (the inner one more) so that both their perpendiculars hit the same ICR. That linkage is called Ackermann steering, and it's why we can replace each pair of wheels by a single wheel in the middle of the axle. Two wheels in a line, the front one steered: a bicycle.

🧩 Predict: you double the steering angle from 10° to 20°. Does the turning radius halve, more than halve, or less than halve? And what if you instead double the wheelbase?
The radius is R = L ÷ tan δ. tan 20° ÷ tan 10° = 0.364 ÷ 0.176 = 2.06, so the radius drops a little more than half: 16.4 m → 8.0 m for a 2.9 m wheelbase. For small angles tan δ ≈ δ, so doubling the angle almost exactly halves the radius. Doubling the wheelbase doubles the radius: that's why buses swing wide and why long-wheelbase cars feel clumsy in parking garages. Check both in the playground.

The model, numbers first

✏️ Worked example: one step of the bicycle

A car with wheelbase L = 2.9 m drives at v = 10 m/s with the front wheels at δ = 10°. We step the model forward by Δt = 0.1 s.

  1. Curvature: κ = tan 10° ÷ 2.9 = 0.1763 ÷ 2.9 = 0.0608 per meter, so the radius is R = 1 ÷ κ = 16.4 m.
  2. Turning speed (yaw rate): ω = v·κ = 10 × 0.0608 = 0.608 rad/s ≈ 35° per second.
  3. Move: in 0.1 s the rear axle goes 1.0 m along its heading (x += 1.0·cos θ, y += 1.0·sin θ), and the heading turns by 0.0608 rad ≈ 3.5°.
  4. Sanity check: sideways (lateral) acceleration v²/R = 100 ÷ 16.4 = 6.1 m/s², about 0.62 g. A normal car on a dry road can just about do that; on ice it can't, and the model doesn't know (Playground 3).
💡 Big idea
x′ = v cos θ    y′ = v sin θ    θ′ = v · tan δ / L    v′ = a The state is four numbers: position (x, y), heading θ and speed v, measured at the rear axle. The inputs are the steering angle δ and the acceleration a. Steering doesn't set where you go; it sets how fast your heading turns per meter driven: κ = tan δ / L.
That's the kinematic bicycle model. “Kinematic” means it only uses geometry (no forces, no tyres, no mass). It turns any steering and throttle sequence into a predicted path, which is exactly what a path tracker or an MPC controller needs.

Two details worth knowing. At very low speed, steering does nothing until you roll: θ′ is proportional to v, which is why you can't turn a parked car by twisting the wheel. And if you measure the state at the car's center instead of the rear axle, the center moves at a small slip angle β = atan(lr/L · tan δ) relative to the body, where lr is the distance from the center to the rear axle. Same model, different reference point; check which one a library uses.

🧠 Analogy: a shopping trolley

Push a supermarket trolley whose back wheels are fixed and front wheels swivel. Push gently and the back end follows a smooth arc around a point you can almost see on the floor, level with the back wheels. That pivot point is the ICR. Push it sideways and the fixed wheels scrape: that's the “no sideways sliding” rule the model is built on, and the one a fast car on a wet road breaks.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A controller must predict what a steering command will do to the car's path: will this wheel angle get me back to the lane center in 2 seconds or swing me into the next lane? A full car has four wheels with tyre forces, suspension, weight transfer, steering backlash, dozens of parameters you'd have to measure.
  2. What people tried. A point that moves wherever you point it (a “unicycle”, fine for robot vacuums but it can spin on the spot, which a car can't). A full dynamic model with tyre forces (accurate, but needs tyre data and fails badly when those parameters are wrong). Hand-tuned rules with no model at all.
  3. Why the bicycle works. Two rules, “wheels don't slide sideways” and “the front wheel sets the turning”, capture what matters at everyday speeds with one parameter, the wheelbase. Numbers: L = 2.9 m, δ = 10° → κ = 0.061 per meter, R = 16.4 m; at 10 m/s the heading turns 35° per second. It's cheap enough to simulate thousands of candidate paths per second, and you can invert it: from a desired curvature, the steering angle is δ = atan(L·κ).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It assumes no slip, so it's wrong when lateral acceleration gets close to the tyres' grip: high speed, tight turns, wet or icy roads. It ignores how long the steering takes to turn (a delay of a few tenths of a second matters at highway speed), body roll and load. It has no limits built in: a real car can't exceed its maximum steering angle or steering rate, so controllers add those explicitly.
  5. What came next. The dynamic bicycle model adds mass, yaw inertia and tyre cornering stiffness, so the wheels can slip at an angle; it's what you use at high speed or low friction (racing, emergency maneuvers). Production stacks add a steering delay (Autoware's default model includes a first-order steering lag). Learned models fit the leftovers from data.

Turning radius, in numbers

For the hook's SUV (L = 2.9 m, 1.9 m wide), R = L / tan δ at the rear axle. The curb-to-curb circle is the path of the outer front wheel, at √((R + 0.95)² + L²) from the center:

Where the bicycle lies: speed and grip

The kinematic model says a given steering angle gives the same circle at 5 m/s and at 30 m/s. Physics disagrees. Driving a circle of radius R at speed v needs a sideways acceleration of v² / R, and the tyres can only provide about μ·g of it, where μ is the friction coefficient (illustrative values: around 0.9 on dry asphalt, 0.5 wet, 0.2 on snow).

✏️ Worked example: a highway lane at 30 m/s

At 30 m/s (108 km/h) with δ = 5°, the model predicts R = 33 m. That would need 30² ÷ 33 = 27 m/s², nearly 3 g. No road car can do that: the tyres saturate, the front slides, and the car runs wide (understeer). Real highway steering is tiny: at 1°, R = 166 m and the lateral acceleration is 5.4 m/s².

Fastest speed around a 40 m bend: v = √(μ·g·R). Dry: √(0.9 × 9.81 × 40) = 18.8 m/s. Wet: 14.0 m/s. Snow: 8.9 m/s.

Below the grip limit the two paths are identical and the kinematic model is perfect. Above it, steering more does nothing: the car is already sliding. That's the regime where you need the dynamic bicycle model (tyre forces and slip angles). For everyday driving well inside the grip limit, the kinematic model is usually accurate enough; at high lateral accelerations, a dynamic model is needed. Kong, Pfeiffer, Schildbach and Borrelli compared both models' prediction errors on experimental driving data to motivate exactly this choice for model-predictive control (IEEE IV 2015).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The model is four lines of NumPy; write it yourself (the Phase 8 lab does). PythonRobotics has bicycle-model path trackers to read; Waymax, Waymo Research's simulator, ships a bicycle model for moving every car in its scenes; Autoware's MPC uses a kinematic bicycle with steering lag.

import numpy as np

def bicycle_step(state, steer, accel, L=2.9, dt=0.05, max_steer=np.radians(30)):
    """Kinematic bicycle about the rear axle. state = [x, y, theta, v]."""
    x, y, th, v = state
    steer = np.clip(steer, -max_steer, max_steer)      # real cars have a steering limit
    x  += v * np.cos(th) * dt
    y  += v * np.sin(th) * dt
    th += v * np.tan(steer) / L * dt                     # curvature = tan(delta) / L
    v  += accel * dt
    return np.array([x, y, np.arctan2(np.sin(th), np.cos(th)), v])

def steer_for_curvature(kappa, L=2.9):
    return np.arctan(L * kappa)                       # invert the model: desired curve → wheel angle

s = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 10.0])
for _ in range(20):                                  # 1 s at 10 m/s with 10° of steering
    s = bicycle_step(s, np.radians(10), 0.0)
print(s, 1 / (np.tan(np.radians(10)) / 2.9))         # heading ≈ 0.61 rad, R ≈ 16.4 m

# Waymax (Waymo Research, JAX): the same idea, with acceleration + curvature actions
from waymax import dynamics
dynamics_model = dynamics.InvertibleBicycleModel()

Classic bug: mixing degrees and radians in tan(). Another: integrating with a big Δt at high speed; Euler steps of 0.5 s at 30 m/s cut every corner. Keep Δt small or integrate the arc exactly.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published the vehicle model in its production controller. Its research is more open: Waymax (2023), Waymo's accelerated simulator for driving research, moves cars with a bicycle model whose actions are acceleration and curvature, with bounds “tuned to minimize error on the Waymo Open Motion dataset”, and an exact inverse that recovers the steering and acceleration a logged human driver must have used.

Sources: Waymax on GitHub, Waymax paper (arXiv 2310.08710)

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published a vehicle model or controller design. Its FSD v12 release notes describe the city-streets stack as “a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code”. A network trained on driving video learns the car's behaviour from data rather than from a written-down model; what, if anything, still uses an explicit model isn't public.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (mirror)

🛸 Drones (and PX4 rovers)

A drone isn't a bicycle: it can move sideways and has no wheels to stop it sliding, so it needs a rigid-body model (Lesson 8.6). But the same autopilots also drive cars: PX4's Ackermann rover (experimental since v1.16) is configured with exactly the bicycle's parameters, RA_WHEEL_BASE and RA_MAX_STR_ANG, plus a steering-rate limit, and its path following uses a pure pursuit controller (PP_LOOKAHD_* parameters; Lesson 8.3).

Sources: PX4 rovers, PX4 parameters

Why they differ: a modular stack (Waymo, Autoware, PX4) passes a planned path to a controller that needs an explicit, invertible model, and the bicycle is the cheapest one that's right at everyday speeds. An end-to-end network like Tesla's describes learns how the car responds from data instead of from equations. Drones need a different model altogether because they're not held by tyres.

🎤 Interview angle

“Write down a vehicle model for a lane-keeping controller and tell me when it breaks.” A strong answer:

  • Writes the four equations and states the assumption: no lateral slip, so the ICR is on the rear axle line and κ = tan δ / L.
  • Gives a number: L = 2.9 m, δ = 30° → R ≈ 5 m; and checks lateral acceleration v²κ against μg.
  • Knows the reference point matters (rear axle vs center of gravity, slip angle β) and that steering has limits and delay.
  • Says when to switch to a dynamic model (high speed, low friction, aggressive maneuvers) and what parameters it needs (cornering stiffness, mass, yaw inertia).
🏭 In the real world
Autoware's MPC lateral controller 🚗 AV Autoware docs

How: the default prediction model is a kinematic bicycle with a first-order steering delay; a kinematic model without delay and a dynamic bicycle model with slip are options.

Why this method: the kinematic model needs only the wheelbase and the steering lag, which are easy to measure on any car the stack is installed on.

Stanley, winner of the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge 📜 History · 2005 Thrun et al., J. Field Robotics (2006)

How: its steering law was analyzed with a linear bicycle model; on the real car, tyre slip was compensated with a bicycle model with slip, calibrated in testing, adding a yaw offset that depends on curvature and speed.

Why this method: the kinematic idea gave a controller with provable convergence; the slip correction fixed what the desert's loose surfaces broke.

Waymax 🧪 Research · 2023 Waymax: an accelerated, data-driven simulator

How: every simulated car moves by an invertible bicycle model; inverting it on logged trajectories gives the “expert” acceleration and steering for imitation learning.

Why this method: it's cheap enough to run on accelerators for huge batches of scenarios, and realistic enough that logged human driving fits inside its bounds.

PX4 Ackermann rovers 🧰 Open source PX4 rover docs

How: wheelbase and maximum steering angle parameters turn a commanded curvature into a wheel angle; a pure pursuit controller follows the path.

Why this method: two numbers describe almost any front-steered vehicle, from a toy chassis to a full-size car.

🤯 Fun fact · the simulator that reads human minds (a little)

Waymax's bicycle model has an exact inverse: give it two consecutive positions of a car from a real log and it tells you the acceleration and curvature the driver must have used. Waymo Research uses that to turn hours of recorded human driving into “expert actions” for training driving policies, and it tuned the model's limits so the logged cars of the Waymo Open Motion Dataset fit inside them (Waymax source). A two-wheel model from textbook geometry, sitting under modern imitation learning (see imitation learning in the RL course).

Check yourself

1. A car with a 2.5 m wheelbase steers its front wheels to 25° (tan 25° ≈ 0.466). What's the turning radius at the rear axle?
R = L ÷ tan δ = 2.5 ÷ 0.466 ≈ 5.4 m (curvature κ ≈ 0.19 per meter). The curb-to-curb turning circle is larger, because it's traced by the outer front wheel and the body overhangs it.
2. In simulation your controller tracks a tight 30 m curve perfectly at 25 m/s, but the real car runs wide on a wet test track. What's the most likely reason?
The kinematic bicycle doesn't know about friction. Lateral acceleration v²/R ≈ 20.8 m/s² is over 2 g; a wet road gives roughly 0.5 g. You'd need a dynamic model (or a speed limit from v = √(μgR) ≈ 12 m/s) to plan this curve honestly.
3. A planner wants the car to follow a curve with curvature 0.05 per meter. With L = 2.9 m, what steering angle should the controller command?
Invert κ = tan δ / L: δ = atan(L·κ) = atan(0.145) ≈ 0.144 rad ≈ 8.3°. In the kinematic model the steering for a given curvature doesn't depend on speed; speed only changes how fast you go around (and whether the tyres can hold it).
📝 TL;DR
  • Wheels don't slide sideways, so the car rotates about a point on the rear axle line; Ackermann steering lets you merge each axle into one wheel: a bicycle.
  • State (x, y, θ, v); inputs (δ, a); x′ = v cos θ, y′ = v sin θ, θ′ = v tan δ / L. Curvature κ = tan δ / L, radius R = L / tan δ, and δ = atan(L·κ) to invert.
  • L = 2.9 m at 30° → R ≈ 5 m, curb-to-curb ≈ 13.3 m: a 12 m street needs a three-point turn.
  • It breaks near the grip limit (v²/R vs μg), with steering delay and at high speed: then use a dynamic bicycle model.
  • It's inside pure pursuit, Stanley, Autoware's MPC, PX4 rovers and Waymo Research's Waymax; Tesla's v12 learns driving from video instead of a written model.
