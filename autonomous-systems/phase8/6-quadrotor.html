Phase 8 · Lesson 6

Quadrotor dynamics: why four rotors

Four fixed propellers that can only spin faster or slower, and yet a quadcopter can climb, hover, turn to face any direction and race sideways. Here's how four speeds become four jobs, why two props spin backwards, and why a drone has to tilt before it can move.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Newton's second law: force = mass × acceleration. Weight is m·g, with g = 9.81 m/s². A 1.5 kg drone weighs 1.5 × 9.81 = 14.7 newtons (N).
  • Torque: a force times its lever arm (N·m). It makes things spin up, like pushing a door far from its hinges.
  • Roll, pitch, yaw: rotation about the forward, sideways and vertical axes (Lesson 1.3).
  • Feedback control: measure the error, correct it, many times a second; PID (Lesson 8.1).
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A quadcopter has no rudder, no wings, no tilting rotors and no swashplate: four fixed-pitch propellers, each with one knob, its speed. So: how does it turn to face north without moving? And how does it fly sideways when every propeller only pushes up?

Here's a hint from a real autopilot. PX4, the open-source flight stack, limits a multicopter's tilt in normal flight with a parameter, MPC_TILTMAX_AIR, whose default is 45° (PX4 parameter reference). That one number caps how hard the drone can accelerate sideways at about 1 g. By the end of this lesson you'll know why tilt and sideways acceleration are the same thing, and you'll fly one with your keyboard.

Numbers first: what does it take to hover?

To hover, the propellers must push up exactly as hard as gravity pulls down. Before you read the answer, try the designer below.

🏆 Challenge: your drone must carry a 1 kg parcel with a thrust-to-weight ratio of at least 2 (enough margin to climb, fight wind and stay controllable), at the lowest all-up mass: 2.65 kg or less wins. Bigger motors push harder but weigh more; more motors add arms.

✏️ Worked example: the 1.5 kg quadcopter
  1. Weight: 1.5 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 14.7 N.
  2. Per motor: 14.7 ÷ 4 = 3.68 N, about 375 g of thrust each. That's all a 1.5 kg drone needs to stay up.
  3. Margin: four 9 N motors give 36 N in total, a thrust-to-weight ratio of 36 ÷ 14.7 ≈ 2.4, so it hovers at about 41% of maximum thrust. The rest is for climbing, braking, wind and, most of all, control: to tilt, one side must be able to push harder than the other.
  4. Thrust isn't linear in motor speed. A propeller's thrust grows with the square of its speed (PX4 models it as thrust = CT · u², with u the motor command). Hovering at 41% of max thrust means about √0.41 ≈ 64% of max speed.

A useful rule of thumb from the designer: with thrust-to-weight 2, the drone can tilt up to acos(1/2) = 60° and still hold its height. Push the payload up and that number collapses.

Four rotors, four jobs

Each propeller does two things: it pushes air down (thrust, up on the drone) and it drags on the air as it spins, which twists the drone the opposite way (reaction torque, Newton's third law). With four motors on an X-shaped frame, four speeds map to exactly four things you can control:

To get…Do thisWhy it works
Climb / sinkAll four faster / slowerTotal thrust vs weight.
Roll rightLeft pair faster, right pair slowerUnequal thrust across the frame makes a torque about the forward axis.
Pitch nose-downRear pair faster, front pair slowerSame, about the sideways axis.
Yaw left (turn CCW)Clockwise pair faster, counter-clockwise pair slowerThe two clockwise props twist the body counter-clockwise; with them stronger, the twists no longer cancel.

The trick is in the last row. Diagonal motors spin the same way, neighbours spin opposite ways: two clockwise (CW), two counter-clockwise (CCW). At hover the four reaction torques cancel, so the drone doesn't spin. To yaw, speed up one diagonal pair and slow the other by the same amount: total thrust stays the same (no climbing), the left-right and front-back balance stays the same (no tilting), only the twist is left over. PX4's own docs put it this way: a multicopter “will yaw right by changing the speed of all motors.”

T = T₁ + T₂ + T₃ + T₄    τroll = d·(Tleft − Tright)    τpitch = d·(Trear − Tfront)    τyaw = kM·(TCW − TCCW) Four motor thrusts in, one total thrust and three torques out: a 4×4 table called the mixer (PX4 calls it control allocation). d is each motor's lever arm, kM the drag-to-thrust ratio; PX4's default moment coefficient CA_ROTOR*_KM is 0.05, so a prop's twist is about 5% of its thrust in N·m. Yaw is the weakest axis: that's why drones turn slowly compared with how fast they roll.
💡 Big idea A quadrotor controls exactly four things, total thrust and three torques, with exactly four motors. Counter-rotating pairs cancel the propellers' twist so yaw becomes just one more knob. But it has six ways to move (three positions, three rotations), so it can't push sideways directly: to move, it must first tilt.
🧠 Analogy: four people carrying a table

Four friends carry a table, one at each corner, and can only lift harder or softer. All lift harder: the table rises. The two on the left lift harder: it tilts right. Want it to slide right? Nobody can push sideways, so they tilt it a little and let gravity's sideways component do the rest. And if each friend also twisted their corner slightly when lifting, two clockwise and two counter-clockwise, the twists would cancel, until one pair lifts harder and the table slowly turns.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. A single rotor spins the body the opposite way, so a helicopter needs a tail rotor to cancel it, and a swashplate to tilt the main rotor's blades as they spin: a precise, expensive mechanism with many moving parts that wears out and crashes badly.
  2. What people tried. Early multi-rotors (the US Army's 1922 “Flying Octopus” had four big six-bladed rotors) needed a pilot to balance every rotor by hand and were abandoned. Coaxial helicopters cancel torque with stacked rotors but still need a swashplate. A tricopter has three motors but needs a tilting servo on one of them to yaw.
  3. Why four works. With fixed-pitch props, the only control is motor speed, and four speeds are the smallest symmetric set that controls total thrust plus all three torques. Counter-rotating pairs cancel the twist for free: 1.5 kg drone, 3.68 N per motor at hover, reaction torques 0.05 × 3.68 ≈ 0.18 N·m each, two CW and two CCW → net zero. Electric motors change speed in milliseconds and a cheap gyro plus a 400 Hz control loop do the balancing a 1920s pilot couldn't.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It's unstable without that fast loop: switch the loop off and the smallest imbalance tips it over within a second or two. It's underactuated: it can't hold a tilt and a position independently. Yaw authority is weak. Small props are less efficient than one big rotor, so flight times are short. And it has no redundancy: lose one motor and a plain quad can't keep both its heading and its level.
  5. What came next. Hexa- and octocopters for payload and motor-out redundancy; flight stacks that compute the mixer from the geometry you type in (PX4 control allocation, which also handles motor failures); cascaded controllers that turn “go there” into tilts and tilts into motor speeds (Lesson 8.7); and research controllers (nonlinear MPC, differential-flatness control) that fly quads at 5 g.

What you just found by hand is the drone's mixer: for “yaw left” you raised the two CW motors and lowered the two CCW ones by the same amount. A flight controller does exactly that, hundreds of times a second, from four requested quantities. And with all props spinning one way, “hover still” is impossible: whatever thrust holds it up also twists it, so it spins forever. That's why your quadcopter came with two kinds of propellers.

Tilt to accelerate

Every propeller pushes along the drone's own “up”. Level, all of that thrust fights gravity. Tilt the drone by an angle θ and the thrust leans: part still holds the drone up, part now pushes sideways. If the drone keeps its height, the up part must still equal the weight, which fixes everything:

T cos θ = m g   ⇒   T = m g / cos θ   and   asideways = T sin θ / m = g · tan θ To accelerate sideways while holding height, tilt by θ and add a little thrust (1/cos θ times the hover thrust). The sideways acceleration is g·tan θ, whatever the drone's mass.

Now count. A drone in 3D can move in 6 ways (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw) but has only 4 inputs. It's underactuated: some motions can't be commanded directly. Sideways motion is the one it lacks, so it borrows it from tilt. That's why every drone controller is a chain: “go there” → “accelerate this way” → “tilt this much” → “these four motor speeds” (Lesson 8.7). A car is underactuated too: it can't slide sideways into a parking spot, it has to steer (Lesson 8.2).

🧩 Predict before Playground 3: in a side view, the drone has a left and a right motor. To start moving to the right, which motor do you speed up?
The left one. More thrust on the left lifts the left side, the drone tips right, and its thrust now leans right. Then you must stop the tipping (briefly speed up the right motor) or it keeps rotating and flips. Starting to move takes two opposite pushes, and stopping takes two more. Try it with the raw sliders: it's surprisingly hard.

You were the slow outer loop (position), the autopilot was the fast inner loop (attitude). That split is the whole design of real flight stacks, and it's Lesson 8.7.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The mixer is one small matrix. Write it from the geometry, invert it, done; that's what PX4's control allocation does from the CA_ROTOR* parameters you enter for each motor (position PX/PY, thrust coefficient CT, moment coefficient KM, positive for CCW rotors). ArduPilot picks it from FRAME_CLASS / FRAME_TYPE. MPC_THR_HOVER (default 0.5) is PX4's starting guess of the hover throttle; a hover-thrust estimator refines it in flight.

import numpy as np

m, g, d, km = 1.5, 9.81, 0.177, 0.05   # mass, gravity, lever arm (0.25 m arm at 45°), moment coeff
# motor order: front-left (CW), front-right (CCW), rear-left (CCW), rear-right (CW)
# rows: total thrust, roll (left − right), pitch (rear − front), yaw (CW − CCW)
A = np.array([[ 1,   1,   1,   1],
              [ d,  -d,   d,  -d],
              [-d,  -d,   d,   d],
              [ km, -km, -km,  km]])
mixer = np.linalg.inv(A)                  # wanted (thrust, torques) → four motor thrusts

hover = mixer @ [m * g, 0, 0, 0]
print(hover)                              # [3.68 3.68 3.68 3.68] N each
yaw_left = mixer @ [m * g, 0, 0, 0.1]          # 0.1 N·m of yaw, nothing else
print(yaw_left)                           # [4.18 3.18 3.18 4.18]: CW pair +0.5 N, CCW pair −0.5 N
tilt = np.degrees(np.arctan(3.0 / g))     # tilt for 3 m/s² sideways: about 17°

With six or eight motors A has more columns than rows, so you use the pseudo-inverse (np.linalg.pinv) and, when a motor saturates, a smarter allocation that gives up yaw first, which is what flight stacks do.

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

Since v1.14 PX4 uses control allocation, which replaced its older hand-written mixer files: the rate controller outputs thrust and torque setpoints, and the control_allocator module turns them into motor commands from the geometry parameters, and “handles motor failures.” Each rotor is described by its position, CT (thrust = CT·u²) and KM (torque = KM·thrust, sign by spin direction). ArduPilot ships built-in frame classes (quad, hexa, octa, Y6…) with fixed motor orders that you must wire to match.

Sources: PX4 control allocation, PX4 parameters (CA_ROTOR*, MPC_THR_HOVER), ArduPilot motor order

🚘 Tesla

Tesla builds cars, not drones, and hasn't published its vehicle-dynamics models. The idea of this lesson still applies to every car: it's underactuated too, with two inputs (steering, throttle/brake) for three planar motions (x, y, heading), so it can't slide sideways and must steer to change lanes (Lesson 8.2). Tesla's public statement about its driving software since v12 is that a single end-to-end network replaced over 300k lines of explicit C++ code; how that output becomes steering commands isn't public.

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (third-party record), Tesla FSD (Supervised)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo doesn't fly, and it hasn't published the dynamics models in its production controller. Its vehicles face the same underactuation as any car. What Waymo does publish on the motion side is research on learned planners such as EMMA, which outputs future trajectories from camera input; turning a trajectory into steering within the vehicle's limits is the job of the controllers in Lessons 8.3–8.5.

Sources: Waymo Research: EMMA

🤖 Research quads

UZH's open-source Agilicious platform pushes the same four-rotor physics to the extreme: high thrust-to-weight and torque-to-inertia ratios, trajectory tracking at up to 5 g and 70 km/h in a motion-capture arena, and vision-based acrobatics using only onboard sensors.

Sources: Foehn et al., Science Robotics 2022, Agilicious

Why they differ: for a drone, these dynamics are the control problem: unstable, underactuated, and fast, so the open flight stacks expose every coefficient. Cars are stable on their own (let go of the wheel and nothing flips), so Tesla and Waymo publish about perception and planning, not about the vehicle model under the controller.

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain how a quadrotor yaws, and why it can't fly sideways without tilting.” A strong answer:

  • Gives the four inputs, four outputs map: total thrust, roll, pitch and yaw torques, and writes the mixer.
  • Explains reaction torque and counter-rotating diagonal pairs; yaw = speed up one pair, slow the other, total unchanged.
  • Derives a = g·tan θ and T = mg/cos θ, and calls the system underactuated (4 inputs, 6 degrees of freedom), which forces the cascade position → attitude → rates.
  • Talks numbers: hover thrust per motor, thrust-to-weight around 2 for a camera or delivery drone, thrust ∝ speed², and motor saturation (why allocators sacrifice yaw first).
🏭 In the real world
PX4's control allocator 🧰 Open source PX4 docs

How: the controllers ask for a thrust and three torques; the allocator turns them into motor and servo commands from the configured geometry, for quads, hexas, vertical take-off and landing aircraft (VTOLs) and more.

Why this method: one controller for every airframe; the geometry lives in parameters, not in code.

ArduPilot frame classes 🧰 Open source ArduPilot frame types

How: you pick a frame class and type (for example quad X), and the firmware uses the matching built-in motor mixing and order.

Why this method: the common frames are covered by tested defaults, so a builder only has to wire the motors in the documented order.

Agile research quadrotors 🧪 Research · 2022 Agilicious (Science Robotics)

How: an open hardware and software quad with a high thrust-to-weight ratio, onboard GPU and vision, flown with model-based and neural-network controllers.

Why this method: the faster you fly, the more the exact rotor dynamics in this lesson matter; a shared platform lets labs compare controllers fairly.

The 1922 “Flying Octopus” 📜 History · 1922 Air & Space Forces Magazine

How: George de Bothezat's helicopter for the US Army had four large six-bladed rotors on a cross-shaped frame plus small stabilizing fans.

Why this method: four rotors gave lift and some balance without a swashplate; but its controls were complex and the project was cancelled in the 1920s.

🤯 Fun fact · the quadrotor that came 90 years early

On its first flight, the US Army's four-rotor “Flying Octopus” hovered just two to six feet off the ground for one minute and 42 seconds, and later lifted a 450-pound payload to four feet (Air & Space Forces Magazine). The layout was right; what was missing was a computer to adjust four rotors hundreds of times a second. A modern flight controller runs its rate loop at 400 Hz by default in PX4.

Check yourself

1. A 2 kg hexacopter (six motors) hovers. How much thrust does each motor produce?
Weight 2 × 9.81 = 19.6 N, shared by six motors: 19.6 ÷ 6 ≈ 3.27 N each (about 333 g). 4.9 N would be the answer for four motors.
2. A pilot wants the drone to turn to face east without climbing, sinking or tilting. What does the flight controller do with the motors?
Diagonal pairs spin the same way. Raising one pair and lowering the other keeps total thrust and the left-right and front-back balance unchanged; only the reaction torques stop cancelling, so the body yaws (opposite to the faster pair's spin).
3. A drone holds its height while tilted 20°. Compared with hovering, what is its thrust and sideways acceleration?
The vertical part of the thrust must still equal the weight, so T = mg/cos θ = 1.064 mg. The sideways part gives a = g·tan θ = 9.81 × 0.364 ≈ 3.57 m/s², independent of the drone's mass.
📝 TL;DR
  • Hover: thrust = weight. A 1.5 kg quad needs 14.7 N, 3.68 N (≈ 375 g) per motor; aim for thrust-to-weight around 2.
  • Four motor speeds → total thrust + roll, pitch, yaw torques: the mixer (PX4 control allocation). Thrust grows with speed².
  • Diagonal props spin the same way so the twists cancel; yaw = speed up one pair, slow the other. Same-direction props would spin the drone.
  • Tilt to accelerate: a = g·tan θ, thrust × 1/cos θ to hold height; PX4's default 45° limit ≈ 1 g sideways.
  • Underactuated (4 inputs, 6 DOF) and unstable: a fast inner attitude loop is mandatory, and position control must go through tilt (Lesson 8.7).
