Phase 8 · Lesson 4

LQR: optimal feedback

Stop guessing gains. Write down what you care about (“stay close to the target, don't use much thrust”) as a cost, and a 300-year-old equation hands you the best possible feedback gains. It balances poles on carts and steers open-source self-driving cars.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • PID (Lesson 8.1): the command is a weighted sum of the error, its integral and its rate of change. You picked the weights by hand; too much gain overshoots.
  • State: the few numbers that describe the system right now, here position x and velocity v. A model says how the state moves: “velocity changes by the acceleration you command, position by the velocity”.
  • Matrices as a compact way to write several linear equations at once: xnext = A x + B u. Refresher: matrix multiplication in the Math course.
🎯 Hook · a puzzle first

A drone hovers 2 m to the right of where it should be, perfectly still. You'll command a sideways acceleration u from two numbers you can see, the offset x and the sideways speed v: u = −k₁·x − k₂·v. Which two gains are best? “Best” has to mean something, so here's a rule: every second you're off by x meters costs x² points, and every second you push with u m/s² costs 0.1·u² points. Lowest total wins.

You could try thousands of pairs. Or you could solve one equation, the Riccati equation, named after the Venetian count Jacopo Riccati (1676–1754) who studied its ancestor (MacTutor). Baidu's open-source Apollo self-driving stack does exactly that to steer: its lateral controller iterates the discrete Riccati equation until it converges, then steers with u = −Kx on four numbers: lateral error, its rate, heading error and its rate (Apollo source). Try beating it by hand first.

Numbers first: feedback on the whole state

Here's the move that makes LQR different from PID: instead of feeding back one error, you feed back every state, each with its own gain, stacked in a row vector K.

u = −K x = −( k₁ · position + k₂ · velocity ) Look at where you are and how fast you're moving, multiply each by its gain, add them up, and push the opposite way.
✏️ Worked example: one step of u = −Kx, and its cost

Take K = [1.0, 1.73] (we'll see in a moment where these come from).

  • Drone at x = 2 m, v = 0: u = −(1.0 × 2 + 1.73 × 0) = −2.0 m/s², a firm push back.
  • Two seconds later it's at x = 0.71 m, moving back at v = −0.60 m/s: u = −(0.71 − 1.73 × 0.60) = −(0.71 − 1.04) = +0.33 m/s². It's braking before it arrives, because the velocity term says “you're coming in fast”.

That's a PD controller (Lesson 8.1): k₁ is the P gain, k₂ the D gain. For this simple system LQR is PD, just with gains chosen for you. Now the cost, with weights q = 1 for error and r = 1 for effort:

For one second of…cost
being 2 m offq · 2² = 4
being 0.2 m offq · 0.2² = 0.04 (100× less, not 10×)
pushing with 2 m/s²r · 2² = 4

Squares make big errors terrifying and small ones almost free, and the same for effort: one hard shove costs more than several gentle ones. That shape is why the optimal answer comes out smooth.

💡 Big idea LQR (the Linear Quadratic Regulator) answers: “for a linear model xnext = A x + B u and a quadratic cost Σ (xᵀQx + uᵀRu), what feedback is best?” The answer is always a fixed gain matrix, u = −Kx. You stop tuning gains and start tuning priorities: Q says which errors you hate, R says how expensive pushing is.
🧠 Analogy: the budget and the deadline

A project manager can finish early by hiring lots of contractors (big effort) or save money and finish late (big error). Nobody tells them “hire 3.7 people”; they're told how much lateness hurts and how much money hurts, and the staffing falls out. Q is how much lateness hurts, R is how much money hurts. Double both and nothing changes; only the ratio matters.

🤔 Why this? (and not something simpler)
  1. The problem before. PID tuning is trial and error, one loop at a time. That's fine for one error and one knob, but a balancing robot, a car's lateral motion or a drone's attitude has several states that interact: fixing one error with a separate PID can make another one worse, and you have no idea whether your gains are anywhere near the best.
  2. What people tried. Hand tuning, Ziegler–Nichols rules (Lesson 8.1), and pole placement: choose how fast each mode should decay and solve for K. Pole placement works but you must pick the poles, and nothing tells you what those choices cost in actuator effort.
  3. Why this works. You state the trade-off once, in units you understand, and the Riccati equation finds the gains that minimize it over all time. For the double integrator with Q = diag(1, 1), R = 1 the answer is K ≈ [1, 1.73]: a well-damped PD controller nobody had to tune. Make effort 10× cheaper (r = 0.1) and K jumps to about [3.2, 4.0]: faster, harder. And K is guaranteed to stabilize the model, as long as the system can be steered at all.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need a linear model (real systems are linearized around one operating point, so big angles or speeds break it). It has no notion of limits: if K asks for 40 N and the motor gives 10 N, the guarantee is gone. It has no integral term, so a steady push (wind, a slope) leaves a steady offset. And Q and R are still choices; you've moved the tuning, not removed it.
  5. What came next. LQI adds an integral state; gain scheduling re-solves for K at several speeds (cars at 5 vs 30 m/s); iLQR and DDP handle nonlinear models by re-linearizing along a trajectory; LQG pairs LQR with a Kalman filter (Lesson 3.3) when the state is estimated; and MPC (Lesson 8.5) adds constraints by solving a finite-horizon version online, every cycle.

Where K comes from: the Riccati iteration

LQR solves the problem backwards in time, and the idea is friendlier than the formula. Let P be “the total future cost of being in state x”, written as xᵀPx. With zero steps left, that's just the cost of being there: P = Q. With one step left, you pick the u that minimizes “cost now + cost of where u takes you”, which gives a new P. Repeat. Each repeat adds one more step of look-ahead; when P stops changing, you have the answer for a horizon that never ends.

K = (R + BᵀPB)⁻¹ BᵀPA     P ← Q + AᵀP(A − BK) Given the current guess of the future cost P, the best gain K balances effort (R) against where you'll end up (BᵀPB). Then update P: today's cost plus the future cost after applying that gain. Start from P = Q and repeat until P stops changing.
✏️ Worked example: the double integrator, by formula and by iteration

Model with a 0.05 s step: position ← position + v·Δt + ½u·Δt², velocity ← velocity + u·Δt, so A = [[1, 0.05], [0, 1]] and B = [[0.00125], [0.05]].

  • For this system the continuous-time answer has a closed form: k₁ = √(q/r), k₂ = √(qv/r + 2k₁). With q = qv = r = 1: k₁ = 1, k₂ = √3 = 1.73.
  • Iterating the discrete equation above from P = Q (in JavaScript, in the next playground) converges after a few hundred passes to K ≈ [0.96, 1.68]: the same answer, nudged slightly by the 0.05 s step.
  • q = 1, r = 0.1 gives k₁ = √10 = 3.16; q = 10, r = 1 gives… exactly the same. Only the ratio Q/R matters.
🧩 Predict: in the next playground the drone must move 2 m without ever commanding more than 2.5 m/s². Which slider will that limit mostly fight with: the position weight, the velocity weight, or R?
The position-to-effort ratio (q vs r). At t = 0 the drone is still, so u(0) = −k₁ × 2 m, and k₁ ≈ √(q/r). A 2.5 m/s² limit means k₁ ≤ 1.25, so q/r can't exceed about 1.6. Once that's capped, the only way to get there faster is to lower the velocity weight, which reduces damping: you let it swing in a bit quicker and accept a few centimeters of overshoot. LQR can't respect the limit itself; you tuned around it. Remember that for MPC (Lesson 8.5).

LQR vs PID, and where LQR breaks

On a single double integrator, LQR and a well-tuned PD controller are the same thing. LQR earns its keep when several states interact. The classic test is the cart-pole: a pole hinged on a cart, balanced by pushing the cart left and right. To catch a pole leaning right you must first push the cart right, under it. A PID that only watches the pole's angle does catch it, but nothing tells it to bring the cart back, so the cart drifts away and rolls off the end of the track. LQR feeds back all four states (cart position, cart speed, pole angle, pole spin) at once.

And two honest failure modes to try below. Nonlinearity: the model was linearized for a nearly upright pole; at 40° the real physics is far from that line. Constraints: the motor force is capped; LQR's gains assume unlimited force, so near the limit it simply saturates and hopes. A third one, offsets: with no integral term, a steady push d settles where k₁·x = d, so a constant 0.5 m/s² wind against k₁ = 1 leaves the drone 0.5 m off for good (LQI fixes it).

🧩 Predict: the PID below balances the pole from 10° perfectly. Roughly how far does the cart travel in 10 s?
Off the 6 m track. Catching the pole leaves the cart rolling at about 0.35 m/s, and an angle-only controller has no reason to stop it: in simulation the cart passes the 3 m end after about 8 s. LQR from the same start stays within about 0.4 m of the center and comes back to zero.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The iteration is ten lines of NumPy (the Phase 8 lab writes it). In practice: python-control's dlqr returns the gain, the Riccati solution and the closed-loop eigenvalues; SciPy's solve_discrete_are gives P directly; Drake and MATLAB have the same. On a robot the gain is usually computed offline (or at a few operating points) and the controller is just a dot product.

import numpy as np

dt = 0.05
A = np.array([[1, dt], [0, 1]])                 # double integrator: [position, velocity]
B = np.array([[dt**2 / 2], [dt]])
Q = np.diag([1.0, 1.0])                         # how much each state error hurts
R = np.array([[1.0]])                           # how much effort hurts

def dlqr_iter(A, B, Q, R, iters=5000, tol=1e-9):
    P = Q.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T @ P @ B, B.T @ P @ A)
        P_next = Q + A.T @ P @ (A - B @ K)
        if np.max(np.abs(P_next - P)) < tol:
            break
        P = P_next
    return K, P

K, P = dlqr_iter(A, B, Q, R)
print(K)                                        # ≈ [[0.96, 1.68]]

# The same with libraries
import scipy.linalg, control                    # pip install scipy control
P2 = scipy.linalg.solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R)
K2 = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T @ P2 @ B, B.T @ P2 @ A)
K3, S, E = control.dlqr(A, B, Q, R)             # gain, Riccati solution, closed-loop eigenvalues
u = -K @ np.array([2.0, 0.0])                   # the whole controller, every cycle

Watch the units when you pick Q and R: a 1 m position error and a 1 rad heading error are not equally bad. A common starting point (Bryson's rule) is Qii = 1 / (largest acceptable error)² and R = 1 / (largest acceptable command)².

🚗 How Tesla / Waymo / drones do it

🚘 Tesla

Tesla hasn't published how its cars turn a planned motion into steering and pedal commands, or whether any LQR-style controller is involved. Its public statement about the software is architectural: FSD v12 replaced the city-streets stack with “a single end-to-end neural network trained on millions of video clips, replacing over 300k lines of explicit C++ code” (release notes, early 2024).

Sources: FSD v12 release notes (third-party archive)

🚕 Waymo

Waymo hasn't published its vehicle controllers. The closest open example of a production-style AV stack is Baidu Apollo: its lateral controller linearizes a dynamic bicycle model (with tyre cornering stiffness), runs at 0.01 s steps, uses Q weights of 0.05 on lateral error and 1.0 on heading error, and iterates the Riccati equation up to 150 times with a tolerance of 0.01, then adds a curvature feedforward term.

Sources: Apollo LQR config, Apollo Riccati solver

🛸 Drones (PX4, ArduPilot)

Mainstream autopilots do not use LQR. PX4's multicopter controller is a cascade of P and PID loops (position P → velocity PID → attitude P → rate PID), with integrator limits against windup; ArduPilot's is similar (PSC_* and ATC_RAT_* PIDs). Reason: cascaded PIDs are easy to tune per axis in the field without a precise model. LQR shows up in research quadrotors and in custom controllers built on these stacks (Lesson 8.7).

Sources: PX4 controller diagrams, ArduPilot Copter parameters

Why they differ: LQR shines when you have a decent model and coupled states, like a car's lateral dynamics at a known speed, so open AV stacks use it. Hobby and commercial autopilots fly thousands of different airframes whose exact mass and inertia nobody measures, so they prefer loops a user can tune one at a time. Tesla's public direction is learning more of the driving task end to end; what remains classical under the hood isn't published.

🎤 Interview angle

“When would you use LQR instead of PID, and how do you pick Q and R?” A strong answer:

  • LQR for multi-state, coupled systems with a usable linear model (lateral car dynamics, balancing, attitude around hover); PID for single loops or when the model is unknown.
  • Explains Q vs R as priorities, only the ratio matters, and starts from Bryson's rule (1/max-error², 1/max-command²) before iterating in simulation.
  • Knows the failure modes: linearization validity, actuator saturation (LQR doesn't see it), steady disturbances (add an integral state, LQI), unmeasured states (pair with a Kalman filter: LQG).
  • Mentions how it's computed: discrete Riccati equation, offline or per operating point (gain scheduling with speed for cars), and that MPC is LQR's constrained, finite-horizon cousin.

🤯 Fun fact · an 18th-century equation steering 21st-century cars

Jacopo Riccati, a Venetian nobleman who preferred his home to academies and turned down Peter the Great's offer to lead the St Petersburg Academy of Science (around 1725), is “chiefly known for the Riccati differential equation” (MacTutor). Its matrix version, which you just iterated in your browser, runs inside Apollo's steering loop on every cycle (source).

🌉 How it connects

LQR is PID's optimal, multi-state grown-up (Lesson 8.1) and the principled version of path tracking's hand-picked gain (Lesson 8.3: its states are the same lateral and heading errors). Its mirror image is the Kalman filter (Lesson 3.3): the same Riccati equation, run forward to weigh measurements instead of backward to weigh actions; together they make LQG. Lesson 8.5's MPC keeps the quadratic cost but solves it over a finite horizon with constraints, every cycle. The cost-to-go P is a value function, the same idea as in the RL course's MDPs; and minimizing a quadratic is the Math course's loss landscapes with a bowl you can solve exactly.

Check yourself

1. A drone (double integrator) uses LQR with q = 4 on position, qv = 0 and r = 1. Using the closed form k₁ = √(q/r), k₂ = √(qv/r + 2k₁), what is K?
k₁ = √(4/1) = 2, k₂ = √(0 + 2 × 2) = 2. So u = −2x − 2v. Starting 1 m off at rest, the first command is −2 m/s².
2. You double every entry of Q and double R. What happens to the LQR gain K?
Scaling the whole cost by 2 doesn't change which controller minimizes it, so K is identical (P doubles). To make the controller more aggressive you must change the balance: raise Q relative to R.
3. An LQR holds a drone's position perfectly in calm air, but in a steady breeze it hovers 40 cm downwind and never comes back. Why, and what's the standard fix?
At steady state u = −k₁·x must cancel the wind's push d, so x = d/k₁ stays nonzero. PID's I term exists for exactly this (Lesson 8.1). Adding ∫error as an extra state and solving LQR on the bigger model (LQI) drives the offset to zero.
📝 TL;DR
  • LQR: linear model + quadratic cost Σ(xᵀQx + uᵀRu) → the best feedback is a fixed gain, u = −Kx, on the whole state.
  • Q = which errors hurt, R = how much effort hurts; only the ratio matters. Double integrator, q = qv = r = 1 → K ≈ [1, 1.73], a well-damped PD.
  • K comes from iterating the discrete Riccati equation backwards from P = Q until it stops changing (a few hundred passes here).
  • It beats separate PIDs on coupled systems (cart-pole: PID on the angle loses the cart), but needs a model, ignores actuator limits, has no integral action and breaks far from the linearization point.
  • Apollo steers with LQR; PX4 and ArduPilot fly cascaded PIDs; constraints are MPC's job (Lesson 8.5).
