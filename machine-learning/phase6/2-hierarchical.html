Phase 6 · Lesson 2

Hierarchical clustering: build the whole family tree, cut it where you like

Instead of asking “how many groups?” up front, agglomerative clustering merges the two closest groups again and again until everything is one tree, a dendrogram. You choose the number of clusters afterwards by cutting the tree. Sometimes the tree itself is the answer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • k-means needs k in advance and likes round, equal-sized clusters (Lesson 6.1).
  • Distance between two points: straight-line (Euclidean) by default, on scaled features (Lessons 1.4 and 3.1).
  • Silhouette: how much closer a point is to its own cluster than to the next one, between −1 and 1 (Lesson 6.1).
🎯 Hook: the picture that made genomics readable

In 1998, Michael Eisen and colleagues at Stanford faced a wall of numbers: the activity of 2,467 yeast genes measured under dozens of conditions. Nobody can read a spreadsheet like that. They ran average-linkage hierarchical clustering (a 1958 method from taxonomy), reordered the rows by the tree, and painted each number as a red or green square.

Suddenly the picture had blocks: genes that rise and fall together sat side by side, with the tree drawn alongside. Genes of known similar function landed together, even when their DNA sequences were unrelated. That red-green “clustered heatmap” is still in thousands of biology papers. Why a tree, and not just k groups? Because nobody knew how many groups there were, and the nesting (big families containing smaller ones) was the interesting part.

🔮 Predict first

Two dense blobs joined by a thin line of 6 points, plus one stray point far away. You merge “closest groups first”, where the distance between two groups is the distance between their closest members (single linkage). Then you cut the tree into 2 clusters. What do you get?

36 points and 1. The bridge points are close neighbours, so single linkage walks across them and joins the blobs early (at distance 0.73), long before it reaches the stray point (2.51). That's called chaining. Ward linkage splits them 16 / 21 instead. See it in Playground 1 (“blobs + bridge”).

Numbers first: merge the closest pair, repeat

Six numbers again: 1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 13. Start with six clusters of one. Merge the two closest clusters, write down the distance at which they merged, repeat. The only question is how to measure the distance between two groups. That choice is the linkage:

LinkageDistance between groups A and BMerge heights for 1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 13
singleclosest pair (one from A, one from B)1, 1, 2, 3, then 5 (4 to 9)
completefarthest pair1, 1, 3, 4, then 12 (1 to 13)
average (UPGMA)average of all cross pairs1, 1, 2.5, 3.5, then 8.33
Wardhow much the within-cluster spread (k-means inertia) grows if you merge1, 1, 2.89, 4.04, then 14.43

All four agree on the order here: {1, 2} and {9, 10}, then 4 joins the first, 13 the second, and finally the two halves. The last merge height differs a lot: it's the “how far apart are the two big groups” number, and a big jump before the last merges is what tells you the data has that many clusters.

✏️ Worked example: average linkage for the final merge

Groups A = {1, 2, 4} and B = {9, 10, 13}. Average linkage averages all 3 × 3 = 9 cross distances:

  • From 1: 8, 9, 12. From 2: 7, 8, 11. From 4: 5, 6, 9.
  • Sum = 29 + 26 + 20 = 75, so the average is 75 ÷ 9 = 8.33.
  • Single linkage takes the smallest (4 to 9 = 5); complete takes the largest (1 to 13 = 12).
💡 Big idea Agglomerative clustering builds a hierarchy: n − 1 merges, each with a height. The dendrogram draws it as a tree. Cutting the tree with a horizontal line at some height gives a flat clustering: every branch crossing the line is one cluster. One fit gives you every number of clusters at once, and the linkage decides what shapes the clusters can take.
🧠 Analogy: merging teams at a growing company

A company starts as single people. The two who work together most become a team; then that team and the next-closest person or team merge; teams merge into departments, departments into divisions. Draw it and you have an org chart, upside down.

“How many groups does the company have?” has no single answer: 40 teams, 8 departments, 3 divisions. It depends on the level you look at, which is exactly a cut through the dendrogram. And how you define “work together” between two teams (their two closest colleagues? everyone on average?) changes which teams merge: that's the linkage.

🤔 Why this? Why hierarchical clustering?
  1. The problem before. k-means needs k up front, returns one flat answer, and changes with the random start. Many questions are naturally nested (species → genus → family; topics → subtopics), and a flat list of k groups throws that structure away.
  2. What people tried. Running k-means for every k and comparing: the answers for k = 3 and k = 4 needn't be consistent (a group can split and reshuffle), so you can't read them as a tree. Taxonomists drew trees by hand from expert judgement, which doesn't scale and isn't reproducible.
  3. Why this works. Greedy merging is deterministic and consistent: the k-cluster answer is always the (k+1)-cluster answer with two groups merged. For 1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 13 one run gives every answer, and the gap between the last merge (5 with single linkage) and the one before (3) says “two groups”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It needs all n(n − 1)/2 pairwise distances: 50 million for 10,000 rows (400 MB as 8-byte numbers), 5 billion for 100,000 (40 GB). Merges are never undone, so an early mistake stays. Single linkage chains through noise; complete and Ward prefer compact blobs and can split long shapes.
  5. What came next. Connectivity constraints (only merge neighbours) and BIRCH make it scale; HDBSCAN (Lesson 6.3) builds a density-based hierarchy and picks the stable clusters automatically. For a few huge flat groups, k-means (6.1) remains the fast choice.

🔮 Predict first

Two crescent moons, cut into 2 clusters. Which linkage recovers the two moons exactly?

Single. Each moon is a chain of close neighbours, and the gap between the moons is wider than any gap along a moon, so single linkage gets 100%. Complete, average and Ward think in blobs and cut across the moons (67 to 76% agreement on the 120 points below). The very property that hurt on the bridge (following chains) is what helps here. The price: every pair of points

To find the closest pair of clusters, you need the distances between all pairs of points: n(n − 1)/2 of them. Even the clever algorithms (a minimum spanning tree for single linkage; the “nearest-neighbour chain” for complete, average and Ward) take time proportional to n² and memory for the full distance table. k-means, by contrast, computes n × k distances per round.

The usual escape routes: cluster a sample (or k-means centroids, say 1,000 of them) hierarchically; add a connectivity graph so only nearby points may merge (scikit-learn notes it's computationally expensive without one); or use HDBSCAN (Lesson 6.3), which builds its hierarchy from a minimum spanning tree with spatial indexes.

When the hierarchy is the product

  • Phylogenetics: average linkage was published by Sokal and Michener in 1958 (as UPGMA) for building evolutionary trees from similarity tables; distance-tree methods are still a staple.
  • Biology heatmaps: order genes and samples by their dendrograms so blocks of co-expression become visible (the Eisen picture; seaborn.clustermap does it in one line).
  • Taxonomies: group products, documents or support tickets into nested categories, then let humans name the branches at the level of detail they need.
  • Feature grouping: cluster features by correlation to find redundant ones (scikit-learn's FeatureAgglomeration).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn AgglomerativeClustering (default linkage="ward"; cut by n_clusters or distance_threshold) for labels; SciPy scipy.cluster.hierarchy (linkage, dendrogram, fcluster) when you want the tree itself.

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster, dendrogram
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=300, noise=0.05, random_state=0)

for link in ["single", "complete", "average", "ward"]:
    labels = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage=link).fit_predict(X)
    print(link, round(adjusted_rand_score(y, labels), 2))   # 1.0 = perfect match with the moons

# the tree itself: one row per merge [cluster a, cluster b, height, size]
Z = linkage(X, method="average")
labels = fcluster(Z, t=2, criterion="maxclust")   # cut into 2 clusters (or criterion="distance", t=height)
# dendrogram(Z, truncate_mode="lastp", p=30)       # draw only the top 30 branches with matplotlib

On these moons single linkage scores an adjusted Rand index (ARI, 1 = identical grouping, about 0 = random) of 1.0; complete, average and Ward score between 0.24 and 0.43. For big data pass connectivity=kneighbors_graph(X, 10), or cluster a sample. Non-Euclidean distances (cosine for text, correlation for genes) work with metric= and any linkage except Ward.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

You have 3,000 support tickets embedded as vectors and no categories. Average-linkage with cosine distance gives a tree; cut it at a few levels and read the branches with the support lead. You get a draft taxonomy in a day, at sizes where n² is no problem.

At scale

With millions of items, teams first compress with k-means into a few thousand centroids, then build the hierarchy over the centroids (or use HDBSCAN). The tree becomes a navigation structure: product categories, topic browsers, coarse-to-fine search.

🎤 Interview angle

“How does agglomerative clustering work? What's a dendrogram? Single vs complete vs Ward?” A strong answer:

  • Explains bottom-up merging of the closest pair, n − 1 merges, and the dendrogram's heights; cutting gives a flat clustering, and big height gaps suggest k.
  • Defines the linkages and their effects: single = chaining but handles non-convex shapes; complete = compact; average = compromise, any metric; Ward = minimal increase in within-cluster variance, k-means-like.
  • States the cost: O(n²) memory for distances, O(n²) to O(n³) time; mitigations: sampling, connectivity, clustering centroids.
  • Knows when the tree is the deliverable (taxonomies, phylogenetics, clustered heatmaps) and that there's no natural predict for new points.
🏭 In the real world
Clustered gene-expression heatmaps 🧪 Research · 1998 Eisen et al., PNAS: Cluster analysis and display of genome-wide expression patterns

How: pairwise average-linkage clustering of 2,467 yeast genes using a correlation-based similarity, then rows ordered by the tree and drawn as a colour grid.

Why this method: no one knew the number of groups, the similarity was a correlation (not a Euclidean distance), and the nested tree itself was informative: genes of similar function clustered together.

Evolutionary trees (UPGMA) 📜 History · 1958 UPGMA: Sokal & Michener's average-linkage method

How: merge the two most similar species (or sequences), average their distances to everyone else, repeat; the merge heights become branch lengths.

Why this method: taxonomy is a hierarchy. UPGMA assumes a roughly constant rate of change; modern phylogenetics uses refinements (neighbour joining, likelihood methods) built on the same distance-tree idea.

The standard implementations 🧰 Open source SciPy: scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage

How: single linkage via a minimum spanning tree and complete/average/Ward via the nearest-neighbour chain algorithm, both O(n²) time; all methods use O(n²) memory.

Why this method: one linkage matrix feeds dendrogram, fcluster and plotting tools such as seaborn's clustermap.

Ward's criterion 📜 History · 1963 Ward's method (J. H. Ward, JASA 1963)

How: at every step merge the pair of clusters that increases the total within-cluster sum of squares the least.

Why this method: it optimizes the same objective as k-means greedily, so it gives regular, k-means-like clusters, plus the whole tree; scikit-learn made it the default.

🤯 Fun fact · one tree, many pictures

A dendrogram's left-to-right order isn't unique: at every merge you can swap the two branches. Eisen's paper points out that a tree of n items has 2n−1 orderings consistent with it. For their 2,467 genes that's a number with over 740 digits, so they used extra information (average expression) to choose one. Two dendrograms that look different can be exactly the same clustering.

🌉 How it connects

Ward linkage is k-means' objective (6.1) done greedily; single linkage's chaining is the weakness HDBSCAN (6.3) fixes by working with density; the silhouette from 6.1 scores any cut. Check yourself

1. Groups A = {0, 2} and B = {5, 9} on a number line. What is the distance between them under single, complete and average linkage?
Cross distances: 0→5 = 5, 0→9 = 9, 2→5 = 3, 2→9 = 7. Single = closest = 3; complete = farthest = 9; average = (5 + 9 + 3 + 7) ÷ 4 = 6.
2. You cluster 300 noisy customer records with single linkage and get one cluster of 296 and four singletons, whatever k you choose. What's happening and what do you try?
Single linkage joins groups as soon as any two members are close, so a trail of noisy points glues everything together and the last merges only pick off isolated points. Ward/average resist this; HDBSCAN (Lesson 6.3) labels the sparse bridge points as noise instead.
3. A colleague wants hierarchical clustering on 2 million user embeddings. Roughly how much memory does the full pairwise distance table need (8 bytes per distance)?
n(n − 1)/2 ≈ (2×10⁶)² ÷ 2 = 2×10¹² distances × 8 bytes = 1.6×10¹³ bytes ≈ 16 TB. Instead: k-means to a few thousand centroids, then a hierarchy over those; or connectivity constraints; or HDBSCAN.
📝 TL;DR
  • Agglomerative clustering merges the closest pair of clusters n − 1 times; the dendrogram records every merge height.
  • Cut the tree at a height to get clusters; a long stretch with no merges suggests the natural k.
  • Linkage sets the shape bias: single chains (and traces curves), complete and Ward make compact blobs, average is the all-rounder for any distance.
  • Cost is O(n²) memory (about 40 GB at 100,000 rows): sample, use connectivity, or cluster centroids.
  • Use it when the hierarchy itself matters: taxonomies, phylogenetics, clustered heatmaps. Next: density-based clustering (6.3).
