Gaussian mixtures: soft, stretchy clusters you can sample from

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) says the data was made by a few bell-shaped clouds, each with its own centre, shape, tilt and size. It fits them with the EM algorithm, gives every point a probability of belonging to each cloud, and doubles as a density model that can score and generate data.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • k-means alternates “assign to the nearest centre” and “move centres to the mean”, and assumes round clusters (Lesson 6.1).
  • A Gaussian (normal) distribution is the bell curve; in 2-D its contours are ellipses set by a covariance matrix (Math 4.3).
  • Likelihood: how probable the data is under a model; fitting = choosing parameters that make it large (Math 5.3).
🎯 Hook: Karl Pearson's crabs and a ninth-degree polynomial

In 1894 the zoologist Walter Weldon measured the forehead-to-body-length ratio of female shore crabs and found a lopsided histogram. Weldon suspected the lopsidedness could mean the population was splitting in two. One kind of crab, or two mixed together? His colleague Karl Pearson tried to split it into a mixture of two bell curves.

Doing it by hand meant solving a ninth-degree polynomial. Pearson did it, in what's usually called the first mixture-model paper. It took another 83 years until Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977) described the general, almost mechanical recipe that a laptop now runs in milliseconds: the EM algorithm. Same idea as k-means' two steps, but with probabilities instead of hard choices.

🔮 Predict first

Two bell curves of the same width: one centred at 0, one at 4, equally common. A point lands at x = 1.5. k-means would just say “cluster 0 (closer)”. What probability does a mixture model give that it came from the curve at 0?

88%. The bell at 0 has height 0.1295 at x = 1.5; the bell at 4 has 0.0175. The responsibility is 0.1295 ÷ (0.1295 + 0.0175) = 0.881. Bell curves fall off with the square of the distance, so being a bit closer counts for a lot. At x = 2 (exactly in between) it's 50/50; at x = 3 it's 1.8%.

Numbers first: responsibilities

A GMM with K components has, for each component k, a weight πk (how common it is), a mean μk and a covariance Σk (its shape). The responsibility of component k for point x is the share of the probability of x that k explains:

rk(x) = πk·N(x | μk, Σk) ⁄ Σj πj·N(x | μj, Σj) “How tall component k's bell is at x, times how common k is, as a share of all the bells together.”

Notice the last column: the weights matter. If the first group is four times as common, a point exactly in the middle is 80% likely to be from it. k-means can't express any of this: every point is 100% one cluster.

💡 Big idea A GMM is a generative story: pick a component with probability πk, then draw a point from that component's Gaussian. Fitting it finds the components that make the data most likely. You get soft cluster memberships, clusters that can be stretched and tilted (full covariance), and a density p(x) you can use to score how normal a point is or to generate new points.
🧠 Analogy: whose dog barked?

Three dogs live on your street: a big one by the park, a small one by the shop, and a restless one who wanders the whole length of the street. You hear a bark near the shop. It's probably the small dog, but the wanderer goes everywhere, so maybe 70/30. That split is a responsibility.

Now you want to learn where each dog hangs out. Count each bark as 0.7 of a bark for the small dog and 0.3 for the wanderer, and work out each dog's typical spot and range from those fractional barks (M-step). Then reassess who probably barked (E-step). Repeat. That's EM.

🤔 Why this? Why Gaussian mixtures?
  1. The problem before. k-means gives hard labels and round clusters of similar size. Real groups overlap (a customer can be part bargain-hunter, part loyalist), have different spreads, and are often stretched along a direction (spend grows with visits). Hard labels also hide uncertainty: a point on the border is as “sure” as one in the middle.
  2. What people tried. Pearson's 1894 method of moments matched averages and spreads by solving a polynomial, which is painful beyond two components. Fuzzy versions of k-means gave soft labels but no probability model to compare, sample or score.
  3. Why this works. EM turns a hard problem (unknown parameters and unknown memberships) into two easy ones: given memberships, means and covariances are weighted averages; given parameters, memberships are the responsibilities above. Each round can only raise the likelihood. On this lesson's three stretched clusters, a full-covariance GMM matches the true groups 98% of the time; k-means manages 83%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You still choose K (BIC helps). EM finds local optima and depends on the start (scikit-learn starts from k-means). Each component is an ellipse, so crescents need several components. Full covariances have many parameters in high dimensions (d(d+1)/2 each: 2,080 for d = 64) and can collapse onto a few points without regularization.
  5. What came next. Bayesian GMMs (BayesianGaussianMixture) switch off unneeded components automatically; mixtures of other distributions model counts or text; and density estimation in high dimensions moved to kernel density estimates and deep generative models (normalizing flows, diffusion), covered in the sibling Deep Learning course.

✏️ Worked example: one M-step on four numbers

Points 0, 1, 3, 4 and two components. After an E-step the responsibilities for component A are 0.9, 0.8, 0.2, 0.1 (component B gets the rest: 0.1, 0.2, 0.8, 0.9).

  • Weight: πA = (0.9 + 0.8 + 0.2 + 0.1) ÷ 4 = 0.5.
  • Mean: μA = (0.9·0 + 0.8·1 + 0.2·3 + 0.1·4) ÷ 2.0 = 1.8 ÷ 2 = 0.9. (Hard k-means would have used only 0 and 1: mean 0.5.)
  • Variance: (0.9·0.81 + 0.8·0.01 + 0.2·4.41 + 0.1·9.61) ÷ 2.0 = 2.58 ÷ 2 = 1.29. Uncertain far points widen the bell.

Covariance types: how much shape freedom?

The covariance matrix sets each ellipse's shape. Constraining it trades flexibility for fewer parameters (here d = number of features):

Switch covariance types in Playground 1 and run to convergence: diag can't tilt, spherical can't stretch, and tied forces all three clouds into one shape. Agreement drops from 98% to the high 70s and 80s.

Choosing K with BIC

Log-likelihood always rises with more components (more bells can hug the data tighter), so it can't choose K by itself. The Bayesian information criterion charges for parameters:

BIC = −2 · log-likelihood + p · ln(n) “Badness of fit, plus a fee for every parameter that grows slowly with the amount of data. Lower is better.”

Example: our 3-component full GMM has p = 2 weights + 6 mean numbers + 9 covariance numbers = 17 parameters. With n = 300, the fee is 17 × ln 300 = 17 × 5.70 = 97.0. Its log-likelihood is −854.4, so BIC = 1,708.8 + 97.0 = 1,805.8. (AIC uses a fee of 2 per parameter instead and tends to pick bigger models.)

🔮 Predict first

The data truly has 3 stretched clouds. You fit spherical GMMs (circles only) with K = 1 to 7 and pick the K with the lowest BIC. Which K wins?

A larger K. Circles can't follow a stretched cloud, so BIC keeps rewarding more circles strung along each cloud: it is still falling at K = 7. With full covariance, BIC bottoms out at exactly K = 3. BIC chooses K given the shape family; the wrong family gives the wrong K. See both curves below.

A density you can score and sample

A fitted GMM gives p(x) everywhere: a smooth estimate of where data lives. That's useful for anomaly scores (low p(x) = unusual, Lesson 6.7) and for generating realistic synthetic points: choose a component by its weight, draw from its Gaussian. Even shapes that aren't Gaussian, like moons, can be approximated by enough small ellipses, but too many components start memorizing the training points.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn GaussianMixture (defaults covariance_type="full", n_init=1, init_params="kmeans") with predict_proba, score_samples (log density), sample, bic, aic; BayesianGaussianMixture to prune components automatically.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, random_state=170)
X = X @ np.array([[0.6, -0.64], [-0.41, 0.85]])          # stretch and tilt the blobs

bics = {}
for cov in ["full", "diag", "spherical", "tied"]:
    for k in range(1, 8):
        gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type=cov, n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
        bics[(cov, k)] = gm.bic(X)
best = min(bics, key=bics.get)
print("lowest BIC:", best)                                     # ('tied', 3): one shared stretch gave all three blobs the same shape

gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="full", random_state=0).fit(X)
print(gm.predict_proba(X[:3]).round(3))      # soft memberships, rows sum to 1
print(gm.score_samples(X[:3]).round(2))    # log p(x): low = unusual
new_points, comp = gm.sample(5)               # generate synthetic data

Scale features first (Lesson 1.4), use n_init of 3 to 10 for steadier fits, and keep reg_covar (default 1e-6) or raise it if components collapse onto a few points. With many features, try "diag", or reduce dimensions with PCA first (6.5).

Week one

Segment users with a GMM instead of k-means when you need a confidence: “this user is 70% power-user, 30% casual” lets marketing target only the sure ones. Use score_samples as a quick anomaly score for sensor readings, with a threshold set from a validation week.

At scale

GMMs were the backbone of speech systems for decades (GMM-HMM acoustic models and GMM-UBM speaker verification) until deep networks took over around the 2010s, and they still run in video pipelines (OpenCV's MOG2 background subtraction keeps a small mixture per pixel). Large teams also use mixtures to model latency or price distributions that have several modes.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between k-means and a GMM? How does EM work?” A strong answer:

  • GMM = soft assignments (responsibilities) + per-component covariance (elliptical, different sizes) + weights; k-means = hard, round, equal-ish clusters, a limiting case.
  • EM: E-step computes responsibilities from current parameters; M-step re-estimates weights, means, covariances as responsibility-weighted averages; log-likelihood never decreases; local optima → several inits, k-means init.
  • Choose K with BIC (or AIC, held-out likelihood); covariance type trades flexibility for parameters.
  • Uses beyond clustering: density estimation, anomaly scores, sampling synthetic data; failure modes: singular covariances, crescents.
🏭 In the real world
Speaker verification (GMM-UBM) 🧪 Research · 2000 Reynolds, Quatieri & Dunn: Speaker Verification Using Adapted Gaussian Mixture Models (MIT Lincoln Lab)

How: a large “universal background” GMM models speech from many people; each speaker's model is adapted from it; a voice is accepted if it's much more likely under the claimed speaker's GMM than under the background.

Why this method: a GMM is a flexible density over short-time audio features, and the likelihood ratio gives a principled accept/reject score.

Background subtraction in video 🧰 Open source OpenCV: BackgroundSubtractorMOG2

How: a “Gaussian Mixture-based Background/Foreground Segmentation Algorithm”: each pixel's recent colours are modelled by a few Gaussians; a pixel that fits none of the background components is foreground (a moving person or car).

Why this method: one pixel can have several normal looks (leaves swaying, a flickering screen); a mixture captures that, and updates cheaply in real time.

The EM algorithm 📜 History · 1977 Dempster, Laird & Rubin: Maximum Likelihood from Incomplete Data via the EM Algorithm

How: a general recipe for models with hidden variables (here: which component produced each point), alternating expected memberships and parameter updates, with the likelihood never decreasing.

Why this method: it unified many special-case methods; the same EM shows up in hidden Markov models, missing-data imputation and topic models.

The first mixture model 📜 History · 1894 Pearson: Contributions to the Mathematical Theory of Evolution (Phil. Trans. A)

How: fitted a mixture of two normal curves to Weldon's crab measurements by choosing its five parameters so that the data's moments (average, spread, skew and beyond) matched the model's.

Why this method: the question “one population or two?” is exactly a mixture question, still asked today about customers, cells and sensor modes.

🤯 Fun fact · two million tiny mixtures

OpenCV's MOG2 background subtractor keeps a separate little Gaussian mixture for every pixel. On a 1920 × 1080 security camera that's 2,073,600 mixtures, each updated on every frame: at 30 frames a second, about 62 million mixture updates per second, on an ordinary CPU. Small, cheap models, used in bulk, are what made real-time video analysis possible long before deep learning.

Check yourself

1. At point x, component A's weighted density is 0.06 and component B's is 0.02. What is A's responsibility for x?
rA = 0.06 ÷ (0.06 + 0.02) = 0.75. Responsibilities are shares, so they always sum to 1 across components; 3 is the ratio A ÷ B.
2. Customer segments overlap, and marketing wants to target only users who are clearly in the “high-value” group. k-means or GMM?
Soft memberships are exactly “how sure are we”. k-means gives every user a hard label, so a user on the border looks as certain as one in the middle. Scale features, pick K with BIC, and set the probability threshold with the marketing budget.
3. You fit GMMs with K = 1 to 20. Training log-likelihood keeps rising all the way to K = 20. What should you conclude?
Extra components can only help on the training data (nested models). BIC adds p·ln n to penalize parameters; held-out likelihood reveals overfitting directly (in Playground 3, test log-likelihood peaks at K = 6 and falls by K = 16).
📝 TL;DR
  • A GMM models data as K Gaussian clouds with weights, means and covariances; responsibilities give soft memberships.
  • EM alternates E (responsibilities) and M (weighted re-estimates); the log-likelihood never goes down; start from k-means, try several inits.
  • Covariance type sets the shape family: full (any ellipse), diag, spherical, tied. k-means is the hard, round limit.
  • Choose K with BIC = −2 log L + p ln n or held-out likelihood; the wrong shape family picks the wrong K.
  • It's also a density: score points (anomalies, 6.7) and sample new ones. Next: PCA, the directions that matter (6.5).
