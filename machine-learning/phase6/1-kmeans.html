Phase 6 · Lesson 1

k-means: drop k pins, let the data pull them into place

No labels, just rows. k-means finds k groups by repeating two dead-simple steps until nothing moves. It compresses images, seeds vector databases and segments customers, and it's the first clustering method everyone reaches for. Here's how to run it, how to pick k, and when it lies to you.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Distance between two rows: the straight-line (Euclidean) length between them, as in k-nearest neighbours (Lesson 3.1).
  • Scaling: a feature measured in thousands drowns one measured in units, unless you standardize (Lesson 1.4).
  • Unsupervised learning: there is no label y to predict; you look for structure in the features alone (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook: a photo with 96,615 colours, redrawn with 64

scikit-learn's documentation has a classic demo: take a photo of the Summer Palace in Beijing and treat every pixel as a point in 3-D colour space (red, green, blue). Run k-means with k = 64. Replace each pixel by its cluster's centre. The photo goes from 96,615 unique colours to 64, and at a glance you can hardly tell.

The algorithm behind it was written down by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs in 1957, for squeezing telephone signals into fewer levels (pulse-code modulation). It wasn't published as a journal article until 1982. Almost 70 years later it still runs inside image tools, vector databases and marketing dashboards. Why does something so simple work so often, and when does it quietly fail?

About 16. With 4 colours you get posterized bands (average error 34 colour levels per pixel); with 16 the error drops to about 12, which is the size of the random grain we added to every pixel. Beyond that you're mostly paying to store noise. Try it in Playground 1.

💡 Big idea k-means describes the data with k representative points (centroids) and gives every row to its nearest one. It searches for the centroids that make the total squared distance from rows to their centroid, the inertia, as small as possible. Two steps on repeat do it: assign each point to its nearest centre, then update each centre to the mean of its points.
🧠 Analogy: opening k coffee shops

You're opening 3 coffee shops in a new town and don't know where people live. Put the shops anywhere. Every resident walks to their nearest shop (assign). Each shop then moves to the middle of its own customers (update). Now some residents have a closer shop, so they switch; the shops move again. After a few rounds nobody switches and the shops stop moving.

Notice what “nearest” means: as the crow flies, in every direction equally. Shops end up serving roughly round neighbourhoods. A town shaped like a long river valley, or a dense centre next to sparse farms, gets carved up oddly. That is exactly where k-means fails too.

🤔 Why this? Why k-means?
  1. The problem before. You have thousands of customers, pixels or documents and no labels, but you suspect natural groups. Checking every possible grouping is hopeless: 200 points into 4 groups can be split in more ways than there are atoms in the universe.
  2. What people tried. Grouping by hand with business rules (“spend over $500 = VIP”), which only finds the groups you already imagined, and building full hierarchies of merges (Lesson 6.2), which needs all n² pairwise distances and gets slow past tens of thousands of rows.
  3. Why this works. Each step can only lower the inertia: moving a point to a nearer centre shrinks its distance, and the mean is the spot with the smallest total squared distance to a set of points. In the worked example below, inertia goes 238 → 46.9 → 13.3 and stops in three rounds. Each round costs only n × k distance computations, so it scales to millions of rows.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You must choose k. It finds a local minimum that depends on the starting centres. It assumes round, similar-sized, similarly spread clusters, and it's sensitive to feature scales and outliers (a mean gets dragged). Every point is forced into some cluster, even pure noise.
  5. What came next. k-means++ starts (2007) and multiple restarts fix most bad starts; MiniBatchKMeans handles huge data. For other shapes: hierarchical clustering (6.2), DBSCAN/HDBSCAN for arbitrary shapes and noise (6.3), and Gaussian mixtures for stretched, overlapping, soft clusters (6.4).

The algorithm: assign, update, repeat

inertia = Σi ‖xi − μc(i)‖² “Add up, over every point, the squared distance to the centre of the cluster it was assigned to. Smaller means tighter clusters.”

Why the loss can only go down, step by step, is shown in Math 5.2b: k-means. Here we focus on using it and choosing it.

✏️ Worked example: six numbers, two clusters, by hand

Points 1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 13. Start (badly) with centres at 1 and 2.

  • Assign: only 1 is nearer to centre 1. Clusters {1} and {2, 4, 9, 10, 13}. Inertia = 0 + (0 + 4 + 49 + 64 + 121) = 238.
  • Update: centres become 1 and (2 + 4 + 9 + 10 + 13) ÷ 5 = 7.6.
  • Assign: 2 and 4 are now nearer to 1 (distances 1 and 3, versus 5.6 and 3.6). Clusters {1, 2, 4} and {9, 10, 13}. Inertia = (0 + 1 + 9) + (1.96 + 5.76 + 29.16) = 46.88.
  • Update: centres 7 ÷ 3 = 2.33 and 32 ÷ 3 = 10.67. Assign: nobody switches, inertia 13.33. Converged.

Better starts: k-means++ and restarts

Random starts often put two centres in the same blob. k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) spreads them out: pick the first centre at random from the data, then pick each next centre with probability proportional to its squared distance to the nearest centre chosen so far. Far-away points are likely picks, so every blob tends to get a centre. The paper proves the expected inertia is within a factor of about log k of the best possible, before a single Lloyd step.

With a 23% chance of a bad run, 10 restarts all failing has probability 0.23¹⁰, about 4 in 10 million. If your data is unusual, set n_init=10 explicitly; it's cheap insurance.

Choosing k: the elbow and the silhouette

Inertia always falls as k grows (with k = n every point is its own centre and inertia is 0), so “smallest inertia” can't pick k. Two common tools:

  • Elbow: plot inertia against k and look for the bend where extra clusters stop buying much. On the 4 blobs: 1,986 → 925 → 539 → 222 → 195 → 171. The big drops stop at 4.
  • Silhouette: for each point, compare a = its average distance to points in its own cluster with b = its average distance to the nearest other cluster.
s = (b − a) ⁄ max(a, b) “How much closer a point is to its own cluster than to the next-best one, as a share. Near 1: snugly placed. Near 0: on the border. Negative: probably in the wrong cluster.”

Example: a = 1, b = 4 gives s = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. The silhouette score is the average of s over all points; pick the k that maximizes it. On our blobs it peaks at k = 4 with 0.625 (k = 3: 0.548, k = 5: 0.538). It needs all pairwise distances, so on big data compute it on a sample (silhouette_score(..., sample_size=10_000)).

The honest lesson of the “uniform” setting: k-means always returns k clusters, even from structureless noise, and the silhouette stays flat (0.37 to 0.40 for every k from 2 to 8) with no clear peak. Low silhouette everywhere is a hint that the groups are an artefact. In practice, usefulness often decides k: a marketing team that can run 5 campaigns wants 5 segments, as long as they're stable and interpretable.

Where k-means fails

k-means draws straight boundaries halfway between centres (a Voronoi diagram), so each cluster is a convex blob, and it prefers clusters of similar size and spread. It also measures distance in raw units.

🔮 Predict first

Two interlocking crescent moons, 100 points each. You run k-means with k = 2 (the right number of groups). What happens?

It slices the plane in two with a straight boundary; each half contains a chunk of each moon. Only about 67% of points land with their own moon (50% is chance). The right k doesn't help when the shape is wrong. DBSCAN (Lesson 6.3) or single-linkage clustering (6.2) handle it.

Big data: MiniBatchKMeans

Each full k-means round touches every row. MiniBatchKMeans instead takes a small random batch (default 1,024 rows), assigns it, and nudges the centres a little towards those points, with a step that shrinks as a centre has seen more data. It converges in a fraction of the time and, in scikit-learn's words, gives results that are “generally only slightly worse” than the full algorithm. It can also learn from a stream with partial_fit.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): KMeans (defaults init="k-means++", n_init="auto", algorithm="lloyd"), MiniBatchKMeans, silhouette_score. For billions of vectors, FAISS has a GPU k-means (faiss.Kmeans).

from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, MiniBatchKMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=2000, centers=4, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=0)

# scale first: k-means measures plain Euclidean distance
for k in range(2, 8):
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0))
    labels = pipe.fit_predict(X)
    Z = pipe[0].transform(X)
    print(k, round(pipe[-1].inertia_), round(silhouette_score(Z, labels), 3))

# millions of rows: mini-batches, nearly the same centres in a fraction of the time
mbk = MiniBatchKMeans(n_clusters=4, batch_size=1024, n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
print(mbk.cluster_centers_.round(2))

After fitting: labels_, cluster_centers_, inertia_, and predict / transform (distances to every centre, a handy feature for a supervised model). On this data the silhouette peaks at k = 4. Clustering has no test set, so judge it by stability (rerun with other seeds or on a bootstrap sample: do the same groups appear?) and by whether the groups mean something to the people who'll use them.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Standardize a handful of behavioural features (orders, spend, recency), run k-means for k = 3 to 8, read the centres, name the segments with the business team. It's a one-afternoon project that often changes how a company talks about its users. Keep a frozen copy of the centres so segment 2 still means the same thing next month.

At scale

k-means is infrastructure: it quantizes vectors for search indexes (FAISS's IVF trains k-means centroids, then searches only the nearest few cells), compresses embeddings, and buckets users for experiments. It runs on GPUs or as mini-batches over billions of rows, often retrained on a schedule.

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain k-means. How do you choose k? When would you not use it?” A strong answer:

  • States the objective (minimize inertia, the within-cluster sum of squared distances) and the two alternating steps, and why each can only lower it.
  • Mentions local minima and the fixes: k-means++ starts and keeping the best of n_init runs.
  • Chooses k with the elbow, silhouette, stability across seeds, and business usefulness; notes inertia always falls with k.
  • Lists the assumptions: round, similar-sized clusters, Euclidean distance, so scale features; sensitive to outliers; can't do crescents or nested rings.
  • Names alternatives: GMM for elliptical/soft, DBSCAN/HDBSCAN for arbitrary shapes and noise, MiniBatchKMeans for big data.
🏭 In the real world
Colour quantization 🧰 Open source scikit-learn example: Color Quantization using K-Means

How: each pixel is a 3-D point; k-means with k = 64 is fitted on a sample of 1,000 pixels, then every pixel is replaced by its nearest centre, shrinking 96,615 colours to 64.

Why this method: the cluster centres are the palette, and “nearest centre” is exactly “closest colour”. The same idea (vector quantization) compresses audio and embeddings.

Vector search indexes 🧰 Open source · Meta FAISS wiki: Faiss indexes (IndexIVF)

How: an IVF index runs k-means over the vectors; each vector is stored in the list of its nearest centroid, and a query only scans the lists of a few nearest centroids.

Why this method: k-means cells are compact, so neighbours usually share a cell; searching, say, 8 of 1,000 equal-sized cells skips over 99% of the vectors. The FAISS paper scales this to a billion vectors on GPUs.

Smarter seeding 🧪 Research · 2007 Arthur & Vassilvitskii: k-means++, The Advantages of Careful Seeding (PDF)

How: choose each new starting centre with probability proportional to its squared distance from the existing ones.

Why this method: a few lines of code that give a provable O(log k) guarantee and, in their experiments, faster and better clusterings; it's now the default almost everywhere.

The origin 📜 History · 1957 k-means clustering: history

How: Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs designed the assign/update loop in 1957 to choose quantization levels for pulse-code modulation (published 1982 as “Least squares quantization in PCM”); James MacQueen coined the name “k-means” in 1967.

Why this method: it was built for compression, which is why “Lloyd's algorithm” and “vector quantization” are the same idea under two names.

🤯 Fun fact · a 25-year publication delay

Lloyd's 1957 Bell Labs memo circulated for decades before it appeared in a journal in 1982. By then others had reinvented the method (Edward Forgy published essentially the same algorithm in 1965), which is why you'll see “Lloyd–Forgy” in some books. The algorithm was so useful that people kept rediscovering it.

Check yourself

1. A point's average distance to its own cluster is a = 2, and to the nearest other cluster b = 5. What is its silhouette value?
s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b) = (5 − 2) ÷ 5 = 0.6. It's well inside its cluster. Silhouette values always lie between −1 and 1; 2.5 is b ÷ a, and 0.4 is a ÷ b.
2. You cluster customers on annual spend (in dollars, 0 to 50,000) and number of support tickets (0 to 10). The segments differ only in spend. Most likely cause?
A difference of 1,000 dollars dwarfs a difference of 5 tickets, so the tickets barely affect “nearest centre”. Put a StandardScaler before KMeans in a pipeline (Lesson 1.4), then deliberately weight features if the business wants one to count more.
3. A teammate runs k-means once with random starting centres, gets an odd result with two centres in one obvious group, and concludes “k-means doesn't work on this data”. Best reply?
k-means only goes downhill from where it starts; running longer doesn't escape a converged local minimum. k-means++ spreads the starts, and the lowest-inertia run of several is usually the global answer (here, 23% of random starts got stuck, versus 8% with k-means++).
📝 TL;DR
  • k-means alternates assign (nearest centre) and update (mean of the cluster) until nothing moves, lowering the inertia every step.
  • It finds local minima: use k-means++ starts and several restarts (n_init).
  • Choose k with the elbow, the silhouette s = (b − a)/max(a, b), stability and usefulness; inertia always falls with k.
  • It assumes round, similar-sized clusters in scaled units, and always returns k clusters, even from noise.
  • Big data: MiniBatchKMeans. Other shapes, sizes or soft groups: Lessons 6.2 to 6.4.
