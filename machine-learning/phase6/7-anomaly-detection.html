Phase 6 · Lesson 7

Anomaly detection: finding the few points that don't belong

Fraud, failing machines, intrusions, broken data pipelines: the interesting rows are rare and often unlabelled. Anomaly detectors score how unusual each point is, from simple z-scores to isolation forests and local outlier factors. The hard part isn't the score; it's choosing a threshold your team can act on.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Precision = of the points you flagged, the share that were real; recall = of the real ones, the share you flagged (Lesson 5.3).
  • Random forests average many randomized trees (Lesson 4.2); k-nearest neighbours (3.1) and density clustering (6.3) measure how crowded a point's neighbourhood is.
  • Imbalanced data: when positives are rare, accuracy is useless (Lesson 5.5).
🎯 Hook: 492 frauds in 284,807 card payments

A public dataset released by researchers including Andrea Dal Pozzolo and Gianluca Bontempi (Université Libre de Bruxelles) holds two days of card transactions by European cardholders in September 2013: 284,807 payments, 492 of them fraudulent. That's 0.172%.

A “model” that says “never fraud” is 99.83% accurate and catches nothing. And in real life you often don't even have the 492 labels: fraud is discovered weeks later, new attacks look like nothing in your history, and a machine that's about to fail has never failed before. So instead of learning “what fraud looks like”, you learn what normal looks like and flag whatever doesn't fit. How do you score “doesn't fit”, and how many alerts can a team actually check?

🔮 Predict first

That dataset has about 142,000 payments a day, 246 of them fraud. Your fraud team can review 500 alerts a day. Even with a perfect detector, what's the best precision those 500 alerts can have?

About 49%. There are only about 246 frauds a day, so at most 246 of your 500 alerts can be real: 246 ÷ 500 = 49%. Alerting randomly would find 500 × 0.00172 ≈ 0.9 frauds a day. That's why anomaly detectors are judged by precision (and recall) at a fixed alert budget, not by accuracy. Playground 3 uses exactly this.

Numbers first: the simplest detectors

Before any machine learning, two classic rules for one number at a time:

RuleFlag a value if…Example: response times 190, 195, 200, 205, 210 ms, then 290 ms
z-scoreit's more than 3 standard deviations from the mean: |x − mean| ÷ std > 3works when the data is roughly bell-shaped and outliers are few
IQR fences (Tukey)it's below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR (IQR = Q3 − Q1, the middle 50%'s width)Q1 = 196.25, Q3 = 208.75 (NumPy's default quartiles), IQR = 12.5 → fences 177.5 and 227.5 → 290 is flagged

The z-score has a trap: the outliers themselves inflate the mean and the standard deviation they're measured against. One giant outlier can mask the others.

Robust fixes: use the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) instead of mean and std, or IQR fences. They're still one-feature-at-a-time rules, though: a 25-year-old with an income of $400,000 is unusual as a combination, while neither number is extreme alone. For that you need multivariate detectors.

💡 Big idea An anomaly detector learns what normal looks like and gives every point an anomaly score. Different detectors define “unusual” differently: far from the bulk (z-score), easy to isolate with random cuts (isolation forest), less dense than its neighbours (LOF), outside a learned boundary (one-class SVM), or improbable under a density model (GMM, Lesson 6.4). Then a threshold, set by how many alerts you can handle, turns scores into flags.
🧠 Analogy: twenty questions at a party

Play “guess who” with random yes/no questions about guests: “taller than 1.8 m?”, “arrived after 9?”. To single out the one person wearing a full astronaut suit you need one or two questions. To single out one of the forty people in jeans and a T-shirt you need many, because they're all alike.

That's the isolation forest: random questions isolate the odd ones out quickly. And LOF? It notices the person standing alone in the empty corner of a crowded room, even though, by the same distance, someone in the sparse garden outside would be perfectly normal.

🤔 Why this? Why isolation-based (and density-based) detectors?
  1. The problem before. Labels for anomalies are rare, late or missing, and new anomalies don't look like old ones, so supervised classifiers can't be trained or go stale. Per-feature rules (z-scores, IQR) miss unusual combinations and suffer from masking.
  2. What people tried. Distance-based methods (flag points far from their k-th neighbour) and density estimates work, but cost about n² distance computations and need careful scaling. Fitting one Gaussian assumes one round blob of normal data.
  3. Why this works. Anomalies are “few and different” (in the isolation forest paper's words), so random axis-aligned cuts isolate them in very few steps. In Playground 2, an outlier is isolated in about 5 cuts on average, while a normal point in the middle needs about 14. Each tree uses a small subsample (256 points by default), so the forest trains in linear time with little memory.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Every detector has a threshold to set, usually with a few labels or an alert budget. Isolation forests struggle with local anomalies next to a dense cluster and with anomalies hidden in many irrelevant features. LOF needs neighbours (slower, sensitive to k). “Unusual” isn't always “bad”: a viral product launch looks anomalous too.
  5. What came next. Streaming variants (AWS's Robust Random Cut Forest), extended isolation forests with tilted cuts, autoencoder reconstruction error for images and logs (sibling Deep Learning course), and semi-supervised setups that learn from the few labels you do get (Lesson 5.5's tricks for imbalance).

Isolation forest: count the cuts

Build a tree by picking a random feature and a random threshold between that feature's min and max, splitting, and repeating on each side until every point is alone (or a depth limit is hit). The number of splits on the path from the root to a point is its path length h(x). Average it over 100 random trees.

score s(x) = 2−E[h(x)] ⁄ c(ψ) “Two to the power of minus (average path length ÷ the path length you'd expect for a typical point). Short paths give scores near 1 (anomaly); average paths give 0.5; long paths give scores below 0.5 (normal).”

c(ψ) is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree of ψ points: 2H(ψ − 1) − 2(ψ − 1)/ψ, where H is the harmonic number. For the paper's default subsample ψ = 256 it is 10.24.

✏️ Worked example: three path lengths with ψ = 256
  • An outlier isolated in 4 cuts on average: s = 2−4 / 10.24 = 2−0.39 = 0.76. Anomalous.
  • A point needing 10.24 cuts (exactly typical): s = 2−1 = 0.50.
  • A point deep in a dense cluster, 13 cuts: s = 2−1.27 = 0.41. Normal.

The paper (Liu, Ting & Zhou, 2008) recommends ψ = 256 and 100 trees; scikit-learn's defaults are the same (max_samples="auto" = min(256, n), n_estimators=100).

Contamination doesn't change the scores at all; it only sets the threshold that predict() uses (“the top 5% are outliers”). scikit-learn's default contamination="auto" uses the original paper's threshold instead. If you have an alert budget, skip predict and take the top-k score_samples yourself.

Local outlier factor: unusual for its neighbourhood

LOF (Breunig, Kriegel, Ng & Sander, 2000) compares a point's local density with the densities of its k nearest neighbours. Density here is the inverse of the average “reachability distance” to the neighbours. The score is a ratio:

LOF(x) = average density of x's k neighbours ⁄ density of x “How much sparser it is around this point than around its neighbours. About 1 = as dense as its neighbours (normal); 2 = half as dense; much bigger = outlier.”

Because it's a ratio, a point 1 unit from a very tight cluster can be an outlier while a point 1 unit from a loose cluster is normal. A global rule like a z-score or a single distance threshold can't make that distinction.

Data: one tight cluster, one loose cluster, 5 “local” outliers just outside the tight cluster (but closer to it than many loose-cluster points are to theirs), and 5 far-away global outliers. You can review 10 alerts. Which detector's top 10 contains the most real outliers?

LOF: 9 of 10. Isolation forest gets 6 (it catches the global ones but ranks loose-cluster edge points above the local outliers); the z-score gets 5. LOF's ratio-to-neighbours is exactly what “just outside a dense cluster” needs. Check it, and change the budget, below.

Outlier vs novelty detection (scikit-learn's terms): outlier detection scores a dataset that already contains anomalies (fit and score the same data: isolation forest, LOF default); novelty detection fits on clean normal data and scores new points (one-class SVM, or LOF with novelty=True).

Evaluating without (many) labels

  • Precision at your alert budget (“of the 50 alerts a day we can check, how many were real?”), and recall at that budget. This is the number the business feels.
  • Get a few dozen labelled examples, from past incidents or by reviewing the top-ranked alerts, and track precision@k over time.
  • Inject synthetic anomalies (like our ■ points) to sanity-check a detector, knowing real ones will be subtler.
  • Once you have enough labels, a supervised model with class weights (Lesson 5.5) usually beats any unsupervised detector on the anomaly types you know; keep the unsupervised one for the types you don't.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (user guide 2.7, “Novelty and Outlier Detection”): IsolationForest (n_estimators=100, max_samples="auto", contamination="auto"), LocalOutlierFactor (n_neighbors=20, novelty=False), OneClassSVM (kernel="rbf", nu=0.5), EllipticEnvelope. The PyOD library collects dozens more behind the same API.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest
from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import OneClassSVM

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X_norm, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=1000, centers=[[0, 0], [6, 3]], cluster_std=[0.5, 1.5], random_state=0)
X_out = rng.uniform(-6, 12, size=(20, 2))                       # 20 injected anomalies
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(np.vstack([X_norm, X_out]))
y = np.r_[np.zeros(1000), np.ones(20)]                          # only for evaluation

def precision_at_k(scores, k=20):                              # higher score = more anomalous
    return y[np.argsort(-scores)[:k]].mean()

iso = IsolationForest(random_state=0).fit(X)
lof = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=20).fit(X)
ocs = OneClassSVM(nu=0.05, gamma="scale").fit(X)
for name, s in [("IsolationForest", -iso.score_samples(X)),          # sklearn: lower = more abnormal, so negate
                ("LOF", -lof.negative_outlier_factor_),
                ("OneClassSVM", -ocs.score_samples(X))]:
    print(f"{name:16s} precision@20 = {precision_at_k(s):.2f}")

# contamination only moves the threshold that predict() uses (-1 = outlier)
flags = IsolationForest(contamination=0.02, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
print("flagged:", (flags == -1).sum())

Output: precision@20 of 0.60, 0.70 and 0.65; perfect scores are impossible here because some of the uniform “anomalies” landed inside the clusters. Watch the sign conventions: scikit-learn's score_samples is higher for normal points, so negate it to rank anomalies first. Isolation forests don't need scaling; LOF and one-class SVM do. For streams, consider a rolling window or AWS's Random Cut Forest.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Robust z-scores (median and MAD) on your key metrics, plus an isolation forest on daily transaction features, top 20 alerts emailed to a human each morning. Record what they find: those labels become your evaluation set, and later your supervised model.

At scale

Streaming detectors on millions of events per second (AWS's Random Cut Forest is built into its managed services), ensembles of detectors per segment (country, merchant type), per-team alert budgets, and feedback loops where analysts' verdicts retrain supervised models. Precision at budget is tracked like any other production metric.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you detect fraud with almost no labels? How does an isolation forest work?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from the base rate and the alert budget: evaluate with precision/recall at k, not accuracy.
  • Baselines first: robust z-scores / IQR per feature; then multivariate detectors.
  • Isolation forest: random feature, random split; anomalies have short average path lengths; score 2^(−E[h]/c(ψ)); subsample 256, 100 trees; contamination only sets the threshold.
  • LOF for local anomalies (density ratio to neighbours); one-class SVM for novelty detection on clean data; scaling matters for both.
  • Closes the loop: collect analyst labels, move to supervised learning with class weights, keep an unsupervised detector for new patterns; monitor drift.
🏭 In the real world
Isolation forest 🧪 Research · 2008 Liu, Ting & Zhou: Isolation Forest (ICDM 2008, PDF)

How: isolate instead of profiling normal data; random trees on subsamples of 256, 100 trees, score from the average path length.

Why this method: anomalies are few and different, so isolation is quick for them; small subsamples reduce masking and swamping and give linear time with low memory.

Streaming anomaly detection at AWS 🏢 Amazon · 2016 Amazon SageMaker: Random Cut Forest

How: an unsupervised algorithm for detecting anomalous data points, based on Guha et al.'s Robust Random Cut Forest (ICML 2016), a cousin of the isolation forest whose trees can be updated as data streams in.

Why this method: random cuts need no labels and no distance computations, and incremental trees suit metrics that arrive continuously.

A public fraud benchmark 🧪 Research · 2013 data OpenML 1597: Credit card fraud detection (ULB)

How: 284,807 card payments over two days with 492 frauds (0.172%); for confidentiality, the features are the output of a PCA transformation (Lesson 6.5) rather than the original fields.

Why this method: it's the standard playground for comparing unsupervised detectors with supervised imbalanced classifiers, measured by precision–recall rather than accuracy (Lessons 5.4 and 5.5).

Detectors behind one API 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Novelty and Outlier Detection

How: isolation forest, LOF, one-class SVM and elliptic envelope, compared on toy datasets, with the outlier-vs-novelty distinction spelled out.

Why this method: each detector makes a different assumption about “normal”; the comparison page shows where each one breaks.

🤯 Fun fact · smaller samples, better trees

Most of machine learning gets better with more data. The isolation forest paper found the opposite for each tree: “a small sample size produces better iTrees because the swamping and masking effects are reduced”. With fewer normal points crowding around, the anomalies stand out more clearly in each random tree. That's why the default is just 256 points per tree, even on millions of rows.

🌉 How it connects

Isolation forests are random forests (4.2) turned inside out; LOF is k-NN (3.1) and DBSCAN's density idea (6.3) as a score; GMM densities (6.4) and PCA reconstruction errors (6.5) are anomaly scores too. Evaluation leans on precision–recall (5.3, 5.4) and imbalanced learning (5.5); in production, drift monitoring (Lesson 9.3) is anomaly detection on whole distributions. For the probability side see Math 4.3: distributions and Math 5.6b: metrics.

Check yourself

1. With ψ = 256 (so c(ψ) ≈ 10.24), a point's average path length is 5.12. What is its isolation forest anomaly score?
s = 2^(−5.12 / 10.24) = 2^(−0.5) = 1 ÷ √2 ≈ 0.71. Half the typical path length: clearly anomalous (scores near 1 are anomalies, about 0.5 is typical, below 0.5 is normal).
2. Your team can review 30 alerts a day. A colleague tunes an isolation forest by trying contamination values and picking the one with the best accuracy. What do you suggest?
With rare anomalies, accuracy is dominated by the normal class (“never anomalous” scores 99.8%). Contamination doesn't change the ranking, only the cut. The business question is “are the alerts we can check worth checking?”, which is precision at the budget.
3. Sensor readings form a dense cluster during the day and a sparse one at night. A reading slightly outside the dense daytime cluster is a known fault, but the isolation forest ranks it low. Which detector is designed for this?
The faulty reading is only unusual relative to its dense neighbourhood; globally, sparse night readings are farther out. LOF's density ratio catches local anomalies (in Playground 3 it found 9 of 10 at budget 10, against 6 for the isolation forest). More trees only make the same ranking steadier.
📝 TL;DR
  • Anomaly detection learns “normal” and scores how unusual each point is; it's the tool when anomalies are rare, unlabelled or new.
  • Baselines: z-score (beware masking), robust MAD, IQR fences. Multivariate: isolation forest (short paths, s = 2^(−E[h]/c(ψ)), 100 trees × 256 samples), LOF (density vs neighbours), one-class SVM (boundary around clean data), GMM density.
  • Contamination only sets the threshold; with an alert budget, take the top-k scores.
  • Evaluate with precision and recall at the budget, using a few labels or injected anomalies; never accuracy.
  • Collect analyst labels and move known anomaly types to supervised models (5.5). That's the end of Phase 6: the recap is next.
