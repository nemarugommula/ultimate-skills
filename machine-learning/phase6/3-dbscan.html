Phase 6 · Lesson 3

DBSCAN & HDBSCAN: a cluster is wherever the points are crowded

Forget centres. Density-based clustering grows clusters through crowded regions and leaves isolated points out as noise. It finds crescents, rings and blobs of any shape without being told how many clusters there are. Its one weakness, a single density threshold, is what HDBSCAN fixes.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • k-means draws straight boundaries between centres, so it fails on crescents and rings, and puts every point in a cluster (Lesson 6.1).
  • Single linkage follows chains of close neighbours, but noise points glue clusters together (“chaining”, Lesson 6.2).
  • Scaling: any method built on a distance threshold needs comparable feature units (Lesson 1.4).
🎯 Hook: the 1996 paper that won a “test of time” award

In 1996 four researchers in Munich (Martin Ester, Hans-Peter Kriegel, Jörg Sander and Xiaowei Xu) wanted to find clusters in spatial databases: maps, satellite images, geographic points. Real clusters there are long, bent and irregular, the number of clusters is unknown, and the data is full of junk points that belong to nothing. k-means could handle none of that.

Their answer, DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise), fits in one paragraph and needs two knobs. Eighteen years later, in 2014, it received the SIGKDD Test of Time award, and it's in every major ML library. Its successor HDBSCAN is the default clusterer inside the popular BERTopic topic-modelling library. What makes “count your neighbours” so durable?

🔮 Predict first

Two noisy moons (200 points) plus 20 random junk points. DBSCAN calls a point “crowded” if at least 5 points (itself included) lie within distance 0.25. On this data the right radius is closer to 0.4. With 0.25, how many clusters will DBSCAN report?

8. With too small a radius, the thinner stretches of each moon don't count as crowded, so each moon breaks into pieces, and 13 of the 20 junk points plus 4 moon points become noise. Raise the radius to 0.35 to 0.5 and you get exactly the 2 moons with 99.5% agreement. Too big (0.6+) and the moons merge into 1. Try it in Playground 1.

Numbers first: core, border, noise

DBSCAN has two knobs: a radius eps (ε) and a count min_samples. For each point, count the points within distance eps, including itself (scikit-learn's convention):

Kind of pointRuleWhat happens to it
coreat least min_samples points within epsit's in a crowd: starts or extends a cluster
bordernot core, but within eps of a core pointjoins that core point's cluster, but doesn't extend it further
noiseneitherlabel −1: belongs to no cluster

A cluster is then everything you can reach by hopping from core point to core point (each hop at most eps), plus the border points at the edges. No centres, no shapes assumed: the cluster takes whatever shape the crowd has.

✏️ Worked example: nine numbers, eps = 0.6, min_samples = 3

Points: 1, 1.5, 2, 2.4, 5, 8.6, 9, 9.3, 9.8. Count neighbours within 0.6 (itself included):

  • 1.5 has {1, 1.5, 2} = 3 → core. 2 has {1.5, 2, 2.4} = 3 → core. 9 has {8.6, 9, 9.3} and 9.3 has {9, 9.3, 9.8}: both core.
  • 1 has {1, 1.5} = 2, but is within 0.6 of core 1.5 → border. Likewise 2.4 (next to core 2), 8.6 (next to 9) and 9.8 (next to 9.3).
  • 5 has only itself and no core neighbour → noise.
  • Result: cluster A = {1, 1.5, 2, 2.4}, cluster B = {8.6, 9, 9.3, 9.8}, noise = {5}. Nobody said “k = 2”.
💡 Big idea A cluster is a connected region of high density, separated from other clusters by regions of low density. DBSCAN measures density by counting neighbours within a radius, links crowded points into clusters of any shape, finds the number of clusters by itself, and is allowed to say “this point belongs to nothing”.
🧠 Analogy: groups at a party

Look down at a party from a balcony. A conversation group is people standing close together. Someone with at least 4 others within arm's reach is clearly in a group (core). Someone hovering at the edge, close to a group member but with few people near them, is at the group (border). The person alone by the snacks is in no group at all (noise).

Groups can snake along a hallway or wrap around a table: what makes them a group is that you can walk from person to person without leaving the crowd. And there's no need to know beforehand how many groups there are. But if one corner is a packed dance floor and another a relaxed sofa area, “arm's reach” can't be the same rule for both. Remember that.

🤔 Why this? Why density-based clustering?
  1. The problem before. k-means and Ward assume roughly round clusters and need k; both force every point into a cluster, so junk points drag centres around. Real spatial and behavioural data has odd shapes, an unknown number of groups, and outliers.
  2. What people tried. Single linkage follows odd shapes but chains through noise, gluing clusters together (Lesson 6.2). Removing outliers by hand first is slow and arbitrary.
  3. Why this works. Requiring min_samples neighbours before a point can extend a cluster stops the chaining: a thin trail of junk points isn't crowded, so it can't bridge two clusters. In the worked example the lone point 5 sits 2.6 away from everything and is simply labelled noise. On our moons, eps = 0.4 recovers both moons with 99.5% agreement, where k-means manages about 67%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. One global eps means one density: clusters of very different densities can't all be found (Playground 3). eps is in data units, so scale first, and it's hard to set in high dimensions where all distances look alike. There's no predict for new points in scikit-learn's DBSCAN.
  5. What came next. OPTICS (1999) orders points so you can read clusters at every eps; HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi & Sander, 2013) builds the whole hierarchy over all eps values and keeps the clusters that persist longest, leaving one main knob: the smallest cluster size you care about.

Notice that min_samples matters too: raise it to 10 and the clusters need eps of at least 0.45. Bigger min_samples means “crowded” is a stricter word: you get fewer, denser clusters and more noise. It also makes results steadier on noisy data.

Choosing eps: the k-distance plot

The original paper's trick: for every point, measure the distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (k = min_samples). Sort those distances and plot them. Points inside clusters have small k-distances; noise points have large ones. The curve stays low and flat, then bends sharply upwards: the bend (the “knee”) is a good starting eps; in practice you often nudge it up a little and check the clusters. The paper fixed MinPts = 4 for 2-D data and read eps off this “4-dist graph”. A 2017 follow-up by the same group recommends min_samples ≈ 2 × the number of dimensions as a starting point.

The weakness: one eps, one density

eps defines “crowded” for the whole dataset at once. When clusters differ in density, you're stuck: an eps small enough to separate two tight neighbouring clusters breaks a sparse cluster into noise, and an eps large enough to keep the sparse one merges the tight ones.

🔮 Predict first

Two tight blobs, close together, plus one big sparse blob (70 points each). You may try any eps from 0.05 to 1.2 and any min_samples from 3 to 15. What's the best agreement DBSCAN can reach with the three true groups?

About 70% at best (eps ≈ 0.27): the two tight blobs are separated, but the sparse blob shatters into small pieces and noise. Push eps up to save the sparse blob and the tight blobs merge into one (about 60 to 63%). HDBSCAN finds all three with 93% agreement (the rest labelled noise), without any eps. Compare both below.

How HDBSCAN removes eps

  1. Core distance: for each point, the distance to its min_samples-th neighbour (small in dense areas, large in sparse ones).
  2. Mutual reachability: the distance between two points becomes max(core distance of a, core distance of b, real distance). Sparse points get pushed away from everything, which stops chaining through noise.
  3. Every eps at once: build the single-linkage tree on those distances (via a minimum spanning tree). Cutting it at height h is exactly DBSCAN at eps = h, so the tree holds every DBSCAN answer.
  4. Keep what persists: walk down the tree; a split only counts if both sides have at least min_cluster_size points; otherwise points just “fall out” as noise. Each candidate cluster gets a stability score (how long, in density terms, its points stay in it), and HDBSCAN picks the most stable set of non-overlapping clusters. Dense blobs and sparse blobs can both win, each at its own density.

In practice: set min_cluster_size to the smallest group you'd care about; leave min_samples at its default (equal to min_cluster_size) or lower it to label fewer points as noise. You also get a membership strength per point (probabilities_). On the moons of Playground 1, scikit-learn's HDBSCAN finds the two moons with min_cluster_size=30; with 15 it splits them into five dense pieces, so it isn't magic either.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn: DBSCAN (defaults eps=0.5, min_samples=5: the eps default means nothing for your units), HDBSCAN (in sklearn.cluster since 1.3; min_cluster_size=5 by default), OPTICS. The standalone hdbscan library (0.8.44) adds prediction for new points and plotting of the condensed tree.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, HDBSCAN
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, _ = make_moons(n_samples=500, noise=0.06, random_state=0)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)            # eps is a distance: scale first

# k-distance plot: distance to the min_samples-th neighbour (counting the point itself), sorted
min_samples = 5
dist, _ = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=min_samples).fit(X).kneighbors(X)
k_dist = np.sort(dist[:, -1])
print("k-distance at 90th / 99th percentile:", k_dist[449].round(3), k_dist[494].round(3))

db = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=min_samples).fit(X)
labels = db.labels_                              # -1 = noise
print("DBSCAN clusters:", labels.max() + 1, " noise:", (labels == -1).sum(), " core:", len(db.core_sample_indices_))

hdb = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=25, copy=True).fit(X)   # no eps at all
print("HDBSCAN clusters:", hdb.labels_.max() + 1, " noise:", (hdb.labels_ == -1).sum())
print("membership strength of first 5 points:", hdb.probabilities_[:5].round(2))

Output: the 99th-percentile k-distance is 0.225, so eps = 0.3 sits above the knee; DBSCAN finds the 2 moons with no noise, and HDBSCAN finds 2 clusters and calls 9 edge points noise. (copy=True silences a 1.9 warning about a changing default.) For geographic points use metric="haversine" on latitude/longitude in radians, with eps in radians (kilometres ÷ 6,371).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

GPS pings from delivery drivers: where do they actually stop? DBSCAN with the haversine metric and eps = 50 metres finds real stopping spots of any shape (a car park, a loading bay) and ignores pings on the move. For text, embed, reduce with UMAP, cluster with HDBSCAN: that's BERTopic's default recipe.

At scale

Teams use density clustering to deduplicate places and listings, group similar error reports, and find hot spots on maps, usually after a dimensionality-reduction step and with spatial indexes or GPU implementations (RAPIDS cuML has DBSCAN and HDBSCAN). The noise label doubles as a first outlier filter (Lesson 6.7).

🎤 Interview angle

“How does DBSCAN work? How do you choose its parameters? When does it fail?” A strong answer:

  • Defines core, border and noise points via eps and min_samples, and clusters as density-connected regions.
  • Contrasts with k-means: arbitrary shapes, no k, explicit noise, deterministic (except border points reachable from two clusters).
  • Chooses eps with the k-distance plot's knee and min_samples ≈ 2 × dimensions; scales features first.
  • Names the failure: varying densities (one global eps) and high dimensions; the fix is HDBSCAN (hierarchy over all eps, stability-based selection, min_cluster_size).
🏭 In the real world
The original DBSCAN 📜 History · 1996 Ester, Kriegel, Sander & Xu: A Density-Based Algorithm for Discovering Clusters… (KDD-96, PDF)

How: clusters as density-connected sets of points in spatial databases, with MinPts set to 4 for 2-D data and eps read off the sorted 4-dist graph.

Why this method: spatial data has arbitrary shapes, unknown cluster counts and noise. It won the 2014 SIGKDD Test of Time award.

Topic modelling with BERTopic 🧰 Open source BERTopic: the algorithm

How: embed documents, reduce dimensions with UMAP (its default), then cluster with HDBSCAN (its default) and describe each cluster with its most distinctive words.

Why this method: topics come in different sizes and densities, the number of topics is unknown, and many documents fit no topic; HDBSCAN handles all three.

Setting the knobs 🧪 Research · 2017 Schubert et al.: DBSCAN Revisited, Revisited (ACM TODS, PDF)

How: revisits DBSCAN's run time and parameter choice, suggesting minPts = 2 · dim and a k-distance plot for eps, and warns against using too large an eps.

Why this method: with sensible parameters and a spatial index, DBSCAN remains competitive; most bad results come from bad parameters.

HDBSCAN explained 🧰 Open source hdbscan docs: How HDBSCAN works

How: mutual reachability distances, a minimum spanning tree, a condensed cluster tree, and selection by stability, illustrated step by step.

Why this method: it turns DBSCAN's hardest knob (eps) into a search over all eps values, keeping only an intuitive minimum cluster size.

🤯 Fun fact · “noise” is a feature

Most clustering algorithms treat outliers as an embarrassment. DBSCAN put “with Noise” into its very name: it was the first widely used method where “this point belongs to no cluster” is a first-class answer. That label −1 is why DBSCAN is also a quick outlier detector: whatever isn't in a crowd is suspicious (Lesson 6.7).

🌉 How it connects

DBSCAN fixes k-means' shape problem (6.1) and single linkage's chaining (6.2); HDBSCAN is single linkage again, on density-adjusted distances. In high dimensions, reduce first with PCA or UMAP (6.5, 6.6). The noise label is a simple anomaly detector, and density ratios become LOF (6.7). Neighbour searches are the same ones as in k-NN (3.1). For distances in many dimensions see Math 1.4: norms.

Check yourself

1. Points 0, 0.4, 0.7, 3.0 with eps = 0.5 and min_samples = 3 (counting the point itself). Which is right?
0.4 has {0, 0.4, 0.7} within 0.5: 3 points → core. 0 has {0, 0.4} and 0.7 has {0.4, 0.7}: only 2 each, but both are within 0.5 of core 0.4 → border. 3.0 has only itself and no core neighbour → noise.
2. You run DBSCAN with the default eps = 0.5 on customer data with columns “annual spend in dollars” and “visits per month”. Every customer is noise. Most likely fix?
In raw dollars, two customers are almost never within 0.5 of each other, so nobody is core. eps is a distance in your feature units: standardize first, then read eps off the knee of the k-distance plot. min_samples = 1 would make every point a cluster of its own.
3. Your data has a dense downtown cluster of shops and a sparse suburban one. DBSCAN either merges downtown streets or shatters the suburbs into noise. What do you use?
A single eps can't fit two densities. HDBSCAN considers every eps at once and keeps the clusters that persist, each at its own density. Changing min_samples in DBSCAN only shifts the same single threshold; k-means can't follow irregular shapes or label noise.
📝 TL;DR
  • DBSCAN: points with ≥ min_samples neighbours within eps are core; clusters are chains of core points plus their border points; the rest is noise (−1).
  • It finds any shape and the number of clusters, and resists chaining. Scale features first.
  • Pick eps at the knee of the sorted k-distance plot; min_samples ≈ 2 × dimensions (MinPts = 4 in 2-D in the paper).
  • One eps = one density. HDBSCAN (in scikit-learn since 1.3) searches all eps and keeps stable clusters; its main knob is min_cluster_size.
  • In high dimensions, reduce first (6.5, 6.6). Next: soft, elliptical clusters with Gaussian mixtures (6.4).
