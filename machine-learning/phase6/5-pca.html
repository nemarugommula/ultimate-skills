Phase 6 · Lesson 5

PCA: find the directions where the data actually varies

Principal component analysis (PCA) rotates your data so the first new axis points where the points spread out most, the second where they spread most among what's left, and so on. Keep the first few and you've compressed 64 columns into 10, made a 2-D map of a 200,000-column dataset, or removed noise. It is linear, it is fast, and it is the first thing to try.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Variance: the average squared distance from the mean, how spread out a feature is (Math 5.1).
  • Projecting a point onto a direction u: the dot product (x − mean) · u, its shadow on that line (Math 1.3).
  • Scaling: features in big units dominate any distance- or variance-based method (Lesson 1.4).
🎯 Hook: a map of Europe drawn from DNA

In 2008, John Novembre and colleagues took genotype data at 197,146 positions in the genome for 1,387 Europeans. No coordinates, no country names went in: just a 1,387 × 197,146 table of 0s, 1s and 2s.

They ran PCA and plotted each person by their first two principal components. The scatter looked like a map of Europe: Iberians in the south-west, Scandinavians up north, Italians in the boot, with each country's dots in roughly its geographic place. Out of almost 200,000 columns, the two directions of largest variation turned out to be, more or less, north–south and east–west. How does a method that only looks for spread find geography?

🔮 Predict first

The digits dataset in scikit-learn has 1,797 handwritten digits, each an 8 × 8 image: 64 pixel columns. How many principal components do you need to keep 95% of the total variance?

29. The first 2 components hold 28.5% of the variance, 21 hold 90%, 29 hold 95%. Many pixels move together (strokes cover neighbouring pixels; the border pixels are almost always blank), so far fewer than 64 directions carry the information. A classifier on 30 components scores 91.0% (5-fold cross-validated), the same as on all 64 (91.3%).

Half the columns carry essentially everything the classifier needs. That's the promise of PCA: most real datasets live near a much smaller subspace than their number of columns suggests, because features are correlated.

💡 Big idea PCA finds new axes (principal components) that are combinations of the original features, at right angles to each other, ordered by how much variance the data has along them. Projecting onto the first k keeps as much variance as any k directions can, which is the same as losing as little as possible when you squash the data down (smallest reconstruction error). The components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; their eigenvalues are the variances.
🧠 Analogy: photographing a teapot

You get one photo to show a friend what a teapot looks like. From the top, you see a circle: useless. From the spout side, you see the body, spout and handle spread out: the most information in one 2-D picture. You instinctively choose the viewing angle where the object looks biggest and most spread out.

PCA picks that angle for any cloud of points, in any number of dimensions, by maths instead of instinct. The shadow it keeps is the most spread-out one possible. It can't bend the camera, though: if the shape is curled up (a rolled-up poster), any flat photo overlaps parts that are far apart along the roll.

🤔 Why this? Why PCA?
  1. The problem before. Many columns are expensive (memory, training time), impossible to look at (you can't plot 64-D), and often redundant: correlated features say the same thing twice, which also destabilizes linear models (Lesson 2.2).
  2. What people tried. Dropping columns by hand or by a variance threshold throws away information without combining it; picking two columns to plot shows only a slice. Neither uses the fact that several columns move together.
  3. Why this works. When features are correlated, the cloud is a stretched ellipsoid; its long axes carry most of the spread. For a covariance of [[4, 2], [2, 3]], the first component (at 38°) has variance 5.56 of the total 7: 79% of the spread in one number instead of two. One eigen-decomposition (or an SVD) gives all components at once, in order, with no iterations or random starts.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Components are mixes of features, harder to explain. It's linear: curved shapes (a rolled-up sheet) get flattened onto themselves. It chases variance, not usefulness: a low-variance direction can be the one that predicts the label. It's sensitive to units (scale first) and to outliers.
  5. What came next. Kernel PCA and manifold methods for curved shapes; t-SNE and UMAP for 2-D maps that keep neighbourhoods (Lesson 6.6); supervised reductions (LDA, partial least squares) when there's a label; autoencoders as the non-linear neural version (sibling Deep Learning course). PCA is still the standard first step before all of them.

Find the direction of most variance yourself

Pick any direction u through the mean of the data. Project every point onto it (drop a perpendicular to the line). The projected points have some variance along u; the perpendicular bits are what you'd lose. Since the total variance is fixed, maximizing the variance kept = minimizing the squared distances lost. Karl Pearson's 1901 paper was literally titled “On lines and planes of closest fit to systems of points in space”.

🔮 Predict first

For the cloud below, the direction that keeps the most variance and the line that minimizes the squared perpendicular distances to the points: are they the same line?

Always the same. Each point's squared distance from the mean splits (Pythagoras) into the part along the line plus the part perpendicular to it. The total is fixed, so more of one means less of the other. Turn the dial below: the two readouts always add up to the same total, 3.87.

✏️ Worked example: PCA of a 2 × 2 covariance by hand

Two features with variances 4 and 3 and covariance 2: Σ = [[4, 2], [2, 3]]. Total variance = 4 + 3 = 7.

  • Eigenvalues solve λ² − 7λ + (4·3 − 2·2) = 0, so λ = (7 ± √17) ÷ 2 = 5.56 and 1.44. They add up to 7, the total variance.
  • PC1 direction: (4 − 5.56)·x + 2·y = 0 → y = 0.78·x → unit vector (0.79, 0.62), about 38° above the first feature's axis.
  • Explained variance ratio: 5.56 ÷ 7 = 79.5% for PC1, 20.5% for PC2. Keeping PC1 alone turns two numbers into one and keeps four-fifths of the spread.

Why eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are the answer, and how SVD computes them without forming the matrix: Math 2.6: SVD & PCA and Math 2.5: eigenvectors.

From 3-D to 2-D: what survives the squash?

With more dimensions, the recipe repeats: PC1 is the direction of most variance, PC2 the direction of most remaining variance at right angles to PC1, and so on. The explained variance ratio of each component, sorted and plotted, is the scree plot (named after the rubble at the foot of a cliff): a steep drop then a flat tail says “keep the components before the tail”.

Scaling matters: PCA follows the units

PCA maximizes variance, and variance depends on units. Measure income in dollars and it has a variance in the hundreds of millions, so PC1 is “income” no matter what else is going on. Measure it in thousands and its variance shrinks a millionfold. The data didn't change; the answer did.

Rule of thumb: if the features are in different units, standardize (that's PCA on the correlation matrix). If they're in the same units and their spreads are meaningful (pixel intensities, gene counts after normalization), you may leave them unscaled. Put the scaler and PCA in one Pipeline so the test data is scaled with the training statistics (Lesson 1.6).

Variance is not importance

PCA never looks at your label. It keeps the directions with the most spread, and those are not necessarily the ones that separate your classes. Dropping “small” components can delete exactly the signal you needed.

🔮 Predict first

Two classes. Along one direction the points spread widely but both classes look the same; along the perpendicular direction the spread is small, but the classes sit on opposite sides. You keep only PC1. Best accuracy a threshold on PC1 can reach?

Not much better than a coin flip. PC1 is the wide, label-blind direction, so the classes overlap completely along it (in the playground below, about 62% at best, barely better than guessing). PC2, the “unimportant” 17% of the variance, separates the classes at about 94%. When there's a label, check what your reduction does to the downstream score, or use a supervised method (LDA, or let the model choose features).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn PCA (exact or randomized SVD; n_components as a count or a variance share), IncrementalPCA for data that doesn't fit in memory, TruncatedSVD for sparse matrices (text), KernelPCA for curved structure.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)                 # 1,797 images × 64 pixels (same units: no scaling)

pca = PCA().fit(X)
cum = np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
print("PC1+PC2:", cum[1].round(3), " components for 95%:", np.searchsorted(cum, 0.95) + 1)   # 0.285, 29

# reduction inside a pipeline, so each CV fold fits its own PCA
pipe = make_pipeline(PCA(n_components=0.95), LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000))
print("CV accuracy:", cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))

# denoise: project to 10 components and back
p10 = PCA(n_components=10).fit(X)
X_back = p10.inverse_transform(p10.transform(X))
print("reconstruction RMSE (pixels 0-16):", np.sqrt(((X - X_back) ** 2).mean()).round(2))   # 2.22

Mixed units? make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(...), model). components_ holds the directions (one row per component, one weight per feature: read them to name the components); explained_variance_ratio_ gives the scree plot.

Week one

Got a 300-column dataset? Standardize, PCA to 2 components, scatter-plot, colour by the label or by date. In ten minutes you'll spot clusters, outliers, a broken data source (one batch sitting off to the side) and how many components matter. Then try PCA(0.95) before a slow model and compare scores.

At scale

PCA shows up as infrastructure: compressing embeddings before indexing them, whitening features, correcting population structure in genetic studies (the top components are added as covariates), and monitoring: a sudden change in the top components' explained variance often means the input data shifted (Lesson 9.3).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain PCA. How do you choose the number of components? When is it a bad idea?” A strong answer:

  • PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance (= minimum reconstruction error); they're the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, computed in practice by SVD of the centred data.
  • Choose k by the explained variance ratio (e.g. 90 to 95%), the scree plot's elbow, or downstream validation score.
  • Centre always, scale when units differ; fit it inside the pipeline (no leakage).
  • Limits: linear; unsupervised, so high variance ≠ predictive; sensitive to outliers; components hard to interpret. Alternatives: kernel PCA, t-SNE/UMAP for plots, LDA when labels exist.
🏭 In the real world
Genes mirror geography 🧪 Research · 2008 Novembre et al., Nature: Genes mirror geography within Europe

How: PCA of genotypes at 197,146 loci for 1,387 Europeans; the first two components, plotted, resemble a map of Europe.

Why this method: 200,000 columns are impossible to look at; PCA found the two directions of most genetic variation, which turned out to track geography. Population geneticists now routinely use top components to correct for ancestry.

Eigenfaces 🧪 Research · 1991 Turk & Pentland: Eigenfaces for Recognition

How: treat each face photo as one long vector of pixels, run PCA on a set of faces, and describe every face by its weights on the top components (“eigenfaces”); recognize by nearest neighbour in that small space.

Why this method: faces vary in a few systematic ways (lighting, shape), so a few dozen components capture most of the variation, making recognition fast on early-1990s hardware.

Lines of closest fit 📜 History · 1901 PCA: Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933)

How: Karl Pearson found the line and plane that fit a cloud of points best (smallest perpendicular distances); Harold Hotelling developed it independently and gave it the name “principal components” in a 1933 paper.

Why this method: both views (best fit, most variance) give the same answer, which is why PCA keeps reappearing in every field under new names (Karhunen–Loève transform, empirical orthogonal functions, SVD).

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Decomposing signals in components

How: PCA, IncrementalPCA, KernelPCA, TruncatedSVD and friends, all with fit / transform / inverse_transform.

Why this method: as a transformer, PCA drops into any Pipeline between a scaler and a model (Lesson 1.6).

🤯 Fun fact · one method, many names

Because the same maths keeps being rediscovered, PCA goes by many names: the Karhunen–Loève transform in signal processing, empirical orthogonal functions in weather and climate science, proper orthogonal decomposition in engineering, and “just take the SVD” in numerical computing. If a paper from another field mentions any of these, it's doing PCA.

🌉 How it connects

PCA's covariance ellipse is one big Gaussian (6.4 fits several); clustering (6.1 to 6.4) often works better on a few principal components than on raw columns; t-SNE and UMAP (6.6) start from PCA; reconstruction error from PCA is an anomaly score (6.7). Check yourself

1. The eigenvalues of a 3-feature covariance matrix are 6, 3 and 1. What share of the variance do the first two components keep?
Total variance = 6 + 3 + 1 = 10. The first two keep (6 + 3) ÷ 10 = 90%; PC1 alone keeps 60%. (67% would be 2 of 3 components counted equally.)
2. A fraud model drops from 0.92 to 0.71 AUC after you reduce 40 features to the first 5 principal components (which keep 93% of the variance). What's the most likely explanation?
“93% of the variance” says nothing about the label. Rare, subtle fraud patterns are often low-variance. Judge the number of components by the validation score of the full pipeline, not just the variance share; tree models usually don't need PCA at all.
3. Before PCA, features are: house price (in dollars), number of rooms, and distance to the centre (in km). You don't scale. What will PC1 be?
Price varies by hundreds of thousands, rooms by a few. PCA maximizes variance in the given units, so PC1 is price with explained ratio near 100%. Standardize first (or use the correlation matrix) unless the units are genuinely comparable.
📝 TL;DR
  • PCA rotates data onto orthogonal axes ordered by variance; the first k keep the most variance and lose the least in reconstruction.
  • Components = eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (via SVD); explained variance ratio = eigenvalue ÷ total; use it or the scree plot to pick k.
  • Centre always; standardize when units differ; fit inside a Pipeline.
  • Linear only, and blind to the label: high variance ≠ useful. Check downstream scores.
  • Uses: visualization, compression, denoising, decorrelation, a pre-step for t-SNE/UMAP (6.6).
