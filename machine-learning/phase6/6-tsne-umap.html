Phase 6 · Lesson 6

t-SNE & UMAP: beautiful maps that keep neighbours, not distances

t-SNE and UMAP squash high-dimensional data into a 2-D picture where similar points sit together, and they reveal clusters that PCA smears into one blob. They're the standard way to look at embeddings, single cells and images. They are also easy to misread: cluster sizes and the gaps between clusters can mean nothing at all.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • PCA projects onto the directions of most variance: linear, fast, and it can't unroll curved shapes (Lesson 6.5).
  • Nearest neighbours: the k closest points to a point (Lesson 3.1).
  • Silhouette: how well separated a clustering looks, from −1 to 1 (Lesson 6.1).
🎯 Hook: two clusters, one ten times wider, drawn the same size

In 2016 three researchers at Google (Martin Wattenberg, Fernanda Viégas and Ian Johnson) published an interactive article in the journal Distill: “How to Use t-SNE Effectively.” One of its first experiments takes two clusters, one 10 times as spread out as the other, and runs t-SNE. The two clusters come out about the same size.

Other experiments are worse: three clusters at very different distances look evenly spaced; and pure random noise, with no structure at all, can come out looking like clumps. Yet t-SNE (and its faster cousin UMAP) appear in countless biology papers and machine-learning blog posts. Both facts are true: these maps are excellent at one thing and silent or misleading about others. This lesson is about telling which is which.

🔮 Predict first

Two groups in 10 dimensions, 75 points each. One is tight (spread 0.2), the other loose (spread 2.0). In the original space the loose group is about 10× wider. On a t-SNE map (perplexity 30), how much wider will it look?

About 1×: our run gives a ratio of 0.99. t-SNE sizes each point's neighbourhood to hold a fixed effective number of neighbours, so a dense cluster is expanded and a sparse one shrunk: it evens out cluster sizes. PCA keeps the difference (about 12× in its 2-D view). Try it in Playground 1.

💡 Big idea t-SNE and UMAP are neighbourhood-preserving maps. For each point they decide which other points are its near neighbours in the original space, then place points in 2-D so that those neighbours stay near. Everything else is negotiable: cluster sizes, distances between clusters, and the axes mean nothing. Use them to see local structure; never as measurements, and never as a clustering by themselves.
🧠 Analogy: a seating chart from a friendship survey

You ask 150 wedding guests who their closest friends are, then arrange the tables so every guest sits near their friends. Friend groups end up together: excellent. But the chart can't tell you how far apart two friend groups are socially, and a group of 30 casual acquaintances gets one table just like a group of 30 inseparable cousins. The tables are about the same size because the room forces it, not because the groups are.

And if the guests were strangers who each named “closest” people at random? You'd still produce a tidy seating chart with tables. It would just mean nothing.

🤔 Why this? Why t-SNE and UMAP?
  1. The problem before. You can't look at 50-, 784- or 20,000-dimensional data. PCA gives a 2-D view, but it's linear: clusters that are separated along curved or many different directions get stacked on top of each other (in our table, PCA kept only 32% of each point's nearest neighbours).
  2. What people tried. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) tries to preserve all pairwise distances, so the big distances dominate and local detail gets crushed. Earlier SNE (2002) preserved neighbourhoods but suffered from the “crowding problem”: there isn't room in 2-D for every point's neighbours, so everything collapses to the centre.
  3. Why this works. t-SNE turns distances into neighbour probabilities (a Gaussian whose width is set per point by the perplexity, “roughly how many neighbours to care about”) and matches them with a heavy-tailed Student-t curve in 2-D. Heavy tails let moderately far points be placed far apart, which fixes the crowding. On our two-cluster data, 50% of each point's 10 nearest neighbours stay among its 10 nearest on the map, versus 32% for PCA.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Sizes and gaps are distorted; results depend on perplexity, random start and the number of iterations; even noise shows clumps. t-SNE is slow on big data and can't embed new points. Clustering its output finds the map's clumps, not the data's.
  5. What came next. UMAP (McInnes, Healy & Melville, 2018) builds a fuzzy nearest-neighbour graph and lays it out with attraction and repulsion; it's much faster, can transform new points, and arguably keeps more global structure. Faster t-SNE implementations (Barnes–Hut, FFT-based) and careful initialization (from PCA) also made t-SNE more reliable.

How t-SNE works, in words

  1. In the original space: for each point, turn distances to the others into probabilities “j is my neighbour” with a Gaussian bump. The width of each bump is tuned so that the perplexity matches your setting: in the original paper's words, “a smooth measure of the effective number of neighbors”. Dense regions get narrow bumps, sparse regions wide ones.
  2. On the map: start points at random (or at their PCA positions) and define map probabilities with a Student-t curve, which has much fatter tails than a Gaussian.
  3. Move the points by gradient descent to make the two sets of probabilities match (minimize their KL divergence). Neighbours attract; everyone repels a little. Early on, the attractions are multiplied by about 12 (“early exaggeration”) so clusters form first.
cost = KL(P ‖ Q) = Σi,j pij log(pij ⁄ qij) “Big penalty when two points are neighbours in the data (p large) but far apart on the map (q small); small penalty the other way round. So neighbours are kept, and distant points are free to go anywhere.”

That asymmetry is the whole story: KL divergence is explained in Math 6.3: cross-entropy & KL.

🔮 Predict first

Now the “pure noise” dataset: 150 points from one round Gaussian blob in 10-D, no groups whatsoever. You run t-SNE, then k-means with k = 3 on the 2-D map, and score that clustering with the silhouette. On the map the silhouette is about 0.38. What is the silhouette of the same three groups measured in the original 10-D space?

About 0.05 (our runs: 0.05 at perplexity 5, 0.06 at perplexity 30). On the map, the three groups look reasonably separated; in the real data they're arbitrary slices of one blob. Clustering the map clusters the map's artefacts. If you must cluster, cluster in the original (or PCA-reduced) space, and use the map only to look.

UMAP: a neighbour graph, laid out fast

UMAP's recipe, in words: (1) for every point, find its n_neighbors nearest neighbours and give each link a weight that fades with distance, measured from the nearest neighbour onwards (so every point is connected to at least one other); (2) merge the two directions of each link into one fuzzy graph; (3) lay the graph out in 2-D: pull linked points together, push random pairs apart (negative sampling). min_dist sets how tightly points may pack on the map.

✏️ Worked example: what perplexity means in practice

Perplexity is roughly the number of neighbours each point “listens to”. With 150 points:

  • Perplexity 5: each point cares about ~5 neighbours; any small accidental clump in the data becomes its own island. Pure noise breaks into blobs.
  • Perplexity 30 (the default): ~30 neighbours, a fifth of this dataset; real groups of 50 to 75 points hold together.
  • Perplexity 49: close to scikit-learn's practical limit here: perplexity must be smaller than the number of points, and implementations cap the neighbourhood at about 3 × perplexity. With 150 points, 3 × 49 = 147 is nearly everyone, so the picture approaches a global blur.

Distill's advice: try several perplexities and run until the picture stops changing; if clusters look oddly pinched, it was stopped too early.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn TSNE (defaults perplexity=30.0, init="pca", learning_rate="auto", max_iter=1000; the parameter was called n_iter before 1.5) and trustworthiness. umap-learn (0.5.12) for UMAP: umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1).

import time
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, trustworthiness

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)                     # 1,797 × 64
X50 = PCA(n_components=50, random_state=0).fit_transform(X)   # common first step: denoise + speed up

t = time.time()
emb = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, init="pca", learning_rate="auto", random_state=0).fit_transform(X50)
print(f"t-SNE took {time.time() - t:.1f} s")
pca2 = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X)

# trustworthiness: are the map's nearest neighbours real neighbours? (1.0 = perfect)
print("PCA  :", round(trustworthiness(X, pca2, n_neighbors=10), 3))    # 0.83
print("t-SNE:", round(trustworthiness(X, emb, n_neighbors=10), 3))     # 0.993

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

You have 20,000 product embeddings and want to see if they make sense. UMAP to 2-D, colour by category, hover to read titles. Islands of one colour = good embeddings; mixed islands = something to investigate. Ten minutes, and the picture convinces people. Just don't put “cluster sizes” in the slide deck.

At scale

Teams run UMAP or t-SNE on millions of points with GPU implementations, inside embedding browsers (TensorFlow's Embedding Projector offers PCA, t-SNE and UMAP side by side) and data-quality dashboards. In single-cell biology, a t-SNE or UMAP map is in nearly every paper, and the community has published careful guidance on reading them.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between PCA and t-SNE? Can you cluster on a t-SNE plot?” A strong answer:

  • PCA: linear, keeps global variance, deterministic, has transform. t-SNE/UMAP: non-linear, keep local neighbourhoods.
  • On t-SNE/UMAP maps, cluster sizes and inter-cluster distances aren't meaningful; random noise can look clustered; results depend on perplexity / n_neighbors and the seed.
  • Explains perplexity (effective number of neighbours, typical 5 to 50) and UMAP's n_neighbors and min_dist.
  • Don't cluster on a 2-D t-SNE map; cluster in the original or PCA space (or a higher-dimensional UMAP) and use the map to visualize; validate with the original features.
🏭 In the real world
How (not) to read t-SNE 🧪 Research · 2016 Wattenberg, Viégas & Johnson: How to Use t-SNE Effectively (Distill)

How: interactive experiments on simple synthetic shapes: cluster sizes mean nothing, distances between clusters might not mean anything, random noise doesn't always look random, and hyperparameters really matter.

Why this method: controlled toy data, where the truth is known, is the only way to learn what the map can and can't tell you. Playground 1 copies their idea.

Single-cell biology 🧪 Research · 2019 Kobak & Berens: The art of using t-SNE for single-cell transcriptomics (Nature Communications)

How: a protocol for more faithful maps of data sets with up to millions of cells: PCA initialization, a high learning rate, multi-scale similarity kernels, and, for very large data, exaggeration and downsampling-based initialization.

Why this method: each cell is a vector of thousands of gene counts; maps of cell types are how biologists see a dataset. The same year, Becht et al. (Nature Biotechnology) argued for UMAP in the same setting.

The UMAP paper 🧪 Research · 2018 McInnes, Healy & Melville: UMAP (arXiv)

How: a fuzzy topological representation of the k-nearest-neighbour graph, optimized in low dimensions with attraction along edges and negative sampling for repulsion.

Why this method: in the authors' words it is competitive with t-SNE for visualization quality and “arguably preserves more of the global structure with superior run time performance”, and it works for any output dimension.

Embedding browsers 🧰 Open source · Google TensorFlow Embedding Projector

How: load any set of vectors (word embeddings, image features) and switch between PCA, t-SNE and UMAP views, with search and nearest-neighbour lists.

Why this method: the side-by-side views make the trade-off visible: PCA for global layout, t-SNE/UMAP for local neighbourhoods.

🤯 Fun fact · the t in t-SNE

The “t” is the Student t-distribution, the one statistics students meet in the t-test. “Student” was a pen name: William Sealy Gosset published under it while working at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, using statistics to judge small samples, such as trials of barley varieties. More than a century later, that distribution's fat tails are what give t-SNE room to separate clusters in 2-D.

Check yourself

1. On a t-SNE map, cluster A looks twice as wide as cluster B. In the original data, A's spread is…
t-SNE adapts each point's neighbourhood width to the local density, expanding dense clusters and shrinking sparse ones. In Playground 1, a 10× difference became 0.99×. Compute spreads on the original features.
2. You have 40 points and set perplexity = 50. What should you expect?
Perplexity is roughly how many neighbours each point considers; you can't have 50 neighbours among 39 other points. scikit-learn requires perplexity to be smaller than the number of samples. Typical values are 5 to 50, scaled to dataset size.
3. A teammate runs t-SNE on customer data, draws circles around 6 islands on the map, and proposes 6 segments. Best response?
Islands depend on perplexity and seed, and even noise forms islands (in Playground 1, k-means on a noise map gave silhouette 0.38 on the map but 0.05 in the real data). The map is a good way to look; the segmentation must stand up in the real feature space.
📝 TL;DR
  • t-SNE and UMAP keep neighbourhoods: points close in the data stay close on the map. That's what makes them great for looking at clusters.
  • Cluster sizes, distances between clusters, density and axes are not meaningful; noise can look clumpy.
  • Knobs: t-SNE perplexity (~number of neighbours, 5 to 50, default 30); UMAP n_neighbors (15) and min_dist (0.1). Try several; run to convergence; fix the seed.
  • Reduce with PCA first; UMAP is faster and can embed new points.
  • Never cluster a 2-D t-SNE map blindly: cluster in the real space, then use the map to show it. Next: anomaly detection (6.7).
