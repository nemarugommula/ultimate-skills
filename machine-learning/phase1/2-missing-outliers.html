Phase 1 · Lesson 2

Missing values & outliers: the holes and the spikes

Real tables have blanks and absurd values. Fill the blanks the wrong way and you quietly bias every number downstream; delete the spikes the wrong way and you throw away the very cases you were asked to find. Here's how to choose.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • describe() counts below the row count mean missing values; a mean far from the median means skew or outliers (Lesson 1.1).
  • A least-squares line minimizes the sum of squared vertical errors, so one huge error counts enormously (Lesson 2.1, Math 8.1).
  • Quartiles: Q1 and Q3 are the values a quarter and three quarters of the way through the sorted column; IQR = Q3 − Q1 (Math 5.1).
🎯 Hook: the blood test that mattered more than its result

In 2018, researchers at Harvard looked at the hospital records of 669,452 patients from two Boston hospitals and asked a strange question: does it matter whether and when a lab test was ordered, ignoring what the result said?

It mattered a lot. Just the presence of a test order was significantly linked to 3-year survival for 233 of 272 test types (86%). And for 118 of 174 tests (68%), the timing of the order (time of day, day of week, how often) predicted survival better than the test result itself. Think about why: a test ordered in the middle of the night says something about how worried the doctors were.

So when a lab value is missing in a medical dataset, that blank is not noise. It says “nobody thought this test was needed”. Fill it with the average and you erase that message.

🔮 Predict first

High earners skip the “income” question on a survey more often than others. You fill every blank with the average of the incomes you do have. Compared with the true average income, your filled-in column's average is…

Too low. The answers you have are missing their high earners, so their mean is already too low, and filling with it copies that bias into every blank. In Playground 1 (MNAR, 40% missing) the true mean is 48.2k; the filled column says 39.9k. Mean filling also shrinks the spread from 16.2k to 9.4k.

Numbers first: three reasons a value goes missing

What you should do depends on why the value is missing. Statisticians (Rubin, 1976) gave the three cases scary names; here they are in plain words, for a survey's income question:

NamePlain wordsIncome exampleDropping rows or filling with the mean…
MCARMissing Completely At Random: pure bad luckA page of forms got coffee on itUnbiased (you just lose rows), but mean filling shrinks the spread
MARMissing At Random: depends on other columns you haveOlder people skip it more, and you know everyone's ageBiased, but fixable: predict income from age (model imputation)
MNARMissing Not At Random: depends on the missing value itselfHigh earners skip it because it's highBiased, and only partly fixable. But the blank itself is a clue: keep a “was missing” flag

The catch: you usually can't prove which one you have from the data alone. You reason about the process (“who fills this in, and when?”), check whether missingness correlates with other columns or the target, and choose a method that is safe under the worse case.

💡 Big idea A blank is data about the data. Impute (fill in) values so models can run, but remember that every fill-in is a guess that shrinks spread and can bias averages, and keep a missing-indicator column so the model can learn from the fact that the value was missing. For outliers, the same rule: first ask what produced the weird value (a typo or a real rare event?), then decide.
🧠 Analogy: the empty seat at the dinner party

A guest list shows one empty chair. If someone missed the train, the empty chair tells you nothing about the party (MCAR). If it's always the person from the far-away office who's missing, you can guess who it is from where they work (MAR). If the guest stayed home because they hate the host, the empty chair is itself the most interesting fact in the room (MNAR). Filling the chair with a cardboard “average guest” makes the photo look complete and the story wrong.

🤔 Why this? Why impute, and why keep a missing indicator?
  1. The problem before. Most models (linear models, k-nearest neighbours, SVMs, neural nets) can't do arithmetic with a blank; scikit-learn raises an error on NaN for most estimators. Something has to happen before fit.
  2. What people tried. Dropping rows with any blank (“complete-case analysis”): with 10 columns each 5% missing at random, only 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 60% of rows survive, and if the missingness isn't random the survivors are a biased sample. Filling with the mean keeps every row but shrinks the spread and weakens correlations (Playground 1: correlation 0.79 → 0.63 at 40% MCAR).
  3. Why this works. Model-based imputation predicts the blank from the other columns, so it fixes the MAR case: with income missing more for older people, mean filling says 41.9k while predicting from age gives 49.6k against a truth of 48.2k. A missing indicator (a 0/1 column “income was missing”) lets the model use MNAR information: in Playground 2 it lifts AUC from about 0.76 to 0.87.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Imputers must be fitted on the training data only, or they leak test statistics (Lesson 1.6). Model imputation is slower and can't recover MNAR. Indicators add columns, and if missingness patterns change in production (a form field becomes required), the model's learned shortcut breaks.
  5. What came next. Trees that handle blanks natively: XGBoost (2016) learns a default direction for missing values at each split, and scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting does the same, so no imputation is needed (Lesson 4.4). Statisticians use multiple imputation (several plausible fills, results combined) when the goal is honest uncertainty rather than prediction.

What to notice: mean and median filling stack every guess on one vertical line, which squashes the spread and the correlation. Dropping rows keeps the shape honest under MCAR but becomes a biased sample under MAR and MNAR. Predicting from age fixes MAR almost completely, because age explains who is missing. Under MNAR nothing fully works, because the reason for the blank is the very value you don't have.

✏️ Worked example: the cost of dropping rows

Your table has 10,000 rows and 12 feature columns. Each column is missing 3% of its values, independently.

  • Chance a row has no blanks: 0.97¹² = 0.694. So dropna() keeps about 6,940 rows and throws away 3,060, almost a third, to fix blanks that were only 3% of the cells.
  • Median filling keeps all 10,000 rows; the price is that 3% of each column becomes a constant guess.
  • If the missingness isn't independent (whole rows from one broken data source), dropping is even more dangerous: you silently delete a whole kind of customer.

Step 2: missingness is information

Back to the hook. If people who later default on a loan are more likely to leave the income field blank, then “income is missing” is itself a predictor. Median filling turns those blanks into a perfectly ordinary income and the clue disappears. A missing indicator column keeps it. In scikit-learn it's one argument: SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True).

🔮 Predict first

Defaulters leave income blank much more often than others. You compare a logistic regression with median filling, with and without a 0/1 “income was missing” column. What happens to the indicator model?

Clearly better. At the default setting in Playground 2, AUC goes from 0.76 to 0.87. Slide the dependence to zero (missing completely at random) and the indicator adds nothing, as it should.

Step 3: outliers, the spikes

An outlier is a value far from the rest. Two very different things produce them, and they need opposite treatment:

KindExampleWhat to do
ErrorsA house price typed with an extra zero (2,000k instead of 200k); age 212; a sensor reporting −9999 for “no reading”Fix if you can, otherwise treat as missing. Never let them steer a model
Real but rareA billionaire's income; a genuine fraud transaction; a 20-second spike in server latencyKeep them. Use models and losses that aren't dominated by them, or a transform (log). When they are the thing you're predicting (fraud, failures), they're the signal (Lesson 6.7)

Two common detectors, on one column:

z-score = (x − mean) ÷ std, flag if |z| > 3   ·   IQR rule: flag if x < Q1 − 1.5·IQR or x > Q3 + 1.5·IQR “The z-score asks how many standard deviations away a value is; the IQR rule asks how far it is outside the middle half of the data.”

The z-score has a weakness: the outliers themselves inflate the mean and the std it uses, so a few big outliers can hide each other (called masking). The IQR rule uses quartiles, which a few extreme values barely move. That's what robust means in statistics: not dragged around by a few extreme points.

🔮 Predict first

20 houses, price vs size, a clear upward trend (about +2.3k per m²). One price is typed with an extra zero. What does a least-squares line do?

The slope turns negative: from +2.27 to −1.22 k$ per m². Squared errors make one point 1,700k away count like thousands of normal points. A robust (Huber) fit keeps +2.13. Try it below.

The pattern to remember: with one typo, both detectors catch it. With three, the z-score flags none of them (they inflated the std until the biggest |z| fell to about 2.5), while the IQR rule still flags all three. The Huber fit needs no detector at all: it treats small errors like least squares but gives large errors only linear, not squared, weight, so a single wild point can't take over.

✏️ Worked example: robust scaling

Prices 200, 215, 230, 240, 255 and one typo, 2,000 (k$).

  • Standard scaling (Lesson 1.4) uses mean = 523.3 and std = 660.6: the typo becomes 2.24 and the five real houses are squeezed into −0.49 … −0.41. The outlier ate the scale.
  • Robust scaling uses median = 235 and IQR = 251.25 − 218.75 = 32.5: the real houses spread over −1.08 … 0.62, and the typo stands out at 54.3, impossible to miss.
  • That's RobustScaler: (x − median) ÷ IQR. It doesn't remove outliers; it stops them from setting the scale for everyone else.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): SimpleImputer (mean, median, most_frequent, constant; add_indicator=True), KNNImputer, IterativeImputer (model-based, still behind an experimental import), MissingIndicator; HistGradientBoostingClassifier/Regressor accept NaN directly. For outliers: RobustScaler, HuberRegressor, and the IQR rule in pandas.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.experimental import enable_iterative_imputer  # noqa: F401 (IterativeImputer is experimental)
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer, KNNImputer, IterativeImputer
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X = X.copy()
X[np.random.default_rng(0).random(X.shape) < 0.2] = np.nan   # knock out 20% of cells at random (MCAR)

imputers = {
    "median + indicator": SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True),
    "kNN (5 neighbours)": KNNImputer(n_neighbors=5),
    "iterative (model-based)": IterativeImputer(max_iter=10, random_state=0),
}
for name, imp in imputers.items():
    pipe = make_pipeline(imp, StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))  # imputer fitted inside each fold
    print(f"{name:24s} {cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")   # 0.960, 0.960, 0.979

hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)   # NaN handled natively: no imputer at all
print(f"HistGradientBoosting     {cross_val_score(hgb, X, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")  # 0.960

Here the blanks are random, so the indicator can't help and the model-based imputer wins a little. IterativeImputer may print a ConvergenceWarning; raising max_iter usually silences it. Find outliers with the IQR rule: q1, q3 = s.quantile([0.25, 0.75]); s[(s < q1 - 1.5*(q3-q1)) | (s > q3 + 1.5*(q3-q1))]. And always put imputers and scalers inside the pipeline, so they're fitted on training folds only (Lesson 1.6).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Median fill + indicator for numeric columns, a constant “missing” category for text, inside a Pipeline. Or skip all of it with HistGradientBoostingClassifier, which takes NaN as is. Ask the data owner why the blanks exist; the answer is often a feature.

At scale

Missing rates per column are monitored: a jump from 2% to 40% usually means a broken upstream service, not a change in customers (Lesson 8.6). Teams document sentinel values (−1, 0, 9999) as missing, and validate ranges so typos are caught at ingestion, not by the model.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your dataset has missing values and outliers. How do you handle them?” A strong answer:

  • Asks why values are missing: MCAR, MAR or MNAR, in plain words, and checks if missingness correlates with the target.
  • Compares drop, mean/median, model imputation and the missing indicator, and mentions models that handle NaN natively.
  • Says imputers are fitted on training data only, inside a pipeline.
  • Separates errors from real rare values for outliers; prefers the IQR rule or robust methods over z-scores (masking); never deletes the rows that are the actual target (fraud).
🏭 In the real world
Missingness predicts survival 🧪 Research · 2018 Agniel, Kohane & Weber (BMJ): biases in electronic health record data

How: 272 lab test types for 669,452 patients; compared predicting 3-year survival from the test result with predicting it from whether and when the test was ordered.

Why this method: it showed that in health records, the pattern of what's measured (and what's missing) carries real information, which is why indicators and careful thought beat blind filling.

Default directions for missing values 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen & Guestrin: XGBoost, a scalable tree boosting system

How: at every split, rows with a missing value go to a learned default branch; the algorithm only scans non-missing entries.

Why this method: no imputation, and the model learns whether “missing” behaves like low values, high values or something else. On one sparse dataset it ran 50 times faster than the naive version.

The vocabulary of missing data 📜 History · 1976 Rubin: Inference and missing data (Biometrika)

How: defined when the missing-data process can be ignored, the root of today's MCAR / MAR / MNAR distinction.

Why this method: it turned “just drop the blanks” into a question you can reason about: what produced the blanks?

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: imputation of missing values

How: SimpleImputer, KNNImputer, IterativeImputer and MissingIndicator, all with the same fit/transform API.

Why this method: they drop into a Pipeline, so imputation is learned on training folds and replayed identically in production (Lesson 1.6).

🤯 Fun fact · NaN is not equal to itself

In Python, np.nan == np.nan is False: the floating-point standard defines “not a number” as unequal to everything, including itself. That's why you test with df.isna(), never == np.nan. And pandas skips blanks silently by default: the mean of [1, NaN] is 1.0, which is convenient right up until you forget there were blanks.

Check yourself

1. A table has 20 feature columns, each missing 2% of values independently. Roughly what share of rows survives dropna()?
A row survives only if all 20 cells are present: 0.98²⁰ ≈ 0.668. Tiny per-column missing rates add up across many columns, which is why dropping rows is rarely the right default.
2. In a churn dataset, “last login date” is blank for 15% of customers, and those customers churn at 60% versus 20% for the rest. What should you do?
The blank carries signal (likely customers who never used the product), so keep it as an indicator. Dropping removes the riskiest customers; mean filling erases the clue. But check the timing: if the field is blanked when an account closes, it's leakage (Lesson 1.1).
3. A column of order values contains several huge amounts from a handful of corporate clients. You flag |z| > 3 and find nothing. Why might that be?
The z-score's yardstick is computed from the same data, outliers included. Quartile-based rules and median-based scales barely move. In Playground 3, three typos push the biggest |z| down to about 2.5 while the IQR rule flags all three. And they may not be errors: corporate clients might deserve their own segment.
📝 TL;DR
  • Ask why values are missing: at random (MCAR), explained by other columns (MAR), or because of the value itself (MNAR).
  • Dropping rows loses data fast and biases non-random cases; mean/median filling shrinks spread; model imputation fixes MAR; nothing fully fixes MNAR.
  • Missingness is information: add a missing indicator (add_indicator=True), or use trees that handle NaN natively.
  • Outliers: fix errors, keep real rare values. Least squares is wrecked by one typo; IQR rules, Huber loss and RobustScaler resist it; z-scores suffer from masking.
  • Fit imputers and scalers on training data only, inside a pipeline (Lesson 1.6).
