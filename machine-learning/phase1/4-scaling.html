Phase 1 · Lesson 4

Scaling: why distance-based models need it

If one column is measured in dollars and another in years, a model that measures distances will listen only to the dollars. Scaling puts features on a common ruler. It's one line of code, it can change accuracy by 25 points, and some models don't need it at all.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Euclidean distance between two rows: square each column's difference, add them up, take the square root (Math 1.4: norms).
  • k-nearest neighbours (kNN) predicts the majority label of the k closest training rows (in depth, Lesson 3.1).
  • Gradient descent walks downhill on the loss, a step proportional to the slope each time (Math 3.4).
🎯 Hook: one line, 69% → 95%

scikit-learn ships a classic dataset of 178 wines from one region of Italy, of three different types, each described by 13 chemical measurements. Fit a k-nearest-neighbours classifier with default settings and 5-fold cross-validation: 69% accuracy.

Now add one line, StandardScaler(), in front of the same model. Nothing else changes: same data, same k, same folds. Accuracy: 95%. A decision tree on the same data scores 89% either way, scaled or not.

What did that one line fix, and why did the tree not care? Hint: one of the 13 columns, proline, runs from 278 to 1,680, while hue runs from 0.48 to 1.71.

🔮 Predict first

Three customers. A: age 25, income $50,000. B: age 65, income $50,500. C: age 26, income $52,000. Using plain Euclidean distance on the raw numbers, who is A's nearest neighbour?

B. A–B = √(40² + 500²) ≈ 502; A–C = √(1² + 2,000²) ≈ 2,000. A $500 difference outweighs 40 years, because dollars are big numbers. After standardizing (age std ≈ 14 years, income std ≈ $36,000) the distances flip: A–B ≈ 2.86, A–C ≈ 0.09. C is the obvious neighbour once both columns count.

Numbers first: the column with the biggest numbers wins

A distance adds up squared differences from every column. A column whose values differ by thousands contributes millions; a column whose values differ by tens contributes hundreds. The model isn't choosing dollars over years because dollars matter more: it's an accident of units.

With raw units the “neighbourhood” is a thin horizontal strip: anyone within a few hundred dollars of the query's income, at any age. 💡 Big idea Models that use distances (kNN, k-means, SVMs with RBF kernels), penalties on weight size (ridge, lasso) or gradient descent (logistic regression, neural nets) are sensitive to the units of each feature. Put features on a common scale first. Models that only compare a feature with a threshold (trees, forests, gradient boosting) don't care: stretching a column doesn't change which side of a threshold any row falls on.
🧠 Analogy: judging a decathlon by raw numbers

Imagine ranking decathletes by adding up their raw results: 100 m in seconds (around 11), shot put in metres (around 15), 1,500 m in seconds (around 270). The 1,500 m would decide everything, just because its numbers are biggest. Real decathlons convert each event to points on a common table first. Scaling is that points table for your features.

🤔 Why this? Why scale features?
  1. The problem before. Features come in arbitrary units: dollars, years, milligrams, clicks. Distance-based and gradient-based models treat a difference of 1 the same in every column, so the column with the largest spread silently dominates (wine: proline, spread of about 1,400, drowns out hue, spread of about 1.2).
  2. What people tried. Hand-picking “sensible” units (thousands of dollars, decades) helps a bit but is arbitrary and doesn't scale to 100 columns. Dropping the big column throws away information.
  3. Why this works. Standardizing gives every column mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so a “1” means “one typical spread” everywhere. In the age/income example the A–B distance changes from 502 to 2.86 and A–C from 2,000 to 0.09: now 40 years counts as far and $1,500 as near. On wine, kNN goes from 69% to 95%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. The scaler's mean and spread must be learned on training data only and reused on new data (a pipeline does this, Lesson 1.6). Standard and min-max scaling are dragged by outliers (use RobustScaler, Lesson 1.2). And equal scales are a choice, not the truth: sometimes a feature should count more, and scaling can't know that.
  5. What came next. Models that learn their own feature weighting (trees ignore scale entirely, Phase 4); learned metrics for kNN; inside neural networks, batch normalization (2015) re-standardizes the activations of every layer during training (the sibling Deep Learning course). And quantile or power transforms (QuantileTransformer, PowerTransformer) for heavily skewed columns.

The three scalers you'll use

✏️ Worked example: five ages, three scalers

Ages 20, 30, 40, 50, 60.

  • Standard: mean 40, std √((400 + 100 + 0 + 100 + 400) ÷ 5) = √200 ≈ 14.14 → −1.41, −0.71, 0, 0.71, 1.41.
  • Min-max: min 20, max 60 → (x − 20) ÷ 40 → 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.
  • Robust: median 40, IQR = 50 − 30 = 20 → −1, −0.5, 0, 0.5, 1.
  • Now add one age typed as 600. Min-max maps the five real ages into 0 … 0.07 (squashed together); robust scaling (median 45, IQR 25) keeps them spread over −1 … 0.6 and puts 600 out at about 22. That's why outliers pick the scaler.

Who needs scaling, and who doesn't

ModelNeeds scaling?Why
kNN, k-means, DBSCANYesPure distances: the largest column dominates (Lessons 3.1, 6.1, 6.3)
SVM (especially RBF kernel)YesKernels are functions of distance (Lessons 3.3–3.4)
PCAYesIt finds directions of largest variance: unscaled, that's just the biggest unit (Lesson 6.5)

In metres, height's spread (about 0.08) is tiny next to weight's (about 12 kg), so kNN ignores height: 68%. Around centimetres the two spreads are comparable and kNN reaches about 91%. In micrometres height dominates and accuracy falls again, to about 74%. The tree's 85% never moves. Tick “standardize” and the unit stops mattering for kNN too: that's the point of a scaler.

Scaling and gradient descent: the zig-zag

For a linear model trained by gradient descent, the loss surface over the weights is a bowl. If one feature's values are 10 times more spread out than another's, the bowl is 10 times steeper in that weight's direction (100 times in curvature). A learning rate small enough not to overshoot the steep direction crawls along the flat one, and anything a bit bigger bounces from wall to wall. Standardize the features and the bowl becomes round: every direction has the same steepness, and gradient descent walks straight to the bottom.

On the raw features, no learning rate is good: above 0.01 the steep direction blows up, and at the best safe rate it still takes over 100 steps. Standardized, a learning rate of 0.5 lands on the minimum in one step. Real problems have dozens of features with spreads differing by thousands, so this is the difference between training in seconds and not converging at all (scikit-learn's LogisticRegression often prints a ConvergenceWarning on unscaled data for exactly this reason).

🚩 The scaling leak

Calling StandardScaler().fit_transform(X) on the whole dataset, then splitting, lets the test rows influence the mean and std used for training. The effect is usually small for scaling, but it's the same mistake that is huge for target encoding (Lesson 1.3) and feature selection (Lesson 1.6). Build the habit now: fit the scaler on training data, or better, put it in a Pipeline.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler, RobustScaler, MaxAbsScaler (keeps sparse data sparse), plus QuantileTransformer and PowerTransformer for skewed columns. Always inside a pipeline.

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler, RobustScaler

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)               # 178 wines, 13 chemical measurements
print(X[["proline", "hue"]].agg(["min", "max"]))                  # proline 278–1680, hue 0.48–1.71

for name, model in [
    ("kNN, raw units", KNeighborsClassifier()),                                   # 0.691
    ("kNN + StandardScaler", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())),  # 0.949
    ("kNN + MinMaxScaler", make_pipeline(MinMaxScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())),      # 0.950
    ("kNN + RobustScaler", make_pipeline(RobustScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())),      # 0.938
    ("tree, raw units", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0)),                 # 0.888
    ("tree + StandardScaler", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0))),  # 0.888
]:
    print(f"{name:22s} {cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")

The scaler sits inside the pipeline, so each CV fold computes its own mean and std from its training rows only. To scale only some columns (not the one-hot ones), use a ColumnTransformer (Lesson 1.6). Scaling one-hot 0/1 columns is harmless but unnecessary.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), model) for anything that isn't a tree. If your first model is gradient boosting, skip scaling entirely and spend the time on features.

At scale

The scaler's mean and std are part of the deployed model: they're versioned and served together, and online systems compute the same transform at request time. Drift monitoring watches feature means and spreads, since a shifted mean silently shifts every scaled value (Lesson 8.6).

🎤 Interview angle

“Which models need feature scaling, and why?” A strong answer:

  • Distance-based (kNN, k-means, RBF SVMs) and PCA: the largest-spread column dominates.
  • Regularized linear models: the penalty depends on the weights' sizes, which depend on units.
  • Gradient descent: badly scaled features make an elongated loss surface; it zig-zags and needs a tiny learning rate.
  • Trees and tree ensembles don't: splits are invariant to monotone transforms of a feature.
  • Fit the scaler on training data only (a pipeline), and choose RobustScaler when there are outliers.

How: standardizes each layer's inputs over every mini-batch during training, with a learned scale and shift.

Why this method: the input-scaling idea, applied to every hidden layer, allowed much higher learning rates in deep networks.

🤯 Fun fact · units can crash spacecraft

NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost in September 1999, nine months after launch, because ground software used English units while the spacecraft's software worked in metric. The trajectory errors sent it too close to Mars and it burned up in the atmosphere. Your kNN won't burn up, but it makes the same mistake every time it adds dollars to years.

Check yourself

1. A feature has mean 50 and standard deviation 8. After StandardScaler, what does the value 66 become?
(66 − 50) ÷ 8 = 16 ÷ 8 = 2: two standard deviations above the mean.
2. Your gradient-boosting model's accuracy doesn't change at all after you add StandardScaler. A colleague says the scaler must be broken. What do you say?
Trees, forests and boosting are invariant to stretching or shifting a feature: the split threshold just moves with it. Scaling only matters for distance, penalty or gradient-descent based models.
3. A column of transaction amounts is mostly $5–$200, with a few $50,000 corporate orders. You'll use an RBF SVM. Which scaler fits best?
With min-max, the $50,000 orders define the range and every normal amount is squashed near 0, so the SVM can't tell $5 from $200. Median and IQR ignore the extremes; a log transform also tames the skew (Lessons 1.2 and 1.5).
📝 TL;DR
  • Distances add up squared differences, so the column with the biggest numbers dominates unless you scale.
  • Standard (mean 0, std 1) is the default; min-max for a fixed range; robust (median, IQR) when there are outliers.
  • Scale for kNN, k-means, SVMs, PCA, regularized and gradient-descent models; don't bother for trees and boosting.
  • Badly scaled features stretch the loss bowl: gradient descent zig-zags and crawls (100+ steps vs 1).
  • Fit the scaler on training data only, inside a pipeline.
