Phase 1 · Lesson 1

Exploratory data analysis: look before you model

Before you train anything, spend twenty minutes looking at the data. It's the cheapest way to catch broken columns, missing values, lopsided classes and the “too good to be true” feature that would have fooled your validation score.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Mean, median, standard deviation: the centre and the spread of a column (Math 5.1).
  • Correlation runs from −1 to +1 and measures how well a straight line links two columns (Math 5.2).
  • Data leakage: a feature or a step that lets information you won't have at prediction time sneak into training (Lesson 0.6).
🎯 Hook: four datasets, one set of numbers

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four tiny datasets of 11 points each. Run the usual summary on any of them and you get the same answers: mean of x = 9, mean of y = 7.50, variance of x = 11, variance of y ≈ 4.12, correlation = 0.816, best-fit line y = 3.00 + 0.500x.

His point was to fight the idea, common among statisticians then, that numbers are exact and graphs are rough. In 2017 two researchers at Autodesk pushed the joke further: thirteen datasets of 142 points, one shaped like a dinosaur, with the same means, spreads and correlation to two decimals. So: if the numbers agree, are the datasets the same?

🔮 Predict first

Of Anscombe's four datasets (identical means, variances, correlation 0.816 and regression line), how many look like “a straight line plus some noise” when you plot them?

Just one. Dataset I is a noisy line. II is a smooth curve, III is a perfect line ruined by one outlier, and IV is a vertical stack of points plus one far-away point that creates the whole correlation. Flip through them in Playground 1.

Summary statistics squash a column into a few numbers, and many different shapes squash to the same numbers. A model sees the data a bit like a summary does: a linear regression fitted to any of the four gives the same line. Only your eyes notice that three of the four lines are nonsense.

💡 Big idea Exploratory data analysis (EDA) means looking at the data, with summaries and pictures, before you model it. Its job isn't to find the answer; it's to find the surprises: wrong types, missing values, impossible numbers, skewed columns, lopsided classes, and features that are suspiciously good. Every surprise you find now is a bug you won't ship.
🧠 Analogy: a doctor examines before prescribing

A good doctor doesn't read your blood test numbers and write a prescription. They look at you, ask questions, and notice that one value is odd because the sample sat in a warm van all afternoon. The numbers matter, but only after someone has checked that they mean what they seem to mean.

EDA is that examination for a dataset. The model is the prescription: powerful, but it will happily treat whatever numbers you give it, including the broken ones.

🤔 Why this? Why spend time on EDA?
  1. The problem before. Models don't complain. Feed one a column where “unknown” was coded as -999, or a label that leaked into a feature, and it trains happily and reports a lovely score. The bug shows up months later, in production.
  2. What people tried. Trusting summary tables and the validation score. But as Anscombe showed, the same summary fits very different data, and a leaked feature fools validation too: it is present in the validation rows as well, so the score looks great right up until deployment.
  3. Why this works. Plots and simple group-bys expose shapes that averages hide. In this lesson's churn table, a group-by takes a few seconds and shows that customers who got a retention offer churned 89% of the time versus 5% for the rest: a flag that this column is recorded after the customer said they were leaving.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Time, and it doesn't scale to thousands of columns by eye. EDA can also mislead: stare at enough plots and you'll “find” patterns in noise, and if you look at the test set you've leaked it into your own head. Explore the training split only.
  5. What came next. Automated profiling reports (ydata-profiling, pandas' own describe) for wide tables, data validation tools that turn EDA findings into tests that run on every new batch (Lesson 8.6), and the rest of this phase: fixing what EDA finds (missing values 1.2, categories 1.3, scales 1.4).

✏️ Worked example: why describe() shows the median too

Five customers' monthly spend: 30, 35, 40, 45 and 1,000 (one is a business account).

  • Mean = (30 + 35 + 40 + 45 + 1000) ÷ 5 = 1150 ÷ 5 = 230. Nobody spends anything like 230.
  • Median = the middle value once sorted = 40. That's the typical customer.
  • When the mean is far above the median, the column is right-skewed or has an outlier. describe() prints both (the median is the “50%” row) precisely so you can spot this. What to do about it is Lesson 1.2.

Explore a real-looking table: who cancels their phone plan?

Here is a churn table of the kind every subscription business has: 400 customers, how long they've been with us (tenure in months), their contract and plan, monthly charge, calls to support, age, whether they got a retention_offer, and the target churned (1 = cancelled). It's synthetic, so we know the truth, but everything in it happens in real data.

🔮 Predict first

29% of these customers churned. A model that simply says “nobody churns” for every customer: what accuracy does it get?

About 71% (283 of 400 are right). That's the baseline any real model must beat, and it's why accuracy alone can flatter a useless model. The more lopsided the classes, the worse this gets: see Playground 3.

Leakage you can spot in EDA

Ask of every strong feature: “would I know this value at the moment I need the prediction?” A retention offer is made when a customer phones to cancel. On the day you want to predict churn (to decide who gets a call), nobody has been offered anything yet. The column is a shadow of the label, written down later. The validation score can't warn you, because the shadow is in the validation rows too.

🔮 Predict first

In the KDD Cup 2008 breast-cancer competition, most teams ignored one column because it obviously can't cause cancer. It turned out to be extremely predictive. Which column?

The patient ID. The data had apparently been merged from several sources, with IDs assigned in consecutive blocks per source, and some sources had far more cancer patients than others (that was the analysts' best explanation). So the ID's range revealed the source, which revealed the odds. A plot of the label against the ID would have shown it in seconds.
🚩 Red flags worth a second look
  • One feature alone gets near-perfect accuracy, or a correlation with the target above about 0.8 when the problem is known to be hard.
  • IDs, row numbers or timestamps that predict the label (data sorted or merged by outcome).
  • Columns named like outcomes: refund_issued, closed_date, final_status, days_to_resolution.
  • A feature whose missingness lines up with the label (it's only filled in after the event).

Class balance: the first number to check about the target

A model that always predicts the majority class gets an accuracy equal to the majority's share. With 29% churn that's 71%; with fraud at 1 in 1,000 transactions it's 99.9%, while catching zero fraud. So you check value_counts on the target first, and pick metrics that care about the rare class (precision and recall, Lesson 5.3; imbalance tricks, Lesson 5.5).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

pandas (3.1 as of late 2026) does nearly all of it; matplotlib / seaborn (sns.pairplot, sns.histplot) draw the pictures; ydata-profiling writes a full HTML report for a wide table in one call. Explore the training split only.

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer

df = load_breast_cancer(as_frame=True).frame          # 569 tumours, 30 measurements + "target"
print(df.shape)                                      # (569, 31)
print(df.isna().mean().max())                        # 0.0: no missing values here (rare!)
print(df["target"].value_counts(normalize=True))     # 1 (benign) 0.627, 0 (malignant) 0.373
print(df.describe().T[["mean", "std", "min", "max"]].head())
print(df.corr()["target"].sort_values().head(5))     # worst concave points −0.79, worst perimeter −0.78…
print(df.groupby("target")["mean radius"].median())  # malignant 17.3 vs benign 12.2

df.hist(figsize=(14, 10), bins=30)                       # one histogram per column

Habits that pay: df.describe(include="all") also summarizes text columns; df[col].value_counts(dropna=False) counts missing values as their own row; df.corr(numeric_only=True) skips text columns (correlation only sees straight-line links, so plot anything important).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

A notebook, describe, a few histograms and group-bys, and a chat with whoever produces the data (“when is this column filled in?”). That conversation finds more leaks than any tool.

At scale

Data contracts and validation suites encode what EDA found (“age is 18–110, never null”), run on every new batch, and alert when the data drifts (Lesson 8.6). Feature stores record when each value became known, which is leakage protection built into the platform (Lesson 8.5).

🎤 Interview angle

“You're handed a new dataset. What do you do before modelling?” A strong answer:

  • Checks shape, types, missing values, ranges and duplicates; asks what one row means.
  • Looks at the target's balance and states the majority-class baseline and a metric that fits.
  • Plots distributions and the relation of each feature to the target; mentions Anscombe as the reason to plot.
  • Hunts for leakage: “would I have this value at prediction time?”, suspiciously strong features, IDs and timestamps that predict the label.
  • Explores the training split only, and writes down findings as checks for the pipeline.
🏭 In the real world
Anscombe's quartet 📜 History · 1973 Anscombe's quartet (overview, with the data)

How: four 11-point datasets with the same means, variances, correlation and regression line, published in The American Statistician.

Why this method: the shortest possible argument for plotting before trusting a summary; still the first slide of many data-science courses.

The Datasaurus Dozen 🧪 Research · 2017 Same Stats, Different Graphs (Matejka & Fitzmaurice, Autodesk Research)

How: starting from Alberto Cairo's dinosaur-shaped dataset, simulated annealing nudged the points toward 12 other shapes while keeping the summary statistics fixed to two decimals.

Why this method: it proves you can get almost any picture behind the same numbers, not just four hand-picked ones.

Leaks in data-mining competitions 🧪 Research · 2011 Kaufman, Rosset & Perlich: Leakage in Data Mining (PDF)

How: catalogues leaks such as KDD Cup 2008's patient IDs and the INFORMS 2010 stock challenge, where about 30 teams scored above 0.9 AUC (area under the ROC curve, Lesson 5.4) thanks to information from the future.

Why this method: it gives the test to apply in EDA: a feature is legitimate only if it would be observable when the prediction is made.

The everyday toolkit 🧰 Open source pandas DataFrame.describe

How: describe, value_counts, groupby, corr and hist cover most first looks; seaborn and ydata-profiling add pictures and reports.

Why this method: one line each, on the same DataFrame you'll feed to scikit-learn (Lesson 1.6).

🤯 Fun fact · a dinosaur took 200,000 nudges

Each of the Datasaurus Dozen's shapes took about 200,000 small random moves of the points, each move accepted only if the summary statistics stayed the same to two decimals. The original dinosaur was drawn by data-visualization expert Alberto Cairo in 2016 to make one point: never trust summary statistics alone.

Check yourself

1. A column of house prices has mean $610,000 and median $420,000. What does that tell you?
The median is the typical house; the mean is dragged toward the long right tail. Prices, incomes and city sizes are usually like this. Plot a histogram, and consider a log transform or a robust model (Lessons 1.2 and 1.5).
2. In a loan-default dataset, collections_agency_contacted alone gives 97% validation accuracy. What's the most likely explanation?
A feature recorded after the outcome is a shadow of the label. Validation can't catch it because it's present in validation rows too. Ask “would I know this at prediction time?” and drop it (Lesson 0.6).
3. 2% of your transactions are fraud. A teammate's model has 97.5% accuracy. Good?
With 2% positives, always predicting the majority class gives 98% accuracy and catches no fraud. Always compare against the majority baseline and use metrics for the rare class (Lessons 5.3 and 5.5).
📝 TL;DR
  • Same summary statistics can hide completely different data (Anscombe 1973, Datasaurus 2017): always plot.
  • First twenty minutes: shape and types, missing values, describe, the target's balance, histograms, group-bys and correlations with the target.
  • Mean far from the median means skew or outliers; counts below the row count mean missing values.
  • A feature that predicts the target “too well” is probably leakage: ask whether you'd know it at prediction time.
  • The majority-class baseline is the first number to beat. Explore the training split only.
