Phase 1 · Lesson 6

Pipelines & ColumnTransformer: one object from raw table to prediction

Everything in this phase (imputing, encoding, scaling, engineering) has to be learned from training data only and replayed identically on new data. A Pipeline makes that automatic: it's leak-proof inside cross-validation and it's the single object you deploy.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Transformers have fit (learn statistics) and transform (apply them); models have fit and predict (Lesson 0.5).
  • Cross-validation (CV): split the training data into k folds; each fold is scored by a model trained on the other k − 1 (Lesson 0.6; in depth 5.1).
  • Imputers (1.2), encoders (1.3) and scalers (1.4) all learn something from the data they're fitted on.
🎯 Hook: 76% accuracy on coin flips

scikit-learn's own documentation has a page called “Common pitfalls”. In one example it builds a dataset of 200 rows and 10,000 columns of pure random numbers, with random labels. Nothing can be learned; the honest accuracy is 50%.

Then it does what many tutorials do: pick the 25 “best” columns with SelectKBest on the whole dataset, then split into train and test and fit a model. Test accuracy: 0.76. Do the same steps in the right order (split first, select on the training part only, or simply put both steps in a Pipeline) and it's 0.5, as it should be.

Same data, same model, same code, in a different order: a 26-point illusion. This lesson is about making the right order the only possible one.

🔮 Predict first

60 rows, 500 columns of random noise, random 0/1 labels. You choose the 10 columns most correlated with the label using all 60 rows, then run 5-fold cross-validation of a kNN on those 10 columns. Roughly what CV accuracy do you see?

Well above 50%. Out of 500 random columns, a few correlate with these particular labels by pure chance, including in the rows that later become test folds. The selection step “saw” the test labels. In Playground 1 the leaky run scores about 75%; the same steps inside each fold score about 50%.

Numbers first: the order of operations is the leak

❌ Leaky order✅ Pipeline order
Step 1Fit imputer / scaler / selector / encoder on all rowsSplit into folds (or train/test)
Step 2Split into foldsIn each fold: fit the preprocessing on the training part only
Step 3Fit the model on each training part, score on the test foldTransform the test fold with those statistics; fit the model; score
Random data, 500 columns, select 10about 75% (an illusion)about 50% (the truth)

The leak is biggest when a step looks at the labels (feature selection, target encoding from Lesson 1.3) and small but real when it only looks at feature statistics (a scaler's mean, an imputer's median). The fix is the same for all of them.

🎯 Goal: make the leaky setup score at least 85% on pure noise, then watch the Pipeline bring it back to about 50%

💡 Big idea A Pipeline chains transformers and a final model into one estimator. Calling fit fits each step on the training data in order; calling predict replays the learned transforms and predicts. Because cross-validation and hyperparameter search call fit on each training fold, every step is automatically refitted inside every fold: no leak is possible. A ColumnTransformer lets different columns get different preparation (numbers imputed and scaled, categories one-hot encoded) and glues the results side by side.
🧠 Analogy: a recipe, not a pre-cooked meal

The leaky version is like a chef tasting every dish of the competition, including the judges' dishes, before deciding how much salt to use. The Pipeline is a written recipe: “measure the salt from the ingredients in front of you”. Hand the recipe to anyone (a CV fold, a grid search, the production server) and they make the dish the same way with their own ingredients. You ship the recipe, not the meal.

🤔 Why this? Why Pipelines and ColumnTransformer?
  1. The problem before. Preprocessing written as loose notebook cells: X = scaler.fit_transform(X) on everything, then split. Cross-validation then scores a model whose preprocessing has seen the test folds (in the scikit-learn pitfalls example: 0.76 instead of 0.5). And production needs the exact same steps in the exact same order, rewritten by someone else, so they drift apart.
  2. What people tried. Carefully fitting each transformer on the training split by hand and transforming the test split. It works for one split, but with 5-fold CV and a grid of 20 settings that's 100 manual fits of every step, and one slip silently leaks.
  3. Why this works. One object owns every step. cross_val_score(pipe, X, y) clones the pipeline for each fold and calls fit on the training part only. In Playground 1 the same random data scores about 75% with selection outside CV and about 50% with it inside the pipeline. The fitted pipeline is also exactly the thing you save and serve: raw rows in, predictions out.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A learning curve (step names, step__param syntax), custom logic must be wrapped as a transformer (FunctionTransformer or a small class), and a pipeline can't protect you from leakage that's already in the data: a leaked column (Lesson 1.1) or rows from the future in a time series (Lesson 7.1) still need your judgement.
  5. What came next. set_output(transform="pandas") (scikit-learn 1.2) keeps column names all the way through; get_feature_names_out tells you what the model actually sees; metadata routing passes sample weights and groups to the right steps; and in production, feature stores and model registries take over versioning the whole thing (Lesson 8.5).

Step 1: a Pipeline is a list of steps

from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline, make_pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

pipe = Pipeline([("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                 ("scale", StandardScaler()),
                 ("model", LogisticRegression())])
pipe = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())  # same, names chosen for you:
# 'simpleimputer', 'standardscaler', 'logisticregression'

pipe.set_params(logisticregression__C=0.1)   # step name + "__" + parameter: how grid search reaches inside
pipe[:-1]                                     # everything except the model (slicing works)
pipe.named_steps["standardscaler"]           # one fitted step, e.g. to read .mean_

Rules of the road: every step except the last must be a transformer (has transform); the last can be anything (a model, or a transformer). fit runs fit_transform on each transformer in turn and fit on the last; predict runs transform on each and predict on the last. That's also why target encoding inside a pipeline cross-fits correctly (Lesson 1.3): training goes through fit_transform.

Step 2: ColumnTransformer, different columns, different treatment

Real tables mix types: numbers with blanks, a small category, a huge category, a date, an ID. A ColumnTransformer takes a list of (transformer, columns) pairs, applies each to its columns, and stacks the outputs side by side. Columns you don't mention are handled by remainder: "drop" (the default) or "passthrough". Afterwards, get_feature_names_out() lists every output column, prefixed by the transformer's name (pipeline__age, onehotencoder__plan_basic).

🔮 Predict first

You list transformers for age and plan in a make_column_transformer, but forget customer_id and signup_date. You don't set remainder. What happens to the forgotten columns?

Silently dropped: remainder="drop" is the default. Great for an ID column, bad if you forgot a useful one. Always check get_feature_names_out(). With remainder="passthrough" they'd be kept as they are, and a text date would then crash the model.

Step 3: the order inside a pipeline matters too

A few rules decide most orderings: fill blanks before anything that can't handle them; encode text before any numeric step; create features before scaling them (so x² gets scaled too); scale before distance- or variance-based steps (kNN, PCA); select features after they exist; and the model goes last.

✏️ Worked example: how many fits does a grid search do?

A pipeline of imputer → scaler → logistic regression, a grid of 4 values of C × 2 imputer strategies, 5-fold CV.

  • Settings: 4 × 2 = 8. Fits: 8 × 5 folds = 40, plus 1 final refit on all training data = 41 fits of the whole pipeline.
  • Each fit refits the imputer and scaler on that fold's training rows: 41 imputers, 41 scalers, 41 models, none of which ever saw its test fold.
  • By hand that's 123 fit calls to get right. With a pipeline it's one line: GridSearchCV(pipe, {"logisticregression__C": [...], "simpleimputer__strategy": [...]}, cv=5) (Lesson 5.7).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): Pipeline / make_pipeline, ColumnTransformer / make_column_transformer, make_column_selector (pick columns by dtype or name pattern), FunctionTransformer for custom steps, set_output(transform="pandas"), get_feature_names_out, and joblib or skops to save the fitted pipeline.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.compose import make_column_transformer
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder, TargetEncoder
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0); n = 1000
df = pd.DataFrame({"age": rng.normal(45, 14, n).round(), "income": rng.lognormal(10.8, 0.5, n).round(-2),
                   "plan": rng.choice(["basic", "standard", "premium"], n),
                   "city": rng.choice([f"city_{i}" for i in range(300)], n), "customer_id": np.arange(n)})
df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.1, "age"] = np.nan
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(df["age"].fillna(45) - 45) / 10 - (df["plan"] == "basic")))).astype(int)

numeric = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True), StandardScaler())
prep = make_column_transformer(
    (numeric, ["age", "income"]),
    (OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="infrequent_if_exist", min_frequency=20, sparse_output=False), ["plan"]),
    (TargetEncoder(target_type="binary"), ["city"]),
    remainder="drop",                                     # customer_id is dropped (the default)
)
model = make_pipeline(prep, LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).set_output(transform="pandas")

print(cross_val_score(model, df, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc").mean().round(3))   # 0.795, every step refitted per fold
model.fit(df, y)
print(model[:-1].get_feature_names_out().tolist())
# ['pipeline__age', 'pipeline__income', 'pipeline__missingindicator_age', 'onehotencoder__plan_basic',
#  'onehotencoder__plan_premium', 'onehotencoder__plan_standard', 'targetencoder__city']

import joblib
joblib.dump(model, "churn_model.joblib")    # ship ONE object: raw DataFrame in, probabilities out

With set_output(transform="pandas"), the one-hot encoder must produce dense output (sparse_output=False), or scikit-learn raises an error. make_column_selector(dtype_include="number") picks numeric columns automatically, but notice it would also pick customer_id: an ID stored as a number is still an ID. Only load joblib/pickle files you trust; they can run code.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Everything in one make_pipeline(make_column_transformer(...), model), scored with cross_val_score, saved with joblib, loaded by a small API. That alone prevents the two most common production bugs: leaky validation and training–serving skew.

At scale

Pipelines are versioned in a model registry with their training data snapshot and metrics; heavy feature computation moves to a feature store, while the light, statistics-dependent steps (encoders, scalers) stay in the model artifact. CI tests check that the served pipeline gives the same outputs as the trained one (Lesson 8.5).

🎤 Interview angle

“Why use a scikit-learn Pipeline?” A strong answer:

  • Leakage: preprocessing statistics are learned inside each CV fold, never from the test fold; give the feature-selection example.
  • Reproducibility and deployment: one fitted object transforms raw input exactly as in training, so there's no training–serving skew.
  • Tuning: GridSearchCV can tune preprocessing and model together via step__param.
  • ColumnTransformer for mixed types, remainder, get_feature_names_out, set_output.
  • Limits: it can't fix leakage baked into the data (future rows, label-derived columns); use time-aware splits for time series.
🏭 In the real world
The leak in the docs 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Common pitfalls and recommended practices

How: 200 rows, 10,000 random features: selecting 25 features before splitting gives 0.76 test accuracy; selecting inside a pipeline gives 0.5.

Why this method: the scikit-learn team's own recommendation is to put every data-dependent step in a Pipeline, because the leak applies to scalers, imputers and PCA too.

Leakage across science 🧪 Research · 2022 Kapoor & Narayanan: Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis in ML-based Science

How: surveyed published work that used ML and found leakage errors reported in 17 fields, affecting 329 papers; in civil-war prediction, every paper claiming complex models beat logistic regression failed to reproduce once leakage was fixed.

Why this method: preprocessing on the full dataset (including the test set) is one of the textbook leaks they list; pipelines and careful splits are the cure.

Why scikit-learn composes so well 📜 History · 2013 Buitinck et al.: API design for machine learning software, experiences from the scikit-learn project

How: describes the shared estimator / transformer / predictor interface and how Pipelines and grid search are built on it.

Why this method: because every step speaks fit/transform/predict, any chain of steps is itself an estimator, which is what makes leak-free CV automatic.

Mixed-type tables 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Column Transformer with mixed types

How: the Titanic dataset with numeric and categorical columns, imputed, scaled and one-hot encoded in one ColumnTransformer, then grid-searched as a whole.

Why this method: the template almost every tabular project starts from.

🤯 Fun fact · names with double underscores

Pipeline parameter names like columntransformer__pipeline__simpleimputer__strategy look odd, but they're a path: step, then sub-step, then parameter, joined by __. That's why scikit-learn forbids double underscores in step names, and why pipe.get_params() on a big pipeline lists a hundred parameters you can tune with one grid search.

Check yourself

1. GridSearchCV on a pipeline with a grid of 3 × 3 settings and cv=5. How many times is the whole pipeline fitted, including the final refit?
3 × 3 = 9 settings × 5 folds = 45 fits, plus one refit of the best setting on all the training data (refit=True by default) = 46. Each one refits every preprocessing step on its own training rows.
2. A teammate runs X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X) and X = SelectKBest(k=20).fit_transform(X, y), then cross_val_score(model, X, y). Which step leaks more, and what's the fix?
Both leak, but feature selection looks at y, so it can pick columns that only correlate with the test folds' labels by chance (the 0.76-on-noise effect). cross_val_score only refits what's inside the estimator you give it.
3. After fitting, get_feature_names_out() shows no columns from last_login_days, a feature you thought was important. Most likely cause?
Unlisted columns go to remainder, which drops them by default. Add it to a transformer's column list (or use make_column_selector), and check the output names every time you change the pipeline.
📝 TL;DR
  • Fitting preprocessing on all rows before CV leaks; feature selection on pure noise can score 76–85% instead of 50%.
  • A Pipeline chains steps into one estimator, so CV and grid search refit every step inside every fold automatically.
  • ColumnTransformer gives each column type its own steps; unlisted columns are dropped by default (remainder).
  • Order: impute → encode → create features → scale → select → model. Check get_feature_names_out(); use set_output(transform="pandas").
  • Ship the fitted pipeline as one object: raw rows in, predictions out, no training–serving skew.
