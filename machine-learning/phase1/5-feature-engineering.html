Phase 1 · Lesson 5

Feature engineering: dates, ratios, interactions, text counts

A model can only combine the columns you give it, in the ways it knows how. Hand it the right columns (a ratio, a time of day on a circle, a customer's history) and a simple model can beat a fancy one. This is where your knowledge of the problem turns into accuracy.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A linear model predicts a weighted sum, w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b: it can stretch and add columns, nothing else (Lesson 2.1).
  • R² is the share of the target's variation a model explains: 0 = no better than predicting the average, 1 = perfect (Lesson 5.2). RMSE (root mean squared error) is the typical miss, in the target's units.
  • Target leakage and target encoding's trap from Lessons 1.1 and 1.3.
🎯 Hook: the class that won KDD Cup 2010

KDD Cup 2010 asked teams to predict whether a student would answer an algebra step correctly on the first try, from logs of about 29 million steps (8.9 million in one dataset, 20 million in the other). The winner of both tracks was a university course at National Taiwan University: 3 instructors, 2 teaching assistants and 19 students.

Their main model was not exotic. It was mostly logistic regression, fed with features the students engineered by hand: one-hot students and problems, combinations of pairs of features, temporal features about each student's recent history. One dataset grew to about a million features. As Pedro Domingos wrote in a famous 2012 essay on what makes ML projects succeed: “Easily the most important factor is the features used.”

🔮 Predict first

Delivery time depends on distance ÷ road speed. You give a linear model the two raw columns, distance and speed. Can it learn the ride time?

No. A linear model only adds weighted columns; no choice of weights turns a·distance + b·speed into distance ÷ speed, however much data you have. Add the ratio as a column and it becomes a single weight. In Playground 1 that one feature lifts validation R² from 0.57 to 0.70.

Numbers first: why a line can't divide

✏️ Worked example: three rides, one line

Ride time in minutes = 60 × distance ÷ speed. Three real rides: 2 km at 10 km/h = 12 min; 8 km at 40 km/h = 12 min; 8 km at 10 km/h = 48 min.

  • Fit a line c + a·distance + b·speed through all three exactly: subtracting the equations gives a = 6, b = −1.2, c = 12.
  • Now predict 2 km at 40 km/h (truth: 3 min): 12 + 6×2 − 1.2×40 = −24 minutes. A delivery that arrives before you order.
  • Add a column ride_min = 60 × distance / speed and the model needs one weight, 1.0, to be exactly right on all four rides.

Trees and boosting can approximate a ratio with many small steps, given lots of data, but even they learn faster and more smoothly when you hand them the ratio. That's the whole game of feature engineering: put the shape of the answer into a column, so the model only has to learn a weight.

💡 Big idea Feature engineering means building new columns from the ones you have, using what you know about the problem: ratios and differences (price per m², time since last visit), dates broken into parts (day of week, hour on a circle), interactions (distance × rush hour), aggregates (a customer's average so far) and counts from text. Each one turns something the model would have to discover into something it only has to weigh. The one rule: every feature must be computable at prediction time, from the past only.
🧠 Analogy: a doctor's BMI

Height and weight alone don't tell a doctor much: 90 kg is healthy at 1.95 m and worrying at 1.60 m. So doctors compute body-mass index, weight ÷ height², one number that captures what matters. Nobody thinks of BMI as cheating; it's domain knowledge written as a formula. Every good engineered feature is a little BMI for your problem.

🤔 Why this? Why engineer features by hand?
  1. The problem before. Simple models can only combine columns in simple ways: a linear model adds, a tree cuts one column at a time. Ratios, cycles (23:00 is next to 00:00), interactions and history are invisible in the raw columns, so the model underfits however long you train it.
  2. What people tried. Bigger models that can discover the shapes themselves: high-degree polynomials (blow up, Lesson 2.5), deep trees (need lots of data to approximate a smooth ratio), neural networks (need even more). They work, slowly and expensively, and they still can't use information you never gave them, like a customer's past orders.
  3. Why this works. One well-chosen column replaces a hard pattern with a single weight. In Playground 1, a linear model on raw delivery columns gets validation R² 0.57 (typical miss 8.2 minutes); adding a ride-time ratio, hour on a circle, a weekend flag, the restaurant's average prep time and a note word count lifts it to 0.86 (4.6 minutes), with the same model.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It takes domain knowledge and time, every feature must be recomputed identically in production, and aggregates over history are the easiest place to leak the future (a customer's average that includes today's order). Hand-made features can also encode bias you didn't intend (Lesson 8.3).
  5. What came next. Automated feature generation (Featuretools' Deep Feature Synthesis builds aggregates across related tables), feature stores that compute features once and serve them consistently (Lesson 8.5), and deep learning, which learns features from raw pixels and text (the sibling Deep Learning course). On tables, hand-made features plus gradient boosting is still the winning recipe (Lesson 4.6).

Dates: break them up, and put the clock on a circle

A raw timestamp (say, 1,793,500,000 seconds since 1970) is useless to most models. Break it into the parts that drive behaviour: day of week, hour, month, is_weekend, is_holiday, days until payday. For values that wrap around (hours, weekdays, months), plain numbers lie: hour 23 and hour 0 are one hour apart but 23 units apart as numbers. Map them onto a circle with sine and cosine:

hour_sin = sin(2π · hour ÷ 24),   hour_cos = cos(2π · hour ÷ 24) “Place each hour on a clock face and use its two coordinates: 23:00 and 00:00 end up neighbours.”

✏️ Worked example: 23:00 vs 00:00
  • As numbers: |23 − 0| = 23, the largest possible gap, though they're an hour apart. Midday (12) looks closer to midnight than 23:00 does.
  • On the circle: 23:00 → (sin, cos) = (−0.26, 0.97); 00:00 → (0, 1). Distance √(0.26² + 0.03²) ≈ 0.26. Noon → (0, −1), distance 2 from midnight: correctly the farthest.
  • Use both sin and cos: sine alone gives 03:00 and 09:00 the same value (0.71), so one coordinate can't tell them apart; the cosine (0.71 vs −0.71) can.

Aggregates: powerful, and the easiest place to leak

Some of the best features come from other rows: a customer's average basket, how many times they called support in the last 30 days, a restaurant's average prep time last week (Uber's engineering blog described exactly this kind of feature for UberEATS delivery estimates in 2017, computed over the last 7 days and the last hour). The rule that keeps them honest: only use rows from before the moment of prediction, and never the row's own target.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

pandas does most feature engineering: .dt for dates, arithmetic for ratios, .str for text counts, groupby(...).transform(...) for aggregates (with shift() to exclude the current row). scikit-learn wraps it for pipelines: FunctionTransformer, PolynomialFeatures(interaction_only=True), SplineTransformer (smooth and periodic splines). Featuretools automates aggregates across related tables.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures

orders = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer": ["ana", "ben", "ana", "ana", "ben"],
    "ordered_at": pd.to_datetime(["2026-10-30 12:10", "2026-10-31 19:45", "2026-11-01 23:30", "2026-11-02 00:15", "2026-11-02 08:05"]),
    "distance_km": [2.0, 6.5, 1.2, 3.0, 4.4], "road_speed_kmh": [10, 30, 25, 40, 12],
    "note": ["", "ring twice please", "no onions", "", "leave at door, dog is friendly"],
    "minutes": [31, 28, 19, 22, 41],
})
t = orders["ordered_at"]
orders["dow"] = t.dt.day_of_week                                   # Monday = 0 … Sunday = 6
orders["is_weekend"] = (orders["dow"] >= 5).astype(int)
orders["hour_sin"] = np.sin(2 * np.pi * t.dt.hour / 24)          # 23:00 and 00:00 end up close
orders["hour_cos"] = np.cos(2 * np.pi * t.dt.hour / 24)
orders["ride_min"] = 60 * orders["distance_km"] / orders["road_speed_kmh"]  # a ratio a line can't build
orders["note_words"] = orders["note"].str.split().str.len()      # text → a count

# leakage-safe aggregate: the customer's average of EARLIER orders (shift() drops the current row)
orders = orders.sort_values("ordered_at")
orders["cust_past_avg"] = orders.groupby("customer")["minutes"].transform(lambda s: s.shift().expanding().mean())
print(orders[["customer", "minutes", "ride_min", "note_words", "cust_past_avg"]])
# ana: NaN, 31.0, 25.0   (her first order has no history; then 31; then (31 + 19) / 2)

inter = PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, interaction_only=True, include_bias=False).set_output(transform="pandas")
print(inter.fit_transform(orders[["distance_km", "is_weekend"]]).columns.tolist())
# ['distance_km', 'is_weekend', 'distance_km is_weekend']

pandas 3 deprecates .dt.dayofweek in favour of .dt.day_of_week. The first order of each customer has no history (NaN): fill it with the global average and add an indicator (Lesson 1.2). To use these steps inside cross-validation, wrap them in a FunctionTransformer or compute history features from strictly earlier timestamps before splitting by time (Lessons 1.6 and 7.1).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Ten good features from a conversation with the people who know the business beat a hundred random ones. Start with dates, a couple of ratios, and “how many / how much in the last N days”. Write each one as a function you can call both in training and in the API.

At scale

Feature stores (Feast, Tecton, in-house platforms like Uber's Michelangelo) compute features once, store them with timestamps, and serve the same values to training (point-in-time joins, no future leakage) and to live predictions (Lesson 8.5). Teams share and reuse features across models.

🎤 Interview angle

“What features would you build to predict X?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from what drives the target in the real world, then turns each driver into a column: ratios, differences, recency, frequency, monetary value.
  • Handles time properly: date parts, cyclical sin/cos for hours and weekdays, holidays.
  • Proposes aggregates (per user, per item, per time window) and immediately states the point-in-time rule: computed from the past only, never including the current label.
  • Mentions interactions for linear models, and that trees find some interactions on their own.
  • Explains how features are kept identical between training and serving.
🏭 In the real world
Feature engineering wins KDD Cup 2010 🧪 Research · 2010 Yu et al. (National Taiwan University): Feature Engineering and Classifier Ensemble for KDD Cup 2010 (PDF)

How: student sub-teams expanded the logs into sparse one-hot, combination and temporal features (about a million for one dataset) for logistic regression, plus condensed statistical features for a random forest, then blended the sub-teams' predictions.

Why this method: with millions of rows, a linear model on rich features is fast to train and tune; the features carried the knowledge, the model just weighed them. They won both tracks.

“It's the features” 🧪 Research · 2012 Pedro Domingos: A Few Useful Things to Know about Machine Learning (PDF)

How: an essay of practical lessons; one section argues that feature engineering is where most of the effort and success in ML projects lies.

Why this method: learning is easy when you have many independent features that each correlate well with the class; building them is the hard, domain-specific part.

Cyclical and spline time features 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: time-related feature engineering (bike sharing demand)

How: compares raw hour numbers, one-hot hours, sine/cosine pairs and periodic splines for predicting hourly bike rentals, with linear models and gradient boosting.

Why this method: the same model gets much better with the right time encoding; it shows each encoding's strengths and failure modes.

Automated aggregates across tables 🧰 Open source Featuretools: Deep Feature Synthesis

How: given related tables (customers, orders, items), stacks aggregation and transform functions (“mean of order totals per customer”, “month of first order”) to generate candidate features automatically.

Why this method: relational data hides most of its signal in aggregates; automating them speeds up the search, though you still judge which ones make sense and are leak-free.

🤯 Fun fact · day 0 is a Monday

In pandas, day_of_week numbers Monday as 0 and Sunday as 6, while JavaScript's getDay() starts at Sunday = 0. A weekend flag written as dow >= 5 is correct in one and silently wrong in the other. Train in Python, serve in JavaScript, and your model thinks Friday is the weekend: a classic training–serving skew bug (Lesson 8.5).

Check yourself

1. Using hour_sin = sin(2π·h/24) and hour_cos = cos(2π·h/24), what are the two values for 06:00?
2π × 6/24 = π/2, a quarter turn: sin(π/2) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0. Midnight is (0, 1), noon (0, −1), 18:00 (−1, 0): the four points of a clock face.
2. You predict house prices with linear regression from two columns, size_m2 and neighbourhood_price_per_m2. Which single engineered feature probably helps most?
A price is roughly size × price per m²: a product (an interaction), which a weighted sum can't build. A sum is already available to the model; a ratio has no meaning here.
3. A churn model uses “number of support tickets in the customer's lifetime”, computed on today's database for every historical row. Validation looks great; production disappoints. Why?
Computing a history feature from today's data gives old rows future information. Count only tickets before each row's prediction date (a point-in-time join), exactly as production will see it.
📝 TL;DR
  • A model can only combine columns in its own way; feature engineering puts the shape of the answer into a column.
  • Linear models can't divide or multiply: add ratios (distance ÷ speed) and interactions explicitly.
  • Break dates into parts; put cyclical values (hours, weekdays) on a circle with sin and cos.
  • Aggregates are powerful and leak-prone: from earlier rows only, never including the current label.
  • Playground 1: same linear model, validation R² 0.57 → 0.86 from features alone.
