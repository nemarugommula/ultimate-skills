Phase 2 · Lesson 2

Regularization: ridge, lasso, elastic net

Give a linear model too many features and it memorizes the noise with huge, wild weights. Charging a small “tax” on the size of the weights fixes it, and one version of the tax even switches useless features off. It's the most used fix for overfitting in all of machine learning.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Linear regression picks the weights that minimize the mean squared error (MSE); a weight is an effect per unit of its feature (Lesson 2.1).
  • Overfitting: great on training data, bad on new data (Lesson 0.6). Scaling puts features on comparable units (Lesson 1.4).
  • The size of a weight vector can be measured as Σ|w| (the L1 norm) or √Σw² (the L2 norm) (Math 1.4).
🎯 Hook: twelve points, ten parameters, one disaster

Take 12 noisy points from a gentle curve whose values stay between about −1.2 and +1.2. Fit a degree-9 polynomial (features x, x², …, x⁹, plus an intercept) by ordinary least squares. The fit passes almost through every point: training MSE 0.028.

On 60 fresh points from the same curve, the MSE is 10.5, ten times worse than predicting the plain average (0.96). And the weights on the raw powers of x include +141 and −173, for a curve that never leaves ±1.2. Huge weights of opposite sign, cancelling each other on the training points and exploding between them.

You could throw features away by hand. Or you could keep all ten parameters and simply make big weights expensive. With the right price, the same degree-9 model gets a test MSE of 0.086, better than a plain cubic (0.109). That's regularization.

🔮 Predict first

You add a penalty λ·(sum of squared weights) to the loss and slowly raise λ from 0. What happens to the training error and the test error?

The penalty stops the model from fitting the training points as closely, so training error always rises. Test error falls at first (less wiggle, less memorized noise), reaches a minimum, then rises when λ is so big the model becomes too stiff to follow the real curve. Finding the bottom of that U is what RidgeCV and LassoCV do. See it in Playground 1's chart.

💡 Big idea Minimize MSE + λ × (size of the weights). The penalty pulls every weight toward zero; only weights that reduce the error by more than they cost survive. Ridge measures size as Σw² (shrinks everything smoothly), lasso as Σ|w| (pushes some weights to exactly 0), elastic net mixes both. λ is a knob you tune by cross-validation, and the features must be on the same scale first.
🧠 Analogy: packing for a flight with a baggage fee

With no fee, you pack everything “just in case”: that's least squares, using every feature as hard as it can. A ridge fee charges by the square of each bag's weight: very heavy bags become ruinous, so you spread a lighter load across all bags. A lasso fee charges a flat rate per kilo in every bag: a bag with only one sock in it isn't worth its fee, so you leave it at home. You end up with fewer bags.

Set the fee too high and you board with nothing (every weight zero: the model predicts the average). The right fee is found by trying several and checking which trip went best: cross-validation.

🤔 Why this? Why regularization?
  1. The problem before. Least squares has no brakes. With many features, correlated features, or more features than rows, it finds huge weights that cancel each other on the training data (the hook's +141 and −173) and explode on new data. When the number of features reaches the number of rows, it can fit pure noise perfectly.
  2. What people tried. Dropping features by hand, or stepwise selection (add or remove one feature at a time by some score): slow, unstable (a small data change picks different features) and greedy. Lower-degree models: safe, but they can miss real curvature.
  3. Why this works. Adding λ·Σw² makes every weight pay rent. For a single standardized feature, ridge's answer is simply the least-squares weight divided by (1 + λ): with λ = 1, a weight of 3 becomes 1.5. Weights backed by lots of evidence shrink a little in relative terms; weights that only fit noise collapse. Hoerl and Kennard proposed it in 1970 for exactly the correlated-features problem; Tibshirani's lasso (1996) swapped in Σ|w| and got feature selection for free.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. The weights are biased toward zero on purpose (a little bias for a lot less variance). You must scale features first, or the penalty hits features unevenly (Playground 3). λ must be tuned. The lasso picks one of a group of correlated features fairly arbitrarily, and with more features than rows it can keep at most as many features as there are rows.
  5. What came next. Elastic net (Zou & Hastie, 2005) mixes L1 and L2 to keep correlated groups together. Fast path algorithms (glmnet, 2010) fit every λ in one sweep. The same idea is everywhere: C in logistic regression and SVMs (Lessons 2.3, 3.3), leaf penalties in XGBoost (4.4), weight decay in neural networks (2.5).

Step 1: turn the λ knob

The loss being minimized, in words first: “the usual squared error, plus λ times the size of the weights”.

loss = MSE + λ · Σ wⱼ² (ridge)  or  + λ · Σ |wⱼ| (lasso) “Pay for your errors, and also pay for every unit of weight you use.” The intercept is never penalized: shifting the whole curve up or down is free.

✏️ Worked example: ridge vs lasso on one weight

One standardized feature, least-squares weight w = 0.8. What does each penalty do as λ grows?

  • Ridge divides: w = 0.8 ÷ (1 + λ). λ = 1 → 0.4; λ = 3 → 0.2; λ = 100 → 0.008. Always smaller, never exactly zero.
  • Lasso subtracts (in the form scikit-learn uses, with the MSE halved): w = 0.8 − λ, but stops at zero. λ = 0.3 → 0.5; λ = 0.8 → 0; λ = 2 → still 0. This is called soft thresholding.
  • So a weight whose least-squares value is smaller than λ is switched off entirely by the lasso. Ridge keeps every feature, just quieter.

Step 2: why L1 makes exact zeros (the diamond and the circle)

There's a beautiful picture behind the difference. Penalizing is equivalent to a budget: “minimize the error, but the weights must fit inside a region”. For two weights, the L2 budget w₁² + w₂² ≤ t² is a circle; the L1 budget |w₁| + |w₂| ≤ t is a diamond with corners on the axes. The error's contour lines are ellipses around the unpenalized answer. The solution is where the smallest ellipse first touches the region.

🔮 Predict first

Drop the ellipse's centre (the unpenalized answer) at 1,000 random places outside the budget. In what share of them does the diamond's answer land exactly on an axis (one weight exactly 0)?

With the default ellipse, about 58% of the time; the circle's answer lands on an axis in 0 of 1,000. An expanding ellipse is far more likely to hit a pointy corner first than a flat edge, while a circle has no corners at all. In many dimensions the diamond has even more corners and edges where several weights are zero at once, which is why the lasso zeroes many features. Press 🎲 below to check.

Step 3: scale first, or the penalty plays favourites

The penalty looks at the numbers in the weights, not at what they mean. Measure a feature in kilometres instead of metres and its weight must become 1,000× bigger to have the same effect, so the penalty crushes it. Measure it in millimetres and its weight is tiny, so it's barely penalized at all. Same information, different treatment.

🔮 Predict first

Two equally useful features. You re-express feature 2 in units 100× smaller (its numbers become 100× bigger, like metres → centimetres) and fit ridge without scaling. Which weight gets shrunk more?

Feature 2 needs a weight 100× smaller to do the same job, and the penalty on a tiny weight is almost nothing, so feature 2 keeps 99–100% of its least-squares weight while feature 1 keeps 47%. Flip it (numbers 100× smaller) and feature 2 is crushed to ~0%. With standardization, both keep 50%. Try it.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, and their cross-validated versions RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV, which try many λ values (called alpha in scikit-learn) and keep the best. lasso_path computes the whole path. In R, glmnet is the classic.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

models = {
    "ridge": RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 13)),               # efficient leave-one-out CV by default
    "lasso": LassoCV(cv=5, random_state=0),                      # tries 100 alphas along the path
    "enet":  ElasticNetCV(l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 1.0], cv=5, random_state=0),
}
for name, reg in models.items():
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), reg).fit(X_tr, y_tr)   # scale first, inside the pipeline
    m = pipe[-1]
    print(f"{name:5s} alpha={m.alpha_:.3g}  zeros={int(np.sum(m.coef_ == 0))}/10  test R²={pipe.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
# ridge alpha=31.6  zeros=0/10  test R²=0.366
# lasso alpha=1.86  zeros=3/10  test R²=0.356   (drops age, s4, s6)
# enet  alpha=1.86  zeros=3/10  test R²=0.356   (it picked l1_ratio = 1.0: pure lasso)

Watch the units of alpha: Lasso and ElasticNet minimize (1/2n)·Σ(error²) + alpha·penalty, while Ridge minimizes Σ(error²) + alpha·Σw² with no 1/n, so the same number means different strengths across classes. Always pick alpha by cross-validation, never by habit. In ElasticNet, l1_ratio is the mix r (1 = lasso).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Never ship a plain least-squares model with more than a handful of features. make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV()) is the safe default; try LassoCV when you suspect most features are noise and want a short list to explain to stakeholders.

At scale

With millions or billions of sparse features (ad IDs, words, crosses), L1 keeps models small enough to serve from memory, and L2 keeps rare features from getting extreme weights. Online learners such as FTRL-Proximal apply both penalties while streaming data.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between L1 and L2 regularization?” A strong answer:

  • Both add a penalty on weight size to the loss to trade a little bias for less variance; λ is tuned by cross-validation.
  • L2 (ridge) shrinks all weights smoothly (one feature: divide by 1 + λ), never to zero, and handles correlated features well.
  • L1 (lasso) soft-thresholds (subtract λ, stop at zero), giving exact zeros and feature selection; geometrically, the diamond's corners.
  • Elastic net mixes them for correlated groups; features must be standardized first; the intercept isn't penalized.
  • Bonus: L2 is a Gaussian prior on the weights and L1 a Laplace prior (MAP estimation, Math 7.4).
🏭 In the real world
Ad click prediction at Google 🏢 Google · 2013 Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches (PDF)

How: regularized logistic regression trained online with FTRL-Proximal, using L1 and L2 penalties, on features with billions of dimensions.

Why this method: the L1 penalty makes most weights exactly zero, which matters when models with billions of coefficients are replicated to many data centres; the paper also lists other memory-saving tricks.

The lasso itself 📜 History · 1996 Robert Tibshirani: The Lasso Page

How: minimize the squared error subject to a bound on the sum of absolute weights, “a shrinkage and selection method for linear regression”.

Why this method: it gave ridge's stability and subset selection's interpretability in one convex problem, inspired by Breiman's nonnegative garrote. The page lists its links to wavelet denoising, boosting and forward stagewise regression; a whole family of sparse methods grew from it.

Elastic net for correlated groups 🧪 Research · 2005 Zou & Hastie: Regularization and variable selection via the elastic net (PDF)

How: combined the L1 and L2 penalties.

Why this method: with more features than rows the lasso can select at most n of them, and it picks one out of a correlated group; the elastic net keeps groups together (the “grouping effect”), e.g. genes that act together.

Every λ in one sweep 🧰 Open source glmnet: An Introduction

How: coordinate descent with warm starts fits ridge, lasso and elastic net for a whole sequence of λ values, for linear, logistic, Poisson and Cox models.

Why this method: the path costs about as much as one fit, so cross-validating λ is cheap. scikit-learn's LassoCV uses the same idea.

🤯 Fun fact · a lasso that catches features

“Lasso” stands for least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, and the name fits the picture: it ropes in a few features and lets the rest go. Tibshirani's paper was received by the journal in January 1994 and published in 1996.

🌉 How it connects

Regularization is how linear regression (2.1) survives many features. The same penalty appears as C in logistic regression (2.3, where C = 1/λ), keeps polynomial features in check (2.5), and returns in SVMs (3.3), gradient boosting (4.3–4.4) and neural networks as weight decay. Choosing λ honestly is cross-validation (5.1) and hyperparameter search (5.7); the U-shaped curve is bias–variance (5.8). Check yourself

1. One standardized feature has least-squares weight 0.6. With ridge λ = 2, and with lasso λ = 0.4 (soft thresholding: subtract λ, stop at zero), what are the weights?
Ridge divides: 0.6 ÷ (1 + 2) = 0.2. Lasso subtracts: 0.6 − 0.4 = 0.2. Same number here, different mechanics: raise λ further and the lasso hits exactly 0 at λ = 0.6, while ridge only approaches it.
2. You have 300 patients and 20,000 gene-expression features, and biologists want a short list of genes. Which model do you start with?
More features than rows: least squares can fit noise perfectly. Ridge keeps all 20,000 genes (no short list). The L1 penalty gives exact zeros; elastic net keeps co-regulated genes together and isn't limited to 300 selected features.
3. A teammate fits Lasso(alpha=0.1) on raw features: income in dollars and age in years. Age always gets weight 0, income never. What's the most likely issue?
A weight's size depends on the feature's units, so the penalty hits features unevenly (Playground 3). Put StandardScaler before the lasso in a Pipeline and choose alpha by cross-validation.
📝 TL;DR
  • Regularization adds λ × (weight size) to the loss: a little bias for much less variance. Training error goes up, test error goes down, until λ is too big.
  • Ridge (L2) shrinks smoothly and never zeros; lasso (L1) soft-thresholds and zeros features (the diamond's corners); elastic net mixes both for correlated groups.
  • Standardize features first, in a Pipeline; the intercept isn't penalized.
  • Pick λ by cross-validation: RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV. Next: logistic regression, where C = 1/λ (2.3).
