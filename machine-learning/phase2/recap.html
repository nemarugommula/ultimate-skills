Recap: Linear models

One weighted sum, five lessons: least squares, the penalties that tame it, the sigmoid that turns it into a probability, the softmax and log links that handle classes and counts, and the features that bend it, all the way to a neural network. Here's the phase on one page, a diagnosis game, a model to design yourself and Lab 2.

🧾 Cheat sheet

📏

2.1 Linear regression

ŷ = w·x + b; minimize MSE. 1 feature: slope = cov(x, y) ÷ var(x). R² = 1 − SSE/SST.

Closed form (normal equations) or gradient descent (scale features!). Squares let outliers pull hard: Huber or MAE resist. A weight is an effect per unit holding the others fixed: conditional, not causal.

🧰 LinearRegression, HuberRegressor, SGDRegressor, statsmodels OLS

🎚️

2.2 Regularization

Loss = MSE + λ·Σw² (ridge) or λ·Σ|w| (lasso). Ridge divides by (1 + λ); lasso subtracts λ and stops at 0.

A little bias for much less variance. Lasso zeros features (the diamond's corners); elastic net keeps correlated groups. Standardize first; tune λ by CV.

🧰 RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV, glmnet

🔀

2.3 Logistic regression

p = σ(w·x + b); log-loss; ew = odds ratio. sklearn: C = 1/λ (small C = strong penalty).

Straight boundary; the threshold is a separate business decision that trades precision for recall. class_weight="balanced" for rare classes.

🧰 LogisticRegression, LogisticRegressionCV, TunedThresholdClassifierCV

🎲

2.4 Multiclass & GLMs

Softmax: ezk ÷ Σez. Counts: log(mean) = w·x + b, so ew is a rate ratio.

Softmax beats one-vs-rest on in-between classes. GLM = linear score + link + matching loss: Poisson for counts, Gamma for amounts, Tweedie for amounts with zeros. Default alpha = 1!

🧰 LogisticRegression, OneVsRestClassifier, PoissonRegressor, TweedieRegressor

🧠

2.5 Features → neural nets

Polynomial columns = C(d + k, k). XOR: add x₁·x₂. Splines: local bumps, stable edges.

Linear in the weights, flexible in the inputs. High degrees explode and wiggle; regularize. A neural network is a GLM on hidden features it learns itself.

🧰 PolynomialFeatures, SplineTransformer, MLPClassifier

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “We predict the probability that a customer cancels next month, from 40 features. Walk me through a linear-model approach: the model, the loss, how you'd keep it from overfitting, how you'd read its weights, and how the team should turn probabilities into actions. Then: the model underfits. What do you try before switching to boosted trees?”

A strong answer covers: logistic regression (sigmoid of a weighted sum) trained with log-loss; a Pipeline with scaling and one-hot encoding; an L2 (or L1 to prune features) penalty with C tuned by cross-validation; weights as log odds ratios, conditional on the other features and not causal, unstable when features correlate; a threshold chosen from the costs of a missed churner vs a wasted offer, separate from training, with class weights if churn is rare. For underfitting: residual or partial-dependence checks, then targeted interactions, splines on curved features, log transforms, and only then a more flexible model.

🏗️ Build it yourself: hourly bike rentals

✏️ The problem

A city bike-share company wants to forecast rentals per hour, 7 days ahead, to plan how many bikes to move between stations each night. You have two years of hourly history (2 × 8,760 = 17,520 hours, a few missing) with: hour, weekday, holiday flag, month, temperature, humidity, wind speed, a weather category (clear / mist / rain / heavy rain), and the rental count. Counts range from 1 to about 1,000 an hour, with a morning and an evening commuter peak on working days and a single midday hump at weekends.

The operations team wants a model they can understand and retrain weekly on a laptop. Using this phase's ideas, choose the model, the features, the validation plan and the metrics, with numbers. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Requirements

  • Predict the expected count for each hour of the next 7 days (168 predictions), never negative.
  • Explainable factors (“rain cuts demand by about a third”), retrainable weekly in seconds.

Model: a Poisson GLM, and why

  • The target is a count whose noise grows with its level (quiet nights vary by a few bikes, rush hours by dozens): PoissonRegressor with a log link (Lesson 2.4). Least squares would predict negative counts at night and treat a miss of 20 at 3 a.m. like a miss of 20 at 8 a.m.
  • Effects are naturally multiplicative: rain lowers every hour by a similar percentage, which is exactly what log-link weights (rate ratios ew) express.

Features (about 47 columns)

  • Hour of day: periodic spline, 12 columns (SplineTransformer(extrapolation="periodic")), so 23:00 and 00:00 are neighbours (Lesson 2.5).
  • Hour × working day: the commuter double peak only exists on working days, so add the hour splines multiplied by the working-day flag: 12 more columns. Without this interaction the model averages two different daily shapes into one wrong one.
  • Weekday one-hot (7), month periodic spline (6), temperature spline (5 knots → 7 columns: demand rises, then falls on very hot days), humidity and wind (2), holiday (1).
  • Total ≈ 12 + 12 + 7 + 6 + 7 + 2 + 1 = 47 columns for about 17,000 rows: roughly 370 rows per weight, comfortable with a small L2 penalty (alpha tuned).

Validation

  • Time-based, never random: train on the first 21 months, test on the last 3; tune alpha with TimeSeriesSplit inside the training period (Lessons 5.1, 7.1). A random split leaks next week's weather-and-demand pattern into training.
  • Forecasts 7 days ahead must use forecast weather, not observed weather: evaluate once with observed weather (model quality) and once with archived forecasts (what production will see).

Metrics

  • Mean Poisson deviance (the loss the model optimizes) and MAE in bikes per hour (what operations understands), against a baseline: the same hour one week earlier. If the GLM can't beat that clearly, something is wrong.
  • Check the residuals by hour and weekday: systematic misses at 8 a.m. mean a missing interaction.

Classic mistakes

  • Using columns that are parts of the target: the public UCI Bike Sharing dataset (2011–2012) has separate casual and registered counts that add up to the total. Feeding them in gives a perfect, useless model (Lesson 0.6).
  • One-hot hours with no interaction: a single daily shape for weekdays and weekends.
  • Forgetting that scikit-learn's GLMs default to alpha=1.0, and not scaling the numeric features the penalty acts on.
  • Polynomial degree 10 on temperature: wild forecasts in the first unusually hot week (Lesson 2.5's edges).

What this design doesn't cover yet: special events, station-level forecasts, recursive lag features and comparing with gradient boosting with a Poisson loss. Those are Lessons 4.3, 7.1 and 7.2.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. One standardized feature has a least-squares weight of 1.2. What's its ridge weight with λ = 0.5, and its lasso weight (soft thresholding) with λ = 0.5?
Ridge divides: 1.2 ÷ (1 + 0.5) = 0.8. Lasso subtracts and stops at zero: 1.2 − 0.5 = 0.7. The lasso would reach exactly 0 at λ = 1.2; ridge never does (Lesson 2.2).
2. In the Challenger fit, the weight on temperature is −0.232 per °F. By roughly what factor do the odds of O-ring damage change for a launch 10°F colder?
10°F colder adds +2.32 to the log-odds, so the odds multiply by e2.32 ≈ 10. In logistic regression, weights add on the log-odds scale and multiply the odds (Lesson 2.3).
3. A linear model predicting delivery time has train R² 0.99 and test R² 0.31, with 180 features for 220 rows and weights in the thousands. What's the best first move?
Huge, cancelling weights and a big train–test gap are textbook overfitting (Lesson 2.2's hook). A penalty tuned by cross-validation shrinks the weights; the solver doesn't matter, and more features make it worse.
4. A Poisson model of support tickets per day has weight 0.2 on “releases shipped this week”. A week with 3 releases vs a week with none: how do the expected tickets compare?
Log link: 3 × 0.2 = 0.6 added to log(mean), so the mean multiplies by e0.6 ≈ 1.82 (Lesson 2.4). Weights in a log-link GLM are rate ratios.
5. You have 5 numeric features and use PolynomialFeatures(degree=2) (bias included) before a ridge model. How many columns does the model see?
C(5 + 2, 2) = 21: 1 constant + 5 originals + 5 squares + 10 pairwise products. The count grows like dk, which is why high degrees need a penalty or a smarter feature choice (Lesson 2.5).
