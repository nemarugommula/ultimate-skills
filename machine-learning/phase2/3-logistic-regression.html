Phase 2 · Lesson 3

Logistic regression: log-odds, boundaries, thresholds

Keep the weighted sum of linear regression, squash it into a probability between 0 and 1, and you have the most widely deployed classifier in industry: fraud flags, click predictions, credit scores and medical risk scores. Plus the knob that matters most in practice, the threshold.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A linear model computes a weighted sum w·x + b of the features (Lesson 2.1); a penalty on the weights controls overfitting (Lesson 2.2).
  • Odds = p ÷ (1 − p): a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1. The log turns multiplying into adding (Math 0.3).
  • Precision = of the cases you flagged, the share that were real; recall = of the real cases, the share you flagged (Lesson 5.3 goes deeper).
🎯 Hook: the night before Challenger

On 28 January 1986 the Space Shuttle Challenger launched at an air temperature of 36°F (2°C) and broke apart 73 seconds later: an O-ring seal in a booster joint had failed. The coldest launch before it had been 53°F, and engineers had argued the night before that they had no evidence the seals would work in such cold.

The data existed. In the 23 previous flights, 7 had shown O-ring damage (erosion or blow-by). Look only at those 7 and their temperatures (53, 57, 58, 63, 70, 70, 75°F) seem all over the place. Add the 16 flights without damage, all at 66°F or warmer, and the pattern jumps out: every one of the 4 flights below 65°F had damage.

A 1989 paper by Dalal, Fowlkes and Hoadley analysed exactly this data with logistic regression models: a model that turns temperature into a probability of damage. You'll fit it yourself below.

🔮 Predict first

Fit a logistic regression of “any O-ring damage” on launch temperature, using the 23 flights. What probability of damage does it give at 53°F, the coldest flight in the data?

94%. The fitted curve crosses 50% at about 65°F and keeps climbing as it gets colder: at 53°F it's 0.94, and extrapolated to 36°F it's 0.999. (Extrapolating 17°F beyond the coldest data point is risky for any model, which is itself the lesson: the data said “we've never been here, and the trend points the wrong way”.) Numbers first: why not just use linear regression?

Code the outcome as 1 (damage) or 0 (no damage) and fit an ordinary straight line to the 23 flights. It does find the trend, but read off its predictions:

Launch temperatureStraight line (least squares on 0/1)Logistic regression

36°F (Challenger)1.56 (a 156% chance?)0.999

A straight line runs off to ±infinity, so it can't be a probability. It also lets extreme, confident points drag the line (Lesson 2.1's outlier problem), which moves the boundary in the middle. Logistic regression keeps the line but feeds it through an S-shaped curve that always stays between 0 and 1.

💡 Big idea Compute a score z = w·x + b exactly as in linear regression, and read it as log-odds. The sigmoid σ(z) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z) turns it into a probability. Training minimizes log-loss (cross-entropy), not squared error. Points where z = 0 (p = 0.5) form a straight-line boundary; the decision you act on comes from a threshold on p that you choose for your costs.
🧠 Analogy: a dimmer switch, not a light switch

Linear regression is a ruler: it can point anywhere, off the end of the scale. Logistic regression is a dimmer: the score z turns the knob, and the light (the probability) goes smoothly from fully off to fully on but never past either end. Turning the knob one notch always multiplies the odds by the same factor, whether the light was dim or nearly full.

And a threshold is the decision “at what brightness do I call the room lit?”. Picky about false alarms? Demand more light. Afraid to miss anything? Call it lit at a glimmer.

🤔 Why this? Why logistic regression?
  1. The problem before. We want a probability (“how likely is fraud?”), not just a yes/no, and we want it from a simple weighted sum of features. Linear regression on 0/1 labels gives “probabilities” below 0 and above 1 (−0.12 and 1.56 above) and is pulled around by easy, far-away points.
  2. What people tried. Linear regression with clipping to [0, 1]; linear discriminant analysis (Fisher, 1936), which assumes each class is a bell-shaped cloud with the same spread; probit models with the normal curve's S shape. All useful, all with stronger assumptions or awkward maths.
  3. Why this works. Modelling log-odds as linear means each feature multiplies the odds by a fixed factor. In the Challenger fit, the weight on temperature is −0.232, so each 1°F colder multiplies the odds of damage by e0.232 = 1.26, and 10°F colder by e2.32 ≈ 10. Log-loss is convex, so training always finds the one best answer, and the outputs are reasonably calibrated probabilities.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. The boundary is a straight line (a flat plane in many dimensions): it can't separate classes that need a curve, like XOR, without engineered features (Lesson 2.5). When classes are perfectly separable, the unpenalized weights grow without limit, so a penalty (C) is needed. Features should be scaled for the penalty and the solver.
  5. What came next. Softmax regression handles many classes (Lesson 2.4). Feature crosses and splines bend the boundary (2.5). SVMs use a margin-based loss (3.3). Gradient-boosted trees often beat it on raw tabular data (4.3), and every neural network classifier still ends in a logistic or softmax layer.

Three ways to say the same thing. The model works in log-odds (where adding is natural), you report probabilities (what people understand), and odds are the bridge.

✏️ Worked example: reading the Challenger weights by hand

The fitted model is z = 15.04 − 0.232 × temperature (°F).

  • At 70°F: z = 15.04 − 16.24 = −1.20 → odds e−1.20 = 0.30 → p = 0.30 ÷ 1.30 = 0.23.
  • At 53°F: z = 15.04 − 12.30 = 2.74 → odds e2.74 = 15.5 → p = 15.5 ÷ 16.5 = 0.94.
  • Where is p = 0.5? Where z = 0: temperature = 15.04 ÷ 0.232 ≈ 65°F. That's the decision boundary at threshold 0.5.
  • Odds ratio: e−0.232 = 0.79 per 1°F warmer, so the odds of damage fall by about 21% per degree, and multiply by about 10 for every 10°F colder.

Step 2: how it learns (log-loss)

Squared error is the wrong ruler for probabilities. Logistic regression is trained to make the true outcomes likely: for each example it pays −log(probability it gave to what actually happened). Say 0.9 for a damaged flight: cost 0.11. Say 0.1: cost 2.3. Say 0.001: cost 6.9. Confident and wrong is punished hard. The average of these costs is the log-loss (also called cross-entropy); its gradient for each example is simply (p − y)·x, the derivation is in Math 8.1 and the information view in Math 6.3.

Step 3: a straight boundary, and the threshold you choose

With two features, the points where p = 0.5 satisfy w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0: a straight line. Every other probability level is a parallel line. Picking a threshold other than 0.5 just slides the decision line, trading one kind of mistake for the other.

🔮 Predict first

A fraud model flags transactions with p ≥ 0.5. You lower the threshold to 0.3. What happens to precision and recall?

A lower bar flags more transactions: you catch more of the real frauds (recall up, here 0.85 → 0.95) but more of your flags are false alarms (precision down, 0.89 → 0.76). The model didn't change at all, only your decision rule. Slide the threshold below to see the gold line move.

🔮 Predict first

In Playground 3, set C = 0.01 (a very strong penalty) and leave the threshold at 0.5. How many positives does the model flag?

None. The penalty shrinks the weights to about 0.2, so every probability drifts toward the base rate (25% positives) and nothing reaches 0.5: recall 0. Yet the ranking is still good: at threshold 0.3 the same model gets recall 0.90 with precision 0.82. Lesson: a too-strong penalty or a badly chosen threshold can hide a useful model; tune C by cross-validation and choose the threshold separately (Lesson 5.4).

Three practical notes, all visible in the playground:

  • Coefficients as odds ratios. ew is the factor by which the odds multiply for +1 unit of that feature, holding the others fixed (the same caution as Lesson 2.1: conditional, not causal). Standardize first and “+1 unit” means “+1 standard deviation”, which makes features comparable.
  • C = 1/λ. In scikit-learn, C multiplies the data term instead of dividing the penalty, so smaller C = stronger regularization. The default is C = 1.0 with an L2 penalty; since scikit-learn 1.8, choose L1 or elastic net with l1_ratio (the old penalty= argument is deprecated) and use C=np.inf for no penalty.
  • class_weight="balanced" makes each class count equally in the loss (weights n ÷ (2 × class count): here 0.67 for negatives, 2.0 for positives). It pushes the boundary toward the majority class, much like lowering the threshold. Lesson 5.5 compares it with resampling and threshold moving.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): LogisticRegression (default solver lbfgs, C = 1.0, L2), LogisticRegressionCV to tune C, TunedThresholdClassifierCV (since 1.5) to tune the threshold by cross-validation, and SGDClassifier(loss="log_loss") for huge or streaming data. statsmodels' Logit gives standard errors and p-values.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import precision_score, recall_score
from sklearn.model_selection import TunedThresholdClassifierCV, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
y = 1 - y                                  # make 1 = malignant (the class we must not miss)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=1000)).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
p = clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]          # P(malignant)
for thr in (0.5, 0.2):
    pred = (p >= thr).astype(int)
    print(f"threshold {thr}: precision {precision_score(y_te, pred):.3f}  recall {recall_score(y_te, pred):.3f}")
# threshold 0.5: precision 1.000  recall 0.962
# threshold 0.2: precision 0.852  recall 0.981

lr = clf[-1]
odds = sorted(zip(X.columns, np.exp(lr.coef_[0])), key=lambda t: -abs(np.log(t[1])))[:3]
for name, o in odds:
    print(f"{name:22s} odds × {o:.2f} per +1 standard deviation")   # radius error × 3.46, …

tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(clf, scoring="balanced_accuracy", cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"CV-tuned threshold {tuned.best_threshold_:.2f}")   # 0.57 for this metric; pick the metric that matches your costs

predict() always uses 0.5; for any other threshold use predict_proba (or TunedThresholdClassifierCV). Breast-cancer features are strongly correlated, so individual odds ratios are less trustworthy than the predictions: the Lesson 2.1 caveat again.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

For any yes/no question (churn, fraud, conversion), a scaled logistic regression is the baseline: it trains in a second, the odds ratios are a free sanity check on your features, and the probabilities let product people pick a threshold from costs (“a missed fraud costs $200, a false alarm $5”).

At scale

Logistic regression on hashed and crossed sparse features, trained online, has powered ad click prediction at Google and, on top of tree-derived features, at Facebook. It's cheap to serve billions of times a day, easy to update continuously, and its outputs combine cleanly into auctions and rankings.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why is it called logistic regression if it's a classifier, and how does it work?” A strong answer:

  • It's a regression on the log-odds: z = w·x + b; the sigmoid maps z to a probability; classification is a threshold on that probability.
  • It's trained by minimizing log-loss (maximum likelihood), which is convex; gradient (p − y)·x; no closed form, so iterative solvers.
  • Coefficients are log odds ratios: ew multiplies the odds per unit of the feature, other features fixed.
  • The boundary is linear; use feature crosses or splines for curves; regularize (C = 1/λ), especially with separable or high-dimensional data.
  • The threshold is a business decision: tune it for precision/recall or costs, separately from training.
🏭 In the real world
Pre-Challenger risk analysis 📜 History · 1989 UCI: Challenger USA Space Shuttle O-Ring data (cites Dalal, Fowlkes & Hoadley, JASA 1989)

How: logistic-type models of O-ring damage against launch temperature (and leak-check pressure) over the 23 earlier flights, the data you fitted in Playground 2.

Why this method: the outcome is yes/no and the question is “how does the risk change with temperature?”, which is exactly an odds ratio; the dataset's notes stress that no previous launch was below 53°F, so any prediction at 31–36°F is a large extrapolation.

Ad click prediction at Google 🏢 Google · 2013 Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches (PDF)

How: regularized logistic regression trained online (FTRL-Proximal with L1 and L2) on billions of sparse features, making predictions billions of times per day.

Why this method: simple, fast and naturally probabilistic, which an ad auction needs; sparse weights keep the models small enough to replicate.

Trees + logistic regression at Facebook 🏢 Facebook · 2014 Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook

How: boosted decision trees turn raw features into leaf indicators, and a logistic regression on top makes the final click prediction.

Why this method: the trees learn interactions a linear model can't, the logistic layer stays cheap to retrain often; the combination beat either alone by over 3%.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: LogisticRegression

How: six solvers (lbfgs by default), L2 / L1 / elastic-net penalties via C and l1_ratio, class weights, multinomial for many classes (Lesson 2.4).

Why this method: the docs' solver table tells you which solver supports which penalty; for large sparse data, saga or SGDClassifier.

🤯 Fun fact · born counting people

The S-shaped logistic curve was invented by Pierre-François Verhulst in 1838 to model population growth that speeds up and then levels off as resources run out; he named it “logistique” in a paper presented in 1844. A century later, in 1944, Joseph Berkson coined the word logit for its inverse, the log-odds, and popularized logistic regression as a general statistical model.

Check yourself

1. A churn model has weight 0.7 on “support tickets last month” (not scaled). A customer files 2 more tickets than another, everything else equal. By what factor do their churn odds differ?
Each ticket multiplies the odds by e0.7 ≈ 2.01, so two tickets multiply them by e1.4 ≈ 4.06. Odds multiply, log-odds add. (The probability itself changes by less than 4×, depending on where it started.)
2. A hospital's sepsis model flags patients with p ≥ 0.5 and misses too many real cases. The model itself is well validated. What's the first thing to change?
The threshold is a decision rule, separate from the model. Missing sepsis is far costlier than an extra check, so trade precision for recall. TunedThresholdClassifierCV or a precision–recall curve (Lesson 5.4) helps choose it.
3. With LogisticRegression(C=0.001) every predicted probability is between 0.2 and 0.3 and predict() never says “yes”. What's going on?
In scikit-learn C = 1/λ: small C, strong penalty (Playground 3 at C = 0.01). The model may still rank cases well, but it can't express confidence. Tune C by cross-validation, then pick the threshold.
📝 TL;DR
  • Logistic regression = a linear score read as log-odds, squashed by a sigmoid into a probability, trained with log-loss.
  • ew is an odds ratio: the factor the odds multiply by per unit of the feature (Challenger: ×10 per 10°F colder).
  • The boundary is a straight line; the threshold slides it, trading precision for recall. Choose it from your costs.
  • In scikit-learn, C = 1/λ (small C = strong penalty); scale features; class_weight="balanced" for rare classes. Next: many classes and counts (2.4).
