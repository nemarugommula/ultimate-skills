Phase 2 · Lesson 4

Multiclass & beyond: softmax, one-vs-rest, Poisson

Logistic regression answers yes or no. Real questions have three, ten or a thousand answers, or they're counts: claims per year, visits per hour. Two small changes to the same linear model, a different output function and a matching loss, cover all of them. That family is called generalized linear models.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Logistic regression: a linear score z = w·x + b, squashed by a sigmoid into a probability, trained with log-loss; ew is an odds ratio (Lesson 2.3).
  • exp and log: ez is always positive; log turns multiplying into adding (Math 0.3).
  • A count (0, 1, 2, …) of rare events in a fixed window is often modelled by a Poisson distribution, whose mean equals its variance (Math 4.3).
🎯 Hook: three irises and a straight line

In 1936 Ronald Fisher published measurements of 150 iris flowers, 50 from each of three species (setosa, versicolor, virginica): sepal and petal lengths and widths. It's still the most famous dataset in machine learning, and it poses a simple puzzle.

A logistic regression draws one straight line and says “this side or that side”. With three species there is no single line that does the job. So what do you do: train three yes/no models and let them argue, or build one model that hands out three probabilities at once? On iris, the two answers score 92.7% and 96.0% in 5-fold cross-validation. Which is which, and why? Then, in the second half: what to do when the target isn't a class at all, but a count.

🔮 Predict first

Three classes sit in a row: left, middle, right. You train three yes/no classifiers, one per class (“is it this class, or any other?”). Which one struggles?

The middle one. “Middle vs the rest” means separating one cloud from clouds on both sides, which no single straight line can do. Its best effort is a nearly flat probability: at the very centre of its own class it says only 0.33. In Playground 1, lined-up blobs drop the one-vs-rest accuracy on the middle class to 66%, while softmax keeps 86%.

💡 Big idea A generalized linear model (GLM) = a linear score z = w·x + b + an output function that maps z into the right range (the inverse link) + a loss that matches the kind of target. Sigmoid for one probability, softmax for K probabilities that sum to 1, exp for a positive rate of counts. Same weights, same penalties, same solvers; only the last step changes.
🧠 Analogy: a vote count vs a jury

One-vs-rest is three separate judges, each asked only “is it mine?”. They never talk, so their answers can contradict each other: two can say yes, or all three can say no, and you have to referee afterwards. Softmax is a jury that must split exactly 100 votes among the three options. Giving more to one option automatically means less for the others, so the answers are always consistent.

🤔 Why this? Why softmax and GLMs?
  1. The problem before. Logistic regression only does yes/no. And plain linear regression on counts predicts nonsense: in Playground 2 it gives negative expected claims to 63 of 300 low-mileage drivers, and it assumes every driver's count is equally noisy.
  2. What people tried. For classes, one-vs-rest: easy, but K unrelated models whose “probabilities” don't add up and that fail on in-between classes. For counts, regressing log(y + 1) with least squares: the +1 is arbitrary, the zeros (often most of the data) get distorted, and converting predictions back underestimates the mean.
  3. Why this works. Keep the linear score, change only the output. Softmax turns scores (2, 1, 0) into e², e¹, e⁰ = 7.39, 2.72, 1.00, divided by their sum 11.1: probabilities 0.67, 0.24, 0.09. Poisson regression predicts the mean count as ez, always positive, and each feature multiplies it: a weight of 0.0817 per thousand km means every extra 10,000 km multiplies expected claims by e0.817 = 2.26. Each loss is the negative log-likelihood of its distribution, so training is convex.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Effects are still linear in z: straight boundaries between classes, and purely multiplicative effects for counts. Poisson assumes the variance equals the mean; real counts are often more spread out (“overdispersed”), which makes standard errors too optimistic. Softmax needs K sets of weights; with thousands of classes that's a lot of parameters.
  5. What came next. Gamma and Tweedie GLMs for positive amounts and for amounts with many exact zeros; negative binomial models for overdispersed counts (statsmodels). Gradient-boosted trees with a Poisson loss (Lesson 4.3). And softmax became the output layer of every neural network classifier (Lesson 2.5).

Step 1: softmax

P(class k) = ezk ⁄ (ez1 + ez2 + … + ezK) “Make every score positive with exp, then share 100% in proportion.” With K = 2 it is exactly the sigmoid of the difference z₁ − z₂.

Why exp, why it's numerically tricky, and how its gradient comes out as (p − y) again: Math 8.2: softmax.

✏️ Worked example: softmax by hand, and what OvR would say

A flower gets scores z = (2.0, 1.0, −1.0) for (setosa, versicolor, virginica).

  • exp: e2 = 7.39, e1 = 2.72, e−1 = 0.37. Sum = 10.48.
  • Softmax: 7.39 ÷ 10.48 = 0.705, 2.72 ÷ 10.48 = 0.259, 0.37 ÷ 10.48 = 0.035. They sum to 1.
  • Add 5 to every score: (7, 6, 4) gives exactly the same probabilities. Only differences between scores matter, which is why one class's weights can be fixed at zero.
  • OvR instead runs each score through its own sigmoid: σ(2) = 0.88, σ(1) = 0.73, σ(−1) = 0.27. They sum to 1.88, so two classifiers both say “mine!”; scikit-learn divides by the sum to get 0.47, 0.39, 0.14, a much less decisive answer from the same scores.

Step 2: when the target is a count

In 1898 Ladislaus Bortkiewicz showed that the number of Prussian soldiers killed by horse kicks, per army corps per year, followed a Poisson distribution: rare, independent events counted in a fixed window. Insurance claims per policy-year, support tickets per day, visits per hour and defects per batch behave the same way. Two facts about counts break linear regression:

  • They can't be negative, and they're mostly 0 or small. In scikit-learn's French motor insurance example, the average is about 0.10 claims per policy-year and 93.9% of the exposure has no claim at all.
  • Their noise grows with their mean: a driver expected to have 2 claims varies much more than one expected to have 0.05.

Poisson regression fixes both: predict the mean as ez (the log link: log(mean) is linear), and train with the Poisson loss, which expects bigger errors where the mean is bigger.

expected count = ew·x + b  ⇔  log(expected count) = w·x + b “Features add on the log scale, so they multiply the expected count. Each weight is a rate ratio: ew per unit.”
🔮 Predict first

300 drivers; expected claims grow with distance driven. You fit an ordinary straight line to claims vs thousands of km. What does it predict for a driver who covers only 2,000 km a year?

About −0.25 claims a year: the straight line crosses zero at about 10,800 km, so 63 of the 300 drivers get negative predictions. The Poisson model predicts 0.035, always positive, and bends upward like the real rate. See it below.

Two practical details for real count data. Exposure: a policy held for 3 months has less time to produce claims than one held all year. In scikit-learn you model the frequency (claims ÷ exposure) and pass the exposure as sample_weight; statsmodels lets you add log(exposure) as an offset. Amounts: claim costs are positive and skewed (use a Gamma GLM with a log link), and cost per policy is mostly exact zeros plus a skewed positive part (use Tweedie with a power between 1 and 2).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): LogisticRegression is multinomial (softmax) for 3+ classes with every solver except liblinear; the old multi_class argument is gone, so wrap with OneVsRestClassifier if you want OvR. GLMs: PoissonRegressor, GammaRegressor, TweedieRegressor. statsmodels adds negative binomial models, offsets and standard errors. Boosted trees accept loss="poisson" too (Lesson 4.3).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, PoissonRegressor, TweedieRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.multiclass import OneVsRestClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

# --- many classes: softmax (the default for 3+ classes) vs one-vs-rest
X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
softmax = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
ovr = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), OneVsRestClassifier(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)))
for name, m in [("softmax", softmax), ("one-vs-rest", ovr)]:
    print(f"{name:12s} CV accuracy {cross_val_score(m, X, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")   # 0.960 vs 0.927
print(softmax.fit(X, y).predict_proba(X[:1]).round(3))      # [[0.985 0.015 0.   ]]: sums to 1

# --- counts: Poisson regression with a log link
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
km = rng.uniform(2, 50, size=(2000, 1))                     # thousand km driven per year
claims = rng.poisson(np.exp(-3.2 + 0.07 * km[:, 0]))        # true rate ratio e^0.7 ≈ 2.0 per 10k km
pois = PoissonRegressor(alpha=0).fit(km, claims)            # alpha=0: no penalty (the default is alpha=1.0!)
print(f"rate ratio per 10k km: {np.exp(10 * pois.coef_[0]):.2f}")          # 1.99
print(f"predicted claims/year at 2k and 50k km: {pois.predict([[2], [50]]).round(3)}")   # [0.05 1.365]

# --- claim amounts with many exact zeros: Tweedie, 1 < power < 2
tweedie = TweedieRegressor(power=1.5, link="log", alpha=0).fit(km, claims * rng.gamma(2.0, 1500, size=2000))
print(f"Tweedie: expected cost at 50k km ≈ {tweedie.predict([[50]])[0]:.0f}")   # ≈ 4178 (≈ 1.37 claims × 3,000 average)

Watch the default: PoissonRegressor, GammaRegressor and TweedieRegressor all have alpha=1.0, an L2 penalty that is on by default, unlike LinearRegression. Scale features or set alpha deliberately. Score count models with mean_poisson_deviance, not MSE alone.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Support ticket routing into 8 queues? Softmax logistic regression on TF-IDF text features (Lesson 7.5) is a strong, explainable first model. Forecasting daily orders or signups? Start with a Poisson GLM before reaching for anything fancier.

At scale

Insurers price policies with GLMs because each factor is a readable multiplier (“young drivers × 1.8”) that actuaries and regulators can review; gradient boosting is often used alongside to find missing interactions. Very large classifiers (thousands of classes) use softmax output layers, often with sampled or hierarchical variants to keep training fast.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you model the number of claims a customer files next year?” A strong answer:

  • Rejects plain least squares: negative predictions, mostly zeros, variance grows with the mean.
  • Proposes a Poisson GLM with a log link: log(expected count) = w·x + b; coefficients are rate ratios ew.
  • Handles exposure (frequency with exposure as sample weight, or a log-exposure offset) and checks overdispersion (negative binomial if variance ≫ mean).
  • Evaluates with Poisson deviance on a time-based holdout, and compares against gradient boosting with a Poisson loss.
  • For many classes: softmax vs one-vs-rest, and why softmax's probabilities are consistent.

How: frequency (Poisson), severity (Gamma) and pure-premium (Tweedie) GLMs with log links, so each rating factor becomes a multiplier.

Why this method: the monograph notes GLMs are widely used in personal lines insurance, especially at meaningful scale; the log link turns additive terms into the multiplicative factors that rating tables are made of.

One framework for all of it 📜 History · 1972 Generalized linear model (Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972)

How: John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn unified linear, logistic and Poisson regression as one model: linear predictor + link function + a distribution from the exponential family, fitted by one algorithm (iteratively reweighted least squares).

Why this method: one theory, one fitting routine and one set of diagnostics for many kinds of targets, the reason R's glm() and scikit-learn's GLM classes look alike.

🤯 Fun fact · death by horse kick

Bortkiewicz's 1898 book on Poisson counts was called The Law of Small Numbers, and its most famous table counted soldiers killed by horse kicks in Prussian cavalry corps, year by year. Rare, random, independent events in fixed windows: the same maths now prices your car insurance and sizes your servers for requests per second.

Check yourself

1. A 3-class model outputs scores z = (1, 1, 0). What softmax probabilities does it give?
e¹ = 2.72, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1; sum 6.44 → 2.72/6.44 = 0.42 twice and 1/6.44 = 0.16. A score of 0 still gets some probability; only differences between scores matter.
2. A Poisson model of website visits per hour has weight 0.4 on “promo running” (0 or 1). With no promo it predicts 50 visits. What does it predict during a promo?
Log link: the promo multiplies the expected count by e0.4 = 1.49, so 50 × 1.49 ≈ 75. Weights in a log-link GLM are rate ratios, not additive amounts.
3. You're predicting the yearly cost per car-insurance policy: most policies cost 0, a few cost thousands. Which starting model fits the target best?
Tweedie with power between 1 and 2 is a compound Poisson–Gamma: exact zeros plus a skewed positive part, predicting the expected cost. Least squares can go negative and is dominated by the big claims; logistic only predicts whether there's a claim, not how much.
📝 TL;DR
  • Many classes: one-vs-rest trains K yes/no models (inconsistent, fails on in-between classes); softmax shares 100% among K scores in one model and is scikit-learn's default.
  • A GLM = linear score + an output function (identity, sigmoid, softmax, exp) + the matching loss. Same weights, penalties and solvers.
  • Counts → Poisson with a log link: never negative, weights are rate ratios ew; handle exposure; Gamma for amounts, Tweedie for amounts with many zeros.
  • scikit-learn's GLMs default to alpha=1.0. Next: bend the straight line with feature engineering, and meet the neural network (2.5).
