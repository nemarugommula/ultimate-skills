Phase 2 · Lesson 1

Linear regression: least squares, intuitively

Draw the straight line that best fits a cloud of points. It's the oldest trick in machine learning (1805), still the first model you should try for a number, and the building block of logistic regression, GLMs and every neural network's last layer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A model is a function from features x to a prediction ŷ; training picks its parameters to make the error small (Lesson 0.1).
  • Train / test split: score a model on rows it never saw (Lesson 0.6).
  • A straight line is ŷ = w·x + b: w is the slope (“rise per step”), b is where it crosses x = 0 (Math 0.2).
🎯 Hook: the planet that got lost behind the Sun

On 1 January 1801 the astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted a new object, Ceres. He observed it 24 times until 11 February; then it slid into the glare of the Sun and was lost. Astronomers had a few weeks of slightly wrong measurements and needed to predict where it would reappear months later.

A 24-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss computed an orbit by a method that let every measurement have its say while no single one dominated. On 31 December 1801, Franz Xaver von Zach found Ceres almost exactly where Gauss said it would be. The method was published in 1805 by Adrien-Marie Legendre under the name it still has: least squares.

Today the same idea fits house prices, ad budgets and sales forecasts. Why squares, and not something simpler? And what does the fitted line actually tell you? That's this lesson.

🔮 Predict first

In 1886 Francis Galton studied about 930 adult children and their 205 pairs of parents. Take parents whose average height is 3 inches above the population average. On average, how tall were their grown children?

About 2 inches. Galton found the children's deviation was about two-thirds of the parents' deviation: tall parents have tall children, but less extremely tall. He called it “regression towards mediocrity”, and that's where the word regression comes from. The fitted line has slope ≈ 2/3, not 1; a slope below 1 is exactly what “regression to the mean” looks like.

Numbers first: what “best line” means

Here are the 10 points you'll play with below (think: x = hundreds of square metres, y = price). Each candidate line makes an error on each point, called its residual: actual minus predicted. Compare three lines by adding up the squared residuals (SSE, the sum of squared errors):

“Best” always means best according to a loss. Least squares picks the line whose total squared error is smallest. Because the squares are areas, you can literally see it: draw a square on every residual; the least-squares line is the line with the smallest total red area.

💡 Big idea Linear regression predicts ŷ = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b: a weighted sum of the features. Training picks the weights that minimize the mean of the squared residuals (MSE, mean squared error). Squares make that minimum easy to find (one formula, no search), but they also let a single far-off point pull the whole line.
🧠 Analogy: a rod held by springs

Put a stiff rod among the points and tie every point to the rod with a vertical spring. A stretched spring pulls harder the more it's stretched, and its stored energy grows with the square of the stretch. Let go: the rod settles where the total spring energy is smallest. That resting place is the least-squares line.

It also explains the weakness: a point far from the rod has a very stretched spring, pulling with huge force. One wild point can tilt the whole rod. Keep that picture for Playground 2.

🤔 Why this? Why least squares?
  1. The problem before. Astronomers and surveyors had more measurements than unknowns, and the measurements disagreed slightly. Any two points give a line; a third point is off it. Which line do you believe when you have 24 slightly wrong observations?
  2. What people tried. Tobias Mayer (1750) averaged groups of equations together, which depends on how you group them. Roger Boscovich (1757) minimized the sum of absolute deviations for the shape of the Earth: sensible and robust, but with the tools of the time it was hard to compute, with no neat formula.
  3. Why this works. Squared errors make the total a smooth bowl with one bottom, and the bottom has a closed formula: slope = covariance(x, y) ÷ variance(x). For the 4 points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 6) that's 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4, intercept 4 − 1.4 × 2.5 = 0.5, and the line explains 98% of the spread. Gauss also showed it's the most likely line if the errors follow a bell curve (Math 5.3).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Squaring makes a residual of 10 count as much as a hundred residuals of 1, so outliers pull hard (Playground 2). It only fits straight-line relationships in the features you give it. When features are strongly correlated, the individual weights become unstable and hard to interpret (Playground 4).
  5. What came next. Regularization (ridge, lasso) tames unstable weights (Lesson 2.2). Robust losses (absolute error, Huber) resist outliers. Logistic regression and GLMs keep the weighted sum but change what it predicts: probabilities and counts (Lessons 2.3, 2.4). Feature expansion fits curves with the same machinery (Lesson 2.5).

✏️ Worked example: the least-squares line for 4 points, by hand

Points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 6). Means: x̄ = 2.5, ȳ = 4.

  • Deviations from the means: x − x̄ = −1.5, −0.5, 0.5, 1.5 and y − ȳ = −2, −1, 1, 2.
  • Slope w = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ(x − x̄)² = (3 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 3) ÷ (2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25) = 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4.
  • Intercept b = ȳ − w·x̄ = 4 − 1.4 × 2.5 = 0.5. The line always passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  • Predictions 1.9, 3.3, 4.7, 6.1 → residuals 0.1, −0.3, 0.3, −0.1 → SSE = 0.01 + 0.09 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.2.
  • Compare with the flat line ŷ = ȳ = 4: total squared spread SST = 4 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 10. So R² = 1 − 0.2 ÷ 10 = 0.98.

R²: how much better than guessing the average?

A raw SSE of 5.38 means nothing alone: is that good? R² (“R squared”, the coefficient of determination) compares your model with the laziest possible one, always predicting the average:

R² = 1 − SSEmodel ⁄ SSTpredict the mean “One minus the share of the spread that your line still gets wrong.”
R²Meaning
1perfect fit: every residual is 0 (on test data, suspicious: check for leakage, Lesson 0.6)
0.84Playground 1's line: it removes 84% of the squared spread (1 − 5.38 ÷ 33.59)
0no better than predicting the average for everyone
below 0worse than the average: possible on test data when the model is badly wrong

What counts as a “good” R² depends on the field: physics experiments often reach 0.99; models of human behaviour are often useful with much lower values. Always compare against a baseline on the same data. Lesson 5.2 compares R² with MAE, RMSE and MAPE.

Step 2: one outlier, two losses

Squares punish big misses a lot. A residual of 3 costs 9; a residual of 6 costs 36. So least squares will bend the whole line to shrink one huge residual. The alternative is to minimize the absolute residuals (MAE, mean absolute error; Boscovich's 1757 idea): a miss of 6 costs only twice a miss of 3.

🔮 Predict first

15 points lie neatly along a line with slope about 0.62. One data-entry slip moves a single point from y = 6.0 down to y = 0.5. What happens to the two fitted slopes?

The MSE (least-squares) slope falls from 0.62 to 0.28, less than half. The MAE slope stays at 0.64. Squares give that one point enormous pull; absolute values give every point the same pull, however far away. Do it yourself below.

So why not always use absolute error? It has no closed formula (it needs an iterative solver), its minimum can be a whole range of lines rather than one, and it predicts the median rather than the mean, which isn't what you want if, say, total revenue matters. In practice: clean obvious errors first (Lesson 1.2), and if heavy tails remain, use a compromise such as Huber loss (squared for small residuals, absolute for large ones; scikit-learn's HuberRegressor).

Step 3: two ways to find the bottom of the bowl

The MSE, viewed as a function of the two parameters (w, b), is a smooth bowl. There are two ways to reach its lowest point:

w = (Xᵀ X)⁻¹ Xᵀ y “Multiply the features by themselves, multiply them by the targets, and divide the second by the first.” The 1-feature version is exactly covariance ÷ variance.

The derivation (and why it's the same as setting the gradient to zero) is in Math 8.1: regression; gradient descent itself is Math 3.4.

🔮 Predict first

On Playground 1's data, gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.02 gets within 0.001 of the best MSE in about 310 steps. You're impatient and double it to 0.04. What happens?

It diverges. The bowl is long and narrow because x isn't centred (its average is about 5), so steps bigger than about 0.030 overshoot the steep walls further each time. Tick “center x first” below: the bowl becomes round and learning rates up to about 0.15 work, and 0.1 reaches the bottom in about 23 steps. That's one reason we scale features (Lesson 1.4).

Step 4: reading the weights (and the trap)

With several features, each weight is an effect per unit: “holding all the other features fixed, one more unit of this feature changes the prediction by w.” That “holding the others fixed” is doing a lot of work. Watch a weight change sign depending on what else is in the model.

🔮 Predict first

200 houses. With bedrooms as the only feature, each extra bedroom adds about +33k to the predicted price. Now you add floor area to the model. What happens to the bedroom weight?

It turns negative (about −11k). Big houses have more bedrooms and cost more; alone, “bedrooms” was standing in for size. With size held fixed, an extra bedroom means the same area cut into smaller rooms, and in this data that lowers the price. Neither number is “wrong”: they answer different questions. Try it below.

Three rules for reading weights, worth memorizing:

  • A weight is conditional on the other features in the model. Add or drop one and the others can change, even flip sign. This is confounding when a left-out variable drives both a feature and the target.
  • A weight is not a cause. “Holding the others fixed” is about the data you have, not what happens if you intervene. To know what a change does, you need an experiment or causal methods (Lesson 8.4).
  • Units matter. Weights are per unit of each feature, so you can't compare their sizes unless the features are on the same scale. Standardize first (Lesson 1.4) if you want “which feature matters more”, and see Lesson 8.1 for better importance tools. With correlated features (like the diabetes data's s1 and s2, correlation 0.90) individual weights also become unstable, which is what Lesson 2.2 fixes.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): LinearRegression (least squares), HuberRegressor (robust to outliers), SGDRegressor (gradient descent for huge data). statsmodels (0.15) adds the statistician's view: standard errors, confidence intervals and p-values for every weight. NumPy's np.linalg.lstsq solves least squares directly.

from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, HuberRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)   # 442 patients, 10 features, disease progression after 1 year
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

lr = LinearRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"test R² {lr.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}   intercept {lr.intercept_:.1f}")   # 0.359, 153.1
for name, w in sorted(zip(X.columns, lr.coef_), key=lambda t: -abs(t[1]))[:3]:
    print(f"{name:4s} {w:8.1f}")   # s5 703.2, bmi 593.4, s1 -560.2 (s1 and s2 correlate at 0.90: unstable)

huber = HuberRegressor(epsilon=1.35, max_iter=1000).fit(X_tr, y_tr)   # squared loss near the line, absolute far away
print(f"Huber test R² {huber.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")   # 0.344

# Need p-values and confidence intervals? statsmodels:
# import statsmodels.api as sm; print(sm.OLS(y_tr, sm.add_constant(X_tr)).fit().summary())

The diabetes features come pre-scaled (centred, all on the same scale), so their weights are comparable. On your own data, put a StandardScaler in a Pipeline first (Lesson 1.6). LinearRegression(positive=True) forces all weights ≥ 0 when that's physically required.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Fit a linear regression before anything fancy. It takes a second, the weights tell you whether the data makes sense (“why is the price weight negative?”), and it's the baseline any gradient-boosted model must clearly beat to earn its complexity.

At scale

Linear models stay everywhere for speed and stability: variance reduction in A/B tests, forecasting components, the final layer on top of learned embeddings, and models with millions of sparse features trained with stochastic gradient descent.

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain linear regression and its assumptions.” A strong answer:

  • Defines it: ŷ = weighted sum of features + intercept; weights chosen to minimize MSE; closed form via the normal equations or gradient descent.
  • Explains R² as 1 − SSE/SST: improvement over predicting the mean, and that it must be measured on held-out data.
  • Lists what can go wrong: non-linear relationships, outliers (squares pull hard), correlated features (unstable weights), and residuals whose spread changes with x (the fit is fine; the classic standard errors aren't).
  • Reads weights correctly: effect per unit holding the other features fixed, not causal, only comparable after scaling.
  • Knows the fixes: regularization (2.2), robust losses (Huber), feature expansion (2.5), and GLMs for non-normal targets (2.4).
🏭 In the real world
Sharper A/B tests with CUPED 🏢 Microsoft · 2013 Improving the Sensitivity of Online Controlled Experiments by Utilizing Pre-Experiment Data (PDF)

How: regress each user's metric during the experiment on the same metric before it, and compare the groups on what the regression can't explain.

Why this method: one linear regression removes the predictable part of user-to-user noise. On Bing's experiments it cut variance by about 50%: the same statistical power with half the users or half the duration.

Where the word “regression” comes from 📜 History · 1886 Galton: Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature (PDF)

How: collected heights from family records, converted women's heights to male equivalents (× 1.08), and compared children with their “mid-parent” height.

Why this method: the children's deviation was a constant fraction, about two-thirds, of the parents'. A straight line through the averages summarized hundreds of families in one number.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Linear Models

How: LinearRegression, Ridge, Lasso, HuberRegressor, SGDRegressor and the GLMs of Lesson 2.4, all with the same fit/predict API.

Why this method: they drop into Pipelines and cross-validation like any other model, so a linear baseline costs one line.

Statistical inference on the weights 🧰 Open source statsmodels: Linear Regression

How: sm.OLS(y, X).fit().summary() reports every weight with its standard error, confidence interval and p-value, plus diagnostics.

Why this method: when the question is “is this effect real, and how big?” (economics, medicine, product analytics) rather than “predict well”, you need uncertainty, not just a point estimate.

🤯 Fun fact · a regression computer made of pulleys

In the same 1886 paper, Galton wrote that the regression law is so simple that he built “an arrangement with pulleys and weights” to mechanically reckon the probable average height of the children of known parents. A physical linear-regression predictor, a century before scikit-learn.

Check yourself

1. A model's SSE on the test set is 30. Predicting the test-set average for everyone gives an SSE of 120. What's the R²?
R² = 1 − SSE/SST = 1 − 30/120 = 0.75: the model removes three quarters of the squared spread around the mean. 0.25 is the share it still gets wrong.
2. Your price model has a negative weight on “number of bathrooms”. A product manager concludes that adding a bathroom lowers a home's value. What's the best response?
Weights are conditional on the other features. If area is in the model, an extra bathroom at fixed area means less space elsewhere, and correlated features make individual weights unstable. Causal claims need experiments or causal methods (Lesson 8.4).
3. You fit sales against ad spend. One row has spend typed as 50,000 instead of 5,000, and its residual is huge. What does that typo do, and what's a sensible fix?
A residual 10× larger costs 100× more under squared error, so least squares bends the line toward it (Playground 2). Clean data errors first (Lesson 1.2); HuberRegressor or absolute loss limit the pull of genuine extreme values.
📝 TL;DR
  • Linear regression predicts a weighted sum of features; least squares picks the weights that minimize the total squared residual (the smallest total red area).
  • Closed form (normal equations) solves it in one go; gradient descent walks downhill and needs a learning rate and scaled features.
  • R² = 1 − SSE/SST: the improvement over always predicting the average, judged on held-out data.
  • Squares make outliers pull hard; absolute error or Huber loss resist them.
  • A weight is an effect per unit holding the other features fixed: conditional, not causal, and unstable when features correlate. Next: regularization (2.2).
