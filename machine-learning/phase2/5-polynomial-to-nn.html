Phase 2 · Lesson 5

Polynomial features & the step to neural networks

A linear model can only draw straight lines, in the features you give it. So give it better features: squares, products, splines. Do that by hand and the number of features explodes; let the model learn its own features and you've built a neural network.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Logistic regression draws a straight boundary w·x + b = 0 between classes (Lesson 2.3).
  • Regularization keeps many features from overfitting; scale features first (Lesson 2.2).
  • Feature engineering: new columns computed from old ones, like ratios or interactions (Lesson 1.5).
🎯 Hook: the function that stalled a field

XOR (“exclusive or”) is the simplest pattern there is: the answer is “yes” when exactly one of two switches is on. Put it on a plane and the yes-points sit in two opposite corners, the no-points in the other two. No straight line can separate them.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book Perceptrons made this precise: a single-layer perceptron (a linear classifier) can't compute XOR. It's often claimed that the book's pessimism pushed research away from neural networks for years. The fix came in two flavours, and both are this lesson: add the right feature by hand, or add a hidden layer that learns the feature itself, which took off after the 1986 backpropagation paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams.

🔮 Predict first

Logistic regression on raw XOR data scores about 48% on test points, a coin flip. You may add one extra feature. Which one rescues it?

The product x₁·x₂. In XOR, the class depends on whether x₁ and x₂ have the same sign, and x₁·x₂ is positive exactly when they do. With that one column, logistic regression jumps to 95% on test points; adding squares instead does nothing (45%). The model is still linear, in a richer set of features. Try it below.

A linear model means linear in its weights, not in the raw inputs. Feed it x₁·x₂ and its boundary in the original (x₁, x₂) plane becomes a curve: here, the two axes, which is exactly what XOR needs.

💡 Big idea Transform the features first (powers, products, splines), then fit an ordinary linear model on the new columns. The model stays simple and convex; the boundary or curve becomes flexible. The catch: the number of hand-made features explodes with degree, so you need regularization, and you need to know which features to make. A neural network removes that guesswork: its hidden layer learns the features, and its last layer is just a linear or logistic model on top.
🧠 Analogy: flattening a crumpled map

Two kinds of points are mixed together on a crumpled sheet of paper; no straight cut separates them. Smooth the sheet the right way and they fall onto two sides of a straight fold. Feature engineering is you choosing how to smooth the paper. A neural network learns how to smooth it, from the data, before making the straight cut.

🤔 Why this? Why expand the features?
  1. The problem before. Linear and logistic regression only capture straight-line effects and straight boundaries. Real effects bend (demand peaks at 8 a.m. and 6 p.m.) and interact (a discount matters more on weekends). On XOR, a linear model is a coin flip.
  2. What people tried. Switching to a completely different model (trees, k-NN) loses the linear model's speed, stability and readable weights. Binning a feature into ranges works but makes jumpy, step-shaped curves.
  3. Why this works. One new column can turn a curved problem into a straight one: for XOR, x₁·x₂ is positive exactly when the class is “same sign”, so a single weight on it separates the classes (95% test accuracy). Polynomials and splines do the same for curves: a weighted sum of a few smooth bumps can trace almost any shape, and fitting the weights is still least squares.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Feature counts explode: all products up to degree 3 of 100 features is 176,851 columns. High-degree polynomials swing wildly near the edges and explode outside the data (degree 15: a prediction of 358 where the truth is 0.1). You must know or guess which features to build; regularization and scaling become mandatory.
  5. What came next. Splines (local bumps instead of global powers) for smooth 1-D effects; kernels that use huge feature spaces without building them (Lesson 3.4); trees that find interactions automatically (Phase 4); and neural networks, whose hidden layers learn the features (Playground 4, and the sibling Deep Learning course).

Step 2: the explosion

scikit-learn's PolynomialFeatures(degree=k) builds every product of the inputs up to total degree k (x₁², x₁x₂, x₁²x₃, …). With d inputs that's C(d + k, k) columns including the constant. With interaction_only=True it skips powers of a single feature (no x₁²), which helps, but not much.

🔮 Predict first

You have 100 numeric features and ask for all polynomial features up to degree 3. Roughly how many columns come out?

176,851 (checked with scikit-learn's n_output_features_). Degree 2 would give 5,151. With a million rows of float64 that's about 1.4 TB for degree 3. That's why nobody does this blindly: pick a few meaningful interactions, use splines per feature, hash the crosses, or let a tree or a neural network find them.

Step 3: curves without the wild edges: splines

For one feature with a curved effect (temperature on demand, age on risk), polynomials are a poor tool: a single x⁹ term affects the curve everywhere, so fixing one region bends another, and the curve shoots off past the last data point. Splines use a row of overlapping local bumps (B-splines) placed at knots across the feature's range. Each weight only changes the curve near its own bump. scikit-learn's SplineTransformer builds them: with the default 5 knots and degree 3 you get 7 columns per feature.

Inside the data the two can score similarly (here a test MSE around 0.18–0.19 at the defaults, against a noise floor of 0.09), but the polynomial's behaviour at the edges and beyond is a liability: degree 9 predicts −31 at x = 11. The spline's default (extrapolation="constant") simply holds the edge value. For cyclic features like hour of day, SplineTransformer(extrapolation="periodic") makes 23:00 and 00:00 neighbours.

Step 4: let the model learn its own features

Look again at what we did: compute some new features h = (h₁, h₂, …) from x, then fit a logistic regression on h. A neural network with one hidden layer does exactly this, except each hidden feature is itself a small learned model, hj = tanh(wj·x + bj), and all the weights, hidden and output, are trained together by gradient descent (backpropagation).

p = σ( v₁·h₁ + … + vH·hH + c ),   hj = tanh(wj·x + bj) “A logistic regression whose inputs are H learned features, each a soft yes/no about which side of its own line a point is on.”

🔮 Predict first

A network with one hidden unit, then with two, trained on the XOR data. Which can solve it?

One hidden unit gives the output layer a single learned line to work with: still one cut, about 70% train accuracy here. Two lines are enough to fence off the two diagonal corners: about 90% on most random starts (one start in five gets stuck near 74%). With 3–4 units it's around 98% train and 92–95% test. Watch the dashed lines: those are the features the network invented.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): PolynomialFeatures (degree, interaction_only, include_bias), SplineTransformer (n_knots=5, degree=3, extrapolation), and small multi-layer perceptrons in sklearn.neural_network. Always inside a Pipeline with a scaler and a regularized model.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, RidgeCV
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, SplineTransformer, StandardScaler

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(400, 2))
y = (X[:, 0] * X[:, 1] > 0).astype(int)                     # XOR: same sign → class 1

linear = LogisticRegression()
crossed = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, interaction_only=True, include_bias=False),
                        StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression())   # adds x1·x2
mlp = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(8,), max_iter=2000, random_state=0))
for name, m in [("linear", linear), ("+ x1·x2", crossed), ("MLP, 8 hidden", mlp)]:
    print(f"{name:14s} CV accuracy {cross_val_score(m, X, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")   # 0.490, 0.967, 0.978

print(PolynomialFeatures(degree=3).fit(np.zeros((1, 100))).n_output_features_)   # 176851

x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, 60))[:, None]
t = np.sin(x[:, 0]) + 0.1 * x[:, 0] + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 60)
spline = make_pipeline(SplineTransformer(n_knots=8, degree=3), RidgeCV()).fit(x, t)
print(f"spline R² {spline.score(x, t):.3f}, features {spline[0].n_features_out_}")   # 0.920, 10 = 8 knots + 3 − 1

Put PolynomialFeatures before the scaler (products of scaled features are fine, but the new columns need scaling too), and pair it with Ridge/LogisticRegression's penalty. For sparse categorical crosses (“country × device”), build only the pairs you need, or hash them.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

A handful of meaningful interactions (“discount × weekend”) and splines on the 2–3 features with obviously curved effects, inside a ridge or logistic Pipeline, often closes most of the gap to a boosted model while staying explainable.

At scale

Recommenders and ad systems combine both worlds: a “wide” linear part on hashed feature crosses (memorizes specific combinations) next to a “deep” network on embeddings (generalizes), trained together, as in Google's Wide & Deep.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your logistic regression underfits. What do you try before switching models?” A strong answer:

  • Checks residuals or partial dependence for curvature and interactions, then adds targeted features: interactions, splines on curved features, log transforms.
  • Knows the cost: polynomial degree k on d features gives C(d + k, k) columns, so regularize (ridge/lasso), scale, and cross-validate.
  • Mentions splines' local, stable behaviour vs high-degree polynomials' edge wiggles and extrapolation.
  • Explains why XOR fails linearly and how x₁·x₂ or one hidden layer fixes it.
  • Frames a neural network as a linear/logistic model on learned features, trained end to end, and knows when that's worth it (lots of data, unstructured inputs).
🏭 In the real world
Wide & Deep for app recommendations 🏢 Google · 2016 Wide & Deep Learning for Recommender Systems (arXiv)

How: a wide linear model on cross-product feature transformations trained jointly with a deep network on learned embeddings, deployed on Google Play (over one billion active users).

Why this method: the crosses memorize specific feature combinations interpretably, while the network generalizes to unseen ones; online experiments showed more app acquisitions than wide-only or deep-only models.

Bike demand with splines 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Time-related feature engineering

How: predicts hourly bike-sharing demand with linear models on periodic spline features of the hour, weekday and month, and compares them with boosted trees.

Why this method: demand follows daily and weekly cycles; periodic splines let a linear model trace those curves (and wrap midnight around), with far fewer columns than one-hot hours plus interactions.

The XOR objection 📜 History · 1969 Perceptrons (Minsky & Papert)

How: proved what single-layer perceptrons can't compute, XOR and connectedness among them.

Why it matters: it marked the limit of linear models on raw inputs; the two escapes are exactly Playgrounds 1 and 4. The 1988 expanded edition responded to the 1980s revival of multilayer networks.

Learning the features 📜 History · 1986 Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams: Learning representations by back-propagating errors (Nature)

How: trained the hidden units of multi-layer networks by propagating the output error backwards with the chain rule.

Why it matters: the title says it: networks learn representations, the features a linear output layer needs, instead of being handed them.

🤯 Fun fact · one hidden layer can (in principle) do anything

In 1989 George Cybenko, and separately Hornik, Stinchcombe and White, proved the universal approximation theorem: a network with a single hidden layer of sigmoid-like units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region as closely as you like, given enough units. “Enough” can be astronomically many, which is one reason modern networks go deep instead of wide. Check yourself

1. You have 10 features and use PolynomialFeatures(degree=2) (with the bias column). How many columns come out?
C(10 + 2, 2) = 66: 1 constant + 10 originals + 10 squares + 45 pairwise products. At degree 3 it would be 286; with 100 features at degree 3, 176,851.
2. Ice-cream sales rise with temperature but drop on very hot days (people stay inside). Your linear model on temperature underfits. Best first fix?
One curved effect of one feature: a few spline columns (or temperature²) capture the rise-and-fall without wild edges. Degree 12 overfits and explodes past the data; a deep network is overkill for one curve.
3. A teammate says “a neural network is just logistic regression with extra steps”. What's the most accurate version of that?
The last layer is exactly a GLM; the difference is where its inputs come from. Learned features make the whole model non-linear and non-convex (Playground 4), which is both its power and its training difficulty.
📝 TL;DR
  • Linear models are linear in their weights; feed them transformed features (products, powers, splines) and boundaries and curves bend.
  • XOR: one feature, x₁·x₂, takes logistic regression from a coin flip to 95%.
  • Polynomial columns explode as C(d + k, k); high degrees wiggle at the edges and explode outside the data. Splines are local and stable.
  • A neural network = a logistic/linear output layer on hidden features learned by backpropagation. Phase 3 next: distance and probability models.
