Phase 9 · Lesson 1

ML system design: a framework for the whiteboard and for real projects

A model is one box in a system that also has goals, labels, features, tests, servers and alarms. This lesson gives you a ten-step framework that turns a fuzzy request (“use ML to stop fake reviews”) into a design you can defend with numbers, in an interview or on Monday morning.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The ML workflow: data → features → model → evaluation → deployment (Lesson 0.2), and when rules beat ML (0.4).
  • Leakage: information in training that won't exist at prediction time makes offline scores lie (Lesson 0.6); split by time or group when the future or new users are what you predict (5.1).
  • Metrics: precision, recall, AUC (area under the ROC curve), MAE (mean absolute error) measure different things (Lessons 5.2–5.4).
🎯 Hook: the million-dollar model that never shipped

In 2006 Netflix offered $1 million to anyone who could make its movie-rating predictions 10% more accurate. A year in, the leading team won a progress prize with an 8.43% improvement after more than 2,000 hours of work and a blend of 107 algorithms. Netflix took the two best pieces (matrix factorization at 0.8914 RMSE, root mean squared error, lower is better; a restricted Boltzmann machine at 0.8990; blended to 0.88), rebuilt them for 5 billion ratings instead of 100 million, and put them into production.

The final Grand Prize ensemble, which blended hundreds of models, was a different story. Netflix's engineers wrote in 2012 that its extra accuracy, measured offline, “did not seem to justify the engineering effort” needed to run it. Meanwhile the business had moved from mailing DVDs to streaming, which changed what “a good recommendation” even meant.

A better model, paid for in cash, lost to cost, maintenance and a moved goal. That is what ML system design is about.

🔮 Predict first

In Google's well-known diagram of a real-world ML system (Sculley et al., 2015), boxes are drawn roughly to scale: data collection, feature extraction, serving, monitoring, configuration… and the ML code itself. How big is the ML code box?

A small box in the middle. The caption says it plainly: “only a small fraction of real-world ML systems is composed of the ML code”. The rest, data pipelines, features, serving, monitoring and configuration, is where most of the work and most of the bugs live. The framework below walks through all of it.

None of these is about choosing between XGBoost and a random forest. They're about the goal, the data, the baseline, freshness and monitoring. Senior ML engineers spend most of their thinking there, and interviewers know it.

💡 Big idea Design backwards from the decision: what action will the prediction trigger, what does a mistake cost, and how fast must it happen? Those answers pick the metric, the labels, the features you're allowed to use, the model and how it's served. The model choice comes sixth, not first.
🧠 Analogy: an architect doesn't start with the door handle

Ask an architect to design a house and they'll ask who lives there, the budget, the plot, the climate, the local building rules. Only then do they sketch rooms, and the door handles come last. A junior designer who opens with “I'll use brass handles” has said something true and useless.

“I'd use gradient boosting” is the brass handle of ML design. It might be right, but nobody can tell until the goal, the labels and the latency budget are on the table.

🤔 Why this? Why a design framework instead of “pick a good model”?
  1. The problem before. Teams trained on whatever table was handy, optimized an offline metric, and discovered at launch that the labels were biased, a feature didn't exist at prediction time, or the model was too slow. Sculley et al. called the result hidden technical debt: glue code, pipeline jungles, feedback loops, and “Changing Anything Changes Everything”.
  2. What people tried. Competition-style workflows (one fixed dataset, one metric, maximize it) produced great offline scores and models nobody could run, like the Netflix ensemble. Ad-hoc checklists helped, but without an order people still debated models before agreeing what success meant.
  3. Why this works. Each step fixes constraints for the next, so mistakes surface while they're cheap. Tiny example (illustrative numbers): rules catch 60% of fake reviews for the 500 a moderator team can check per day; a model catches 75%. Knowing the baseline first tells you the model is worth 15 points of recall, so you can price it, and a 120 ms model is ruled out before anyone trains it if the budget is 100 ms.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A framework recited as a list is a ritual: in interviews and real projects it only works with numbers at every step (traffic, costs, label delay). At a five-person startup, all ten steps for a first model is overkill; do steps 1, 3, 5 and 9 well and keep the rest in your head.
  5. What came next. The same skeleton applied to three classic problems (fraud, churn, ads click-through) is Lesson 9.2; the offline half done properly is the Kaggle-style project of Lesson 9.3; the operations half (registries, feature stores, drift) is Lessons 8.5–8.6. For systems built on LLMs, the sibling AI Engineer 9.1 adapts it.

The framework: ten steps, in order

Use it out loud in an interview, or as the outline of a design doc at work. Every step ends with a number or a decision, not a vibe.

Clarify the goalWhat decision does the prediction drive? Who acts on it, how often, what does a wrong answer cost? Constraints: latency, scale, privacy, explainability, budget. Could rules do it (Lesson 0.4)?
Business metric vs ML metricThe business metric is what the company cares about (fraud losses, retained revenue, late deliveries). The ML metric is what you can compute offline (recall at a review budget, log loss, MAE). Say how one maps to the other, and pick guardrail metrics that must not get worse.
Data & labelsWhere do labels come from (user reports, chargebacks, moderators, future sales)? How delayed are they? How biased (you only see outcomes for cases you acted on)? How many positives a week?
FeaturesOnly what exists at prediction time. Group them: entity (user, item), context (time, device), history (counts over windows), cross features. Note which need real-time computation (Lessons 1.5, 8.5).
BaselineA rule, a heuristic or a simple model, scored on the same metric. It's the number the ML must beat, and often the first thing you ship.
Model choice, with whyTabular → gradient boosting (Lesson 4.6); need probabilities → check calibration (5.6); tight latency → smaller model or precompute; must explain decisions → linear model or SHAP (8.2). Say what you'd try second.
Offline evaluationA validation split that mirrors production: by time, by user, after label delay (Lessons 5.1, 9.3). Slice metrics by segment. Pick the threshold from costs (5.5, 9.2).
Online A/B testOffline gains are hypotheses. Randomize users, measure the business metric and guardrails, size the test so it can detect the effect you care about (Lesson 8.4).
ServingBatch (precompute predictions on a schedule, store them in a table) or online (compute per request within a latency budget). Back-of-envelope: requests per second, items per request, feature fetch time, model size, cost.
Monitoring & retrainingWatch inputs (drift, missing values, stale tables), outputs (score distribution) and outcomes once labels arrive. Retrain on a schedule set by how fast the world changes; have a rollback (Lesson 8.6).
The serving path is the top row; the learning loop is the bottom row.

✏️ Worked example: a back-of-envelope for restaurant ranking

A delivery app ranks restaurants on its home screen. 2 million people open it on a typical day, 5 times each, and each open scores 50 candidate restaurants.

  • Average traffic: 2,000,000 × 5 = 10 million requests a day ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 116 requests per second (QPS, queries per second).
  • Peak: lunch and dinner run about 3× the average: ≈ 347 QPS. Design for the peak.
  • Predictions: 347 × 50 ≈ 17,400 rows scored per second. If a boosted model takes 20 µs per row in a batch, that's 0.35 seconds of CPU per second: one core at peak, two for headroom.
  • Latency: one batched feature fetch (10 ms) + 50 rows × 20 µs (1 ms) + network and serialization (5 ms) ≈ 16 ms, well inside a 100 ms budget.
  • Batch alternative: precompute every user × restaurant score each night: 2 M × 50 = 100 million rows, about 0.56 core-hours and 0.8 GB at 8 bytes per score. Cheap, but it can't know it's raining now.

Five lines of arithmetic told you the model is cheap and the feature fetch is the latency to watch. Now break it in the calculator.

Offline is a hypothesis; the A/B test is the verdict

Offline metrics are computed on yesterday's data with yesterday's labels. Users react to the model, other systems change, and a +0.01 AUC may move nothing that matters. So the last offline step is a randomized A/B test: half the users get the new model, half the old, and you compare the business metric and the guardrails (Lesson 8.4, and the statistics in Math 5.5).

The question to ask first is how many users you need. Tiny effects on rare outcomes need enormous tests:

n per group ≈ 16 · p(1 − p) ⁄ δ² “Sixteen times the variance of one user's outcome, divided by the square of the smallest difference you want to detect” (5% false-alarm rate, 80% chance of catching a real effect).
🔮 Predict first

Checkout conversion is 5%. Your new ranking model should lift it by 2% relative (5% → 5.1%). Roughly how many users do you need in each group?

δ = 0.001, so n ≈ 15.7 × 0.05 × 0.95 ⁄ 0.001² ≈ 746,000 per group, about 1.5 million users in total. Small lifts on small rates are expensive to prove, which is why teams pick metrics that move more (or run tests longer). Check it below.

Serving: batch or online?

Batch (precompute)Online (per request)
HowA scheduled job scores everyone, writes a table; the app looks up the answerA service fetches features and scores within a latency budget
Good forWeekly emails, demand forecasts, churn lists, nightly recommendationsFraud checks, search ranking, ETAs, anything that depends on now
FreshnessAs old as the last runAs fresh as the features
Cost & riskCheap, easy to debug, wastes work on users who never show upNeeds a feature store or streaming features, a p99 latency target (the slowest 1% of requests), on-call
Common hybridBatch-computed slow features (30-day history) + a few real-time ones (last 5 minutes), scored online. Rules of ML calls the danger here training/serving skew: log the features you actually served and train on those (Rule #29).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

For the estimates, a notebook and a stopwatch: measure your model's size and latency instead of guessing. For the system: a training pipeline (scikit-learn Pipelines, Lesson 1.6), experiment tracking and a model registry (MLflow 3.17 as of late 2026), a feature store for online features (Feast 0.67), and drift reports (Evidently 0.7) (Lessons 8.5–8.6).

import pickle, time
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20_000, n_features=30, random_state=0)
model = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(max_iter=300, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
print(f"trees {model.n_iter_}, model size {len(pickle.dumps(model)) / 1e6:.2f} MB")

t = time.perf_counter()
for i in range(200):
    model.predict_proba(X[i:i + 1])          # one request = one row
one_row_ms = (time.perf_counter() - t) / 200 * 1e3

t = time.perf_counter()
model.predict_proba(X[:10_000])               # a batch of 10,000 rows
per_row_us = (time.perf_counter() - t) / 10_000 * 1e6
print(f"one row at a time: {one_row_ms:.2f} ms   in a batch: {per_row_us:.1f} µs per row")

On our laptop (scikit-learn 1.9.1) this printed about 0.3 MB for 62 trees (early stopping kicked in), ~9 ms per single-row call and ~1 µs per row in a batch. That gap is per-call overhead, not the trees, and it's why serving code scores all 50 candidates in one call. Your machine will give different numbers: that's the point of measuring.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Ship the heuristic, log everything (inputs, predictions, outcomes), and write the one-page design: goal, metric, labels, baseline. Your first model is a boosted tree retrained by a cron job and served as a batch table or a single small service. No feature store, no streaming. Measure before you build.

At scale

The framework becomes a design review: written docs, privacy and fairness sign-off (Lesson 8.3), shared feature stores, shadow deployments before A/B tests, automated retraining with validation gates, and dashboards per segment. Small gains are worth months of work because they multiply across billions of predictions, so the A/B and monitoring steps get the most investment.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a system that detects fake reviews / predicts delivery time / recommends restaurants.” In a 45-minute ML design round, a strong candidate:

  • Spends the first 5–8 minutes clarifying: the decision, the scale (reviews per day, QPS), latency, the cost of each mistake, what exists today.
  • Names a business metric and an ML metric and how they connect, plus a guardrail.
  • Says where labels come from, how delayed and biased they are, and proposes a fix (a random audit sample, waiting for labels to mature).
  • Starts from a baseline, then picks a model with a reason (tabular, latency, need for probabilities) and a fallback.
  • Validates with a split that mirrors production, then an A/B test sized with numbers; covers serving with a back-of-envelope; ends with monitoring and retraining.

A common split of the time: clarify 5–8 min, metrics and data 10, features and model 10–12, evaluation 8, serving and monitoring 8–10. It's a habit, not a rule: follow the interviewer's lead when they dig into one step.

🏭 In the real world
The Netflix Prize, in production 🏢 Netflix · 2012 Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 stars (Part 1)

How: took the two strongest prize algorithms (matrix factorization and RBMs), re-engineered them for billions of ratings and continuous updates, and shipped them; evaluated the final ensemble offline and didn't ship it.

Why this method: production value is accuracy minus engineering and running cost, judged against a business that had moved to streaming.

Rules of Machine Learning 🏢 Google Martin Zinkevich: Rules of ML

How: 43 rules from Google's product teams, ordered like this framework: launch without ML first, design metrics first, keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right, watch for silent failures, log features at serving time.

Why this method: most gains and most failures in their products came from data, features and infrastructure, not from the learning algorithm.

Hidden technical debt 🧪 Research · 2015 Sculley et al.: Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems (NeurIPS)

How: catalogues the ways ML systems rot: entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers, unstable data dependencies, glue code, configuration debt.

Why this method: naming the failure modes lets a design review check for them before launch; the “small box of ML code” figure came from here.

Freshness as a design input 🏢 Facebook · 2014 He et al.: Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook (PDF)

How: boosted trees retrained daily or every few days feed a logistic regression trained online as new clicks arrive.

Why this method: accuracy decayed day by day after training; splitting the slow and fast parts gave freshness without retraining the whole model constantly (details in Lesson 9.2).

🤯 Fun fact · the best launch of the quarter was a refresh

Rules of ML describes tables joined into features that silently stop updating: the model adapts and degrades so gradually that nobody notices, and “a simple refresh improves performance more than any other launch that quarter”. Google Play had one table stale for 6 months; refreshing it gave a 2% boost in install rate. No new model, no new feature: just a monitor that should have been there.

Check yourself

1. A feature has 3 million daily users, each triggering 4 predictions a day, and traffic peaks at 3× the average. Roughly what peak load must the service handle?
3,000,000 × 4 = 12 million a day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 139 per second on average; × 3 ≈ 417 at peak. Remember 86,400 s/day ≈ 10⁵: “a million a day is about 12 per second”.
2. Your new ranking model beats the old one by +0.02 AUC offline. In a two-week A/B test the business metric doesn't move. What do you check first?
Offline gains are hypotheses. Common reasons they vanish: features computed differently in production (Rules of ML #29, #37), an underpowered test (n ≈ 16·p(1−p)/δ²), or a proxy metric that doesn't drive the outcome (AUC rewards ranking all pairs, while users only see the top 5). A bigger model doesn't fix any of these.
3. Marketing wants “10 recommended products” in a weekly email to 5 million customers. Fraud wants a score on every card payment before it's approved. How should each be served?
The email is sent on a schedule and nothing about it changes in the seconds before sending: precompute. A fraud score depends on the payment happening right now (amount, merchant, velocity in the last minutes), so it must be computed online, typically in well under 100 ms (Stripe Radar: under 100 ms, Lesson 9.2).
📝 TL;DR
  • ML code is a small box in an ML system; most wins and failures live in goals, labels, features, serving and monitoring.
  • Ten steps in order: goal → business vs ML metric → data & labels → features → baseline → model (with why) → offline eval → A/B test → serving → monitoring & retraining.
  • Every step ends in a number: QPS (users × requests ÷ 86,400 × peak), latency budget, label delay, test size (≈ 16·p(1−p)/δ²).
  • Batch when the answer can be precomputed; online when it depends on now. Log served features to avoid training/serving skew.
  • Launch a heuristic first, keep the first model simple, and monitor for silent failures (a stale table cost Google Play 2% of installs).
