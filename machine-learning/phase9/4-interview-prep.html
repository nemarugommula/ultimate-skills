Phase 9 · Lesson 4

Interview prep: the questions, and the answers that land

Classical-ML interviews ask the same dozen questions in a hundred disguises. This lesson shows what a strong answer contains (an idea, a reason, a number, a failure mode), drills you against a clock, trains your ear for weak answers, and walks through the take-home and ML-design formats.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bias–variance: too simple underfits (high bias), too flexible memorizes noise (high variance); learning curves tell them apart (Lesson 5.8).
  • Metrics for rare positives: precision, recall and the PR curve, not accuracy (Lessons 5.3–5.5).
  • The ML design framework: goal → metrics → labels → features → baseline → model → evaluation → A/B → serving → monitoring (Lesson 9.1).
🎯 Hook: “our fraud model is 99.8% accurate”

A classic opener: the interviewer says the team's fraud model is 99.8% accurate and asks what you think. Here's a real benchmark to test your instinct: the widely used credit-card fraud dataset from the Université Libre de Bruxelles has 492 frauds among 284,807 transactions, 0.172%.

A “model” that answers not fraud to every single transaction is right 284,315 times out of 284,807: 99.83% accurate, and it catches zero fraud. The question isn't really about fraud. It's testing whether you reach for the right metric before you reach for praise.

The second: it computes the trivial baseline, names the right metrics, and ties the threshold to costs. The third is half right: ROC AUC can look rosy on extreme imbalance, and it still doesn't say where to set the threshold (Lessons 5.4, 5.5). That four-part shape, idea + reason + number + decision, is what this lesson trains.

💡 Big idea A strong answer has four beats: what it is (one plain sentence), why it works (the intuition, with a small number), when it fails (the cost or failure mode), and what you'd do (a decision in a concrete situation). Definitions alone sound memorized; the number and the failure mode prove you've used it.
🧠 Analogy: the doctor who explains

Two doctors look at the same X-ray. One says “it's a stress fracture”. The other says “it's a stress fracture: see this hairline here; it's common in runners who add mileage fast; it won't show on an X-ray for the first two weeks, so if it still hurts we'd do an MRI; rest for six weeks”. Both are correct. Only the second one convinces you they'd handle the case where the X-ray looks normal.

Interviewers are listening for the second doctor: the reason, the number, and the edge case.

🤔 Why this? Why practise answer structure instead of memorizing definitions?
  1. The problem before. Candidates memorize glossaries (“regularization penalizes large weights”) and freeze when the question arrives in disguise: “your model does great in training and badly in production, why?” That is the same question, and a definition doesn't answer it.
  2. What people tried. Grinding long question lists helps recall but not delivery: answers come out as unstructured streams, too long, with no decision at the end. Interviewers then can't tell understanding from recitation.
  3. Why this works. A fixed shape (what → why → when it fails → what I'd do) works for any question, keeps you under ~90 seconds, and forces a number. Tiny example: “bagging 100 trees whose errors correlate at 0.3 leaves 0.3 + 0.7/100 = 0.307 of one tree's variance, so more trees stop helping and decorrelation (max_features) matters” is one sentence that proves you understand random forests (Lesson 4.2).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A rigid template can sound robotic, and it can't save you on topics you've never used. Practise out loud, vary the order when the interviewer steers, and back each answer with something you've actually run (the labs, Lesson 9.3's project).
  5. What came next. The same habit scales up to the ML design round (Lesson 9.1's framework) and to the project deep-dive, where your portfolio (the graduation page) supplies the numbers.

✏️ Worked example: “How would you handle a dataset with 1 positive in 1,000?”

A strong 90-second answer, beat by beat:

  • What it is / baseline. “Predicting ‘no’ for everyone is 99.9% accurate, so accuracy is out. A random ranking has a PR AUC (area under the precision–recall curve) equal to the positive rate, 0.001: that's my floor.”
  • Why the metric. “I'd report recall at the number of cases the team can review, and precision–recall overall, because the cost is in the positives we miss and the reviews we waste.”
  • What I'd do. “Start with class weights in a boosted tree or logistic regression, keep the scores calibrated, then choose the threshold from costs: if a missed case costs $200 and a review costs $5, flag when p > 5 ⁄ (5 + 200) ≈ 0.024.”
  • When it fails. “Oversampling like SMOTE must happen inside each CV fold or it leaks, and it distorts probabilities, so I'd recalibrate. With only a few hundred positives, CV estimates are noisy: I'd report a confidence interval.”

Four beats, two numbers, one decision. Now train your ear to hear the opposite.

The take-home and the ML design round

A take-home is a small version of Lesson 9.3's project: a dataset, a question, a few hours. Reviewers aren't ranking your leaderboard score; they're reading your judgement. The ML design round is Lesson 9.1's framework spoken out loud. Both reward the same things:

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Back every interview claim with something you've run. This 15-line script answers the hook's question with real numbers (scikit-learn 1.9; also runs on 1.6):

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, average_precision_score, roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

# 1 positive in about 100: the classic "is 99% accuracy good?" interview question
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20_000, n_features=20, n_informative=5,
                           weights=[0.99], flip_y=0, class_sep=0.8, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)

for name, m in [("always 'no'", DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent")),
                ("logistic regression", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))]:
    p = m.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:20s} accuracy {accuracy_score(y_te, p > 0.5):.3f}  "
          f"ROC AUC {roc_auc_score(y_te, p):.3f}  PR AUC {average_precision_score(y_te, p):.3f}")
print(f"positive rate (the PR AUC of a coin flip): {y_te.mean():.3f}")

Output: always-“no” gets accuracy 0.990, ROC AUC 0.500, PR AUC 0.010; logistic regression gets accuracy 0.993, ROC AUC 0.953, PR AUC 0.613. Accuracy barely tells them apart; PR AUC shows a 60× difference. 🚀 Startup vs big tech

Startups

Fewer rounds, more practical: a take-home or a live session on a messy dataset, then a long conversation with the founders about how you'd ship it. They probe breadth (data, model, serving, monitoring) and speed: can you get a baseline into production next week?

Big tech

Structured loops with separate fundamentals, coding, ML design and behavioural rounds, scored against written rubrics by different interviewers. Expect depth questions (“derive why L1 gives zeros”, “how would you calibrate a boosted model?”) and design problems at very large scale.

🎤 Interview angle

“Tell me about an ML project you're proud of.” This question decides many loops. A strong answer:

  • States the decision and the metric in one sentence, then the baseline and the final number (“recall at 500 reviews/day went from 41% with rules to 63%”).
  • Explains one hard choice with its alternative (“we split by account because random splits leaked fake rings: random CV said 0.91, the grouped split 0.78”).
  • Admits one thing that failed and what you learned.
  • Ends with impact or what you'd do next, and survives “why not X?” follow-ups because you actually ran X.

🤯 Fun fact · 76% accuracy on pure noise

scikit-learn's own docs show the leakage interview question in action: 200 rows, 10,000 columns of random numbers, random labels. Select the 25 “best” features using all the data, then split and train a boosted model: 0.76 accuracy, on data with no signal at all. Do the selection inside the training split only: 0.5, exactly chance. Quote that pair and you've answered the leakage question better than most candidates.

Check yourself

1. An interviewer says: a false alarm costs $10 of analyst time, a missed case costs $90, and the model's probabilities are calibrated. Above what probability should you flag a case?
Flag when the expected cost of ignoring (p × $90) exceeds the cost of flagging ((1 − p) × $10): p > 10 ⁄ (10 + 90) = 0.1. Saying the formula cFP ⁄ (cFP + cFN) and that it needs calibrated scores (Lesson 5.6) is a complete answer.
2. “How would you cross-validate a model that forecasts next week's sales?” Which answer is strongest?
Shuffling lets the model see the future: scores look great and fail in production. Rolling-origin CV mirrors how the forecast is used (Lessons 5.1, 7.1); naming the feature rule, the gap and a baseline turns a definition into a strong answer.
3. In a take-home your random-forest AUC is 0.99 after 30 minutes. What should you do before anything else?
Too-good-to-be-true scores are the most common take-home trap, and reviewers plant them. A top feature like “refund_issued” or “account_closed_date” is a leak; so is the same customer on both sides of a random split (Lessons 0.6, 5.1). Saying “that looks too good, here's how I checked” earns more credit than the 0.99.
📝 TL;DR
  • Strong answers have four beats: what it is, why it works (with a number), when it fails, what you'd do.
  • Ten questions cover most fundamentals rounds; for each, know the trade-off, a number and the common mistake.
  • Always compute the trivial baseline (99.83% for “never fraud”) and tie thresholds to costs: p > cFP ⁄ (cFP + cFN).
  • Take-homes are graded on framing, baseline, leakage-safe validation, error analysis and the write-up more than on the final score.
  • Bring two or three projects with real numbers; suspect leakage whenever a score looks too good.
