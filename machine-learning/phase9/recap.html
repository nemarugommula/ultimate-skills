🗣️ Teach-back: the senior-level question

“You're our first ML hire. Support wants to predict which tickets will escalate so senior agents see them first. We have two years of tickets in a database and no ML in production. Walk me through your first six weeks, and how we'll know it worked.”

A strong answer covers: the decision and its capacity (senior agents can take N tickets a day) and the cost of a missed escalation (9.1); a business metric (escalations resolved within SLA) and an ML metric (recall at N per day) (5.3, 9.2); labels from the escalation field, checked for delay and for policy changes over two years (9.2); a rules baseline (“priority customer + keyword”) shipped in week one (9.1, Rules of ML #1); a time-based split, a Pipeline with text counts and ticket metadata, a boosted tree, and threshold set by capacity (0.6, 1.5, 1.6, 4.4, 5.5); calibration if scores are shown to agents (5.6); an A/B test by agent team (8.4); batch or online scoring depending on how fast tickets must be routed; drift and outcome monitoring with a monthly retrain (8.6); and a model card with limits (Lab 9).

🏗️ Build it yourself: the final problem

✏️ The problem

You're the first ML engineer at InvoiceFlow, a B2B invoicing company. Customers send about 40,000 invoices a month; 6% end up paid more than 30 days late. A collections team of four can make 300 friendly reminder calls a week; a call costs about $8 of staff time. Finance wants the calls to go where they prevent the most late payments, and wants a forecast of late cash for the next month.

Design it: the goal and metrics, labels, features, baseline, model with why, validation, decision rule, test, serving, monitoring, and the pitfalls you'd warn the team about. Put numbers on it.

Reveal a strong answer

Numbers. 40,000 a month × 12 ÷ 52 ≈ 9,230 invoices a week; 6% late ≈ 554 late a week. The team can call 300, so even a perfect model reaches only about 54% of them: capacity, not accuracy, is the binding constraint. Calling 300 at random finds about 6% × 300 = 18 future-late invoices; that's the baseline to beat. Scoring is tiny: ~9,000 rows a week, so a daily batch job is plenty (Lesson 9.1).

Goal and metrics. Business: late-paid amount (in dollars, not count) and days-sales-outstanding; guardrail: customer complaints about calls. ML: precision and recall among the top 300 per week, weighted by invoice amount, plus calibration for the cash forecast (Lessons 5.3, 5.6, 9.2).

Labels. “Paid more than 30 days after due date” is known only 30 days after the due date: label delay. Train on invoices whose due date is at least 30 days old; never evaluate on immature ones (9.2). Watch label bias too: invoices that were already called have different outcomes.

Features (as of the invoice's creation or the scoring day): the customer's history (share of past invoices paid late, average days late, trend), invoice amount relative to the customer's usual, payment terms, industry and size, days until due, number of open invoices, recent disputes. Customer-history features are computed only from invoices already settled at that date, or they leak (0.6, 1.5).

Baseline and model. Rule: “customers with 2+ late invoices in the last 6 months”. Model: a HistGradientBoosting classifier in a Pipeline (mixed types, missing values, nonlinear history effects, fast), with class weights or threshold by capacity; logistic regression as an explainable fallback (4.4, 2.3).

Validation. Time-based: train on months 1–15, validate on 16–18 with a 30-day gap for label delay; group by customer when checking how it does on new customers (5.1, 9.3). Report precision@300 per week with a range across weeks.

Decision and test. The top 300 by expected late amount (p × amount). But a high-risk invoice might be late whatever you do: run an A/B test (call vs don't call, randomized among high scorers) to measure the effect of a call, then move to uplift targeting if effects differ by segment (8.4, 9.2). The cash forecast sums p × amount, which needs calibrated p.

Serving and monitoring. Nightly batch writes a ranked list to the collections tool; a model registry entry and a model card (8.5, Lab 9). Monitor input drift (new industries, a change in payment terms), score distribution, weekly precision@300 once labels mature, and calibration of the cash forecast; retrain monthly (8.6).

Pitfalls. Using the “reminder sent” or “dispute opened after due date” fields as features (they happen after scoring); evaluating on invoices not yet 30 days past due; counting late invoices instead of dollars; assuming calls cause on-time payment without a test; and a feedback loop where called customers' outcomes change the labels the next model learns from.

💻 Lab 9: The capstone, an end-to-end churn project

💡 What you'll build A complete small project on a synthetic subscription business built inside the file (3,000 customers, 3 monthly snapshots, a planted leaky column and a pure-noise column): leakage-safe split, a ColumnTransformer + HistGradientBoosting Pipeline, cross-validation against a baseline, a cost-based threshold, a calibration check, permutation importance and a model card. On our run it reaches a test ROC AUC of about 0.77, close to its grouped-CV estimate (about 0.75), and the cost-based threshold lands near the 0.17 that theory predicts.

Setup once: pip install -r labs/requirements.txt, then run it cell by cell in VS Code or Jupyter, or python3 phase9_capstone.py from the labs folder; it stops at the first TODO you haven't done. Runs offline in well under a minute on a laptop CPU (scikit-learn 1.6 or newer).

🏆 Boss quiz: the whole course

1. A missed fraud costs $150 and a manual review costs $10. Your model's probabilities are calibrated. Roughly which threshold minimizes expected cost?
Review when p × $150 > (1 − p) × $10, i.e. p > 10 ⁄ 160 ≈ 0.0625. Cheap reviews and expensive misses push the threshold far below 0.5 (Lessons 5.5, 9.2). It only works if the scores are calibrated (5.6).
2. A teammate target-encodes zip_code (replaces it with the average label per zip) on the whole dataset, then runs 5-fold CV and gets AUC 0.94; the holdout month gives 0.81. What's the fix?
Encoding with all labels lets each validation row's own label leak into its feature: a classic leak (Lessons 0.6, 1.3, 1.6). Inside a Pipeline, the encoder is refit per fold; TargetEncoder also cross-fits internally on the training data.
3. Your gradient-boosting model's validation log loss falls until round 400, then rises steadily to round 2,000 while training loss keeps falling. What do you do?
Unlike adding trees to a random forest, adding boosting rounds can overfit: each round fits the remaining errors, eventually the noise (Lessons 4.2, 4.3, 5.8). Early stopping picks the round where validation is best.
4. You run k-means on customers using annual_income (in dollars, 20,000–200,000) and age (18–90). The clusters are just income bands. Why?
A 1,000-dollar income difference outweighs a 50-year age difference in raw units. Distance-based methods (k-means, k-NN, SVMs with RBF kernels, PCA) need scaling (Lessons 1.4, 3.1, 6.1, 6.5).
5. Overnight, the share of applications your model rejects jumps from 8% to 30%. Nothing was deployed. What's the first move?
A sudden jump with no deployment is usually a data problem upstream, a silent failure (Rules of ML #10; Lessons 8.6, 9.1). Retraining on broken inputs bakes the bug in; moving the threshold hides it. Fix the pipeline, then decide whether the world really changed.
