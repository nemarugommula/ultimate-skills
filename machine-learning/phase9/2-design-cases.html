Phase 9 · Lesson 2

Design cases: fraud detection, churn and ads click-through

Three problems every ML interviewer loves, and every big company runs in production. Each one looks like “just train a classifier”, and each one is really decided by something else: what a mistake costs, when the labels arrive, and what the prediction is used for.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • False positives and false negatives, precision and recall, from the confusion matrix (Lesson 5.3); thresholds on rare classes (Lesson 5.5).
  • Calibration: a predicted 0.2 should come true about 20% of the time (Lesson 5.6). Log loss punishes confident wrong probabilities (Math 6.3).
  • The design framework: goal → metric → labels → features → baseline → model → offline and online evaluation → serving → monitoring (Lesson 9.1).
🎯 Hook: 100 milliseconds to catch a thief

Every time you pay on a site that uses Stripe, a model called Radar looks at more than 1,000 characteristics of the payment and decides, in less than 100 milliseconds, whether to let it through, block it or send it to extra checks. Fraud is rare, on the order of 1 in every 1,000 payments, and Stripe says Radar wrongly blocks just 0.1% of legitimate ones.

Here's the puzzle. A model that simply says “never fraud” would be right 999 times out of 1,000. So what is Radar actually optimizing, where do its labels come from (nobody tells Stripe “that one was fraud” at payment time), and how do you build anything in 100 ms? This lesson walks through that design and two other classics.

🔮 Predict first

A famous public fraud dataset (European card payments, two days of September 2013) has 492 frauds among 284,807 transactions. What accuracy does a “model” that always answers “not fraud” get?

99.83% (1 − 492/284,807), while catching zero fraud. Accuracy is the wrong scoreboard when one class is 0.17% of the data. The right one counts what each kind of mistake costs. You'll find that cost-minimizing threshold yourself in Playground 1.

Same toolbox (gradient-boosted trees and logistic regression would make a fine first model for all three), completely different systems. The column you read decides the design, not the model.

💡 Big idea Before choosing a model, answer three questions: what does each kind of mistake cost (that sets the metric and the threshold), where and when do the labels come from (that sets the training data, the validation split and the bias you inherit), and how is the score used and how fast (a threshold, a ranking or a calibrated probability; online in milliseconds or a nightly batch). The model is the easy part once those are pinned down.
🧠 Analogy: the smoke detector in your kitchen

A smoke detector beeps at burnt toast, and you put up with it. Why not make it “more accurate” so it only beeps at real fires? Because a missed fire costs a house and a false beep costs a waved tea towel. The right sensitivity comes from those two prices, not from the detector's accuracy.

A fraud system is a smoke detector with prices you can measure. A churn campaign is a fire brigade with a budget, sent only where water helps. An ad auction is a weather forecast: if “30% rain” doesn't mean 30%, everyone who plans on it loses money.

🤔 Why this? Why design from costs, labels and serving, not from the model?
  1. The problem before. Teams start with “train the best classifier” and optimize accuracy or AUC (area under the ROC curve, Lesson 5.4). The fraud model scores 99.8% and catches nothing; the churn model finds customers who were leaving anyway; the ad model ranks well but overpredicts clicks 2×, so the auction overcharges.
  2. What people tried. Bigger models, more features, resampling the data to 50:50. Those help ranking a little, but the gap was never in the model: it was the threshold (set at 0.5 by default), the label (propensity instead of response to the offer) and the probability scale (destroyed by resampling).
  3. Why this works. Put prices on mistakes and the decision falls out. With a calibrated fraud probability p, a missed fraud costing $150 and a false block costing $10, blocking pays when p × $150 > (1 − p) × $10, that is when p > 10 / 160 = 0.0625, not 0.5. On Playground 1's data that one change cuts the cost from $459 to $287 per 1,000 payments.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Costs are estimates and change (fraud amounts, review staff); labels arrive late and only for what you allowed; uplift needs randomized experiments you must pay for; calibration drifts as the world changes. The design must re-measure all of these, not just retrain.
  5. What came next. Tools that bake this in: TunedThresholdClassifierCV picks a threshold by business cost (scikit-learn 1.5+), uplift models target response to treatment (Lesson 8.4), online learning and daily retraining keep CTR models fresh, and drift monitoring catches the rest (Lesson 8.6). Lesson 9.3 runs one project end to end.

Case 1 · Fraud detection: a smoke detector with prices

🎯 Goal and metric

Minimize expected money lost = missed fraud × its cost + false blocks × their cost (lost sale, angry customer) + reviews × analyst time. Report precision and recall at the chosen threshold, and the PR curve (precision–recall, Lesson 5.4), never accuracy.

🏷️ Labels

Mostly chargebacks: the cardholder disputes a payment with their bank. Card networks typically allow disputes within 120 days, so last month's labels are incomplete. And you never learn the truth about payments you blocked.

🧮 Features

Velocity (payments on this card in the last hour), amount vs this customer's usual, country mismatches, graph links (how many cards one IP or device has used), history of the merchant.

⚡ Serving

Online, inside the payment request: < 100 ms including fetching the velocity counters from a fast store. A rules layer stays in front for hard blocks and business policy.

The features fraudsters can't hide

A single payment rarely looks suspicious: $49 for headphones is normal. What gives fraud away is context: the same card tried 14 times in 10 minutes (card testing), a brand-new account shipping to an address shared by 30 other accounts, one device used with 9 different cards. Stripe's write-up names features like payment velocity, “unusual volume on a card” and the number of cards previously associated with an IP address.

Every one of these must be computed as of the payment time, from data that existed then. A velocity count computed in a nightly batch over the whole day leaks the future into training (Lesson 0.6) and is unavailable at serving time: the classic training–serving skew of Lesson 9.1.

Labels arrive late, and only for what you let through

Two traps hide in the labels. Delay: if you evaluate on last week's payments, most frauds haven't been disputed yet, so they look legitimate: recall looks better and precision worse than reality. Use a time-based split (Lesson 7.1) and only evaluate on periods old enough for labels to have matured. Selection: blocked payments never get a label, so the next model learns only from what the last model allowed. A common fix is to let a tiny random slice of risky-but-not-certain payments through (or send them to review) to keep learning about the region the model blocks.

✏️ Worked example: the threshold from two prices

A payment scores p = 0.10 (a calibrated probability of fraud). Missing a fraud costs $150 (the chargeback plus a fee); blocking a good customer costs $10 (lost margin and goodwill).

  • Allow it: expected loss = 0.10 × $150 = $15.00.
  • Block it: expected loss = 0.90 × $10 = $9.00. Blocking is cheaper.
  • The break-even point: p × 150 = (1 − p) × 10 → p* = 10 / (10 + 150) = 0.0625. Block everything above 6.25%.

The rule works only if p is calibrated (Lesson 5.6). If your model was trained on resampled 50:50 data, its 0.10 might really mean 0.002; recalibrate first, or pick the threshold directly by measured cost on validation data.

🔮 Predict first

Fraud rate 0.5%, a decent model (AUC 0.98). Compared with the default threshold of 0.5, how does the cost-minimizing threshold behave if fraud becomes four times more common (2%)?

For calibrated probabilities the best threshold depends only on the two costs: 0.0625 at 0.1%, 0.5% and 2% fraud. What changes is how many payments cross it (the probabilities themselves shift up). Switch the fraud rate in the playground and watch the gold line stay put while the costs move.

Case 2 · Churn: predict who leaves, or who you can keep?

🎯 Goal and metric

Not “predict churn”, but keep the most value per dollar of retention budget. Metric: net value of a campaign = customers actually kept × their value − cost of offers, measured against a random control group.

🏷️ Labels

You define churn. Contractual (subscriptions): a cancellation event. Non-contractual (shops, apps): “no purchase or login in N days”, a choice you must justify. The label is known only after the prediction window closes.

🧮 Features

Usage trends (logins this month vs last), support tickets, failed payments, plan and price changes, tenure, engagement with key features. All computed before the cutoff date.

⚡ Serving

A batch job (nightly or weekly) scores every customer and writes a list to the CRM or email tool. No latency budget; the hard part is the experiment design.

Windows: the leak that hides in a date

A churn dataset is built around a cutoff date. Features use only the observation window before it; the label asks “did they churn in the prediction window after it?”. A short gap in between gives the business time to act on the prediction.

The leak: a feature like “support tickets in the last 30 days” computed when you extract the data (May 10) instead of as of the cutoff (Apr 1) includes the angry “please cancel my account” ticket. Validation AUC looks amazing; production is useless. Build features as of the cutoff, and validate on a later cutoff than you train on (Lesson 5.1).

Propensity vs uplift: the four kinds of customer

A churn model ranks people by propensity: P(churn). But a retention offer only creates value where it changes the outcome. That difference is the uplift: P(churn without offer) − P(churn with offer).

Leaves without an offerStays without an offer
Stays if offered🎯 Persuadables: the only people worth paying for😌 Sure things: the offer is wasted money
Leaves if offered🚪 Lost causes: already decided; high propensity, no uplift🐕 Sleeping dogs: the offer reminds them to cancel

Targeting by propensity fills your list with lost causes. Eva Ascarza showed exactly this with two field experiments (“Retention Futility: Targeting High-Risk Customers Might Be Ineffective”, Journal of Marketing Research, 2018): the highest-risk customers were not the best targets, and targeting by measured sensitivity to the offer worked significantly better. Estimating uplift needs a randomized test (some customers get the offer at random, some don't), then a model of the difference (Lesson 8.4).

Case 3 · Ads click-through rate: probabilities that are spent

An ad system shows an ad when it wins an auction, and a common ranking is bid × predicted CTR (click-through rate: clicks ÷ impressions). The predicted probability isn't a score to sort by; it's multiplied into money. A model that ranks perfectly but predicts every CTR twice too high makes the system overvalue ads against everything else it could show and price them wrong. So the CTR design is about calibrated probabilities at enormous scale.

🎯 Goal and metric

Offline: log loss and normalized cross-entropy (NE), plus calibration = average predicted CTR ÷ observed CTR (want 1.0). Online: an A/B test on revenue, advertiser value and user experience.

🏷️ Labels

Fast and plentiful: a click within some window after the impression. Billions a day, heavily imbalanced, and shaped by position (the top slot gets clicked more for being on top).

🧮 Features

Huge sparse categoricals (ad id, advertiser, query words, user segments) and their crosses (ad × country), plus historical CTRs of this ad and this user so far.

⚡ Serving

Score hundreds of candidate ads per request in milliseconds; retrain constantly because new ads and trends appear every day.

Normalized cross-entropy: log loss you can compare

Log loss depends heavily on the base rate: predicting a 2% event is “easier” than a 50% one, so a log loss of 0.08 means little on its own. Facebook's 2014 paper divides it by the log loss of a dumb model that always predicts the background CTR:

NE = average log loss of the model ⁄ log loss of always predicting the base CTR “How much of the uncertainty the base rate leaves does the model remove? 1.0 means nothing; lower is better.”

The denominator is the entropy of the base CTR, H(p) = −p ln p − (1 − p) ln(1 − p) (Math 6.2). Google's 2013 paper makes the same point from the other side: they always report relative changes against a baseline on exactly the same data, because absolute log loss moves with click rates that differ by country and hour.

✏️ Worked example: NE and the downsampling fix by hand
  • Base CTR 2%: H = −0.02 ln 0.02 − 0.98 ln 0.98 = 0.0782 + 0.0198 = 0.0980 nats per impression.
  • Your model's log loss is 0.0900 → NE = 0.0900 ÷ 0.0980 = 0.918: it removes about 8% of the uncertainty. In ads that's a decent model; Facebook calls a relative NE gain of a few tenths of a percent a typical feature-engineering win.
  • To cut training cost you keep every click but only w = 1% of non-clicks. A true CTR of 0.1% now looks like 0.001 ÷ (0.001 + 0.999 × 0.01) = 9.1% in the training data, so the model learns to say about 9%.
  • Fix it at serving time with Facebook's formula q = p ⁄ (p + (1 − p) ⁄ w): 0.091 ⁄ (0.091 + 0.909 ⁄ 0.01) = 0.001. Back to 0.1%.
🔮 Predict first

Your CTR model predicts exactly twice the true probability for every impression. You multiply every prediction by 0.5. What happens to AUC and to NE?

Multiplying by a constant keeps the order of the predictions, so AUC can't move (0.846 in the playground). Log loss cares about the values: NE drops from 0.893 to 0.803. Facebook's paper uses this very example to explain why NE reflects calibration and AUC doesn't.

Play with it: at base CTR 2% and signal 1.5, the calibrated model has log loss 0.079 and NE 0.803. Raise the base CTR to 20%: log loss jumps to about 0.38 (five times worse-looking) while NE says the model got slightly better (0.766). At w = 0.025 (the rate Facebook found worked best), a 2% CTR looks like 45% during training: serve that without the fix and every bid is off by more than 20×.

The model: trees for features, a linear model for speed and freshness

Facebook's design concatenates two models. Gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT, Lessons 4.3–4.4) are trained on the raw features; each tree's leaf index becomes a one-hot feature, so a 600-leaf forest turns every impression into a sparse vector of “which leaf in each tree”. A logistic regression (Lesson 2.3) on top learns a weight per leaf. The trees find interactions and thresholds; the linear model is cheap to retrain online as new clicks arrive.

Model (Facebook, 2014)NE relative to trees only
Boosted trees only100% (reference)

Three more lessons from the same paper shape the design. Freshness: models degrade day by day after training, and going from weekly to daily retraining cuts NE by about 1%. Historical features dominate: past CTR of the ad and the user beat contextual features like time of day; the top 10 features carry about half of the total importance. Downsampling: keeping a share of the non-clicks speeds training, but you must recalibrate. Google's paper took the other route, one giant logistic regression trained online with FTRL-Proximal (Follow The Regularized Leader, an online optimizer whose L1 penalty gives many exactly-zero weights) and per-coordinate learning rates (rare features get bigger steps), plus an isotonic calibration layer on top.

Feature crosses and the hashing trick

A linear model can't learn that “sports ad × user in Brazil” behaves differently unless you give it that combination as a feature: a feature cross. Crosses of high-cardinality columns explode (a million ads × 200 countries = 200 million columns). The hashing trick maps each feature string into a fixed number of buckets, hash("ad=123|country=BR") mod 2²⁴, so memory is bounded and new values need no vocabulary. The price is collisions, where unrelated features share a weight. It's a size-versus-accuracy dial, not free memory: Google reported that for them hashing couldn't go below several billion features without a measurable loss, so they kept interpretable unhashed features.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): TunedThresholdClassifierCV picks the threshold that maximizes a business score by cross-validation (Lesson 5.5); CalibratedClassifierCV and calibration_curve fix and check calibration (Lesson 5.6); FeatureHasher does the hashing trick; log_loss gives you NE in one line. At ad scale teams use Vowpal Wabbit, XGBoost/LightGBM and deep learning frameworks; uplift has scikit-uplift and causalml (Lesson 8.4).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, log_loss, make_scorer
from sklearn.model_selection import TunedThresholdClassifierCV, train_test_split

# 1% positives, like a (generous) fraud problem
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20_000, n_features=20, n_informative=6,
                           weights=[0.99], class_sep=0.8, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)

def cost(y, y_pred, c_fn=150, c_fp=10):        # $150 per missed fraud, $10 per false alarm
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y, y_pred, labels=[0, 1]).ravel()
    return c_fn * fn + c_fp * fp

gbm = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)
tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(gbm, scoring=make_scorer(cost, greater_is_better=False), cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
p = tuned.estimator_.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
print(f"tuned threshold {tuned.best_threshold_:.3f}")
print(f"cost at 0.5: ${cost(y_te, p >= 0.5)}   tuned: ${cost(y_te, tuned.predict(X_te))}")

# Calibration and normalized cross-entropy (NE), as in the Facebook CTR paper
print(f"calibration (avg predicted / avg observed) {p.mean() / y_te.mean():.2f}")
ne = log_loss(y_te, p) / log_loss(y_te, np.full_like(p, y_tr.mean()))
print(f"NE {ne:.3f}  (1.0 = no better than predicting the base rate)")

On our runs the tuned threshold came out near 0.05 (theory for calibrated scores: 10/160 = 0.0625) and cut the test cost by about a quarter to a third versus 0.5; exact numbers differ a little between scikit-learn versions. The calibration ratio printed well below 1 (0.6–0.7): a reminder to check calibration before trusting a cost formula, and to fix it with CalibratedClassifierCV if needed.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Fraud: use your payment provider's built-in tool plus a few rules; add a gradient-boosted model when you have a few hundred labelled frauds. Churn: a simple propensity model and a random holdout on every campaign, so next quarter you can measure uplift. Ads: you probably buy ads rather than sell them; if you rank anything, logistic regression on counts and crosses goes a long way.

At scale

Streaming feature platforms for velocity and graph features, dedicated label pipelines that wait for disputes to mature, uplift models refreshed from always-on experiments, and CTR models retrained daily or online with calibration layers, all monitored per slice (country, device, advertiser). Stripe moved Radar from an XGBoost + neural network ensemble to a neural network only, partly to retrain faster.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a fraud detection system for a payments company.” A strong answer:

  • Starts with the decision and the costs: allow / review / block, the dollar cost of each mistake, and a threshold set from them (p* = cFP ⁄ (cFP + cFN) for calibrated scores), not 0.5 and not accuracy.
  • Explains the labels: chargebacks arrive over months, blocked payments are never labelled; time-based validation on matured data and a small exploration slice.
  • Names features computed as of event time: velocity, deviation from the customer's history, graph links between cards, devices and accounts.
  • Gives the serving plan: online scoring inside a ~100 ms budget, a rules layer, a fast store for counters, fallbacks if the model is down.
  • Ends with monitoring: fraudsters adapt, so track score distributions, block rate, review queue size and matured precision/recall weekly (Lesson 8.6). Swap “fraud” for churn and the same answer turns into uplift and experiments; for ads, into calibration and NE.
🏭 In the real world
Stripe Radar 🏢 Stripe · 2023 How we built it: Stripe Radar

How: scores every payment on more than 1,000 characteristics in under 100 ms; features include payment velocity and how many cards an IP has used. In mid-2022 they replaced an XGBoost + deep neural network ensemble with a neural network only, cutting training time by over 85% to under two hours.

Why this method: fraud is rare and adversarial, so speed of retraining and new features matter as much as the model; the business cost of blocking good payments is tracked as closely as missed fraud.

Disputes and their timing 🏢 Stripe docs How disputes work

How: card networks typically allow cardholders to dispute a payment within 120 days, sometimes longer.

Why this method: that window is your label delay; a fraud model's “last month” of data is not fully labelled yet, which shapes validation and retraining.

Clicks at Facebook 🧪 Research · 2014 Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook (He et al., PDF)

How: boosted trees transform features into leaf indices for an online logistic regression; evaluated with normalized entropy and calibration, with daily retraining and negative downsampling (best rate 0.025) followed by recalibration.

Why this method: with over 750 million daily active users and over 1 million advertisers, the hybrid beat either model alone by more than 3% NE while keeping the linear part cheap to update.

Ad click prediction at Google 🧪 Research · 2013 Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches (McMahan et al., PDF)

How: FTRL-Proximal logistic regression with per-coordinate learning rates over billions of features, probabilistic feature inclusion, 16-bit weights, relative metrics and a calibration layer.

Why this method: predictions run many billions of times a day, so sparsity and memory are design constraints; the paper also lists ideas that didn't pay off for them (aggressive hashing, dropout, feature bagging).

The public fraud benchmark 🧪 Research · 2015 Credit card fraud dataset (Dal Pozzolo et al., OpenML)

How: 284,807 European card transactions over two days, 492 frauds (0.172%), features anonymized with PCA.

Why this method: its accompanying paper is about calibrating probabilities after undersampling, the same fix as Facebook's: resampling changes the probability scale.

🤯 Fun fact · 16 bits were enough

Google stored its ad-model weights not as 64-bit floats but in a 16-bit fixed-point format called q2.13 (a sign bit, two bits before the binary point, thirteen after). They saw no measurable loss in accuracy and saved 75% of the memory for coefficients. Meanwhile Facebook noted that a typical feature-engineering experiment improves NE by a couple of tenths of a percent, which is why a 3% jump from adding trees was a big deal.

Check yourself

1. A missed fraud costs $200 and a manual review of a flagged payment costs $5. Your scores are calibrated. Roughly which threshold minimizes expected cost?
Review when p × $200 > (1 − p) × $5, so p* = 5 / (5 + 200) ≈ 0.024. Cheap reviews and expensive misses push the threshold far below 0.5. 0.976 is the same fraction upside down (it would be right only if reviews cost 40× more than misses).
2. The retention team sent a 20% discount to the 5% of customers with the highest predicted churn risk. A comparison with similar customers who got nothing shows almost no difference in churn. What's the best next step?
The highest-risk customers are often lost causes: already leaving, whatever you offer (Ascarza, 2018). A better propensity model finds them even more precisely. Randomize who gets the offer, estimate the treatment effect per customer (Lesson 8.4), and spend the budget where it changes behaviour, as in Playground 2.
3. You evaluate a new fraud model on last week's payments and find its precision is much lower than on data from four months ago. What's the most likely explanation?
Chargebacks arrive over weeks to months (disputes are typically allowed within 120 days). On fresh data, true frauds still look legitimate, so precision looks worse than it is (and recall can look better). Evaluate on periods old enough for labels to have settled, with a time-based split (Lesson 7.1), and track label maturity explicitly.
📝 TL;DR
  • Design from three questions: what mistakes cost, where and when labels come from, and how and how fast the score is used.
  • Fraud: rare, adversarial, late labels. Pick the threshold from costs (p* = cFP ⁄ (cFP + cFN) for calibrated scores), use velocity and graph features as of event time, serve online in ~100 ms.
  • Churn: define it, build features before a cutoff, and target by uplift measured with randomized tests, not by risk: high-risk customers are often lost causes.
  • Ads CTR: probabilities are spent in auctions, so judge by log loss, NE and calibration; trees → logistic regression, crosses or hashing, fresh retrains, and recalibrate after downsampling.
