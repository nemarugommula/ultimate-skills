Phase 9 · Lesson 3

An end-to-end project, Kaggle style: from a blank notebook to a result you can defend

Every lesson so far was one tool. A real project is the order you use them in, and the discipline that stops you fooling yourself. This lesson walks a whole competition-style project, then shows you, with a leaderboard simulator, exactly how people fool themselves.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Cross-validation (CV): split the training data into k folds, train on k − 1, score on the one left out, average; group and time-series variants keep related rows together (Lesson 5.1).
  • Gradient-boosted trees (GBM): trees added one at a time, each fixing the last ones' mistakes; the default winner on tables (Lessons 4.3, 4.4).
  • A Pipeline with a ColumnTransformer bundles preprocessing and model, so CV refits everything per fold and nothing leaks (Lesson 1.6).
🎯 Hook: climbing a leaderboard with zero knowledge

On Kaggle, the test labels are hidden. Your submissions are scored on one part of the test set, the public leaderboard (LB), and at the end everyone is re-ranked on the other part, the private leaderboard. In 2015 Avrim Blum and Moritz Hardt pointed out that Kaggle's forums were full of posts about “overfitting to the holdout”: public scores that turned out inflated once the private ranking appeared.

To show how bad it can get, they described a “boosting attack” that uses no model and no features at all: submit random guesses, keep the ones that happen to score above chance on the public part, and combine them by majority vote. Their experiment used 12,000 test labels, 4,000 of them public.

No information about the problem, just feedback from a leaderboard. Can that really move the score?

🔮 Predict first

A 50/50 yes/no problem, 4,000 public labels. You make 700 submissions of pure coin flips, keep those that beat 50% on the public board, and submit their majority vote. Roughly what does the public leaderboard say?

In Playground 2's simulator, after 700 coin-flip submissions the combined vote scores 59.2% on the public board and 50.6% on the private one. Nine points of “skill” made entirely of leaked leaderboard feedback. You'll run the attack yourself in Playground 2.

The map: one project, nine steps

Here's the order experienced competitors and working data scientists follow. The steps are boring on purpose: each one exists because skipping it has burned someone.

Frame it. What is predicted, for whom, when, and how it's scored. Read the metric and the data description twice.

Trap: optimizing accuracy when the metric is log loss or AUC.

Look at the data (EDA). Target rate, column types, missing values, IDs, dates, duplicates, and how train differs from test (Lesson 1.1).

Trap: a customer or time overlap you never noticed.

Baseline. Predict the base rate (DummyClassifier), then a simple model. Now every later idea has a number to beat.

Trap: a fancy model with no baseline “scores 0.81”, which might be worse than guessing the average.

Build a CV that mirrors the test. If test rows are new customers, hold out whole customers; if test is the future, validate on the future (Lesson 5.1).

Trap: random K-fold on grouped or time-ordered data. Playground 1.

Features. Ratios, counts, dates, aggregates per group, target encoding fitted inside folds (Lessons 1.3, 1.5).

Trap: an aggregate computed on all rows, including the validation fold.

GBM + tuning. LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost or scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting; early stopping; a small random or Optuna search (Lessons 4.4, 5.7).

Trap: tuning against the public leaderboard instead of CV. Playground 2.

Ensemble. Blend or stack models that make different mistakes, using out-of-fold predictions (Lesson 4.5).

Trap: blending five copies of the same GBM.

Error analysis. Where is the model wrong? Slice the errors by segment and look at the worst rows (Lesson 8.1).

Trap: staring at one global number.

Write it up. Problem, CV design, what worked, what didn't, with numbers and uncertainty.

Trap: “I tried a lot of stuff and got 0.83.”

💡 Big idea The real product of the first days of a project is not a model; it's a validation score you can trust: one that is computed the same way the world will judge you (same metric, same kind of split, same kind of unseen rows). Once you trust it, every idea becomes a cheap experiment. Without it, you're tuning against noise, and the leaderboard (or production) will tell you so later.
🧠 Analogy: the practice exam

Your teacher hands out last year's exam to practise on. If you study the ideas, your practice score predicts your real score. If you memorize the practice answers, the practice score climbs and the real exam is a disaster.

The public leaderboard is a practice exam you can retake hundreds of times, and each retake leaks a little about the answers. Cross-validation that mirrors the test is studying the ideas: you make your own practice exams from fresh questions, never the same ones twice.

🤔 Why this? Why a CV that mirrors the test (instead of trusting the leaderboard)?
  1. The problem before. A single holdout score is a noisy estimate, and the moment you choose things by it (features, models, seeds, submissions) it becomes optimistic: you pick whatever got lucky. Random splits make it worse when rows aren't independent: a customer's five orders end up on both sides, and the model gets graded on people it has already met.
  2. What people tried. Trusting the public leaderboard (it's “real test data”), or one random train/validation split. Kaggle itself limits submissions per day and rounds scores to slow overfitting. Both help a little; neither stops selection bias, and random splits keep the grouped-data leak.
  3. Why this works. K-fold CV scores every training row once, so the estimate uses far more data than a public board of a few thousand rows; and splitting by customer or by time recreates the real question (“how good on new customers, in the future?”). Tiny numbers: an 80%-accurate model scored on 4,000 public rows has a standard error of √(0.8 × 0.2 / 4000) ≈ 0.63 points. Pick the best of 100 equally good submissions and you pick about +2.5 standard errors of luck: the public board says about 81.6%, the private says 80%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. K times the training time; CV is still noisy on small data (report the spread across folds, not just the mean); and it only mirrors the test if you understood how the test differs. If train and test come from different worlds (other regions, other months), CV can't see that. Adversarial validation (below) is how you check.
  5. What came next. Repeated and nested CV for small data (Lesson 5.7), adversarial validation to measure train/test drift, and leaderboard designs that leak less: Blum and Hardt's Ladder only reveals a new score when it is significantly better than your previous best. In production the same idea becomes backtesting on past months and online A/B tests (Lessons 7.1, 8.4, 9.1).

Steps 1–3: frame it, look at it, baseline it

Framing is reading the fine print: one row per what (order, customer, day)? Predicting what, how far ahead? Scored by which metric (accuracy, AUC, log loss, RMSE; Lessons 5.2–5.4)? EDA (exploratory data analysis, Lesson 1.1) then answers the questions that decide your validation design:

Adversarial validation, in words: glue train and test together, label every row “came from train” or “came from test”, and train a classifier to tell them apart. If its cross-validated AUC (area under the ROC curve, 0.5 = coin flip, Lesson 5.4) is about 0.5, train and test look alike and a random-style CV is trustworthy. If it's well above 0.5, the classifier has found what's different (a date column, a new region); its most important features tell you what, and the training rows it thinks “look like test” make a better validation set. The FastML blog that popularized the trick ran it on Kaggle's Santander competition and got AUCs of about 0.50 with both logistic regression and random forests: train and test were indistinguishable.

Finally, the baseline. Predict the base rate for everyone (DummyClassifier): AUC 0.5, and log loss equal to the target's entropy. Then the simplest sensible model. Write both numbers down: they are the floor every later idea must clear.

Step 4: a CV that mirrors the test

The commonest way a careful-looking project goes wrong. Your training table has several rows per customer (orders, sessions, visits), and the test set is new customers. A customer's rows look alike, so with a random split the model sees four of someone's orders in training and is graded on the fifth: it recognises the person instead of learning the pattern.

🔮 Predict first

100 customers, 5 rows each. A 1-nearest-neighbour model (it copies the label of the most similar training row). Random 5-fold CV says about 89%. What will it score on new customers?

Averaged over 10 datasets in Playground 1: random CV 89%, group CV 73%, new customers 73%. Random CV was measuring “can you recognise a customer you've seen?”. Group K-fold asks the real question.

Steps 5–6: features, a GBM, a little tuning

The leaderboard trap, live

Now the hook, measured. Every submission's public score is the true quality plus luck from which rows landed in the public part. If you submit many slightly different models and keep the one with the best public score, you're partly selecting luck. The private board then takes the luck away: that drop is the shake-up.

🔮 Predict first

You make 100 submissions that are all about equally good, and choose your final one by the best public score. Which choice gives the smaller drop on the private board?

CV wins. Averaged over 20 simulated competitions (100 submissions each, 4,000 public rows), choosing by public score overstates the private score by about 1.3 points; choosing by CV, by about 0.5. CV scores all 20,000 training rows and you didn't tune it by peeking at it hundreds of times. Try it below.

Three lessons hide in that chart. The public line only goes up (it's a running maximum), so it always looks like progress. The private score of your pick barely moves, because the submissions were never really different. And with fewer public rows (slide n down) the luck is bigger and the shake-up grows: small public boards shake hardest. Real competitions behave like the honest mode when teams pick by CV; the attack shows the worst case when they pick by the board.

✏️ Worked example: how big is the luck?

Accuracy on n rows is an average of 0/1 outcomes, so its standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n).

  • p = 0.80, n = 4,000 public rows: √(0.16 / 4000) = 0.0063, about 0.63 points.
  • 100 equally good submissions: the best of 100 normal draws sits about 2.5 standard errors above the mean, so the best public score ≈ 80% + 2.5 × 0.63 ≈ 81.6%. Private: 80%. Shake-up ≈ 1.6 points, from nothing but selection.
  • Your 5-fold CV covers 20,000 training rows: √(0.16 / 20000) ≈ 0.28 points of noise, less than half the public board's. Selecting by it still adds a little optimism, but much less.
  • Shrink the public board to n = 1,000 and the standard error doubles to 1.26 points, so the expected shake-up doubles too.

The simulator lands a bit below the 1.6-point estimate (about 1.3) because there its submissions are not quite identical: the best public pick is also slightly better for real. The standard-error arithmetic is in Math 5.6.

Steps 7–8: ensembling and error analysis

Blending is averaging the predicted probabilities of different models. It helps for the same reason a random forest helps (Lesson 4.2): errors that differ cancel. To find the blend weight without cheating, use out-of-fold (OOF) predictions: during 5-fold CV, every training row gets a prediction from the model that did not train on it. Tune the weight on those, never on the test. Stacking (Lesson 4.5) is the same idea with a model learning the weights.

Error analysis asks where the model is wrong. Slice the OOF errors by segment (region, device, new vs old customers, a feature range) and compare models slice by slice. Two models that tie overall often win in different slices, which is exactly when blending pays, and exactly the clue for your next feature.

🎮 Playground 3 · Blend out-of-fold predictions, then slice the errors

Step 9: the write-up

In a competition the write-up is how winners teach; at work it's how your model gets trusted and maintained. A good one fits on two pages and answers:

  • Problem and metric, in one paragraph. What a 0.01 improvement means for the business, if you know.
  • Validation design and why: “GroupKFold by customer because test customers are new; CV and public LB agreed within 0.3 points.”
  • Baseline → final, as a table: each step, its CV score ± fold spread, and the gain.
  • What didn't work (just as useful to the reader).
  • Error analysis: the slices where it's weakest, and the top features (permutation importance, SHAP; Lessons 8.1, 8.2).
  • Risks: leakage checks, drift, what would break it in production (Lesson 8.6).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026) covers the whole skeleton: DummyClassifier, Pipeline + ColumnTransformer, HistGradientBoostingClassifier (native categorical support), and the splitters KFold, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold, StratifiedGroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit. Competitors usually swap in LightGBM, XGBoost or CatBoost for the model and Optuna for tuning; pandas or Polars for features.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OrdinalEncoder

# A tiny "orders" table: 300 customers, several orders each; a customer's orders look alike
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
cust = np.repeat(np.arange(300), 5)
taste = rng.normal(size=300)[cust]                              # hidden per-customer trait
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": cust,
    "basket_eur": np.round(40 + 15 * taste + rng.normal(0, 2, cust.size), 1),
    "device": rng.choice(["app", "web", "phone"], cust.size),
    "hour": rng.integers(0, 24, cust.size),
    "signup_day": rng.integers(0, 2000, 300)[cust],               # same for every order of a customer
})
y = (taste + 0.5 * (df["device"] == "app") + rng.normal(0, 1, 300)[cust] > 0.3).astype(int)

X = df.drop(columns="customer_id")
pre = ColumnTransformer(
    [("cat", OrdinalEncoder(handle_unknown="use_encoded_value", unknown_value=-1), ["device"])],
    remainder="passthrough",
)
gbm = make_pipeline(pre, HistGradientBoostingClassifier(categorical_features=[0], random_state=0))
base = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior")                       # the score to beat: predict the base rate

groups = df["customer_id"]                                     # test customers are new: keep each one in one fold
for name, cv, g in [("random KFold", KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), None), ("GroupKFold", GroupKFold(5), groups)]:
    s_gbm = cross_val_score(gbm, X, y, cv=cv, groups=g, scoring="roc_auc")
    s_base = cross_val_score(base, X, y, cv=cv, groups=g, scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"{name:12s}  baseline AUC {s_base.mean():.3f}   GBM AUC {s_gbm.mean():.3f} ± {s_gbm.std():.3f}")

Output with scikit-learn 1.9.1: random K-fold says AUC 0.938, GroupKFold says 0.814; the baseline is 0.500 either way. The 0.12 gap is the model recognising customers by their signup_day and basket size, not learning anything that transfers. With time-ordered data use TimeSeriesSplit instead. For adversarial validation, concatenate train and test, add a column is_test, and run cross_val_score(HistGradientBoostingClassifier(), X_all, is_test, scoring="roc_auc").

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Small team

The same nine steps in a week: framing with the person who'll use the prediction, one honest split (usually by time, because next month is what you'll face), a baseline, one GBM, a short error analysis, a one-page write-up. Skip big ensembles: the extra 0.3% rarely beats a model you can retrain and debug in an afternoon.

At scale

The “private leaderboard” is production. Offline CV must mirror it (backtests on past weeks, splits by user), and the final judge is an online A/B test on the business metric (Lessons 8.4, 9.1). Ensembles are judged on latency and maintenance cost as well as accuracy.

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through how you'd approach a new tabular prediction problem.” A strong answer:

  • Starts with framing and the metric, then EDA aimed at the validation design: IDs, dates, train/test differences.
  • Names a baseline first, then a GBM, with a CV that mirrors the test (group or time split) and explains why random K-fold would lie.
  • Mentions leakage checks (features computed after the outcome; aggregates fitted on all rows) and fitting all preprocessing inside a Pipeline.
  • Iterates one change at a time, keeping only gains larger than fold-to-fold noise; ensembles models that disagree using OOF predictions.
  • Ends with error analysis by slice and a write-up; knows public leaderboards (and offline metrics generally) can be overfit by selection, and that adversarial validation checks train/test drift.
🏭 In the real world
Why public leaderboards overfit 🧪 Research · 2015 Blum & Hardt: The Ladder: A Reliable Leaderboard for Machine Learning Competitions

How: analyses Kaggle's public/private split, introduces the boosting attack, and proposes the Ladder: show a participant a new score only when it is significantly better than their previous best.

Why this method: limiting what each submission reveals bounds how much the leaderboard can be overfit, even by an adversary; on real Kaggle submissions the Ladder's rankings stayed very close to Kaggle's.

Adversarial validation 🧰 Practitioner blog · 2016 FastML: Adversarial validation, part one

How: train a classifier to distinguish training rows from test rows; on Kaggle's Santander data, logistic regression and random forests scored AUC ≈ 0.50.

Why this method: one cheap model answers “will my validation set behave like the test?” and, when it won't, points at the columns that differ.

Splitters for grouped and time data 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Cross-validation: evaluating estimator performance

How: GroupKFold, StratifiedGroupKFold, LeaveOneGroupOut and TimeSeriesSplit, plus cross_val_score / cross_validate that refit a whole Pipeline per fold.

Why this method: the user guide's own advice is that data with groups (e.g. several samples per patient) needs group-aware splits to estimate performance on new groups.

Competitions as a training ground 🏢 Kaggle Kaggle competitions

How: hidden test labels, a public board during the contest and a private board at the end; winners often publish their solutions in the discussion forums.

Why this method: a fixed metric and an untouchable test set are the cleanest place to practise the whole loop; reading top write-ups teaches the validation tricks faster than any textbook.

🤯 Fun fact · the leaderboard that underfit

Blum and Hardt checked a real contest, Kaggle's “Photo Quality Prediction”: 12,000 test labels (3,600 public, 8,400 private), 200 teams, 1,830 submissions. There, the top teams' public scores were, if anything, slightly worse than their private ones, and the gap was within normal statistical wobble. Honest teams with good CV don't have to fear the shake-up; it's the ones steering by the public board who fall.

Check yourself

1. Your model is about 85% accurate and the public leaderboard has 2,000 rows. Roughly what is the standard error of a public score?
√(0.85 × 0.15 / 2000) = √0.0000638 ≈ 0.008, i.e. 0.8 points. So two submissions 0.5 points apart on that board are indistinguishable, and the best of many will look about 2 standard errors (1.6 points) better than it is.
2. You predict which patients will be readmitted. The table has one row per hospital visit, and patients appear many times. Test patients are new. Your random 5-fold CV AUC is 0.91; your first submission scores 0.78. What's the most likely cause and fix?
A big CV-vs-test gap with repeated entities is the classic group leak (Playground 1). Group K-fold by patient asks the real question; expect it to land near 0.78. Then check per-patient aggregate features for leakage too.
3. A GBM and a logistic regression both score 0.59 out-of-fold log loss. A teammate also has five GBMs that differ only by seed. Which blend is most likely to help?
Blending helps when errors are different (low correlation), like trees vs a linear model (Playground 3). Seed variants of one GBM are highly correlated, so their average barely moves. Tune the weight on OOF predictions, never on the test or public board.
📝 TL;DR
  • The order: frame → EDA → baseline → CV that mirrors the test → features → GBM + tuning → ensemble → error analysis → write-up.
  • Split the way the world will: group K-fold for repeated entities, time splits for the future. Random K-fold on grouped data can overstate by 15+ points.
  • Selecting the best of many noisy scores selects luck: on a 4,000-row public board, best-of-100 overstates by 1–2 points. Choose by CV; treat the public board as one more noisy fold.
  • Adversarial validation (train vs test classifier, AUC ≈ 0.5 is good) checks whether your CV can be trusted at all.
  • Blend models that disagree, using out-of-fold predictions; slice the errors to find the next feature; write down every number.
