Phase 0 · Lesson 1

What machine learning is: learning a function from examples

Normal programming: you write the rules, the computer applies them. Machine learning (ML): you show the computer examples with the right answers, and it finds the rule itself. This lesson makes that idea concrete, with numbers you can drag.

🎯 Hook: the program that beat its own author

In 1959 Arthur Samuel, an engineer at IBM, published Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers. His claim, in the first lines of the paper: a computer can be programmed so that it will learn to play a better game of checkers than the person who wrote the program, in only 8 or 10 hours of machine-playing time.

He didn't give it a list of good moves. He gave it the rules, a sense of direction, and a list of board measurements “whose correct signs and relative weights are unknown”. Samuel was a modest player himself. So where did the skill come from?

🔮 Predict first

How did Samuel's program end up better than Samuel?

It learned the weights. The program scored a board with a weighted sum of features (piece advantage, mobility, and so on), and after games it nudged the weights toward whatever predicted winning. The paper compares two ways of learning: rote learning (memorize positions it has already scored) and learning by generalization (tune the formula so it works on positions it has never seen). That second idea, a formula with knobs tuned on examples, is this whole course. (Searching every game was, and still is, far too big.)

Numbers first: 12 deliveries

You run a small food-delivery app. A customer asks: “How long for my order? I'm 5 km away.” You have the last 12 orders:

No order was exactly 5 km, and two orders at the same distance (1.1 km) took 6 and 12 minutes. So you can't just look the answer up. You need a rule that turns any distance into a time: a function f(km) → minutes. Writing that function by guessing is programming. Finding it from the examples is machine learning. An algorithm called least squares (Lesson 2.1) finds this one in microseconds:

ŷ = 6.0 + 4.29 × km “Predicted minutes: about 6 minutes to get going, plus about 4.3 minutes for every kilometre.”

For 5 km it says 6.0 + 4.29 × 5 ≈ 27 minutes. The two numbers 6.0 and 4.29 are the model's parameters: nobody typed them in; they were learned from the 12 rows.

💡 Big idea Machine learning = pick a family of functions (here: straight lines), then let an algorithm choose the member of that family that fits the examples best, so that it gives good answers on inputs it has never seen. The examples are the program's “source code”; the learned parameters are the compiled result.
🧠 Analogy: how a child learns “dog”

Nobody gives a toddler a definition of a dog (“four legs, fur, barks, but not a cat, not a fox…”). Try writing that rule: it breaks on the first three-legged dog or the first fluffy cat. Instead the toddler sees hundreds of examples, each with a label (“dog!”, “no, that's a cat”), and builds their own internal rule. Then they correctly name a breed they've never seen before.

That last part is the whole point. Memorizing every dog on your street is easy. Recognizing a new one is generalization, and it's what we'll measure in every lesson of this course.

🤔 Why this? Why learn from examples instead of writing rules?
  1. The problem before. For many tasks nobody can write the rules: what pixel pattern is a face, which words make an email spam, how long a delivery takes in this city. Samuel said it in 1959: lacking such knowledge, you must specify solutions “in minute and exact detail”, which is slow and costly.
  2. What people tried. Hand-written rules, often thousands of them, collected from experts. They work for small, stable problems, but every exception needs another rule, rules start to contradict each other, and when the world changes (spammers adapt, a new neighbourhood opens) someone has to rewrite them by hand.
  3. Why this works. An algorithm can search a whole family of functions for the one that matches the examples. Here, from 12 orders it finds 6.0 + 4.29 × km; on 30 new orders that line is off by 2.95 minutes on average, against 9.77 minutes for the hand rule “always say 30 minutes”. More data? Re-run the algorithm; no one rewrites anything.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need many examples with correct answers, and they must look like the future. A model can memorize instead of learning (you'll break one below), it inherits every bias in its data, and its answers are statistical guesses, not guarantees. Sometimes a simple rule is still the better product (Lesson 0.4).
  5. What came next. A repeatable workflow around the model (Lesson 0.2), many kinds of learning beyond “examples with answers” (0.3), honest testing on held-out data (0.6), and, decades later, deep learning, which also learns the features themselves from raw pixels or text (the sibling Deep Learning course).

Predicting a number (minutes, price) is called regression; predicting a category (spam or not, which digit) is classification. Both are “learn a function from labelled examples”, called supervised learning. Lesson 0.3 tours the other kinds.

Be the learner

Before the algorithm does it, do it yourself. You have two knobs: the minutes it takes to get going (where the line starts) and the minutes per kilometre (how steep it is). Your score is the MAE on the 12 training orders: lower is better.

🔮 Predict first

You tune your line until its training MAE is about as good as the algorithm's. Then 30 brand-new orders arrive. What happens to your MAE on them?

A little worse, and that's normal. The algorithm's line gets 2.48 minutes on the 12 training orders and 2.95 on the 30 new ones. You tuned your knobs to these 12 rows, including their luck. The new-orders number is the honest one: it's what customers will experience. Reveal them below.

✏️ Worked example: one prediction, one error, one score
  • Learned line: ŷ = 6.0 + 4.29 × x. Order 1 was 7.4 km: ŷ = 6.0 + 4.29 × 7.4 = 6.0 + 31.7 = 37.7 minutes.
  • It actually took 39. The error is 39 − 37.7 = 1.3 minutes.
  • Do that for all 12 orders, drop the minus signs, average: MAE = 2.48 minutes. On 30 new orders: 2.95.
  • The hand rule “always 30 minutes” has no knobs to learn. Its MAE on the same 30 new orders: 9.77. Learning from just 12 examples cut the error by more than two thirds.

Rules vs learned functions

Here's the classic case where people first wrote rules: spam. Each email gets two features: the number of links, and the share of the text in CAPITALS. A rule-writer might say if links ≥ 4 or caps ≥ 30%: spam. A learner instead looks at 200 labelled emails and draws its own boundary.

📜 Traditional program

You write: rules. Computer gets: data. Out comes: answers.

def is_spam(links, caps):
    return links >= 4 or caps >= 30

🤖 Machine learning

You give: data + answers. Out comes: the rule (a model).

model = LogisticRegression()
model.fit(X_emails, y_is_spam)   # learn the rule
model.predict([[3, 41]])          # use it

What you should find: the starting rule gets 79%, and with a few tries you can push it to about 93% (the best rule here is links ≥ 4 or caps ≥ 24%), level with the learned model's 94%. With only two features, a carefully tuned rule keeps up. That's honest, and it's why Lesson 0.4 says “try a rule first”. The difference shows up when the world moves. In month 2 that best rule slips to 87% and the month-1 model to 86%; the model gets back to about 92% with one click on fresh labelled emails, while the rule needs a human to notice, investigate and re-tune. Now imagine 500 features instead of 2. Nobody can hand-tune that; an algorithm does it in seconds.

Real spam filters do exactly this at scale: in 2019 Google reported that new TensorFlow-based models were blocking about 100 million additional spam messages a day in Gmail, on top of its existing ML and rules-based protections (source in “In the real world” below). Rules and models usually work together.

Generalization: memorizing isn't learning

Samuel's rote learning memorized board positions. It helped in openings it had seen before, but it couldn't help in a position it had never met. A learning algorithm can fall into the same trap: fit the training examples perfectly by memorizing them, and be lost on anything new. That's called overfitting.

The playground below uses the simplest possible learner: to classify a new point, ask the k nearest training points and take a vote (k-nearest neighbours, Lesson 3.1). With k = 1 it is a pure memorizer: every training point is its own nearest neighbour, so training accuracy is 100%, always.

🔮 Predict first

k = 1 gets 100% on the training points. On 120 new points from the same source, which k will do best?

On this data, k = 1 scores 100% on training but only 79% on new points; k = 5 gets 87%; k = 119 (ask all 120 training points) falls to 75%, because every point gets nearly the same vote. Too flexible memorizes noise; too rigid ignores the pattern. Find the sweet spot yourself.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026) is the standard Python library for classical ML. Every model has the same two verbs, fit (learn from examples) and predict (use the learned function). Lesson 0.5 covers the whole API.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor

km      = np.array([7.4, 1.1, 1.1, 5.7, 6.0, 6.1, 6.0, 4.0, 2.5, 3.1, 4.1, 1.2]).reshape(-1, 1)  # features: one column
minutes = np.array([39, 6, 12, 29, 30, 27, 34, 26, 15, 25, 25, 11])                          # labels

line = LinearRegression().fit(km, minutes)                 # learn the parameters
print(f"minutes ≈ {line.intercept_:.2f} + {line.coef_[0]:.2f} × km")  # 5.99 + 4.29 × km
print(line.predict([[5.0]]))                              # [27.43]
print(mean_absolute_error(minutes, line.predict(km)))      # 2.48 on the training orders

memo = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=1).fit(km, minutes)  # a memorizer, for contrast
print(mean_absolute_error(minutes, memo.predict(km)))      # 0.83: near-perfect on rows it has seen

Note the shape: features are always a 2-D table (rows × columns), even with one column, hence reshape(-1, 1). Labels are a 1-D array. That convention holds across the whole library.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

You have little data and need something today. Ship a rule or a simple average (“delivery = 6 + 4.3 min/km” is already a learned rule!) and log every prediction and outcome. Those logs become the labelled examples for your first real model.

At scale

Millions of labelled examples arrive every day, so models retrain automatically and rules are kept for hard constraints (block known-bad senders, legal limits). The product is a mix: rules for what must never happen, models for everything fuzzy.

🎤 Interview angle

“What is machine learning, and how is it different from regular programming?” A strong answer:

  • Regular code: humans write the rules. ML: an algorithm fits a function from examples (features → label) by choosing parameters that minimize error.
  • Defines the vocabulary precisely: features, labels, parameters vs hyperparameters, training vs prediction, regression vs classification.
  • Says the goal is generalization: performance on unseen data, measured on held-out data, not training accuracy. Mentions overfitting.
  • Knows when not to use it: when a simple rule works, data is scarce, or errors must be fully explainable (Lesson 0.4).
🏭 In the real world
The checkers player that learned 📜 History · 1959 Samuel: Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers (PDF)

How: scored boards with a weighted sum (a “linear polynomial”) of board features and adjusted the weights from play; separately, memorized scored positions (rote learning).

Why this method: checkers is far too big to search to the end, and Samuel couldn't say what the right weights were. Letting the program tune them gave a better player than its author.

Gmail spam filtering 🏢 Google · 2019 Ridding Gmail of 100 million more spam messages with TensorFlow

How: new learned models complement existing ML and rules-based protections, which already block more than 99.9% of spam, phishing and malware.

Why this method: spammers change tactics (image-only messages, brand-new domains) faster than anyone can write rules; models retrain on fresh examples and catch about 100 million more messages a day.

The everyday toolkit 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Getting started

How: dozens of learning algorithms behind the same fit / predict interface, plus tools to split data and measure errors.

Why this method: trying a line, a tree or a forest on the same examples is a one-line change, so you compare learned functions instead of hand-writing them.

🤯 Fun fact · the famous quote isn't in the paper

You'll often read that Samuel defined machine learning as giving computers the ability to learn “without being explicitly programmed”. Search his 1959 paper and that sentence isn't there. What the paper does say is gentler: programming computers to learn from experience should eventually remove the need for much of the detailed programming effort. Sixty-plus years later, that's still the pitch.

Check yourself

1. The learned line is ŷ = 6.0 + 4.29 × km. An order 3 km away took 22 minutes. What is the model's error on it, roughly?
ŷ = 6.0 + 4.29 × 3 = 6.0 + 12.9 = 18.9 minutes. The order took 22, so the error is 22 − 18.9 ≈ 3.1 minutes. A straight line can't pass through every point, so even training examples have errors.
2. A teammate's model scores 100% on the 5,000 emails it was trained on. They want to ship it. What's your first question?
100% on training data can mean pure memorization (like k = 1 in Playground 3). Only accuracy on held-out, unseen examples tells you how it will behave on tomorrow's email. Lesson 0.6 shows how to hold data out properly.
3. Which of these is a hyperparameter rather than a learned parameter?
The slope and Samuel's weights are chosen by the algorithm from the examples: parameters. k is chosen by you before training (ideally by comparing scores on held-out data): a hyperparameter.
📝 TL;DR
  • Machine learning = an algorithm picks a function (from a family you chose) that maps features to labels using examples.
  • The learned numbers are parameters; the knobs you set are hyperparameters. Predicting numbers is regression; categories, classification.
  • From 12 orders, a line cut the error on new orders from 9.8 minutes (a fixed rule) to 3.0.
  • The goal is generalization: good answers on unseen inputs. 100% on training data can be pure memorization.
  • Rules still win when they're simple and stable; models win when patterns are fuzzy, many or moving.
