Phase 0 · Lesson 3

Kinds of learning: supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised, RL

What kind of learning you're doing depends on one question: what feedback do you have? Right answers for every example, for a few, for none, answers hidden inside the data itself, or just a score after you act. Each answer leads to a different family of methods.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An example has features x; a label y is its right answer; a model learns a function x → y (Lesson 0.1).
  • Regression predicts a number, classification a category (Lesson 0.1).
🎯 Hook: 3.3 billion words, zero labels

In 2018 Google released BERT, a language model that set new records on a long list of language-understanding tests. It was pre-trained on the BooksCorpus (800 million words) plus English Wikipedia (2,500 million words).

Supervised learning needs a right answer for every example. Nobody wrote 3.3 billion right answers. Yet BERT was trained with exactly the same machinery as a spam classifier: input, prediction, compare with the label, adjust. Where did the labels come from?

🔮 Predict first

How did BERT get labels for 3.3 billion words of text?

It made its own labels. The paper's data generator picks 15% of the word pieces in each passage, hides them, and asks the model to predict the originals. The text supplies the questions and the answers for free. That's self-supervised learning, and next-word prediction (how GPT-style large language models (LLMs) are pre-trained) is the same trick. You'll build a label factory below.

This course is mostly about the first three rows, done with classical methods: Phases 2–5 are supervised, Phase 6 is unsupervised, Lesson 7.7 is semi-supervised. Self-supervision mostly powers deep learning (the sibling Deep Learning course); RL has a whole sibling course of its own.

💡 Big idea The kind of learning is set by the feedback signal, not by the algorithm. Labels for everything → supervised. No labels → unsupervised (find structure). A few labels → semi-supervised (let the unlabelled data shape the boundary). Labels manufactured from the data itself → self-supervised. A reward for actions → reinforcement learning. Labels are the expensive part, so most of the variety is about needing fewer of them.
🧠 Analogy: five ways to learn a language
  • Supervised: a workbook with an answer key at the back. Every exercise, checked.
  • Unsupervised: a shoebox of foreign postcards and no dictionary. You still notice that some words always appear together, and sort the cards into piles.
  • Semi-supervised: the shoebox, plus a friend who translates six of the postcards. Those six anchor all the piles.
  • Self-supervised: fill-in-the-blank on any book: cover a word, guess it, uncover it. Infinite exercises, no teacher.
  • Reinforcement: moving to the country. Nobody tells you the right sentence; you just notice whether you got the coffee you ordered.
🤔 Why this? Why are there so many kinds of learning?
  1. The problem before. Supervised learning works brilliantly but needs a right answer for every example, and labels are expensive: a radiologist's time, a fraud investigation, a human judging a translation. Most of the world's data has no labels at all.
  2. What people tried. Paying for more labels (crowdsourcing), or giving up on labels and only clustering. Clustering finds groups, but nothing guarantees they're the groups you care about: below, k-means splits two moon shapes along the wrong line and agrees with the true classes only 74% of the time.
  3. Why this works. Unlabelled data still carries the shape of the problem. In the playground, 6 labels alone give 86% on new points; the same 6 labels plus 194 unlabelled points, spread along the shapes, give 100% on the default draw, as good as labelling all 200. Self-supervision goes further: hide part of each example and the data labels itself.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Semi-supervised learning assumes points close together share a label; when classes overlap, propagated labels spread mistakes. Self-supervised pre-training needs huge data and compute. RL needs an environment where trial and error is cheap and safe, and a reward that really means what you want.
  5. What came next. Pre-train on unlabelled data, then fine-tune on a few labels: the recipe behind modern language and vision models (sibling courses). RL fine-tuning of language models from human preferences (the RL course, Phase 7). In this course: semi-supervised and active learning in Lesson 7.7, clustering in Phase 6.

You know the class of only 6 of the 200 points (3 per moon). You can either fit on those 6 alone, or also use the 194 unlabelled points. Does using the unlabelled points help?

On the default draw, 6 labels alone score 86% on new points, and the semi-supervised version 100%. The unlabelled points trace each moon, so a label can “flow” along its moon from neighbour to neighbour. Try a few 🎲 draws: it isn't always a win, which is the honest part.

Self-supervised: a label factory

Self-supervised learning is supervised learning where the labels are cut out of the data itself. Two classic recipes for text: predict the next word from the words before it (GPT-style language models), or hide random words and predict them (BERT's masked words). Images work the same way: hide a patch, predict it.

Reinforcement learning: a score, not an answer

In RL there is no table of right answers. An agent acts in an environment, gets a reward (a number), and must figure out which actions led to it, often many steps later. Two differences from supervised learning matter most:

SupervisedReinforcement
Feedback“The right answer was B.”“You scored 3.” (Which of your 40 moves earned it? Your problem.)
DataA fixed dataset collected beforehandCreated by the agent's own actions: what it tries decides what it learns
Classic exampleSpam filterGame-playing programs, robot control, tuning an LLM from human ratings

Real systems mix kinds. DeepMind's AlphaGo (2016) first learned by supervised learning from human expert games, then improved by reinforcement learning from games against itself, and beat the European Go champion 5 games to 0. Samuel's checkers program (Lesson 0.1) was an early cousin: it learned from the outcomes of its own games. The full story starts at RL 0.1: learning by trial and error, and RL 0.6 asks when a problem really needs RL (usually it doesn't).

✏️ Worked example: how many labels does each kind cost?

You want to sort 1,000,000 support tickets into 8 topics. A human labels about 2 tickets a minute.

  • Fully supervised: label all of them: 1,000,000 ÷ 2 = 500,000 minutes ≈ 8,300 hours. Nobody does this.
  • Supervised on a sample: label 5,000 (about 42 hours), train, predict the rest. The usual first move.
  • Semi-supervised / active: label 1,000 (about 8 hours), let the unlabelled 999,000 shape the model, and have humans label only the tickets the model is least sure about (Lesson 7.7).
  • Unsupervised: cluster all of them for free, then spend an hour naming the clusters. Fast, but the clusters may not match the 8 topics you need.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn covers the classical side of all three main kinds with the same API: classifiers and regressors (supervised), KMeans, DBSCAN (density-based clustering), PCA (principal component analysis: squeeze many columns into a few) (unsupervised), and LabelSpreading, LabelPropagation, SelfTrainingClassifier (semi-supervised, where −1 means “no label”). Self-supervised pre-training lives in deep-learning libraries; RL in libraries such as Gymnasium.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.semi_supervised import LabelSpreading

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.1, random_state=0)
X_new, y_new = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.1, random_state=1)

# Supervised: every training point has a label
sup = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=7).fit(X, y)
print("supervised, 600 labels:", sup.score(X_new, y_new))           # 1.0

# Unsupervised: no labels; k-means finds 2 groups (it can't know which is which)
groups = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
agree = (groups == y).mean()
print("k-means agrees with the hidden labels:", max(agree, 1 - agree))  # 0.748

# Semi-supervised: only 6 labels; -1 marks "unlabelled"
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y_partial = np.full_like(y, -1)
for c in (0, 1):
    keep = rng.choice(np.flatnonzero(y == c), size=3, replace=False)
    y_partial[keep] = c
only6 = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=1).fit(X[y_partial >= 0], y[y_partial >= 0])
print("6 labels only:", only6.score(X_new, y_new))                    # 0.9425
semi = LabelSpreading(kernel="rbf", gamma=20).fit(X, y_partial)
print("6 labels + 594 unlabelled:", semi.score(X_new, y_new))       # 0.9975

Same story as the playground: clustering finds the wrong split, 6 labels alone are decent, 6 labels plus the unlabelled shape are nearly perfect. With overlapping classes the gain shrinks or vanishes, so always compare against the labelled-only model on held-out data.

⚖️ Trade-offs
Kind✅ Gains❌ Costs
SupervisedDirectly optimizes the thing you care about; easy to evaluateNeeds many labels; labels can be noisy, biased or slow to arrive
UnsupervisedNo labels; finds structure and anomalies you didn't know to look forNo “right answer” to check against; groups may not mean anything useful
Semi-supervisedFew labels go a long way when classes follow the data's shapeSpreads mistakes when classes overlap; harder to validate
Self-supervisedUnlimited free labels; learns general-purpose representationsHuge data and compute; the pretext task isn't your task, so you still fine-tune
ReinforcementLearns sequences of decisions and can exceed human playNeeds a safe, fast environment and a well-designed reward; sample-hungry; unstable
🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Almost always supervised: find a label you already have (a cancellation, a refund, a click), or label a few hundred examples yourself in an afternoon. Use a pre-trained model's embeddings as features rather than training anything self-supervised. Cluster only to explore.

At scale

Labels become a budget line: labelling teams, active learning to choose what to label, weak labels from rules. Big labs pre-train self-supervised models on enormous data and others fine-tune them. RL shows up in ranking, ads and LLM fine-tuning, where feedback arrives as clicks or ratings.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between supervised, unsupervised and self-supervised learning? Where does RL fit?” A strong answer:

  • Frames it by the feedback signal: labels for every example, none, labels made from the data, or rewards for actions.
  • Gives one concrete example each, and splits supervised into classification and regression.
  • Explains self-supervision as supervised learning with manufactured labels (masked or next-word prediction), followed by fine-tuning.
  • Notes that RL's data depends on the agent's own actions and that rewards are delayed, which is why it's harder.
  • Mentions semi-supervised and active learning as answers to expensive labels.
🏭 In the real world
BERT: masked-word pre-training 🧪 Research · 2018 Devlin et al.: BERT (arXiv)

How: hide 15% of the word pieces in each passage of BooksCorpus and Wikipedia (3.3 billion words) and train the model to predict them; then fine-tune on small labelled datasets.

Why this method: labelled language datasets are small, raw text is nearly unlimited, and the fill-in-the-blank task forces the model to learn how language works.

AlphaGo 🧪 Research · 2016 Silver et al.: Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search (Nature)

How: supervised learning from human expert games, then reinforcement learning from games of self-play, combined with tree search.

Why this method: human games give a fast, good start; self-play provides unlimited new experience with a clear reward (win or lose), letting it go beyond its teachers. It beat the European champion 5–0.

Semi-supervised learning in scikit-learn 🧰 Open source User guide: semi-supervised learning

How: self-training (a classifier labels its own most confident predictions) and label propagation / spreading over a similarity graph; unlabelled rows get the label −1.

Why this method: plugs into the same fit / predict API, so you can test whether your unlabelled data actually helps.

🤯 Fun fact · BERT doesn't always use [MASK]

Of the 15% of positions BERT picks to predict, only 80% are actually replaced by the [MASK] token; 10% are swapped for a random word and 10% are left unchanged. Why? The [MASK] token never appears when the model is later used on real text, so always masking would teach it a habit it can't use. Keeping the model unsure which words are real makes it pay attention to every word.

Check yourself

1. A 200-word paragraph is turned into masked-word training examples by hiding 15% of the words. Roughly how many words are hidden, and how many human labels did it take?
15% × 200 = 30 words become prediction targets, and the “labels” are just the original words, which were already in the text. No human was involved. That's why self-supervision scales to billions of words.
2. A bank has 5 million card transactions and investigators have confirmed 3,000 as fraud or not. Which approach should you try first, and what should you compare it against?
3,000 labels is enough for a first supervised model. The 5 million unlabelled transactions can help via semi-supervised learning or an unsupervised anomaly score as an extra feature (Lesson 6.7). Whatever you add, check on held-out labelled data that it beats the labelled-only model.
3. k-means splits your customers into 5 clusters. A manager asks: “What's the accuracy of these clusters?” What's the honest answer?
Unsupervised learning has no answer key. You can measure how tight and separated clusters are (Lesson 6.1), whether they're stable across samples, and whether marketing can act on them. In the playground we could score k-means only because we secretly had the labels.
📝 TL;DR
  • The feedback decides the kind: labels for all (supervised: classification or regression), none (unsupervised), a few (semi-supervised), made from the data (self-supervised), rewards for actions (RL).
  • Clusters aren't classes: k-means agreed with the true moons only 74% of the time.
  • 6 labels plus unlabelled points reached 100%, versus 86% for the 6 alone, when classes follow the data's shape.
  • Self-supervision manufactures labels (next word, masked word): BERT learned from 3.3 billion unlabelled words.
  • Real systems mix kinds: AlphaGo was supervised first, then reinforcement learning.
