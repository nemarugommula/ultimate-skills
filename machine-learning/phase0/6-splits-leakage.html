Phase 0 · Lesson 6

Train, validation & test splits, and data leakage

A model's score is only worth something if it was measured on data the model, and you, never used to make choices. This lesson splits data three ways, shows how quietly “peeking” inflates scores, and walks through the leaks that have fooled hundreds of published papers.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Generalization: what counts is the score on unseen data; 100% on training data can be memorization (Lesson 0.1).
  • fit only on training data; everything else gets transform / predict / score (Lesson 0.5).
  • A hyperparameter is a knob you choose, like k in k-nearest neighbours (Lesson 0.1).
🎯 Hook: 294 papers, 17 fields

In 2023 Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan of Princeton published Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science in the journal Patterns. Surveying fields that adopted ML, they found 17 fields where leakage had been found, collectively affecting 294 papers, in some cases leading to wildly overoptimistic conclusions.

Then they checked one field themselves: civil war prediction, where complex ML models were believed to vastly outperform old-fashioned logistic regression. Once the errors were corrected, the complex models did not perform substantively better than decades-old logistic regression.

These were careful researchers, peer-reviewed. What kind of mistake survives all that?

🔮 Predict first

What does “leakage” mean here?

Leakage is when the model (or the modeller) gets information during training that it won't have when it's really used. The code runs, the numbers look great, and nobody notices until the model meets truly new data. Kapoor and Narayanan list eight types, from textbook errors (no test set, preprocessing on all the data) to subtle ones (time and non-independent samples). You'll cause five of them below.

Numbers first: three piles of data, three jobs

You have 10,000 labelled rows. Split them once, at the start, before you look closely:

train 6,000
validation 2,000
test 2,000
PileIts jobHow often you use it
Training setfit: the model learns its parameters here; preprocessing learns its statistics hereConstantly
Validation setChoose things: hyperparameters, features, which model. Score many candidates, keep the bestMany times
Test setOne final, honest estimate of how the chosen model will do on new dataOnce, at the end

Why two held-out piles? Because choosing is a kind of fitting. Each time you pick the best of several options by their validation score, a bit of the validation set's luck gets baked into your choice. After 50 comparisons, the validation score is optimistic. The test set, untouched, is what tells you the truth. (Cross-validation, Lesson 5.1, reuses the training data cleverly so you don't need a large separate validation pile.)

💡 Big idea A score is honest only if the data behind it played no part in any decision: not in fitting the model, not in fitting preprocessing, not in choosing features or hyperparameters, and not via copies, relatives or the future. Split first, then do everything else on the training part. Anything that “sees” the test data before the final score is leakage.
🧠 Analogy: practice problems, mock exam, final exam

You learn from practice problems (training). You take mock exams to decide how to study and which tricks work (validation). The final exam (test) tells you what you really know. If someone slips you the final's questions in advance, you'll ace it, and learn nothing about how you'll do on the job. If you take the same mock exam 30 times, your mock score stops meaning anything too. Leakage is every way the final exam's questions can reach you early.

🤔 Why this? Why a separate test set, used once?
  1. The problem before. Reporting accuracy on the training data. A model that memorizes scores 100% there (Lesson 0.1), so the number says nothing about new data. Kapoor and Narayanan's first leakage type is exactly this: no test set.
  2. What people tried. One train/test split, but then trying many models and settings and reporting the best test score. That's still peeking: the test set picked the winner, so its score is the winner's lucky draw.
  3. Why this works. A held-out set that took no part in any decision is an unbiased sample of new data. How much peeking hurts: score 100 models that are pure coin flips on a 100-row test set and the best one gets about 63% on average, though each is really 50%. Re-scored on fresh data, it's back to 50%. Playground 1 lets you watch it.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Held-out rows aren't used for training, and small test sets are noisy: at 90% accuracy on 100 test rows, the 95% margin of error is about ±6 points. A test set also fails silently if it isn't independent of training (duplicates, the same patient twice, the future in the past).
  5. What came next. Cross-validation to use data more efficiently (Lesson 5.1), group- and time-aware splits (5.1, 7.1), Pipelines that make preprocessing leak-proof (1.6), and checklists such as Kapoor and Narayanan's model info sheets and the REFORMS checklist for scientific ML.

Peeking: how tuning on the test set inflates the score

Here's the cleanest possible experiment. The labels are pure coin flips: no model can do better than 50% on new data. You try many “models” (each is just random guessing) and keep whichever scores best on your test set.

🔮 Predict first

You try 100 random-guessing models on a 100-row test set and keep the best. What test score does the winner report, typically?

About 63% (the average over many repeats is 62.6%; with 1,000 models it's 66%). Each model's score wobbles around 50% by about ±5 points just from luck, and the maximum of 100 wobbles is far out in the tail. A fresh holdout set, never used for choosing, brings the winner straight back to about 50%.

✏️ Worked example: how big should the test set be?
  • A model that is truly 90% accurate, scored on n test rows, gets a score that wobbles by about √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ n). For n = 100 that's √0.0009 = 0.03, so the 95% range is about ±1.96 × 0.03 ≈ ±6 points: anything from 84% to 96%.
  • For n = 2,000: √(0.09 ÷ 2,000) ≈ 0.0067, so about ±1.3 points. Now 90% vs 92% is a real difference.
  • Rule of thumb: the test set must be big enough that the differences you care about are bigger than this wobble. The margin-of-error math is in Math 4.7.

Five leaks you can cause yourself

Peeking is one leak. The others hide in the data pipeline. Each mode below runs the same experiment twice: the leaky way a hurried analyst might do it, and the honest way. The gap is how much the leak lies.

🔮 Predict first

A dataset of 100 rows has 1,000 columns of pure random noise and random labels. You pick the 10 columns most correlated with the label (using all 100 rows), then split 50/50, train, and test. What test accuracy do you get?

Around 75% on pure noise: 78% on the lab's default draw, 73% averaged over 20 draws, against about 51% when done honestly. With 1,000 noise columns, a few will match the labels by chance on all 100 rows, test rows included. Choosing them used the test labels. It's one of the oldest traps in the book; The Elements of Statistical Learning calls it “the wrong way to do cross-validation”.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In scikit-learn: train_test_split (twice, for three piles; stratify=y keeps class shares equal), Pipeline so preprocessing and selection are refit inside each training fold, GroupKFold / GroupShuffleSplit for grouped rows, TimeSeriesSplit for time order. The snippet reproduces the feature-selection leak on pure noise.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(200, 5000))       # 200 rows of pure noise...
y = rng.integers(0, 2, size=200)       # ...and random labels: the honest accuracy is 50%

# Train / validation / test: split twice (60 / 20 / 20)
X_trval, X_test, y_trval, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=0)
X_train, X_val, y_train, y_val = train_test_split(X_trval, y_trval, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)
print(len(X_train), len(X_val), len(X_test))           # 120 40 40

# ❌ Leaky: pick the 20 "best" columns using ALL rows, then cross-validate
X_sel = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit_transform(X, y)
leaky = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(), X_sel, y, cv=5).mean()

# ✅ Honest: selection is a step inside the pipeline, refit on each training fold only
pipe = make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression())
honest = cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean()
print(f"leaky {leaky:.2f}   honest {honest:.2f}")   # leaky 0.79   honest 0.51

# Rows that belong together (same patient, same user) must stay on one side: GroupKFold
# Time-ordered data: always train on the past, test on the future: TimeSeriesSplit
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(50), 4)                    # 50 patients × 4 scans each
print(next(GroupKFold(n_splits=5).split(X, y, groups))[1][:8])  # test rows come in whole patients
print([len(te) for _, te in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4).split(X)])   # [40, 40, 40, 40], each after its training rows

79% on random labels, from one misplaced line. The scikit-learn guide Common pitfalls states the rule as: never call fit on the test data.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Split by time (train on older months, test on the latest) and by customer if rows repeat. Put preprocessing in a Pipeline. Write the test set to its own file and don't open it until the end. Small data? Use cross-validation for choices and keep a modest test set anyway.

At scale

The ultimate test set is the future: shadow deployments and A/B tests measure the model on live traffic it couldn't have seen. Teams keep frozen benchmark sets, review feature lists for anything created after the prediction time, and check overlap between train and test (hashes of rows, user IDs) automatically.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's data leakage, and how do you prevent it?” A strong answer:

  • Defines it: information unavailable at prediction time (test data, the future, the label itself) influencing training or model choice, giving optimistic scores.
  • Lists types: preprocessing or feature selection on all data, target leakage, duplicates/groups across splits, time leakage, tuning on the test set.
  • Prevention: split first; Pipelines; time-based and group-based splits; a feature-availability review; test set used once.
  • Detection: suspiciously high scores, one feature dominating importance, big drops in production. Mentions a real case (Kapoor and Narayanan's 294 papers, or hospital shortcuts in X-ray models).
🏭 In the real world
Leakage across science 🧪 Research · 2023 Kapoor & Narayanan: Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in ML-based science (Patterns)

How: surveyed fields using ML (17 fields, 294 affected papers), built an eight-type taxonomy of leakage, proposed model info sheets, and re-ran civil-war prediction studies.

Why this method: with leakage fixed, complex models lost their advantage over logistic regression. Their running list has since grown (41 papers from 30 fields, 648 affected papers, as of the May 2024 update on their site).

The X-ray model that recognized hospitals 🧪 Research · 2018 Zech et al.: Variable generalization performance of a deep learning model to detect pneumonia (PLOS Medicine)

How: 158,323 chest X-rays from three hospital systems. Networks could tell which hospital an image came from with over 99.9% accuracy, and pneumonia was far more common at one of them (34% vs about 1%), so merely sorting images by hospital already ranked pneumonia cases well.

Why this method: models trained and tested on pooled hospitals looked better internally than on a held-out hospital. Hold out whole sites (groups), not random images.

COVID-19 imaging models 🧪 Research · 2021 Roberts et al.: Common pitfalls and recommendations for using ML to detect COVID-19 (Nature Machine Intelligence)

How: systematic review of 2,212 studies from 2020; 61 passed quality screening.

Why this method: none of the models was judged of potential clinical use, because of methodological flaws and/or underlying biases. A sobering base rate for “great results on a random split”.

The library's own warning 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Common pitfalls and recommended practices

How: worked examples of inconsistent preprocessing and of leakage during feature selection, each fixed with a Pipeline.

Why this method: the tool can't stop you calling fit on the wrong data, but a Pipeline makes the right order automatic.

🤯 Fun fact · the leak was a metal token in the corner

When the X-ray study's authors looked at which pixels drove the network's decisions, they found it had learned to spot a metal token that radiology technicians place on the patient, in the corner of the image, which appeared in a site-specific way. The networks could also tell which department within a hospital an image came from, and departments differ a lot in how sick their patients are. The model didn't need to look at the lungs to look good. A leak doesn't have to be a column in a table; it can be a corner of an image.

Check yourself

1. You have 5,000 rows and want a 70 / 15 / 15 train / validation / test split. With train_test_split, what test_size do you pass in the second split (on the 85% left after removing the test set)?
First split: test_size=0.15 → 750 test, 4,250 left. Validation should be 750 of those 4,250: 750 ÷ 4,250 ≈ 0.176. Passing 0.15 again would give only 0.15 × 4,250 ≈ 638 validation rows.
2. A hospital readmission model scores 0.97 on its test set. Its most important feature is “number of discharge-planning notes written”. Most such notes are written after a readmission is scheduled. What's going on?
Ask of every feature: would I know it at the moment I make the prediction? Here the answer is no, so drop it and rebuild features from data timestamped before the prediction point. Expect a lower, honest score.
3. You compared 40 model settings on the test set and picked the best, which scored 0.88. Your manager asks what accuracy to expect in production. What's the honest answer?
The best of 40 scores includes the winner's luck on that particular test set, just like Playground 1. The fix: choose with a validation set or cross-validation, and score the final choice once on data that played no part.
📝 TL;DR
  • Three piles: train (fit), validation (choose), test (one final honest score). Split first, before looking.
  • Choosing is fitting: the best of 100 coin-flip models scores about 63% on a 100-row test set and 50% on fresh data.
  • Leaks: preprocessing or selection on all data (pure noise scored 78% here), target-leaking features, duplicates and groups across splits, random splits of time series, tuning on test.
  • Fixes: Pipelines, group and time splits, a “known at prediction time?” review of every feature, test set used once.
  • It's not a beginner's problem: 294 papers in 17 fields were affected (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023).
