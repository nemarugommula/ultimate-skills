Recap: Distance & probability models

Four classic models that think about data differently: kNN asks the neighbours, naive Bayes counts evidence, SVMs build the widest street, and kernels bend that street without ever leaving linear algebra. Here's the phase on one page, a game where you guess which model drew a boundary, a system to design, and the lab.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🏘️

3.1 k-nearest neighbours

Vote of the k closest points. k = 1 → 100% train (jagged); large k → blurry. Scale first. Nearest ÷ farthest: 0.03 in 2-D, 0.9 in 1,000-D.

No training, all cost at prediction (O(n·d) per query). Wine: 69% → 95% with a scaler. At scale: trees in low dimensions, approximate (ANN) indexes such as HNSW for embeddings.

🧰 KNeighborsClassifier / Regressor, NearestNeighbors, Faiss, Annoy

📨

3.2 Naive Bayes

log-odds = base rate + Σ word scores, score = log P(w | spam) / P(w | ham). α = 1 (Laplace) so no word vetoes.

“Independent given the class” is wrong, so probabilities are overconfident (81% → 97% confidence, same accuracy), but rankings and decisions are often right. Training is counting.

🧰 MultinomialNB, ComplementNB, BernoulliNB, GaussianNB

🛣️

3.3 Support vector machines

Street width 2/‖w‖. Only support vectors matter. ½‖w‖² + C·Σ hinge: small C = wide street, more support vectors.

Hinge loss is 0 beyond margin 1, so far points have no say and there are no probabilities: calibrate (CalibratedClassifierCV). Always scale (66% → 98% on wine).

🧰 SVC, LinearSVC, SGDClassifier(loss="hinge"), LIBSVM / LIBLINEAR

🌀

3.4 Kernels

K(x, y) = φ(x)·φ(y) without building φ: (x·y)² = degree-2 features. RBF exp(−γ‖x − y‖²): reach ≈ 1/√γ.

Rings + z = x² + y² → a flat plane separates them. Large γ memorizes (all points support vectors). n² kernel values: beyond tens of thousands of rows use Nystroem / random features or trees.

🧰 SVC(kernel="rbf"), Nystroem, RBFSampler, GaussianProcessRegressor

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “You have 5,000 labelled rows with 20 numeric features and a boundary you suspect is curved. Compare kNN, naive Bayes and an RBF SVM for this problem: how does each one decide, what preprocessing does each need, what would you tune, and what goes wrong if the data grows to 5 million rows?”

A strong answer covers: kNN votes among neighbours (scale, tune k and weights, dies with irrelevant features and high dimensions, prediction cost O(n·d) so ANN or trees at scale); naive Bayes multiplies per-feature likelihoods (no scaling for Gaussian NB, fast, but fails on correlated features and gives overconfident probabilities); the RBF SVM builds a max-margin boundary from support vectors (scale, tune C and γ together on a log grid, calibrate for probabilities, training at least O(n²) so at 5 million rows switch to kernel approximations + linear models or boosted trees).

🏗️ Build it yourself: the support-ticket router

✏️ The problem

A SaaS company receives 1,500 support tickets a day and has 6,000 labelled past tickets in 12 categories (billing, login, bug, feature request…), the largest category 30% of tickets and the smallest 2%. The vocabulary after cleaning is about 15,000 words. They want: (1) automatic routing, but only when the model is at least 90% sure, otherwise a human triages; (2) a “similar past tickets” panel for agents; (3) retraining every night on a small CPU box; (4) under 50 ms per ticket.

Using this phase's models, choose features, models, a validation plan and the numbers that matter. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Requirements, restated

  • A classifier with trustworthy probabilities (the 90% rule), good on rare categories, cheap to retrain.
  • A nearest-neighbour search over past tickets for the “similar tickets” panel.

Features

  • Text → TfidfVectorizer (TF-IDF: word counts reweighted so rare, telling words count more, Lesson 7.5) (words and 2-word phrases, min_df=2): sparse, about 15,000+ columns. Fit it inside the pipeline so cross-validation folds don't leak vocabulary (Lesson 0.6).
  • For the similar-tickets panel: either the same TF-IDF vectors with cosine distance, or sentence embeddings (say 384 dimensions).

Models, with why

  1. Baseline: ComplementNB (3.2). Trains in well under a second by counting, handles the 30% vs 2% imbalance better than plain multinomial NB. Its probabilities are overconfident, so it's a baseline, not the router.
  2. Main model: LinearSVC (3.3) on TF-IDF. Sparse text is usually close to linearly separable; liblinear trains on 6,000 × 15,000 sparse rows in seconds. A kernel SVM would need a 6,000 × 6,000 kernel matrix (288 MB as 8-byte floats) for little gain on sparse text.
  3. Probabilities: CalibratedClassifierCV around the LinearSVC (Platt scaling, cross-validated), because the 90% rule needs “0.9” to mean 90% (Lesson 5.6).
  4. Similar tickets: kNN search (3.1). 6,000 vectors × 384 dimensions × 4 bytes ≈ 9.2 MB; brute force is about 2.3 million multiplications per query, well under a millisecond. No ANN index needed until the archive grows to millions.

Validation plan

  • Stratified 5-fold cross-validation (Lesson 5.1) to tune C (log grid) and the TF-IDF settings; report macro-averaged F1 (F1 = harmonic mean of precision and recall, averaged over categories) so the 2% category counts as much as the 30% one (Lesson 5.3).
  • Better still, hold out the most recent month as the test set: new products and new bugs change the vocabulary (Lesson 7.1).
  • For the routing rule: on held-out data, measure the share of tickets above 0.9 (coverage) and the accuracy among them (it should be ≥ 90%); plot both against the threshold.

Numbers to report

  • Macro F1 of ComplementNB vs calibrated LinearSVC; coverage and accuracy at the 0.9 threshold; per-category recall (who gets misrouted).
  • Latency: TF-IDF transform + one sparse dot product per class, a few milliseconds per ticket.

Classic mistakes

  • Trusting raw naive Bayes or SVM scores as probabilities for the 90% rule.
  • Fitting the vectorizer on all tickets before splitting (vocabulary leak), or shuffling across time.
  • Judging by accuracy: 30% “billing” makes a lazy model look fine.
  • An RBF SVM “because it's more powerful”: slower, more knobs, rarely better on sparse text.

What this design doesn't cover yet: tickets with several categories (multi-label), drift as the product changes (Lesson 8.6), and embeddings fine-tuned for your tickets (the sibling AI Engineer course).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. (3.2) In training, “invoice” appears 3 times among 100 spam words and 12 times among 200 ham words. Ignoring smoothing, what is its log-odds score, log P(w | spam) / P(w | ham)?
P(invoice | spam) = 3/100 = 0.03, P(invoice | ham) = 12/200 = 0.06. The ratio is 0.5 and log 0.5 = −0.69: it nudges toward ham. Counting raw occurrences (3 vs 12) would mislead you; normalize by each class's word total.
2. (3.1) A 5-NN model searches 2 million product embeddings of 256 dimensions for every page view, and latency is too high. Best first move?
Brute force is 2,000,000 × 256 ≈ 512 million multiplications per query. KD-trees degrade beyond about 20 dimensions (the curse again). ANN indexes are built for exactly this: millions of high-dimensional vectors, millisecond queries, high recall.
3. (3.3) You retrain a linear SVM after deleting 500 training rows. None of them were support vectors. What changes?
Non-support vectors have αᵢ = 0 and hinge loss 0, so they don't appear in w = Σ αᵢyᵢxᵢ. Removing them leaves the same optimization problem with the same solution (Lesson 3.3's 🗑 button). Logistic regression, by contrast, would change a little.
4. (3.4) An RBF SVM gets 100% train and 68% validation accuracy, and 97% of training rows are support vectors. Which change most likely helps?
Almost every point being a support vector, plus perfect training accuracy, means each point has its own narrow hill: γ is too large. Lower γ so each support vector reaches further, and search C and γ together with cross-validation.
5. (all) Which of these models need standardized features to work well: kNN, Gaussian naive Bayes, RBF SVM?
kNN and RBF kernels compute distances across features, so big-unit features dominate (wine: kNN 69% → 95%, SVC 66% → 98% with a scaler). Gaussian NB models each feature separately in its own units, so rescaling a feature doesn't change its decisions. Trees (Phase 4) don't need scaling either.
🌉 What's next: Phase 4, trees and ensembles

This phase's models all depend on geometry (distances, margins, dot products) or on independence, so they need scaled, numeric, mostly relevant features. Decision trees ask yes/no questions about one feature at a time instead: no scaling, mixed types, interactions for free (4.1). Then you'll average hundreds of them (random forests, 4.2), let each fix the last one's mistakes (boosting, 4.3), meet XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost (4.4), and see why boosted trees still rule tabular data (4.6). Keep kNN, naive Bayes and SVMs as fast baselines and as the right tool for text, small dense data and similarity search.
