Phase 3 · Lesson 3

Support vector machines: build the widest street

Many straight lines separate two classes. An SVM picks the one with the widest empty street around it, and the result depends only on the handful of points standing at the kerb. That idea, plus the kernel trick of the next lesson, made SVMs one of the most popular classifiers of the late 1990s and 2000s.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A linear classifier computes a score f(x) = w·x + b and predicts by its sign; the boundary is where the score is 0 (Lesson 2.3, Math 1.3).
  • Loss + regularization: fit = minimize mistakes plus a penalty on large weights (Lesson 2.2).
  • Scaling features so no column dominates just by its units (Lessons 1.4 and 3.1).
🎯 Hook: second place out of 179

In 2014, a research team ran 179 classifiers from 17 families on 121 datasets to answer a blunt question: do we really need hundreds of classifiers? Random forests came first, reaching on average 94.1% of the best accuracy achieved on each dataset. Second, with a difference that wasn't statistically significant, was a support vector machine with a Gaussian kernel running on the LIBSVM library: 92.3%.

The SVM was invented at AT&T Bell Labs in the early 1990s by Vladimir Vapnik and colleagues, and its central idea fits on a napkin. Here's a puzzle that captures it: train an SVM on 24 points, then delete 21 of them and retrain. Nothing changes. Not the line, not a single prediction. Why?

🔮 Predict first

Two clouds of points are perfectly separable by a straight line. You drag one point that sits far from the boundary, on its correct side, even farther away. What happens to the SVM's line?

Nothing at all. The SVM's line is decided only by the points on the edge of the street, the support vectors. A point beyond the street has zero loss and zero influence. Logistic regression, by contrast, shifts a little with every point. Try it in Playground 1.

💡 Big idea Among all lines that separate the classes, choose the one with the largest margin: the widest street with no points in it. The street's edges are where the score f(x) = ±1. Only the points on (or inside) the edges, the support vectors, determine the solution. When classes overlap, allow some points inside the street or on the wrong side, each at a price set by C: that's the soft margin, and the loss it uses is the hinge loss.
🧠 Analogy: building a road between two villages

Two villages sit on a plain and you must build a straight road between them, as wide as possible, without demolishing any house. Where the road goes is decided only by the few houses closest to it: they touch the kerb on either side. The farmhouse a kilometre away doesn't matter; move it anywhere (on its own side) and the road stays.

A wide road is also a safe road: a new house built slightly off from where the old ones are still lands on the correct side. That's why a big margin tends to generalize: the decision isn't balanced on a knife-edge between the training points.

🤔 Why this? Why support vector machines?
  1. The problem before. If two classes are separable, infinitely many lines separate them with 100% training accuracy. The perceptron (late 1950s) stops at whichever one it bumps into first, and a line that just grazes the training points will misclassify new points that land a little off.
  2. What people tried. Logistic regression picks a line by maximizing likelihood, but on separable data its weights grow without limit unless regularized, and every point (even far, easy ones) keeps pulling on the line. Neural networks of the time were hard to train and had many local minima.
  3. Why this works. Maximize the margin: one convex problem with one best answer, decided by the boundary points only. In Playground 1, three support vectors fix a street 2.68 wide; a new point at (2, 1) gets score 0.643·2 + 0.378·1 − 0.141 = 1.52, beyond the +1 edge, a confident class 1. A wider margin means fewer ways to overfit, which Vapnik's theory turned into error bounds.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Training a kernel SVM costs at least quadratic time in the number of rows (scikit-learn warns it gets impractical beyond tens of thousands of samples). It needs scaled features, outputs scores rather than probabilities, and you must tune C (and kernel settings). On heavily overlapping data, many points become support vectors and the model loses its compactness.
  5. What came next. The kernel trick (Lesson 3.4) made SVMs draw curved boundaries. Linear SVMs trained by fast coordinate descent (LIBLINEAR) scaled to millions of rows of text. Then gradient-boosted trees (4.3) took over tabular data and deep networks took over images and text, but the hinge loss and the margin idea live on in many models.

Step 1: the widest street

In words: keep every point at least one unit of score away from the boundary, and make the weights as small as possible, because small weights mean the score changes slowly, so “one unit of score” is a wide distance.

minimize ½‖w‖²   such that   yᵢ · (w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1 for every training point (y = ±1) “Find the smallest weights such that every point scores at least +1 on its own side. The street between score −1 and +1 is then 2/‖w‖ wide.”

This is a constrained optimization problem. Solving it with Lagrange multipliers gives one multiplier αᵢ per training point, and αᵢ is non-zero only for support vectors: Math 7.5: constraints shows the machinery. The solution is w = Σ αᵢyᵢxᵢ, a weighted sum of support vectors only.

✏️ Worked example: score, side and distance

With w = (0.643, 0.378) and b = −0.141, take a new point x = (2, 1):

  • Score: f(x) = 0.643 × 2 + 0.378 × 1 − 0.141 = 1.523. Positive → class 1 (■).
  • It's beyond the +1 edge, so outside the street. Its distance from the boundary is f / ‖w‖ = 1.523 / 0.746 = 2.04; the street's half-width is 1 / 0.746 = 1.34.
  • If this were a training point, its hinge loss would be max(0, 1 − 1.523) = 0: it has no say in where the line goes.

Step 2: the soft margin and the C knob

Real classes overlap, so no empty street exists. The soft-margin SVM lets points into the street or across the line, but charges for it: a point with score margin y·f(x) below 1 pays 1 − y·f(x). C sets the price of those violations against the width of the street.

minimize ½‖w‖² + C · Σi max(0, 1 − yᵢ·f(xᵢ)) “Keep the street wide (small weights), plus C times the total amount by which points intrude into the street.”
🔮 Predict first

You lower C from 1 to 0.01 (violations become 100× cheaper). What happens to the street and the number of support vectors?

Wider street, more support vectors. Cheap violations mean the SVM happily lets points into a wide street, and every point in the street is a support vector. On the overlapping data below, C = 1 gives a street 1.44 wide with 30 support vectors; C = 0.01 gives 3.97 and 67. Small C = strong regularization.

On this noisy data the widest streets test best: C = 0.01 gives 85% on test, C = 1 gives 83%, and C = 100 drops to 80%. Small C averages over many points (stable); large C chases the few points near the line (jumpy). Like k in kNN, you choose C by cross-validation, usually on a log grid (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100).

Both losses are convex stand-ins for the 0-1 loss (which has no useful slope to optimize). The difference is the flat part: beyond the kerb (m ≥ 1) the hinge loss is exactly zero, so those points exert zero pull, and that's why only support vectors matter. The logistic loss never quite reaches zero, so every point keeps pulling a little and the model gives you probabilities; the SVM gives you a sparse, margin-based score. With strong regularization the two often draw very similar lines.

Step 4: always scale before an SVM

The margin is measured in feature units. If one feature is in the thousands and another in fractions, “wide street” means “wide in the big feature”, and the small feature barely counts (it's the kNN problem from Lesson 3.1 again). The LIBSVM authors' own practical guide puts it bluntly: scaling before applying an SVM is very important.

An SVM outputs a score (decision_function), the signed distance to the boundary in units of the street half-width. It is not a probability. To get one, fit a small sigmoid that maps scores to probabilities on held-out data: Platt scaling (Platt, 1999). In scikit-learn 1.9 the old SVC(probability=True) switch, which ran Platt scaling with internal 5-fold cross-validation, is deprecated (to be removed in 1.11); the documented replacement is CalibratedClassifierCV(SVC(), ensemble=False). Calibration in general is Lesson 5.6.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): SVC, LinearSVC, SVR/LinearSVR for regression, NuSVC, and OneClassSVM for anomaly detection (Lesson 6.7). Always inside a Pipeline with a scaler.

from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC, LinearSVC

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

# Linear SVM: fast, a strong baseline for many features (e.g. text)
lin = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LinearSVC(C=1.0)).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("LinearSVC", round(lin.score(X_te, y_te), 3))

# Kernel SVM: tune C on a log grid (the kernel itself is Lesson 3.4)
svc = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="linear"))
grid = GridSearchCV(svc, {"svc__C": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100]}, cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
best = grid.best_estimator_
print(grid.best_params_, "support vectors:", best[-1].n_support_.sum(), "of", len(X_tr))
print(best.decision_function(X_te[:3]).round(2))        # scores, not probabilities

# Probabilities: Platt scaling with cross-validation (replaces SVC(probability=True), deprecated in 1.9)
cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="linear", C=0.1)), method="sigmoid", cv=5, ensemble=False)
print(cal.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict_proba(X_te[:3]).round(3))

Useful attributes: support_ (indices of support vectors), n_support_ (per class), dual_coef_ (αᵢyᵢ), and coef_ for linear kernels. For imbalanced classes use class_weight="balanced" (Lesson 5.5). Multiclass works out of the box: SVC trains one-vs-one, LinearSVC one-vs-rest.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

For text classification with TF-IDF features (word counts reweighted so rare words count more, Lesson 7.5), LinearSVC is a fast, strong baseline that is hard to beat without deep learning. For a small, dense, numeric dataset (a few thousand rows), a scaled RBF SVC with a small grid over C and gamma is a solid contender next to a random forest.

At scale

Kernel SVMs rarely survive millions of rows; teams use linear SVMs or logistic regression (liblinear, stochastic gradient descent) for sparse, huge data, boosted trees for tabular data and neural networks for images and text. The hinge loss shows up inside other systems, and one-class SVMs appear in anomaly detection.

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain SVMs. What are support vectors and what does C do?” A strong answer:

  • Maximize the margin: the widest street between classes, width 2/‖w‖; equivalent to minimizing ‖w‖² subject to every point scoring at least 1 on its side.
  • Support vectors are the points on or inside the margin; the solution is a weighted sum of them only (non-zero dual coefficients αᵢ). Points beyond the margin don't affect it.
  • Soft margin: ½‖w‖² + C·Σ hinge. Small C = wider margin, more regularization, more support vectors; large C = fewer violations, risk of overfitting. Tune on a log grid.
  • Hinge vs logistic loss: hinge is zero beyond margin 1 (sparse, no probabilities); logistic never zero (probabilities). Use Platt scaling / CalibratedClassifierCV for SVM probabilities.
  • Practicalities: scale features; LinearSVC for big sparse data; kernel SVC is at least O(n²) to train; kernels for curved boundaries (Lesson 3.4).
🏭 In the real world
179 classifiers on 121 datasets 🧪 Research · 2014 Do we Need Hundreds of Classifiers to Solve Real World Classification Problems? (JMLR)

How: compared classifiers from 17 families on 121 datasets with the same evaluation protocol.

Why this method: the Gaussian-kernel SVM via LibSVM came second (92.3% of the maximum accuracy on average), statistically tied with the best random forest. A tuned kernel SVM is a serious general-purpose model on small and medium data.

The library behind most SVMs 🧰 Open source LIBSVM (Chang & Lin, National Taiwan University)

How: an SMO-style solver for classification, regression and one-class SVMs; scikit-learn's SVC, SVR and OneClassSVM are built on it.

Why this method: a reliable, well-tested solver with a practical guide (scale, use the RBF kernel, grid-search C and gamma) that became the standard recipe.

Linear SVMs for millions of rows 🧰 Open source LIBLINEAR

How: coordinate-descent and Newton solvers for linear SVMs and logistic regression on data with millions of instances and features; behind scikit-learn's LinearSVC.

Why this method: for sparse, high-dimensional data such as text, a linear model is often enough and trains in seconds. It won the linear SVM track of the ICML 2008 large-scale learning challenge.

Practical guide to SVM classification 🧪 Research · LIBSVM team A Practical Guide to Support Vector Classification (PDF)

How: shows on real user datasets how scaling alone and then a grid search over C and gamma turn poor SVM results into good ones (one test accuracy goes 66.9% → 96.2% from scaling).

Why this method: most “SVMs don't work” reports were unscaled data or untuned C; the guide fixed the workflow.

🤯 Fun fact · it wasn't called a machine at first

In 1992 Boser, Guyon and Vapnik at AT&T Bell Labs combined maximum margins with the kernel trick. The soft margin that every library uses today was proposed by Corinna Cortes and Vapnik in 1993 and published in 1995 in the journal Machine Learning, under the title “Support-vector networks”. The name “support vector machine” stuck later.

🌉 How it connects

SVMs are linear classifiers like logistic regression (2.3) with a different loss (hinge) and the same kind of L2 penalty (2.2). Like kNN (3.1) they keep training points, but only the support vectors, and like kNN they need scaling. The kernel trick (3.4) turns the same machinery into curved boundaries. Calibration of SVM scores is Lesson 5.6, tuning C with cross-validation 5.1 and 5.7, and the one-class SVM for anomalies 6.7. The math: Math 7.5: constrained optimization (where the support vectors come from), Math 1.3: dot products and Math 7.4: regularization.

Check yourself

1. A linear SVM has w = (3, 4). How wide is its street (the margin between the score −1 and +1 lines)?
‖w‖ = √(3² + 4²) = 5, so the width is 2/‖w‖ = 2/5 = 0.4. Bigger weights mean the score changes faster, so the ±1 lines are closer together: that's why minimizing ‖w‖ widens the street.
2. Your RBF SVM on 50,000 customer rows (12 numeric features in very different units) scores barely above baseline, and training took 40 minutes. What do you do first?
Unscaled features are the classic reason an SVM does poorly (the LIBSVM guide's example goes 66.9% → 96.2% from scaling alone). Kernel SVC training is at least quadratic in rows, so tune on a subsample, and compare with models that scale better on tabular data.
3. A training point has margin y·f(x) = 2.5. What is its hinge loss, and how does it affect the trained SVM?
Hinge loss is max(0, 1 − 2.5) = 0, with zero slope. Its αᵢ is 0, so it drops out of w = Σ αᵢyᵢxᵢ. Move it anywhere with margin above 1 and nothing changes. With logistic loss it would still contribute log₂(1 + e−2.5) ≈ 0.11.
📝 TL;DR
  • An SVM picks the separating line with the widest margin (2/‖w‖); only the support vectors on or inside the margin determine it.
  • Soft margin: ½‖w‖² + C·Σ hinge loss. Small C = wide street, more support vectors, more regularization; tune C on a log grid.
  • Hinge loss is zero beyond margin 1 (far points don't matter, no probabilities); logistic loss never is.
  • Always scale features. LinearSVC for big sparse data; kernel SVC is at least quadratic to train. Probabilities via Platt scaling (CalibratedClassifierCV; probability=True is deprecated in scikit-learn 1.9).
