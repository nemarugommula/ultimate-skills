Phase 3 · Lesson 1

k-nearest neighbours: ask the neighbours (and why it breaks in 1,000 dimensions)

The simplest model in machine learning doesn't learn anything at all: it remembers every example and, for a new one, copies the answer of the most similar ones. It's surprisingly strong, it powers search and recommendations, and it teaches you the single most important fact about high-dimensional data.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Distance between two rows of features: the straight-line (Euclidean) length √(Δx₁² + Δx₂² + …) (Math 1.4).
  • Scaling: putting features on comparable ranges, for example with a z-score (Lesson 1.4).
  • Overfitting: 100% on training data, much worse on new data; that's why we score on a held-out test set (Lesson 0.6).
🎯 Hook: the laziest rule that is never terrible

In 1951 two statisticians, Evelyn Fix and Joseph Hodges, wrote a report for the US Air Force School of Aviation Medicine describing a rule with no training at all: to classify something new, find the most similar example you've already seen and copy its label.

In January 1967 Thomas Cover and Peter Hart proved something startling about it. With enough data, this nearest-neighbour rule makes at most twice as many mistakes as the best possible classifier, the one that knows the true probabilities. In their words, “half the classification information” in an infinite sample is contained in the nearest neighbour.

So why isn't nearest neighbours the only model anyone uses? Two reasons, and both are in this lesson: it needs you to choose how many neighbours to ask, and in many dimensions the word “nearest” quietly stops meaning anything.

🔮 Predict first

You classify each training point by asking its single nearest neighbour (k = 1) in the training set. What training accuracy do you get on noisy data?

100%. Every training point's nearest neighbour is itself, so it copies its own label. That number says nothing about new data: on this lesson's noisy moons, k = 1 scores 100% on training and only 79% on test. Training accuracy is meaningless for k = 1. Watch it below.

Small k memorizes: each noisy point gets its own little territory (high variance). Huge k over-smooths: every prediction is close to “whatever the majority is around here, broadly” (high bias). The best k is in between, and you find it the usual way: on validation data, never on training data.

💡 Big idea k-nearest neighbours (kNN) has no training step: it stores the data. To predict, it measures the distance from the new point to every stored point, takes the k closest, and lets them vote (classification) or averages their values (regression). Everything depends on two choices: k, and what “close” means, which is decided by your features and how you scale them.
🧠 Analogy: how an estate agent prices your house

Ask an estate agent what your house is worth and they don't fit an equation. They pull up “comps”: the 3 to 5 most similar houses sold nearby recently (same street, similar size, same number of bedrooms) and average their prices. That's kNN regression.

Notice what “similar” means to them. A house 2 km away with the same layout is a better comp than the neighbour with twice the floor space. They weigh features sensibly. A computer won't do that by itself: if you measure size in square metres and distance in metres, it will think “1,000 m away” is far more different than “100 m² bigger”. That's the scaling problem, below.

🤔 Why this? Why k-nearest neighbours?
  1. The problem before. Classic statistical classifiers assumed a shape for the data (for example, two bell curves with the same spread, giving a straight boundary). Real classes are often curved, patchy or multi-part, and a wrong shape assumption means a wrong boundary however much data you collect.
  2. What people tried. More flexible formulas (quadratic boundaries, polynomial features, Lesson 2.5): you still have to guess the shape, and the number of terms explodes with the number of features.
  3. Why this works. kNN assumes only that nearby points tend to share a label; the boundary takes whatever shape the data has. Tiny example: a new point has 5 nearest neighbours labelled ■ ■ ● ■ ●, so the vote is 3 to 2 for ■, and P(■) ≈ 3/5 = 0.6. With enough data, Cover and Hart showed even k = 1 is at most twice as wrong as the best possible rule.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Every prediction scans the stored data: 1 million rows × 100 features is 100 million subtractions per query, and the whole dataset must stay in memory. It is only as good as its distance: unscaled features or irrelevant columns wreck it. And in high dimensions all distances look alike (the curse of dimensionality, Playground 4).
  5. What came next. Fast neighbour search (KD-trees, ball trees, and approximate nearest neighbours such as HNSW for vector search), learned distances (embeddings, so that “close” means “similar in meaning”), and models that compress the data into parameters instead of storing it: naive Bayes (3.2), SVMs (3.3), trees and forests (Phase 4).

Step 1: the vote, and the boundary it draws

There is nothing to fit. For a query point q, compute the distance to all n training points, sort, keep the first k. The predicted class is the most common label among them, and the share of votes is a (rough) probability. Colour every pixel of the plane by its vote and you see the decision boundary kNN draws.

Distance weighting: let closer neighbours speak louder

With equal votes, a neighbour 3 units away counts as much as one touching the query. weights="distance" gives each neighbour a vote of 1/d, so the closest points dominate. It makes large k safer (far neighbours barely count) and it makes training accuracy 100% again for any k (a point's distance to itself is 0, so it shouts infinitely loud), which is one more reason to judge only on test data.

✏️ Worked example: one vote, two ways

A query has k = 3 neighbours: a ● at distance 0.5 and two ■ at distances 2 and 2.5.

  • Equal votes: ■ wins 2 to 1, P(■) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67.
  • Distance weights: ● gets 1/0.5 = 2.0; ■ gets 1/2 + 1/2.5 = 0.5 + 0.4 = 0.9. Now ● wins: P(■) = 0.9 / 2.9 ≈ 0.31.
  • Which is right depends on the data, so treat weights as a hyperparameter and cross-validate it (Lesson 5.1) along with k.

Step 2: kNN regression, the estate agent's average

For a number instead of a class, average the k neighbours' targets. The prediction is a staircase: it only changes when the set of nearest neighbours changes.

🔮 Predict first

The training data covers x from −4 to 4, and the curve keeps rising to the right. What does kNN regression with k = 5 predict at x = 6, outside the data?

The same as at the edge. The 5 nearest neighbours of x = 6 are just the 5 right-most points, so the prediction is flat beyond the data (here 0.31, while the true curve is at 3.39). Like trees and forests (Lesson 4.2), kNN cannot extrapolate.

Step 3: what “near” means is up to you (scale your features!)

kNN has no weights to learn, so it can't discover that one feature matters more than another. Whatever feature has the biggest numbers dominates the distance.

✏️ Worked example: income in dollars, age in years

Customer A earns $52,000 and is 25. Customer B earns $53,000 and is 60. Customer C earns $60,000 and is 26. Who is A's nearer neighbour?

  • A to B: √(1,000² + 35²) ≈ 1,000.6. A to C: √(8,000² + 1²) ≈ 8,000.0.
  • To kNN, the 60-year-old is eight times closer to the 25-year-old than the other 25-year-old is. Age, a range of 50, can never compete with income, a range of 100,000.
  • After standardizing each column (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation, Lesson 1.4), one standard deviation of age counts as much as one of income, and C becomes the nearer neighbour.

On scikit-learn's real wine dataset (13 chemical measurements, one of them in the hundreds to thousands, others below 1), 5-fold cross-validated kNN goes from 69% to 95% just by adding a StandardScaler.

Step 4: the curse of dimensionality

kNN's assumption, “nearby points share labels”, needs nearby points to exist. In 2 dimensions, 150 points fill the plane nicely. Add dimensions and something strange happens: space grows so fast that every point is far from every other point, and all the distances become nearly the same. The term was coined by Richard Bellman, who ran into it in dynamic programming.

🔮 Predict first

Scatter 500 random points in a cube. Pick a random query and divide its nearest distance by its farthest distance. In 2 dimensions that ratio is about 0.03 (the nearest point is 30× closer than the farthest). What is it in 1,000 dimensions?

About 0.9. In 1,000 dimensions the closest of 500 points is 90% as far away as the farthest. “Nearest” has almost lost its meaning: tiny noise can reorder the whole neighbour list. Slide the dimension up below and watch it climb.

Read the four numbers as one story. In 10 dimensions, 65% of the cube's volume is within 5% of its surface (1 − 0.9¹⁰), so almost every point is an “edge” point. To capture just 10% of the data around a query, your neighbourhood must span 79% of every axis (0.11/10): it's not local any more. To keep 10 points per axis you'd need 10¹⁰ points. And in 100 dimensions the volume within 5% of the surface is 99.997%.

✏️ Why it isn't hopeless in practice

Real data rarely fills its space uniformly. A 784-pixel image of a handwritten digit lives near a much lower-dimensional surface (strokes, thickness, slant), its intrinsic dimension. kNN works when that intrinsic dimension is low, and fails when many features are irrelevant noise, because each noisy feature adds its own random contribution to every distance. Practical fixes: drop or select features, reduce dimensions first (principal component analysis, PCA, Lesson 6.5), or learn an embedding where distance means similarity.

Step 5: making it fast, exact and approximate

The brute-force search costs O(n·d) per query: compute every distance. scikit-learn can also build a KD-tree or ball tree that skips whole regions of space, which is very fast in low dimensions. Its documentation notes that KD-trees are fast below about 20 dimensions and become inefficient beyond that, another face of the curse, and algorithm="auto" switches to brute force when there are more than 15 features.

For millions of high-dimensional vectors (text and image embeddings), systems give up exactness: approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes such as HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) graphs or IVF (inverted-file) indexes find, say, 95% of the true top 10 in a millisecond instead of scanning everything. That is how vector databases and semantic search work; the AI Engineer course covers it in 2.2: approximate nearest neighbour search.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): KNeighborsClassifier, KNeighborsRegressor, RadiusNeighborsClassifier and NearestNeighbors (just the search). Always put a scaler in the same Pipeline, so cross-validation scales each fold with its own training statistics (Lesson 1.6). For large-scale vector search: Faiss (Meta), Annoy (Spotify), hnswlib, or a vector database.

from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)

raw = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)                       # default k = 5, equal votes
scaled = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))
print("raw   ", cross_val_score(raw, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))      # 0.691
print("scaled", cross_val_score(scaled, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))   # 0.949

# Tune k and the weighting together, inside cross-validation
grid = GridSearchCV(scaled, {"kneighborsclassifier__n_neighbors": [1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25],
                             "kneighborsclassifier__weights": ["uniform", "distance"]}, cv=5)
grid.fit(X, y)
print(grid.best_params_, round(grid.best_score_, 3))      # k = 9, uniform: 0.966

Other knobs: metric (default Minkowski with p=2, i.e. Euclidean; p=1 is Manhattan; "cosine" suits text and embeddings) and algorithm ("auto" picks brute force, KD-tree or ball tree for you). predict_proba returns vote shares: with k = 5 there are only six possible values (0, 0.2, …, 1).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

kNN is a five-minute baseline and a great debugging tool: “show me the 5 most similar past cases” is an explanation customers and colleagues understand instantly. For “similar items” features, nearest neighbours on embeddings is often the whole product.

At scale

Plain kNN classifiers are rare in production (too slow and memory-heavy at millions of rows). But nearest-neighbour search is everywhere: retrieval for recommendations, semantic search and RAG (retrieval-augmented generation for LLM apps) runs ANN indexes over billions of vectors, often as the first stage before a heavier ranking model (Lesson 7.4).

🎤 Interview angle

“How does kNN work, how do you choose k, and when would you not use it?” A strong answer:

  • Lazy learner: store the data; predict by majority vote (or average) of the k closest points.
  • k trades variance (small k memorizes noise; k = 1 always scores 100% on training) for bias (large k blurs); pick k and the weighting by cross-validation.
  • Scaling is mandatory, and irrelevant features hurt because every feature contributes to every distance.
  • The curse of dimensionality: distances concentrate (nearest ≈ farthest), neighbourhoods stop being local, and you'd need exponentially more data.
  • Cost: O(n·d) per query and all data in memory; trees help in low dimensions, ANN indexes (HNSW, IVF) at scale.
🏭 In the real world
“Customers who bought this…” 🏢 Amazon · 2003 Amazon Science: the history of Amazon's recommendation algorithm

How: item-to-item collaborative filtering: for each item you bought, pull up a list of related items (its nearest neighbours by co-purchase similarity), and recommend items that show up across many of those lists. The 2003 paper describing it was later chosen as the IEEE Internet Computing paper that best stood the “test of time”.

Why this method: matching every customer against every other customer is a huge computational burden; item neighbour lists are cheaper, stable, and easy to explain (“because you bought…”).

Music recommendations 🧰 Open source · Spotify Annoy (Approximate Nearest Neighbors Oh Yeah)

How: after matrix factorization (Lesson 7.3), every user and track is a vector; Annoy builds tree-based indexes that find similar vectors quickly, saved as files that many processes can memory-map.

Why this method: exact search over millions of tracks per request is too slow; approximate neighbours are good enough for recommendations.

Vector search at billion scale 🧰 Open source · Meta Faiss

How: a library of exact and approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (inverted files, product quantization, HNSW), with GPU versions; compressed indexes can hold billions of vectors in memory on one server.

Why this method: semantic search, deduplication and retrieval for LLM apps all reduce to “find the nearest vectors”.

The twice-the-Bayes-error theorem 📜 History · 1967 Cover & Hart: Nearest Neighbor Pattern Classification (PDF)

How: proved that, with unlimited data, the 1-nearest-neighbour rule's error is at most twice the lowest error any classifier could achieve; they credit Fix and Hodges's 1951 Air Force report as the first formulation.

Why this method: it made the simplest possible rule a respectable baseline, and kNN is still the yardstick new methods are compared with.

🤯 Fun fact · nearest neighbours from aviation medicine

The first nearest-neighbour classifier wasn't written for computers: Fix and Hodges's 1951 report came from the USAF School of Aviation Medicine at Randolph Field, Texas, as part of research on “discriminatory analysis”. And the “curse of dimensionality” comes from Richard Bellman, the same Bellman whose equation sits at the heart of reinforcement learning.

🌉 How it connects

kNN depends entirely on distances, so it needs the scaling of Lesson 1.4 inside a pipeline (1.6) and the honest validation of Lessons 0.6 and 5.1. Naive Bayes (3.2) is its opposite: a model that compresses data into a few probabilities. SVMs (3.3) also keep training points around, but only the few “support vectors” on the boundary, and kernels (3.4) are another way of measuring similarity. The curse of dimensionality returns in PCA (6.5), anomaly detection with LOF (6.7) and recommenders (7.3). Check yourself

1. Data is spread uniformly in a 10-dimensional cube. You want each query's neighbourhood (a smaller cube around it) to hold 1% of the data. Roughly what fraction of each axis must that neighbourhood span?
A sub-cube with edge e holds e¹⁰ of the volume, so e = 0.011/10 ≈ 0.63. A “neighbourhood” covering 63% of every feature's range isn't local, so the neighbours aren't really similar. In 2-D it would be just 10% per axis.
2. Your kNN churn model uses annual_spend (0 to 50,000) and support_tickets (0 to 10). Tickets are the strongest signal, yet accuracy is barely above baseline. Most likely fix?
Distances are dominated by annual_spend; a difference of 10 tickets is invisible next to a difference of thousands of dollars. Standardizing gives each feature a comparable voice (Lesson 1.4). Putting the scaler in the pipeline keeps cross-validation honest. Changing k or weights doesn't change what “near” means.
3. A teammate reports 100% training accuracy for a 1-NN model and wants to ship it. What do you say?
With k = 1 every training point finds itself at distance 0, so training accuracy is always 100% (unless duplicate points disagree). It says nothing about new data; on this lesson's moons, k = 1 got 79% on test vs 90% for k = 5.
📝 TL;DR
  • kNN stores the data and predicts by the vote (or average) of the k closest points. No training; all the cost is at prediction time.
  • k = 1 memorizes (100% train, jagged); large k blurs. Choose k and weighting by cross-validation, never by training accuracy.
  • Distance decides everything: scale features and remove irrelevant ones (wine: 69% → 95% with a scaler).
  • Curse of dimensionality: in high dimensions distances concentrate (nearest/farthest ≈ 0.9 at d = 1,000) and neighbourhoods stop being local.
  • At scale, nearest-neighbour search uses trees (low d) or approximate indexes such as HNSW (embeddings). Next: naive Bayes, which compresses data into probabilities (3.2).
