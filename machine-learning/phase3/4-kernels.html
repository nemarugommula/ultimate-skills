Phase 3 · Lesson 4

Kernels & the kernel trick: curved boundaries from straight lines

A straight line can't separate a ring from its centre. Lift the points into a third dimension and a flat sheet can. The kernel trick does that lifting, even into infinitely many dimensions, without ever building the new features. Here's how, what it costs, and why it faded for big data.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The dot product x·y = x₁y₁ + x₂y₂ + … measures how aligned two vectors are (Math 1.3).
  • A linear SVM finds the widest street between classes, and its answer only needs dot products between points (Lesson 3.3).
  • Polynomial features: add x², xy, y² … so a linear model can draw curves (Lesson 2.5).
🎯 Hook: 47 trillion features for the price of 100 multiplications

Suppose you want a linear model on all products of up to 10 of your 100 features (x₁x₇, x₃²x₉⁸, …). Counting them: there are 46,897,636,623,981, about 4.7 × 10¹³. Storing one row would take hundreds of terabytes.

An SVM with a degree-10 polynomial kernel uses exactly that feature space, and computes each similarity between two rows with one 100-term dot product, one addition and one power. No feature is ever written down. That's the kernel trick.

Fast-forward to 2017: the award for the NIPS paper (NIPS, now NeurIPS, is the field's biggest conference) that best stood the test of time went to Ali Rahimi and Ben Recht's 2007 paper on random features, a way to go the other direction and turn kernels back into a few thousand explicit features, because kernels had become too expensive for big data. This lesson tells both halves of that story.

The inner ring's z values go up to 2.4, the outer ring's start at 3.7, so any plane in between works. A curved boundary in the original space is a straight one in a lifted space. The whole lesson is about doing that lifting cheaply.

💡 Big idea Map each point to new features φ(x) where the classes become linearly separable, then run a linear method there. Algorithms like the SVM only ever use dot products φ(x)·φ(y). A kernel K(x, y) is a function that returns that dot product directly from the original x and y, so you get the power of a huge (even infinite) feature space at the cost of computing K. The price: you need K between pairs of training points, which is n² numbers.
🧠 Analogy: coins on a table and a flick from below

Gold coins lie in a ring around a pile of silver coins on a tablecloth. You can't separate them with one straight ruler. Now someone flicks the cloth from underneath right at the centre: the silver pile flies up, the gold ring barely moves. For an instant a flat tray slid in at the right height separates them perfectly.

The kernel trick is a stranger version: you never actually flick the cloth. You only ask, for each pair of coins, “how similar are your positions after the flick?”, and that question has a cheap answer computed from where they lie on the table.

🤔 Why this? Why kernels?
  1. The problem before. Linear models (logistic regression, linear SVMs) are fast and well understood, but many real boundaries are curved: rings, clusters, XOR-like patterns.
  2. What people tried. Add polynomial features by hand (Lesson 2.5). That works for 2 features, but the count explodes: all products up to degree 2 of 100 features is 5,151 columns; up to degree 10, about 4.7 × 10¹³. And you still have to guess the right features.
  3. Why this works. The SVM's solution and predictions use only dot products between points, so replace each dot product with a kernel. Tiny check: x = (1, 2), y = (3, 1) gives x·y = 5 and (x·y)² = 25; the degree-2 features φ = (x₁², √2·x₁x₂, x₂²) give φ(x) = (1, 2.83, 4) and φ(y) = (9, 4.24, 1), whose dot product is 9 + 12 + 4 = 25. Same number, without building φ.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Training needs kernel values between pairs of training points: n² of them. For 100,000 rows that's 10¹⁰ numbers, 80 GB as 8-byte floats, and libsvm's training time grows between n² and n³. Kernels also need tuning (gamma, degree, C) and scaled features, and a too-flexible kernel memorizes the data.
  5. What came next. Approximations: Nystroem (2001) and random Fourier features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) build a few hundred or thousand explicit features whose dot products approximate the kernel, then use a fast linear model. Then gradient-boosted trees (4.3) and deep networks, which learn their features, took over large datasets. Kernels live on in Gaussian processes, kernel PCA and even in research on fast attention.

Step 1: see the lift

Each 2-D point (x, y) gets a height z = x² + y². Points near the centre stay low, points far out rise: the plane becomes a bowl. A flat, horizontal sheet cutting the bowl is a linear boundary in 3-D, and where it meets the bowl is a circle in 2-D.

Step 2: the trick, dot products without the features

Lifting by hand needs you to choose z. A kernel lets you choose a similarity instead. The SVM's decision function (Lesson 3.3) is a weighted vote of support vectors, and it only ever touches the data through dot products:

f(x) = Σsupport vectors i αᵢ yᵢ · K(xᵢ, x) + b “The score of a new point is a weighted sum of how similar it is to each support vector, plus an offset. Swap the plain dot product for any kernel K and the boundary can curve.”

Step 3: the RBF kernel, a hill on every support vector

The RBF (radial basis function) kernel is the default in scikit-learn's SVC, and the LIBSVM guide calls it “a reasonable first choice”. Read K(x, xᵢ) = exp(−γ‖x − xᵢ‖²) as similarity: 1 when x sits on xᵢ, falling toward 0 as it moves away. So f(x) is a landscape of hills (around class-1 support vectors) and valleys (class 0), and γ sets how narrow each hill is.

✏️ Worked example: how far does a support vector reach?
  • γ = 0.5: a point 1 unit away has similarity e−0.5 = 0.61; 3 units away, e−4.5 = 0.011. Each hill is broad: the boundary is smooth.
  • γ = 10: 1 unit away, e−10 ≈ 0.000045. Each support vector only influences its immediate surroundings, so the boundary can wrap tightly around single points: memorization.
  • Rule of thumb: the reach of a hill is about 1/√γ. scikit-learn's default gamma="scale" sets γ = 1 / (n_features × variance of X), so standardized 2-D data gets γ ≈ 0.5.

Step 4: the bill, and why SVMs faded for big data

A kernel SVM must look at kernel values between pairs of training points: the kernel matrix has n × n entries. scikit-learn's SVC documentation says its fit time “scales at least quadratically with the number of samples and may be impractical beyond tens of thousands of samples”. Predictions cost one kernel evaluation per support vector, and on noisy data a large share of the rows become support vectors.

Real solvers such as libsvm don't store the whole matrix (they cache the rows they need), but the work still grows between n² and n³. That's why, as datasets grew into the millions, practitioners moved to linear models on explicit features (LIBLINEAR, stochastic gradient descent), kernel approximations (Nystroem, random Fourier features: scikit-learn's RBFSampler, also called “random kitchen sinks”), and models that learn features themselves (boosted trees, neural networks).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): SVC(kernel="rbf" | "poly" | "linear" | "sigmoid" | callable), sklearn.metrics.pairwise.rbf_kernel / polynomial_kernel, and in sklearn.kernel_approximation: Nystroem and RBFSampler. Tune C and gamma together on a log grid.

from sklearn.datasets import make_circles, make_moons
from sklearn.kernel_approximation import Nystroem
from sklearn.linear_model import SGDClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=400, noise=0.1, factor=0.4, random_state=0)
for kernel in ["linear", "poly", "rbf"]:
    clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel=kernel, degree=2))    # degree only matters for "poly"
    print(kernel, cross_val_score(clf, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))     # linear 0.543, poly 1.0, rbf 1.0

# Tune C and gamma together, on a log grid
X, y = make_moons(n_samples=1000, noise=0.3, random_state=0)
grid = GridSearchCV(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf")),
                    {"svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "svc__gamma": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100]}, cv=5).fit(X, y)
print(grid.best_params_, round(grid.best_score_, 3))                    # C = 1, gamma = 1: 0.913

# Big data: approximate the RBF kernel with 300 explicit features, then a fast linear model
approx = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), Nystroem(gamma=1.0, n_components=300, random_state=0),
                       SGDClassifier(loss="hinge", alpha=1e-4, random_state=0))
print("Nystroem + linear SVM", cross_val_score(approx, X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))   # 0.912

On the circles the linear kernel stays near chance while degree-2 polynomial and RBF kernels are near perfect. gamma="scale" (the default) is a sensible starting point after standardizing. Polynomial kernels of high degree are numerically touchy; RBF is the usual default. For a custom similarity, pass kernel="precomputed" and a kernel matrix, or a Python function.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Small, dense, numeric data (a few thousand rows of sensor readings or lab measurements)? A standardized RBF SVM with a 4 × 5 grid over C and γ is a strong model in minutes. A Gaussian process gives you uncertainty bars too, if n is in the low thousands.

At scale

Exact kernel SVMs rarely fit the budget beyond tens of thousands of rows. Teams use boosted trees for tabular data, neural networks for perception and text, and kernel ideas through approximations (random features, Nystroem) or inside other methods, such as Gaussian-process-based Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning (Lesson 5.7).

🎤 Interview angle

“What is the kernel trick, and why would you use or avoid a kernel SVM?” A strong answer:

  • Some methods (SVM, ridge, PCA) only use dot products between points; replace them with K(x, y) = φ(x)·φ(y) and you work in the lifted space without computing φ.
  • Example: (x·y)² equals the dot product of degree-2 features; RBF exp(−γ‖x − y‖²) corresponds to an infinite-dimensional space.
  • γ controls the reach of each support vector: small = smooth, large = memorizes (100% train, many support vectors). Tune γ and C together with cross-validation; scale features first.
  • Cost: n² kernel values, training between O(n²) and O(n³), prediction cost proportional to the number of support vectors. Beyond tens of thousands of rows use linear models, kernel approximations (Nystroem, random Fourier features) or boosted trees.
🏭 In the real world
Random features 🧪 Research · 2007 Rahimi & Recht: Random Features for Large-Scale Kernel Machines (NIPS 2007)

How: map inputs to a randomized, low-dimensional feature space whose inner products approximate a shift-invariant kernel such as RBF, then train a fast linear model; the paper reports linear models on these features beating state-of-the-art large-scale kernel machines.

Why this method: keeps most of the kernel's flexibility at linear-model cost. It won the NIPS 2017 Test of Time award.

Kernel approximation in scikit-learn 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: kernel approximation

How: Nystroem (subsample rows, approximate the kernel matrix with them) and RBFSampler (random Fourier features) as transformers, so a LinearSVC or SGDClassifier can follow in a pipeline.

Why this method: the SVC docs themselves point here for datasets beyond tens of thousands of samples.

Gaussian processes 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Gaussian processes

How: a kernel defines how strongly two points' outputs are correlated; predictions come with a probability distribution, not just a number.

Why this method: calibrated uncertainty on small data. It's the engine behind classic Bayesian optimization of expensive experiments and hyperparameters.

Kernels inside transformers 🧪 Research · 2020 Choromanski et al.: Rethinking Attention with Performers

How: treats softmax attention as a kernel and approximates it with positive orthogonal random features (FAVOR+), so attention costs linear instead of quadratic time and memory in the sequence length.

Why this method: the same n² problem that slowed kernel SVMs slows attention on long sequences, and the same random-feature cure applies.

🤯 Fun fact · infinite dimensions, finite arithmetic

The RBF kernel's hidden feature space is infinite-dimensional: expanding exp(−γ‖x − y‖²) as a power series gives terms of every degree. No computer could store one lifted point, yet K(x, y) costs a subtraction, a square and an exponential. Random Fourier features flip it around: sample a few hundred random directions and cosines, and their dot products approximate that infinite-dimensional similarity.

🌉 How it connects

Kernels generalize the SVM of 3.3 and the polynomial features of 2.5; the RBF kernel is a smooth cousin of kNN's neighbourhoods (3.1). Kernel matrices reappear in PCA (6.5), and their n² cost is why boosted trees (4.3–4.4) and the sibling Deep Learning course's networks took over large datasets. Check yourself

1. x = (2, 1) and y = (1, 3). What is the polynomial kernel K(x, y) = (x·y)²?
x·y = 2·1 + 1·3 = 5, so K = 5² = 25. Check with features φ = (x₁², √2·x₁x₂, x₂²): φ(x) = (4, 2.83, 1), φ(y) = (1, 4.24, 9), dot product 4 + 12 + 9 = 25.
2. Your RBF SVM scores 100% on training and 70% on validation, and 95% of training rows are support vectors. What do you change?
A huge share of support vectors plus perfect training accuracy is the signature of γ too large: each point gets its own hill. Smaller γ makes each support vector reach further, giving a smoother boundary. Search γ and C together with cross-validation.
3. You have 2 million rows and a curved boundary. Why not a kernel SVC, and what would you try instead?
(2 × 10⁶)² = 4 × 10¹² kernel values, and scikit-learn warns SVC becomes impractical beyond tens of thousands of rows. Kernel approximations give explicit features that a linear model trains on in minutes; on tabular data, boosted trees are the usual choice (Lesson 4.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Curved boundaries become straight after lifting: rings are separated by z = x² + y² (a flat plane in 3-D = a circle in 2-D).
  • The kernel trick: methods that only need dot products can use K(x, y) = φ(x)·φ(y) without building φ. (x·y)² = degree-2 features' dot product.
  • RBF exp(−γ‖x − y‖²): a hill around each support vector; small γ smooth, large γ memorizes. Tune γ and C together, scale first.
  • Cost: n² kernel values, training O(n²)–O(n³); impractical beyond tens of thousands of rows. Approximations (Nystroem, random features) or boosted trees scale.
  • Kernels live on in Gaussian processes, kernel PCA and even attention research. Next: the phase recap, then trees (Phase 4).
