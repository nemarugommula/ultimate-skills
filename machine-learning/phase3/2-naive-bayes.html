Naive Bayes: a wrong assumption that still catches spam

Naive Bayes pretends every word in an email is independent of every other word. That's obviously false, and it still became the spam filter of the early 2000s. Learn why a wrong model can make the right decisions, and when it lets you down.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bayes' rule: P(class | evidence) ∝ P(evidence | class) × P(class): start from the base rate, update with evidence (Math 4.2).
  • Log-odds: log(p / (1 − p)); adding log-odds is multiplying odds, and 0 means 50/50 (Lesson 2.3).
  • Bag of words: represent a text by how often each word appears, ignoring order (more in Lesson 7.5).
🎯 Hook: six months of rules vs one page of probability

In August 2002 Paul Graham published an essay called A Plan for Spam. He had spent about six months writing software that looked for features of spam by hand. Rules work at first: just looking for the word “click” caught 79.7% of his spam, with 1.2% false positives (real emails wrongly flagged). But the last few percent got very hard, and every stricter rule flagged more real mail.

Then he tried something statistical: count how often each word appears in about 4,000 spams and 4,000 non-spams, and combine the words' spam probabilities with Bayes' rule. The result missed fewer than 5 spams per 1,000, with 0 false positives.

The combination step assumed each word is independent evidence, an assumption no linguist would accept (“click” and “here” clearly travel together). This lesson explains why that “naive” assumption still works, and the one thing it gets badly wrong.

🔮 Predict first

In spam, “click” and “here” nearly always appear together. Naive Bayes counts them as two independent pieces of evidence, so it double counts. What does that mostly damage?

Mostly the probabilities. Double counting pushes scores toward 0 or 1, but a spam still tends to outscore a real email, so the yes/no decision is usually right. In Playground 2 you'll add 12 near-copies of one feature: accuracy stays around 78–80%, while the model's average confidence climbs from 81% to 97%.

Numbers first: a 24-message spam filter

Here's a tiny training set: 12 spam and 12 normal (“ham”) messages. You'll use it in the playground below.

Count words. “free” appears 7 times in the 66 words of spam and once in the 76 words of ham. With the smoothing explained below:

Each word becomes a score: the log of how much more likely it is in spam than in ham. A message's score is the starting score (the base rate) plus the sum of its words' scores. Positive total → spam. That's the whole classifier.

💡 Big idea Bayes' rule says P(spam | words) ∝ P(words | spam) × P(spam). Estimating P(words | spam) for whole messages is impossible: almost every message is unique. Naive Bayes assumes the words are independent given the class, so P(words | spam) = P(w₁ | spam) × P(w₂ | spam) × …, and each factor is just a word count. In log space the product becomes a sum of per-word scores. The probabilities it gives are often wrong; the ranking it gives is often right, and the decision only needs the ranking.
🧠 Analogy: a points card at airport security

Imagine a checklist: one-way ticket +2 points, paid cash +2, no luggage +1, frequent flyer −3. Officers add up the points and search anyone above 3. The card treats every clue as separate, even though “one-way” and “paid cash” often go together, so it double counts some travellers and gives them more points than they deserve.

Does that matter? If you only need to decide who gets searched, a little double counting rarely flips the order of “suspicious” and “ordinary”. If you need to say “this person is 97% likely to be a smuggler”, the card's number is nonsense. Naive Bayes is that points card, learned from data.

🤔 Why this? Why naive Bayes?
  1. The problem before. Spam filters were hand-written rules, and every rule traded caught spam for false alarms (Graham's “click”: 79.7% caught, 1.2% false positives). Spammers learn the rules; nobody can maintain thousands of them.
  2. What people tried. Full Bayes: model P(message | spam) directly. With a vocabulary of just 76 words there are already 2⁷⁶ ≈ 7.6 × 10²² possible combinations of present and absent words; you could never count them. Some dependence must be thrown away.
  3. Why this works. Assume independence and each word needs only two counts. The decision is a sum: “click now to claim your free prize” scores 1.86 + 2.01 − 0.34 + 1.17 + 1.17 + 1.45 + 1.45 = +8.78, so P(spam) ≈ 0.9998. Double counting inflates the score, but a spam's score usually stays above a ham's, and the decision only needs which side of 0 you're on (Domingos and Pazzani analysed exactly why it classifies well despite such a gross over-simplification).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Overconfident probabilities (don't use them as real risks without calibration, Lesson 5.6); correlated features get counted twice, which can tip close calls; a word never seen with a class gives probability 0 unless you smooth; and the boundary is simple (linear in word counts), so it can't learn interactions like “free” being fine in “free for lunch?”.
  5. What came next. Logistic regression on the same counts (Lesson 2.3) learns all the word weights jointly, so correlated words share credit instead of double counting; with more data it usually wins. Complement naive Bayes (Rennie et al., 2003) fixed known biases on text; TF-IDF features with linear models (7.5) and then neural text models replaced it for accuracy. Naive Bayes stays as the fastest strong baseline.

Step 1: Bayes' rule with a points card

Say it in words first: start from how common spam is, then let every word nudge the odds up or down. In log-odds, nudges add:

log odds(spam | message) = log P(spam) / P(ham) + Σwords log P(word | spam) / P(word | ham) “The message's spam score is the base-rate score plus one score per word: positive words push toward spam, negative ones toward ham.”

Turn the total back into a probability with the sigmoid: p = 1 / (1 + e−score) (Lesson 2.3). A word that appears twice counts twice: that's the multinomial model, the standard one for text.

✏️ Worked example: the base rate matters

“click now to claim your free prize” has word scores summing to +8.78. With balanced training data the base-rate score is log(12/12) = 0, so P(spam) = 1 / (1 + e−8.78) ≈ 0.9998.

  • Now suppose only 10% of your mail is spam: the base-rate score is log(0.1 / 0.9) = −2.20. Total 8.78 − 2.20 = 6.58, P(spam) ≈ 0.9986. Still spam: strong evidence beats a modest prior.
  • “free lunch at the meeting” scores −3.85 (P ≈ 0.02). If 90% of mail were spam (+2.20), it becomes −1.65, P ≈ 0.16: still ham, but much less sure.
  • This is why scikit-learn learns the base rate from the class frequencies by default (fit_prior=True), and why you should think about it when your training data is balanced but real traffic isn't (Lesson 5.5).

Step 2: Laplace smoothing, or why one word shouldn't veto everything

Without smoothing, P(word | class) = count / total. A word that never appeared in training spam gets P = 0, its log is −∞, and the whole sum is −∞, whatever else the message says.

🔮 Predict first

No smoothing (α = 0). A message is full of spammy words but also contains one word that appeared only in ham during training, such as “meeting”. What is P(spam)?

Exactly 0 (and if the message also has a word seen only in spam, the score becomes ∞ − ∞ and the model breaks). Spammers exploited this by pasting innocent words into spam. The fix is to pretend you saw every word α extra times in every class: Laplace smoothing (α = 1). Drag α to 0 in the playground with the preset “prize … meeting”.
P(word | class) = (count(word, class) + α) ÷ (total words in class + α × vocabulary size) “Add α pretend sightings of every word to every class, so nothing is ever impossible.”

α = 1 is Laplace smoothing; α < 1 (Lidstone) trusts the counts more, and the word scores get more extreme: at α = 0.01 “click” jumps from +1.86 to +6.36. α is a hyperparameter: tune it with cross-validation like any other (it's alpha in scikit-learn, default 1.0).

Step 3: the naive assumption is wrong, and here is what it breaks

Let's measure the damage. Take a simple 2-feature problem and keep adding near-copies of the first feature (the same value plus a little noise), like a spam that says “free” in eight slightly different ways. The copies carry no new information, but naive Bayes counts each one as fresh evidence.

Accuracy and AUC (area under the ROC curve: the chance a random positive is scored above a random negative, Lesson 5.4) barely move: 80% → 78% and 0.88 → 0.85 at 12 copies. The model's confidence climbs from 81% to 97% and the log loss (which punishes confident mistakes, Lesson 5.6) gets five times worse, 0.45 → 2.25. That's naive Bayes in one picture: good at ranking, bad at probabilities. scikit-learn's own guide says it plainly: naive Bayes is a decent classifier but a bad estimator.

Step 4: Gaussian naive Bayes for numbers

For continuous features, replace word counts with a bell curve per feature per class: learn each feature's mean and variance within each class, and multiply the per-feature likelihoods as before. Independence now means: within a class, the features are uncorrelated, so each class's cloud is an axis-aligned ellipse. When the true clouds are tilted, that's exactly where it fails.

On round-ish clouds with different spreads, naive Bayes draws a curved boundary (a wider class wraps around a narrower one) and scores 94%, a hair above logistic regression's 93%. On tilted clouds its ellipses can't rotate, so the boundary cuts across the long axis: 75% vs logistic regression's 94%. If your features are strongly correlated, naive Bayes is the wrong tool; decorrelating them first (principal component analysis, PCA, Lesson 6.5) or using a model that learns weights jointly helps.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): CountVectorizer or TfidfVectorizer to turn texts into counts, then a naive Bayes model, in one Pipeline (Lesson 1.6). Here are this lesson's 24 messages:

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import ComplementNB, GaussianNB, MultinomialNB
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

spam = ["win a free prize now", "free money click here", "claim your free gift card now"]   # … the 12 spams above
ham = ["are we still meeting for lunch", "can you send the report today"]            # … the 12 hams above
texts, labels = spam + ham, [1] * len(spam) + [0] * len(ham)

nb = make_pipeline(CountVectorizer(token_pattern=r"[a-z0-9£$€']+"), MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0))
nb.fit(texts, labels)
print(nb.predict_proba(["click now to claim your free prize"])[:, 1])   # spam probability (overconfident!)

# The model is a table of per-word log-probabilities: inspect the spammiest words
vec, clf = nb.named_steps["countvectorizer"], nb.named_steps["multinomialnb"]
score = clf.feature_log_prob_[1] - clf.feature_log_prob_[0]              # log P(w|spam) − log P(w|ham)
print(sorted(zip(score.round(2), vec.get_feature_names_out()))[-5:])

cnb = make_pipeline(CountVectorizer(), ComplementNB())                     # usually better on real, imbalanced text
X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
print(cross_val_score(GaussianNB(), X, y, cv=5).mean().round(3))             # 0.966, no scaling needed

GaussianNB needs no feature scaling: each feature gets its own mean and variance. For probabilities you'll act on, wrap the model in CalibratedClassifierCV (Lesson 5.6). For huge text streams, HashingVectorizer(alternate_sign=False) + MultinomialNB.partial_fit learns batch by batch in constant memory.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Classifying support tickets, tagging feedback, routing emails with 200 labelled examples? TfidfVectorizer + ComplementNB trains in milliseconds and gives you a baseline and a per-word explanation in an afternoon. It also updates instantly when users correct it.

At scale

Large email providers typically combine many signals (sender reputation, links, user reports) in bigger models. Bayesian token filters live on in open-source mail servers such as SpamAssassin, and naive Bayes remains a standard fast baseline and a feature generator (its per-class log-probabilities can feed a bigger model).

🎤 Interview angle

“Why is naive Bayes called naive, and why does it work anyway?” A strong answer:

  • Bayes' rule + the assumption that features are independent given the class, so the likelihood is a product of per-feature terms; in logs, a sum of per-feature scores.
  • The assumption is false, but classification only needs the right class to score highest: double counting distorts the probabilities more than the ranking.
  • Consequences: overconfident, poorly calibrated probabilities (calibrate before using them as risks); correlated features can tip close decisions.
  • Laplace/Lidstone smoothing (α) prevents zero probabilities; the base rate (prior) matters when training and production class balance differ.
  • Strengths: training is counting, works with tiny data and huge vocabularies; variants for counts, binary and Gaussian features; logistic regression on the same features usually wins with more data.
🏭 In the real world
The first naive Bayes spam paper 🧪 Research · 1998 Sahami, Dumais, Heckerman & Horvitz: A Bayesian Approach to Filtering Junk E-Mail (PDF)

How: a naive Bayesian classifier on 1,789 real emails (1,578 junk), flagging a message as junk only when P(junk) > 99.9%, because losing a real email costs far more than seeing a spam. Words alone gave 97.1% junk precision; adding phrases (“Free Money”) and hand-made features (“!!!!”, the sender's domain type: junk is virtually never sent from .edu) reached 100% precision and 98.3% recall.

Why this method: it learns from examples instead of hand-written rules and produces a score you can threshold according to the cost of mistakes.

A Plan for Spam 📜 History · 2002 Paul Graham: A Plan for Spam

How: word spam-probabilities from about 4,000 spams and 4,000 non-spams, combining the 15 most “interesting” words of each new message with Bayes' rule (a close cousin of naive Bayes). Trained per user on their own mail.

Why this method: each user's vocabulary is personal, so a filter learned from your own mail is very hard for spammers to tune against. His January 2003 sequel reported about 99.75% of spam caught over one month.

Bayes in an open-source mail filter 🧰 Open source · Apache BayesInSpamAssassin

How: SpamAssassin's Bayesian classifier learns tokens common in spam or ham from messages you feed to sa-learn, then raises or lowers each new message's spam score; it only starts scoring after learning from at least 200 spams and 200 hams.

Why this method: it adapts to each site's mail, trains incrementally, and is cheap enough to run on every message.

Fixing naive Bayes for text 🧪 Research · 2003 Rennie et al.: Tackling the Poor Assumptions of Naive Bayes Text Classifiers (PDF)

How: showed that skewed class sizes bias the word weights and that dependent words inflate them, and proposed the “complement class” estimate, now ComplementNB.

Why this method: the fixed classifier approaches the accuracy of SVMs on several text corpora while staying faster and simpler.

🤯 Fun fact · the rule its author never published

Thomas Bayes died in 1761 without publishing his famous result. His friend Richard Price found it in his notes, edited it and published it in 1763 as An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances. Two and a half centuries later, a few lines of it sort billions of emails.

🌉 How it connects

Naive Bayes and logistic regression (2.3) draw the same kind of boundary on word counts; naive Bayes estimates each weight separately by counting, logistic regression fits them together. Its probabilities need calibration (5.6) and its prior matters for imbalanced data (5.5). Text features (bag-of-words, TF-IDF) are Lesson 7.5, and Gaussian mixtures (6.4) reuse the bell-curve-per-class idea without labels. Compare with kNN (3.1), which keeps all the data instead of summarizing it. The probability itself: Math 4.2: Bayes' rule, Math 4.3: distributions and Math 5.3: maximum likelihood (counting is the maximum-likelihood estimate).

Check yourself

1. A message's words sum to a log-odds score of +2.0, and the base rate of spam is 50%. Roughly what is P(spam)?
The base-rate score is log(0.5/0.5) = 0, so the total is 2.0 and p = 1 / (1 + e−2) = 1 / (1 + 0.135) ≈ 0.88. (Odds of e² ≈ 7.4 to 1.)
2. Your naive Bayes ticket router is 92% accurate, and product wants to auto-close tickets when it says P(“spam”) > 0.95. What should you check first?
Double counting correlated words pushes scores toward 0 and 1, so “0.95” may be right far less than 95% of the time. Check a reliability curve on held-out data and calibrate (Lesson 5.6), or choose the threshold from measured precision instead.
3. Why does naive Bayes need smoothing (α > 0)?
The likelihood is a product, so a single zero wipes it out (log 0 = −∞). Adding α pretend counts to every word in every class keeps every probability positive; α = 1 is Laplace smoothing.
📝 TL;DR
  • Naive Bayes = Bayes' rule + “features are independent given the class”. In logs: base-rate score + one score per word.
  • Training is just counting (with smoothing α so no probability is 0), so it's instant and works with little data.
  • The assumption is wrong, so the probabilities are overconfident (12 redundant copies: confidence 81% → 97%, accuracy unchanged), but the ranking, and so the decision, is often right.
  • Pick the flavour by feature type: Multinomial/Complement for word counts, Bernoulli for yes/no, Gaussian for numbers (fails on correlated features).
  • Logistic regression on the same features usually wins with enough data. Next: SVMs, which care only about the points near the boundary (3.3).
