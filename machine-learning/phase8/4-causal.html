Phase 8 · Lesson 4

Causal inference basics: A/B tests & uplift modelling

Everything so far predicted what will happen. Businesses usually want to know what will happen if we act: send the coupon, change the price, ship the feature. That's a causal question, and a model trained on observational data can answer it confidently and wrongly. Here's how to tell the difference, and the tools that get it right.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Correlation measures how two things move together; it says nothing about which causes which (Math 5.2).
  • An A/B test compares two randomized groups and asks if the difference is bigger than chance (Math 5.5).
  • Importance and SHAP describe the model, not the world; the pneumonia model learned that asthma “lowers” risk (Lessons 8.1 and 8.2).
🎯 Hook: the treatment that wins every match and loses the league

A 1986 study in the British Medical Journal compared ways to remove kidney stones. Open surgery succeeded for 273 of 350 patients (78%). A newer keyhole method, percutaneous nephrolithotomy, succeeded for 289 of 350 (83%). The keyhole method looks better.

Now split by stone size. Small stones: surgery 93% (81/87), keyhole 87% (234/270). Large stones: surgery 73% (192/263), keyhole 69% (55/80). Surgery wins for small stones and for large stones, and loses overall.

Nobody made an arithmetic mistake. This is Simpson's paradox, and the reason behind it, a confounder, is the reason most “data-driven” decisions need more than a model.

🔮 Predict first

Why can surgery win in both groups but lose overall?

Surgery got 263 of its 350 patients with large stones (75%); keyhole only 80 of 350 (23%). Large stones fail more with either method, so surgery's overall rate is dragged down by its harder caseload. Stone size confounds the comparison: it affects both the treatment chosen and the outcome. Slide the case mix below.

The overall numbers answer “how did patients who happened to get each treatment do?”. The question a doctor asks is “what would happen to this patient under each treatment?”. Only the second is causal, and here the per-size numbers answer it better: compare like with like.

💡 Big idea A causal effect compares the same units in two worlds, treated and not treated, but we only ever see one world per unit. Every causal method is a trick for building a fair stand-in for the missing world. Randomization (A/B tests) builds it by design: the coin flip makes the groups alike in everything, measured or not. Observational methods (adjusting for confounders, propensity scores, difference-in-differences) try to rebuild it from data, and are only as good as their assumptions. Uplift models go one step further: they predict the effect for each customer, so you act only where acting helps.
🧠 Analogy: umbrellas don't cause rain

A model trained on a city's data would learn that umbrellas predict wet streets perfectly. Great for prediction: see umbrellas, expect wet streets. Terrible for policy: banning umbrellas won't dry the streets. Rain (the confounder) causes both.

Prediction asks “given what I see, what's likely?”. Causal inference asks “if I change this, what happens?”. Same data, different questions, and a model's accuracy on the first tells you nothing about the second.

🤔 Why this? Why A/B tests and uplift models?
  1. The problem before. Teams trained a model on past data, saw that customers who got a coupon bought more, and concluded coupons work. But coupons had been sent to engaged customers, who buy anyway: the effect was confounded, just like the kidney stones. Decisions made on correlations waste money and can do harm.
  2. What people tried. Before/after comparisons (confounded by everything else that changed), comparing users who chose a feature with those who didn't (self-selection), and “predict who will buy, target them”, which targets people who'd buy without you. In the uplift playground, targeting the 30% most likely to buy adds only 8.5 extra purchases per 1,000 customers; random targeting adds 13.6.
  3. Why this works. Randomizing who is treated breaks every arrow into the treatment, so the difference in outcomes is the causal effect (plus noise you can quantify, Math 5.5). Uplift models trained on randomized data estimate the effect per person: targeting the top 30% by predicted uplift adds 35.5 extra purchases, more than four times the purchase-propensity model.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Experiments take time and traffic, can't always be run (you can't randomize smoking or a price for one customer in some markets), and need care with novelty effects, interference between users and peeking at results. Uplift estimates are noisy (they're differences of two noisy predictions), and observational methods fail silently when an important confounder wasn't measured.
  5. What came next. Meta-learners beyond T and S (X-learner, R-learner, doubly robust), causal forests, double machine learning (EconML, CausalML), and causal graphs that make assumptions explicit (DoWhy). The 2021 economics Nobel went to Card, Angrist and Imbens for natural experiments and the analysis of causal relationships.

The “same-mix comparison” is adjustment: weight each treatment's per-group rates by the same mix (here the pooled one: 49% large). It answers the causal question if stone size is the only confounder. In real data there are dozens, some unmeasured, which is why randomization is the gold standard.

A/B tests: build the missing world with a coin

Flip a coin for each user: heads, new version (B); tails, old version (A). Engaged and casual users, phones and laptops, Mondays and Saturdays all land on both sides in equal proportion, including everything you didn't think to measure. Then the difference in average outcome is the causal effect of the change, and statistics tells you how much of it could be chance (Math 5.5 covers sample sizes and p-values).

Uplift modelling: who should get the coupon?

An A/B test says “the coupon adds 4.5 purchases per 100 customers on average”. But the average hides four kinds of customer (the names come from Nicholas Radcliffe's work on uplift modelling):

🎯 PersuadablesBuy only if they get the coupon. The only people worth paying for.
✅ Sure thingsBuy anyway. A coupon just gives away margin.
🪨 Lost causesWon't buy either way. Coupon wasted.
🐕 Sleeping dogsLess likely to buy if contacted (the email reminds them to cancel, or annoys them). Contact costs you a customer.

A normal “propensity” model predicts who will buy, and so ranks sure things and sleeping dogs on top: people who buy anyway. An uplift model predicts the difference a coupon makes for each customer, P(buy | coupon) − P(buy | no coupon), and needs randomized data (or careful observational methods) to learn it. Two simple recipes:

  • T-learner (“two models”): train one classifier on treated customers and one on control customers; uplift = the treated model's prediction − the control model's.
  • S-learner (“single model”): train one model with “got coupon” as an extra feature; uplift = its prediction with the feature set to 1 minus with it set to 0. Simple, but a regularized model may ignore the treatment feature and shrink the uplift toward zero.

✏️ Worked example: a T-learner by hand

Customer Kim: engagement 0.2, price sensitivity 0.9. The treated model (trained on customers who got coupons) says P(buy) = 0.38; the control model says 0.17.

  • Uplift = 0.38 − 0.17 = +0.21: a coupon raises Kim's chance of buying by 21 points. A persuadable.
  • Customer Lee: engagement 0.9, sensitivity 0.1. Treated 0.50, control 0.59 → uplift −0.09: a sleeping dog. A propensity model would rank Lee above Kim (0.50 > 0.38).
  • Send coupons in order of uplift until it falls below the coupon's cost per sale. With a $5 coupon on a $40 margin, you need uplift above 5/40 = 0.125: Kim yes, Lee no.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A T-learner is two scikit-learn models, below. For more: CausalML (0.18, from Uber: meta-learners, uplift trees, Qini curves), EconML (0.17, from Microsoft Research, now in the PyWhy project: double ML, causal forests, DR-learner), DoWhy (0.14, PyWhy: write the causal graph, identify the effect, estimate, then try to refute it), and statsmodels for regressions with difference-in-differences terms.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 20_000
X = rng.uniform(size=(n, 2))                    # engagement, price sensitivity
t = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                        # coupon assigned at random (an A/B test)
p0 = 0.05 + 0.6 * X[:, 0]                        # chance of buying without a coupon
tau = 0.30 * X[:, 1] * (1 - X[:, 0]) - 0.12 * X[:, 0] * (1 - X[:, 1])   # true uplift (unknown in real life)
y = rng.uniform(size=n) < np.clip(p0 + t * tau, 0, 1)

# T-learner: one model per arm, uplift = difference of their predictions
m1 = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X[t == 1], y[t == 1])
m0 = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X[t == 0], y[t == 0])
uplift = m1.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] - m0.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

print(f"average effect: estimated {uplift.mean():.3f}, true {tau.mean():.3f}")
top = np.argsort(-uplift)[: n // 5]              # the 20% we'd send coupons to
buyers = np.argsort(-m1.predict_proba(X)[:, 1])[: n // 5]   # vs the 20% most likely to buy
print(f"true uplift, top 20% by uplift: {tau[top].mean():.3f}; by purchase probability: {tau[buyers].mean():.3f}")

It prints an estimated average effect of 0.038 (true 0.045), and a true uplift of 0.135 among the top 20% by uplift against 0.009 among the 20% most likely to buy: fifteen times more effect per coupon. In real data you don't know tau; you evaluate uplift models on a held-out randomized set with Qini or uplift curves (CausalML has them).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Before you build a “who will buy” model for a campaign, ask whether the goal is prediction or effect. Keep a random holdout (say 10%) out of every campaign; it costs little and turns every campaign into an experiment, so you can measure real lift and later train an uplift model.

At scale

Experimentation platforms run thousands of A/B tests a year with automatic checks (sample ratio, guardrail metrics, variance reduction such as CUPED). Marketing, pricing and retention teams use uplift models and causal forests on that experiment data, and economists use observational methods for questions experiments can't reach.

🎤 Interview angle

“Users who use our new search filter buy 30% more. Should we make the filter more prominent?” A strong answer:

  • Spots self-selection: engaged, high-intent users are the ones who find filters; intent confounds filter use and buying.
  • Proposes an A/B test of prominence (the actual decision), with a fixed sample size, guardrails and a check for sample ratio mismatch.
  • If an experiment is impossible: adjust for confounders or use propensity scores, difference-in-differences around a launch, and state the assumptions.
  • For targeting, distinguishes propensity from uplift and names the four customer types, especially sleeping dogs.
🏭 In the real world
A headline change worth $100M a year 🏢 Microsoft Bing · 2017 Kohavi & Thomke: The Surprising Power of Online Experiments (HBR)

How: an idea for displaying ad headlines sat in the backlog for more than six months; when an engineer finally A/B tested it, revenue rose 12%, estimated at over $100 million a year in the US alone.

Why this method: nobody's intuition had ranked the idea highly; only a randomized test could reveal its causal effect.

The kidney-stone paradox 🧪 Research · 1986 Charig et al.: Comparison of treatment of renal calculi (BMJ)

How: compared open surgery, percutaneous nephrolithotomy and shockwave lithotripsy on 1,052 patients; later reanalyses used it as the textbook example of Simpson's paradox.

Why this method: treatment wasn't randomized, so stone size confounded the overall comparison.

Uplift at Uber 🧰 Open source · 2020 CausalML: Python Package for Causal Machine Learning

How: meta-learners (S, T, X, R), uplift trees and evaluation curves for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects from experiment data.

Why this method: targeting promotions by estimated effect instead of predicted behaviour.

The four customer types 📜 History · 2007 Radcliffe: Generating Incremental Sales through uplift modelling (PDF)

How: uses control groups to model the incremental effect of a campaign; splits customers into persuadables, sure things, lost causes and sleeping dogs.

Why this method: money spent on anyone but persuadables is wasted, or worse.

Causal assumptions, written down 🧰 Open source · 2020 DoWhy: An End-to-End Library for Causal Inference

How: model (a causal graph), identify (is the effect estimable?), estimate, refute (placebo treatments, random common causes).

Why this method: observational estimates are only as good as their assumptions; making them explicit and testing them is the honest way.

Check yourself

1. City T had 200 orders a day before a launch and 260 after; city C (no launch) went from 150 to 180. What's the difference-in-differences estimate of the launch's effect?
T changed by 260 − 200 = 60; C by 180 − 150 = 30. DiD = 60 − 30 = +30 orders a day, assuming T would have followed C's trend without the launch (parallel trends). +60 is the naive before/after; +80 the after-only gap.
2. Marketing wants to send a retention offer to the 10% of customers your churn model rates most likely to leave. What's the risk, and the better approach?
Churn risk predicts behaviour without the offer; what matters is how the offer changes it. Contacting some customers can trigger cancellation. An uplift model trained on randomized data targets persuadables. SHAP explains the churn model, not the offer's effect.
3. Patients who take a supplement have 20% fewer heart attacks in observational data. Which explanation must you rule out before believing the supplement works?
Lifestyle and income affect both who takes supplements and heart risk: a classic confounder. Adjusting for measured confounders helps; a randomized trial settles it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Prediction asks “what's likely?”; causal inference asks “what happens if we act?”. Confounders make the two diverge (Simpson's paradox: kidney stones).
  • Randomized A/B tests build the missing counterfactual by design; mind peeking, novelty, sample ratio mismatch and interference.
  • Uplift = P(outcome | treated) − P(outcome | not treated) per customer. Target persuadables, skip sure things, lost causes and sleeping dogs. T-learner: two models; S-learner: one model with a treatment feature.
  • Ranking by purchase propensity can add fewer extra purchases than random targeting.
  • Without experiments: adjustment, propensity scores, difference-in-differences, each with an assumption you must defend. Libraries: CausalML, EconML, DoWhy.
