Phase 8 · Lesson 1

Interpretability: what is your model actually using?

A model can score well for the wrong reasons. Feature importance, permutation importance and partial dependence plots let you ask a trained model “which inputs matter, and how?”, and they can fool you too. Here's how to read them without getting fooled.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A tree picks each split to reduce impurity (Gini); a random forest averages many deep trees (Lessons 4.1 and 4.2).
  • Scores computed on the training data are optimistic; honest scores come from held-out data (Lesson 0.6).
  • Two variables are correlated when one tends to move with the other (Math 5.2).
🎯 Hook: the model that said asthma protects you

In the 1990s a research team trained models to predict which pneumonia patients were likely to die, so that low-risk patients could be sent home and high-risk ones admitted. The most accurate model was a neural network (AUC 0.86 against 0.77 for logistic regression on one dataset; AUC, the area under the ROC curve, is Lesson 5.4).

They shipped neither the neural net nor anything they couldn't read. Why? A simple, readable rule-based model trained on the same data had learned “has asthma ⇒ lower risk”. The pattern was real in the data: asthmatic pneumonia patients were usually sent straight to intensive care, and that aggressive care worked. A model that sends them home because of it would kill people. Rich Caruana and colleagues told the story in their 2015 KDD paper: if the readable model learned it, the neural net surely had too, and nobody could see it there.

This lesson is about the tools that let you see what a model learned before it hurts someone.

🔮 Predict first

You add a column of pure random numbers (say, a shuffled customer ID: every row a different value) to a dataset and retrain a random forest. Where does the forest's built-in feature_importances_ rank this useless column?

In the middle, often above real features. On scikit-learn's diabetes data a random ID column gets 5.6% of the impurity importance, more than age (4.1%). Deep trees can always find a split on a column with hundreds of distinct values that “purifies” a few training rows. Playground 1 shows it, and the fix.

💡 Big idea Interpretability tools answer narrow questions, and you must know which one. Impurity importance: “how much did splits on this feature clean up the training data?” Permutation importance: “how much worse does the model get on new data if this feature's values are scrambled?” Partial dependence: “on average, how does the prediction change as I slide this feature?” ICE curves: the same, one line per row, so you can see when the average hides two opposite effects. All of them explain the model, not the world.
🧠 Analogy: who's the most valuable player?

Impurity importance is like counting touches of the ball: the player who touched it most looks important, even if half the touches were pointless dribbles. Permutation importance is like benching the player for the next match and watching the score: if the team does just as well without them, they weren't carrying it.

And if two strikers are near-identical twins, benching one costs almost nothing, because the twin covers. Each looks unimportant, though the team would collapse without both. That's the correlated-features trap of Playground 2.

🤔 Why this? Why permutation importance and partial dependence?
  1. The problem before. Linear models came with readable coefficients. Forests and boosted trees (Phase 4) are far more accurate on tables but are hundreds of trees nobody can read, so “why did it predict that?” and “is it using something silly?” had no answer. Regulators, doctors and your own debugging all need one.
  2. What people tried. Breiman's forests and later libraries reported impurity importance (mean decrease in impurity, MDI): add up how much each feature's splits reduced Gini. It's free, but it's computed on training data and favours columns with many distinct values: here a random customer_id gets 10% of the credit (Strobl et al. documented this bias in 2007).
  3. Why this works. Permutation importance measures what you actually care about, held-out performance, with a simple experiment: shuffle one column so its link to the label is broken, and see how much the score falls. Test accuracy 0.795 → shuffle income → about 0.608: income is worth about 0.19 of accuracy. Shuffle customer_id → still 0.795: worth nothing. Partial dependence uses the same “change one input, hold the rest” trick to draw how a feature moves the prediction.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. It needs extra predictions (features × repeats × rows), it's noisy on small test sets (repeat and report ± spread), and it breaks on correlated features: shuffling one creates impossible rows and its twin covers for it, so both look weak. Partial dependence averages away interactions (ICE curves reveal them) and also invents unrealistic rows when features are correlated.
  5. What came next. SHAP gives every feature a fair share of each individual prediction, with a guarantee that the shares add up (Lesson 8.2). For correlated features, people permute groups of features together or drop a column and retrain. And none of these say what would happen if you changed a feature in the real world: that's causal inference (Lesson 8.4).

Step 1: impurity importance, and why it's fooled

Every split in a tree reduces impurity a bit (Lesson 4.1). Impurity importance (MDI, mean decrease in impurity) adds up those reductions per feature, weighted by how many training rows passed through the split, averages over trees, and normalizes to sum to 1. That's feature_importances_ in scikit-learn.

Two flaws hide in that sentence. It's measured on the training rows, where a deep tree splits on noise happily. And a feature with many distinct values offers many candidate thresholds, so by pure chance one of them will separate a few training rows nicely, deep in the tree. A 3-valued column offers only 2 thresholds; a 300-valued ID offers 299.

  • Repeat with a different shuffle (0.60 → 0.20 drop) and average: 0.30. Report the spread too; with 5 rows it's huge.
  • Nothing is retrained. The model is a black box you only call predict on, which is why this works for any model: forests, boosting, neural nets, a vendor's API.

Step 2: the correlated-features trap

Permutation importance asks “how much does the model need this column?” If another column carries the same information, the answer is “not much”, even when the information itself is vital. Real data is full of near-copies: income and tax paid, age and years of work history, temperature in °C and in °F.

🔮 Predict first

You add income_copy, an exact duplicate of income, to the loan data and retrain. What happens to income's permutation importance?

It drops from about 0.19 to 0.09, below debt ratio, though income is the strongest signal in the data. Shuffle income and the trees just read income_copy instead. Impurity importance splits it too: 0.27 each instead of 0.50 for one. Slide the correlation below.

The fix isn't a better formula; it's a better question. Permute correlated groups together (shuffle income and its copy at once: the drop comes back), or drop a column and retrain to ask “could the model do without it?”. Before reading any importance chart, look at the correlations between your features (Lesson 1.1).

Step 3: partial dependence and ICE, the shape of an effect

Importance tells you how much. A partial dependence plot (PDP; Friedman, 2001) shows how: pick a feature, set it to a value v for every row, predict, and average. Repeat for a grid of values v. The curve is the model's average answer to “what if this feature were v, everything else as it is?”

PD(v) = average over rows i of f(xi with the feature set to v) “Force the feature to v for everyone, ask the model, average the answers.”

An ICE curve (individual conditional expectation; Goldstein et al., 2015) is the same thing without the averaging: one line per row. The PDP is just the average of the ICE lines. Why bother? Because an average can hide two groups moving in opposite directions.

🔮 Predict first

An online shop's model predicts profit per customer. The data was generated so that a discount adds profit for new customers and costs profit for loyal ones (they'd buy anyway). Half the customers are new. What does the partial dependence plot of “discount” look like?

Nearly flat. The +2 per unit for new customers and −2 for loyal ones cancel in the average. Turn on ICE lines below and the flat line splits into two opposite fans: the most important pattern in the data was invisible in the PDP.

Two cautions. The grid runs from the 5th to the 95th percentile of the feature (scikit-learn's default), because predictions at values the model never saw are guesses. And when features are correlated, “set income to $20k but keep the tax paid of a $200k earner” creates rows that don't exist; the curve then describes nonsense inputs. Accumulated local effects (ALE) plots are one answer to that.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026) ships all of this in sklearn.inspection: permutation_importance, partial_dependence and PartialDependenceDisplay (with kind="both" for PDP + ICE). Every tree model has feature_importances_ (impurity-based). For glass-box models built to be read, see Microsoft's InterpretML (explainable boosting machines, the descendants of the pneumonia paper's GA²M models).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance, PartialDependenceDisplay
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X["random_id"] = np.random.default_rng(0).permutation(len(X))  # pure noise: 442 unique values
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)
rf = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=300, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

mdi = dict(zip(X.columns, rf.feature_importances_))       # impurity-based, from training data
perm = permutation_importance(rf, X_te, y_te, n_repeats=10, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)  # held-out R² drop
for name, m, s in sorted(zip(X.columns, perm.importances_mean, perm.importances_std), key=lambda t: -t[1]):
    print(f"{name:10s} MDI {mdi[name]:.3f}   permutation {m:+.3f} ± {s:.3f}")

# PDP (thick line) + ICE (one thin line per row) for two features
PartialDependenceDisplay.from_estimator(rf, X_te, ["bmi", "s5"], kind="both", subsample=50, random_state=0)

What it prints: bmi and s5 on top (permutation +0.144 and +0.098 of R²), and random_id with an impurity share of 0.056, more than age (0.041), but a permutation importance of −0.006 ± 0.012: indistinguishable from zero. Always print the ± spread; a negative value just means “no better than shuffled”.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Print permutation importance on the validation set for every model you train, next to the built-in importance. It's ten lines and it catches the classic bugs: an ID column, a leaked “cancellation date”, a feature that's only filled in after the outcome. A surprising feature at the top is usually a bug, not an insight.

At scale

Model reviews and model risk teams (banks, insurers, healthcare) require documented global explanations (importance, PDPs with monotonicity checks) and per-decision explanations (SHAP, reason codes) before launch, and track importance over time in monitoring dashboards: a feature whose importance jumps is a drift alarm (Lesson 8.6).

🎤 Interview angle

“Your random forest says user_id is the third most important feature. What's going on, and what do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Names the cause: impurity importance is computed on training data and is biased toward high-cardinality features, so deep trees split on an ID to memorize rows.
  • Checks with permutation importance on held-out data (with repeats and a ± spread). If it's still important there, suspect leakage (IDs assigned in time order, or per-segment), not magic.
  • Knows the limits: correlated features share and hide credit (permute groups or drop-and-retrain), and importance describes the model, not causation.
  • Uses PDP for the shape and ICE to spot interactions; mentions SHAP for per-prediction explanations (Lesson 8.2).
🏭 In the real world
Pneumonia risk and the asthma rule 🧪 Research · 2015 Caruana et al.: Intelligible Models for HealthCare (KDD 2015)

How: fitted generalized additive models with pairwise interactions (GA²M), whose per-feature curves can be plotted and edited, to pneumonia mortality data; doctors could read every learned effect.

Why this method: the readable curves exposed dangerous patterns like “asthma lowers risk” that came from treatment, not biology, so they could be removed before deployment. An unreadable model with the same pattern was judged too risky.

The random-column test 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Permutation Importance vs Random Forest Feature Importance (MDI)

How: adds a high-cardinality random number and a 3-valued random category to the Titanic data; the forest's impurity importance ranks the random number among the most important features, while permutation importance on the test set puts both near zero.

Why this method: a cheap sanity check you can copy: if your importance method ranks a column of random numbers highly, don't trust its other rankings either.

Where the bias comes from 🧪 Research · 2007 Strobl et al.: Bias in random forest variable importance measures (BMC Bioinformatics)

How: simulated predictors with different numbers of categories, none related to the outcome, and showed the Gini importance prefers those with more categories; proposed conditional inference trees and subsampling as a fix.

Why this method: in genetics, predictors differ wildly in how many values they take, so a biased ranking points researchers at the wrong genes.

ICE curves 🧪 Research · 2015 Goldstein et al.: Peeking Inside the Black Box (ICE plots)

How: draws one partial-dependence curve per observation (and “centered” ICE that start at zero) to show heterogeneity hidden by the average.

Why this method: interactions are where black-box models beat linear ones, and they're exactly what a single PDP averages away.

🤯 Fun fact · permutation importance is as old as random forests

Breiman's 2001 random forest paper already describes it: after each tree is built, randomly permute the values of one variable in that tree's out-of-bag rows, run them down the tree again, and measure the increase in misclassification. Twenty-five years later that “noise it up and see what breaks” experiment is still the first thing most practitioners reach for.

Check yourself

1. On 200 test rows your model's accuracy is 0.85. With the “tenure” column shuffled it's 0.78, 0.80 and 0.79 over three shuffles. What's tenure's permutation importance?
Importance = baseline − average shuffled score = 0.85 − (0.78 + 0.80 + 0.79)/3 = 0.85 − 0.79 = 0.06 of accuracy, with a spread of about ±0.01 across shuffles. It's a drop in the metric, not a ratio and not the shuffled score itself.
2. A churn model's top permutation feature, on held-out data, is “number of calls to the cancellation line”. The product team is delighted: “Let's reduce calls to that line!” What do you say?
Importance explains the model, not the world. Calls to cancel are probably a symptom of the decision to leave, possibly logged after it, which would also make it a leak (Lesson 0.6). Whether an intervention changes churn is a causal question: run an experiment (Lesson 8.4).
3. The partial dependence plot of “price” in a demand model is flat. A colleague concludes the model ignores price. Which check settles it?
A PDP is an average of ICE lines; opposite effects in subgroups cancel, as in Playground 3 where the discount's PDP is almost flat but each customer's ICE line is steep. A large permutation importance next to a flat PDP is the tell-tale sign of an interaction.
📝 TL;DR
  • Impurity importance (feature_importances_) is free but computed on training data and biased toward many-valued columns: a random ID can get 10% of the credit.
  • Permutation importance = held-out score drop when one column is shuffled. Works for any model; repeat it and report the spread.
  • Correlated features share and hide importance: permute them as a group or drop-and-retrain.
  • PDP shows the average shape of an effect; ICE lines (one per row) reveal interactions the average hides.
  • All of these explain the model, not the world. Next: fair per-prediction credit with SHAP (8.2).
