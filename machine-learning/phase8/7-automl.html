Phase 8 · Lesson 7

AutoML: what it automates and what it can't

AutoML tools try hundreds of preprocessing steps, models and hyperparameters, then stack the best into an ensemble, all from one line of code. They're genuinely good at that. They can't tell you what to predict, how to measure success, whether your data leaks, or whether the model can run where it must. This lesson shows both halves.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Hyperparameter search: grid, random and Bayesian search over settings, judged by validation scores (Lesson 5.7).
  • Stacking: a model that combines the predictions of other models (Lesson 4.5).
  • Leakage and honest validation, including time-aware splits (Lessons 0.6, 5.1).
🎯 Hook: nine AutoML systems, 104 tasks, one awkward baseline

The AutoML Benchmark (AMLB; Gijsbers et al., Journal of Machine Learning Research, 2024) ran 9 well-known AutoML frameworks on 71 classification and 33 regression tasks, with fixed time budgets and the same hardware for all. AutoGluon achieved the best average rank in almost every setting.

Two quieter findings matter more for your job. The winner was generally not significantly better than the second-best framework. And the authors note that a tuned random forest is a strong baseline: only AutoGluon and LightAutoML ranked significantly better than it across all settings.

So AutoML is real, useful, and not magic. What exactly is it doing, and what is still your job?

🔮 Predict first

An AutoML-style random search tries 60 configurations on a noisy dataset and picks the one with the best accuracy on a 60-row validation set: 93%. What do you expect on fresh test data?

On this lesson's data the winner scores 78% on test, below a plain default random forest (80%). With 60 tries, the best validation score is partly the best luck. Bigger searches need bigger or cross-validated validation sets. Playground 1.

More search kept raising the validation score and stopped helping, then hurt, on test: that's overfitting the validation set. And when the data contains a leak, AutoML finds it faster and more thoroughly than any human, and proudly reports 98%. A search is only as honest as the validation it optimizes.

💡 Big idea AutoML automates the inner loop: given a table, a target, a metric and a validation scheme, search preprocessing × model families × hyperparameters efficiently (Bayesian optimization, successive halving, meta-learned defaults) and ensemble the best. Everything around that loop stays human: framing the problem, choosing the target and metric, designing validation that mimics production, catching leakage, fixing data quality, and meeting deployment constraints (latency, size, explainability, fairness). Use AutoML to find a strong baseline fast; spend your own time on the outer loop.
🧠 Analogy: a satnav

A satnav is superb at the inner problem: given a start and a destination, find the fastest route among millions, faster than any driver. It can't tell you where to go, notice that the destination you typed is the wrong town, or know that the bridge on its route closed this morning. AutoML is a satnav for model search: trust it on routes, never on destinations.

🤔 Why this? Why AutoML?
  1. The problem before. Model selection was manual: try a few models, tune each by hand, stack a few if there's time. It took days, depended on the person's habits, and rarely explored the space well; experts' time went to tuning instead of data and framing.
  2. What people tried. Grid search (explodes combinatorially), random search (better, Lesson 5.7), and copying Kaggle recipes. Each tuned one model at a time and wasted compute on bad configurations trained to completion.
  3. Why this works. AutoML combines three ideas: smart search (Bayesian optimization learns which regions of the space score well), early stopping of bad candidates (successive halving: train 27 configurations briefly, keep the best third, train those longer… total cost 81 units instead of 243 for training all 27 fully, in Playground 2), and ensembling the good ones (stacking, Lesson 4.5), which is why AutoGluon emphasizes multi-layer stacking.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Compute (hours of CPU for the best presets), big and slow ensembles that may break latency budgets, opacity, and above all garbage in, garbage out: it optimizes whatever metric and split you give it, so a leaky feature or a random split on time-series data produces a confident, wrong model (98% → 56% in our table).
  5. What came next. Meta-learning (start from configurations that worked on similar datasets), time- and cost-aware search (FLAML), presets that trade accuracy for deployment size, and tabular foundation models that predict without per-dataset training (AutoGluon 1.6's top preset already uses some). The outer loop is still yours (Phase 9).

Step 2: spend compute where it matters (successive halving)

🔮 Predict first

You have 27 configurations and a budget of 81 training units. Option A: train 9 random ones fully (9 units each). Option B: train all 27 for 1 unit, keep the best 9 for 3 units, keep the best 3 for 9 units. Which usually finds the better model?

Usually B. Short runs are noisy but informative enough to drop the clear losers, so all 27 get a look for the price of 9. It fails when early scores mislead (a slow starter that would have won): raise the early-score noise below to see when.

✏️ Worked example: a realistic AutoML budget

A team has 200,000 rows and 80 features, and wants a churn model by Friday.

  • Monday (you): define churn (no purchase in 60 days), the decision (retention offer, so maybe uplift later), the metric (recall at the top 5% the team can call), and a time split: train on Jan–Sep, validate on Oct, test on Nov.
  • Tuesday (AutoML): a 1-hour run with a good preset on the train + validation data. Result: a stack of boosted trees and a neural net, plus a leaderboard. Also note the best single model's score.
  • Wednesday (you): check feature importance for leaks (a “cancellation_requested” column at the top?), compare with a default LightGBM baseline, check latency and size against the serving budget; maybe pick the single model if it's within 0.5% of the stack.
  • Thursday–Friday (you): one look at the November test set, a model card (8.5), monitoring (8.6). AutoML saved perhaps a day of tuning; everything that decides whether the model is right was still yours.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Status as of late 2026 (PyPI): AutoGluon 1.6 (Amazon; stacking-heavy, top-ranked in AMLB; presets from medium_quality to best_quality and a GPU-only extreme_quality that uses tabular foundation models), FLAML 2.7 (Microsoft; fast, cost-aware search, scikit-learn-style), H2O AutoML (h2o 3.46; Java backend, good for big data and stacked ensembles), TPOT 1.1 (genetic programming over whole pipelines; the TPOT2 rewrite was merged into the main package), and auto-sklearn (Bayesian optimization plus meta-learning; its last release, 0.15.0, dates from September 2022). Built into scikit-learn: HalvingRandomSearchCV.

from scipy.stats import loguniform, randint
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.experimental import enable_halving_search_cv  # noqa: F401  (still experimental)
from sklearn.model_selection import HalvingRandomSearchCV

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
space = {"learning_rate": loguniform(0.01, 0.5), "max_leaf_nodes": randint(4, 64), "min_samples_leaf": randint(5, 50)}
search = HalvingRandomSearchCV(
    HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0), space,
    n_candidates=27, factor=3,            # 27 configs → keep the best third each round
    resource="max_iter", min_resources=10, max_resources=270,  # cheap rounds first: 10, 30, 90, 270 iterations
    cv=3, random_state=0,
).fit(X, y)
print(search.n_candidates_, search.n_resources_, search.best_params_, round(search.best_score_, 3))

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Run AutoML on day two (after you've framed the problem and built a leak-free, time-aware split) to learn how much signal there is and what the ceiling looks like. If a single LightGBM is within a hair of the ensemble, ship the single model: smaller, faster, explainable.

At scale

Platform teams offer AutoML-like search as a service inside the ML platform, with company-specific validation, fairness checks and deployment constraints baked in, so hundreds of product teams can train decent models. Data scientists' time moves to problem framing, features, experimentation and monitoring.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why not just use AutoML for everything?” A strong answer:

  • It automates the inner loop (preprocessing, model, hyperparameters, ensembling) well, and the AMLB benchmark shows good frameworks beat simple baselines, though often by small margins over a tuned random forest or each other.
  • It can't frame the problem, choose the metric, design validation (time or group splits), detect leakage, fix labels, or meet latency, size, explainability and fairness constraints.
  • Knows the failure modes: overfitting the validation set with big searches, optimizing a leak, giant ensembles that can't be served.
  • Uses it as a fast baseline and a ceiling estimate, then decides on purpose what to ship.
🏭 In the real world
The AutoML Benchmark 🧪 Research · 2024 Gijsbers et al.: AMLB: an AutoML Benchmark (JMLR)

How: 9 frameworks on 71 classification and 33 regression tasks with 1-hour and 4-hour budgets, comparing accuracy, inference time and failures; open-source tooling to rerun it.

Why this method: AutoML papers had compared tools inconsistently; a shared, careful benchmark showed real but modest differences, and that baselines matter.

AutoGluon 🧰 Open source auto.gluon.ai

How: trains many model types with good defaults and combines them by multi-layer stacking and bagging, rather than spending most of the budget on hyperparameter search.

Why this method: ensembles of diverse strong models are robust; stacking reuses the work of every model trained.

FLAML 🧰 Open source Microsoft FLAML

How: a cost-aware search that starts with cheap configurations and expands toward expensive ones only when they look promising, under a time budget.

Why this method: good models in minutes on a laptop, with a scikit-learn-style fit.

TPOT 🧰 Open source EpistasisLab/tpot

How: evolves whole scikit-learn pipelines (preprocessing + model) with genetic programming; the TPOT2 rewrite added multi-objective search and flexible search spaces.

Why this method: outputs a readable pipeline you can inspect and export, not a black-box ensemble.

🤯 Fun fact · AutoML tools now ship foundation models

AutoGluon 1.6's new extreme_quality preset uses tabular foundation models (TabICL, TabDPT and others) that make predictions on a new table without being trained on it from scratch, in the spirit of an LLM answering from examples in its prompt. It needs a GPU and targets datasets up to about 100,000 rows; the sibling Deep Learning course covers how such models work.

Check yourself

1. Successive halving with factor 3 starts 81 configurations at 1 unit each. How many units does it spend in total over 4 rounds (81 → 27 → 9 → 3 configurations, at 1, 3, 9, 27 units)?
Each round costs the same: 81 × 1 = 27 × 3 = 9 × 9 = 3 × 27 = 81, so 4 × 81 = 324 units. Training all 81 to 27 units would cost 81 × 27 = 2,187: almost 7× more.
2. An AutoML run on your sales-forecast table reports 0.98 R² with a random 80/20 split. The data is daily, three years long. What's your first concern?
AutoML optimizes the split you give it. Random splits on time series leak information across neighbouring days and seasons (Lesson 7.1). The fix is a validation design decision, which is your job, not the tool's.
3. AutoML's best ensemble (38 models) beats a single LightGBM by 0.4% AUC, but takes 120 ms per prediction against a 20 ms budget. What do you ship?
Deployment constraints are part of the problem definition. A model that breaks the latency budget isn't a candidate. Many AutoML tools offer deployment-oriented presets for exactly this; the decision is still yours.
📝 TL;DR
  • AutoML automates the inner loop: preprocessing, model choice, hyperparameters and ensembling, with smart search, successive halving and stacking.
  • It's good (AutoGluon tops the AMLB benchmark), but margins over a tuned random forest or other frameworks are often small.
  • Big searches overfit small validation sets; a leak gets optimized, not detected.
  • Your job: framing, target and metric, validation design, leakage, data quality, deployment constraints, fairness, monitoring.
  • Use it for a fast baseline and a ceiling; ship what fits the constraints.
