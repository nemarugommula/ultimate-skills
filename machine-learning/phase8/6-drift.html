Phase 8 · Lesson 6

Drift monitoring & retraining

The day you deploy a model is the best it will ever be. Customers, prices, fraudsters and the weather keep changing; the model doesn't. Here's how to tell what changed, measure it with two simple statistics, watch a model even before the labels arrive, and decide when to retrain.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A model learns P(y | x) from the training data's distribution; validation assumes new data looks the same (Lessons 0.6 and 5.1).
  • A histogram and a cumulative distribution describe a variable's distribution (Math 5.1).
  • A p-value says how surprising a difference would be if nothing had changed (Math 5.5).
🎯 Hook: the week toilet paper broke the models

In the week of 12–18 April 2020, the top Amazon searches included “toilet paper”, “face mask”, “hand sanitizer” and “N95 mask”. Phone cases and chargers had dropped out of the top 10 within days. MIT Technology Review collected what happened next to the models trained on 2019 behaviour.

An Indian supplier of sauces and condiments saw its demand forecasts break under sudden bulk orders: in the words of the consultant who helped them, it “was never trained on a spike like this”. A streaming company's recommendations got less accurate as new subscribers flooded in. Fraud systems coped better: spending had mostly shrunk back to old habits, and engineers only had to step in for a surge in garden equipment and power tools.

Same pandemic, very different damage. The difference is what kind of change hit each model, and whether anyone was watching.

🔮 Predict first

A fraud model's inputs (amounts, merchants, times) look exactly like last year's: every drift alarm on the features is green. Can its accuracy still have collapsed?

Yes. That's concept drift: P(y | x) changes while P(x) stays put. Input monitoring can't see it; only labels (or proxies for them) can. Playground 2 lets you rotate the true boundary and watch the feature alarms stay quiet while accuracy falls.

Monitoring is about catching all three, and the cheapest signals (inputs, predictions) only catch some of them.

💡 Big idea Watch four layers, cheapest first: the data (schema, missing values, ranges), the input distributions (PSI, KS test against a reference window), the prediction distribution (same tests on the scores), and the performance once labels arrive (or an estimate of it before). Then retrain on a schedule, on a trigger, or both, and always validate the new model against the old one before it takes over.
🧠 Analogy: a map of a city that keeps building

Your model is a paper map. Data drift is new neighbourhoods you've never mapped: people start asking about places off your map's edge. Concept drift is a street that became one-way: it's on the map, it looks the same, and your directions are now wrong. Label shift is rush hour: the same streets, just far more people going one way. Checking the map's edges is easy; noticing one-way streets requires someone to actually drive.

🤔 Why this? Why PSI, KS tests and retraining policies?
  1. The problem before. Models were evaluated once, deployed, and forgotten. Accuracy decayed quietly until a business metric broke, often months later, because labels (did the loan default? did the customer churn?) arrive weeks or months after the prediction.
  2. What people tried. Waiting for labels (too slow), eyeballing dashboards (nobody watches 200 features), and retraining daily “just in case” (expensive, and a buggy data day becomes a buggy model the next morning).
  3. Why this works. Distribution tests compare today's inputs with a reference window and need no labels. The Population Stability Index (PSI) sums, over bins, (current share − reference share) × ln(current ÷ reference): shift a standard-normal feature by half a standard deviation and PSI reaches about 0.24, near the usual “significant shift” line of 0.25. The KS test measures the largest gap between the two cumulative distributions and gives a p-value.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Input tests are blind to concept drift; with large samples the KS test flags shifts too small to matter (a 0.05 standard-deviation nudge becomes “highly significant” at 50,000 rows per window); many features means many false alarms; and PSI depends on the binning. Retraining fixes drift only if the new data reflects the new world and has labels.
  5. What came next. Performance estimation without labels (NannyML's confidence-based estimation uses the model's own calibrated probabilities), drift tests on embeddings and on SHAP values (Lesson 8.2), online learning for fast-moving streams, and monitoring platforms (Evidently, NannyML, alibi-detect, cloud model monitors).

Step 1: measure a shift with PSI and KS

PSI: cut the reference sample into 10 bins of equal share (deciles), count what share of the current window falls in each, and add up (current − reference) × ln(current ÷ reference). Identical distributions give 0; a common rule of thumb in credit risk: below 0.10 little change, 0.10–0.25 moderate, above 0.25 a significant shift that needs action.

PSI = Σ over bins (curb − refb) · ln(curb ⁄ refb) “For every bin, how much did its share change, weighted by how big that change is in relative terms.”

KS statistic: the largest vertical gap between the two cumulative distribution curves, from 0 (same) to 1 (no overlap). scipy.stats.ks_2samp also returns a p-value: how likely a gap that big would be if both samples came from the same distribution.

🔮 Predict first

You shift a feature by a tiny 0.05 standard deviations, a change nobody would act on, and compare windows of 50,000 rows each. What do PSI and the KS p-value say?

With 50,000 rows, the KS test detects even a 0.05 nudge with a microscopic p-value, while PSI stays far below 0.1. A p-value answers “is there any difference?”, not “does it matter?”. Big samples make everything significant: use effect sizes (PSI, the KS distance itself) for alerts.

✏️ Worked example: PSI with 3 bins

A credit score split into low / mid / high. Reference shares: 30% / 40% / 30%. This month: 20% / 40% / 40%.

  • Low: (0.20 − 0.30) × ln(0.20 / 0.30) = (−0.10) × (−0.405) = 0.041.
  • Mid: 0 × ln(1) = 0. High: (0.40 − 0.30) × ln(0.40 / 0.30) = 0.10 × 0.288 = 0.029.
  • PSI = 0.041 + 0 + 0.029 = 0.070: “little change” by the rule of thumb, though a 10-point move from low to high scores is worth a look. Every term is ≥ 0 (both factors share a sign), so shifts in different bins never cancel.

Step 3: when to retrain

🔮 Predict first

Labels arrive 6 weeks late. A sudden market shock hits. Which policy recovers fastest: retraining every 13 weeks, or retraining when measured accuracy drops below a threshold?

The trigger is only as fast as the labels: it can't see the shock until 6 weeks of post-shock labels arrive, and the retrain then uses data that's still mostly pre-shock. A schedule ignores the shock entirely until its next slot. Unlabelled signals (input and prediction drift) are what let you react sooner. Try both below.

In practice teams combine both: a schedule (weekly or monthly, matched to how fast the domain moves and how fast labels arrive) plus triggers on drift or estimated performance for surprises. And every retrain goes through the same gate as a new model: validate on recent data, compare with the champion, then promote (Lesson 8.5). Retraining on corrupted data is the fastest way to turn a data bug into a model bug.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

For monitoring: Evidently (0.7.23 as of late 2026; drift presets and reports), NannyML (0.13; performance estimation without labels, CBPE), alibi-detect (0.13, from Seldon; drift and outlier detectors including for embeddings; note it's under the Business Source License, not a classic open-source licence), plus cloud monitors in SageMaker, Vertex AI and Azure ML.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import ks_2samp

def psi(ref, cur, bins=10):
    """Population Stability Index with decile bins taken from the reference sample."""
    edges = np.quantile(ref, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)[1:-1])
    r = np.bincount(np.searchsorted(edges, ref), minlength=bins) / len(ref)
    c = np.bincount(np.searchsorted(edges, cur), minlength=bins) / len(cur)
    r, c = np.clip(r, 1e-4, None), np.clip(c, 1e-4, None)
    return float(np.sum((c - r) * np.log(c / r)))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ref = rng.normal(0, 1, 50_000)
for shift in [0.0, 0.05, 0.5]:
    cur = rng.normal(shift, 1, 50_000)
    ks = ks_2samp(ref, cur)
    print(f"shift {shift:4.2f}: PSI {psi(ref, cur):.3f}  KS D {ks.statistic:.3f}  p {ks.pvalue:.1e}")

Output: shift 0.00 gives PSI 0.000 and p ≈ 0.56; shift 0.05 gives PSI 0.003 but p ≈ 7e-12 (“significant”, irrelevant); shift 0.50 gives PSI 0.241 and p ≈ 0. 🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Log every prediction with its inputs and a timestamp, then join the labels when they arrive. A daily job that computes PSI for the score and the top 10 features against the training data, plus accuracy on whatever labels have arrived, catches most problems. Retrain monthly by hand, comparing against the current model.

At scale

Hundreds of models, each with monitored features, predictions, estimated and realized performance, and fairness gaps by group; alert thresholds tuned to avoid fatigue; automated retraining pipelines with validation gates and canary rollouts; and on-call runbooks that start with “is it a data pipeline bug?” before “is it drift?”.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you monitor a credit-default model in production, where labels take 12 months?” A strong answer:

  • Layers: data quality → input drift (PSI per feature vs the training window) → score distribution and approval rate → estimated performance → proxy labels (early missed payments) → realized performance as labels mature.
  • Distinguishes data drift, concept drift and label shift, and which signals see each; knows KS p-values explode with sample size, so alerts use effect sizes.
  • Retraining: scheduled (e.g. quarterly) plus triggers; validates against the champion on the most recent labelled cohort before promoting; watches for feedback loops (the model's approvals shape future labels).
  • Mentions fairness monitoring by group (Lesson 8.3) and the business metric owner.
🏭 In the real world
The pandemic drift 🏢 Many companies · 2020 MIT Technology Review: Our weird behavior during the pandemic is messing with AI models

How: inventory, recommendation, sentiment and marketing models trained on normal behaviour broke as buying patterns changed in days; teams added human oversight and retrained.

Why this method: some models (fraud) held up because the relationship between inputs and outcome barely changed; others needed retraining or rules.

Zillow Offers wind-down 🏢 Zillow · 2021 Zillow Group: Q3 2021 results and plan to wind down Zillow Offers

How: the home-buying business bought homes based on price forecasts; in Q3 2021 Zillow recorded a $304.4 million inventory write-down for homes bought above its current estimates of future selling prices, and cited home-pricing unpredictability when it decided to wind the business down.

Why this method: a reminder that a forecasting model operating in a fast-changing market needs monitoring of realized errors and a plan for when the world moves faster than the model.

Performance without labels 🧰 Open source NannyML: performance estimation (CBPE)

How: if the model's probabilities are calibrated, the expected confusion matrix can be computed from the scores alone, giving an estimate of accuracy or AUC before labels arrive.

Why this method: covers the long label delay; it can't detect concept drift, which changes what the probabilities should be.

Monitoring a deployed hospital model 🧪 Research · 2020 Lundberg et al.: Explainable AI for Trees (monitoring section)

How: tracked each feature's contribution to the model's loss over time (SHAP values of the loss) to find which features were degrading performance after deployment.

Why this method: tells you which input broke, not just that accuracy fell.

Drift reports 🧰 Open source Evidently on GitHub

How: reference vs current DataFrames in, a report of per-column drift tests, data quality and model quality out, as HTML, JSON or a monitoring dashboard.

Why this method: the boring daily check that catches most production issues, without writing the statistics yourself.

🤯 Fun fact · PSI's thresholds have no p-value

The PSI thresholds everyone quotes (below 0.10 little shift, above 0.25 significant shift, action required) are industry rules of thumb from credit scoring. A 2018 dissertation by Bilal Yurdakul noted that these benchmarks “are being used without reference to Type I or Type II error rates”, and worked out PSI's statistical properties to put them on firmer ground. Banks watch PSI closely because an unsuitable model can trigger internal investigations or regulators' audits.

Check yourself

1. Reference shares in two bins: 50% / 50%. This month: 40% / 60%. What's the PSI (ln 0.8 ≈ −0.223, ln 1.2 ≈ 0.182)?
(0.40 − 0.50) × ln(0.8) + (0.60 − 0.50) × ln(1.2) = (−0.1)(−0.223) + (0.1)(0.182) = 0.0223 + 0.0182 ≈ 0.04. PSI is never negative: each term's two factors have the same sign. 0.04 is “little change”.
2. A spam filter's input-drift dashboard is all green, but users report more spam getting through. What's most likely, and what do you monitor?
Input monitors can't see changes in P(y | x). User reports are fast proxy labels; recent labelled examples are what a retrain needs. Adversarial domains (spam, fraud) drift by design.
3. Your team alerts whenever any of 300 features has a KS p-value below 0.05, on windows of 200,000 rows. The alert fires every day. Best fix?
With 200,000 rows even harmless shifts are “significant”, and 300 tests at 0.05 give ~15 false alarms a day by chance alone. Effect sizes on the features that matter (and on the score) keep alerts meaningful.
📝 TL;DR
  • Data drift = P(x) changes; concept drift = P(y | x) changes (invisible to input monitors); label shift = P(y) changes.
  • PSI = Σ (cur − ref) · ln(cur/ref) over bins; rule of thumb: < 0.10 little, 0.10–0.25 moderate, > 0.25 significant. KS: largest CDF gap; its p-value explodes with sample size.
  • Monitor in layers: data quality, inputs, predictions, estimated performance, realized performance as labels arrive.
  • Retrain on a schedule plus triggers; triggers are only as fast as your labels; validate every retrained model against the champion.
