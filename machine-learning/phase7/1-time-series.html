Phase 7 · Lesson 1

Time series: lags, seasonality & leakage-safe validation

Sales, traffic, energy demand, sign-ups: half the numbers a business cares about arrive one day at a time. Turn them into an ordinary table of past-only features and any regression model can forecast them. The catch is validation: shuffle the rows and your score quietly reads the future.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Data leakage: any information at training time that won't exist at prediction time makes scores look better than reality (Lesson 0.6).
  • k-fold cross-validation (CV) splits the rows into k parts, trains on k − 1 and tests on the rest, k times (Lesson 5.1).
  • MAE, mean absolute error, is the average size of a miss in the target's own units; lower is better (Lesson 5.2).
🎯 Hook: yesterday or last week?

In 2020 the M5 competition asked 5,507 teams to forecast 28 days of Walmart unit sales: 42,840 series, from single products in single stores up to the national total. The organizers also scored dumb baselines. One of them, naive, says “tomorrow = today”. Another, seasonal naive, says “next Tuesday = last Tuesday”.

Neither needs any machine learning. One of them was more than twice as accurate as the other, and it set a bar that plenty of fancy models failed to clear.

🔮 Predict first

For daily retail sales, which copy-paste forecast wins?

Seasonal naive, by a lot: in the organizers' published scores its error (WRMSSE, a scaled RMSE) was 0.847 against naive's 1.752. Shoppers behave like their calendar: Saturday looks like last Saturday, not like Friday. Every forecast you build should beat this baseline, and in this lesson you'll see when that's harder than it sounds.

Numbers first: a time series is a table in disguise

Our running example is 420 days (60 weeks) of a shop's daily sales: a slow upward trend, a strong weekly rhythm (Saturdays about 16 units above average, Mondays about 10 below) and noise that lingers for days, like real demand. A forecasting model never sees “a curve”. It sees rows like these, where every feature is computed only from days before the one we predict:

💡 Big idea Forecasting with ML = supervised learning where every feature must be knowable at the moment you make the forecast. If you forecast h days ahead, a lag shorter than h doesn't exist yet. And validation must respect the arrow of time: train on the past, test on the future, never the other way round.
🧠 Analogy: the weather forecaster's notebook

A good forecaster asks three questions: what happened recently (lags), what's normal for this time of year (seasonality), and is it slowly getting warmer (trend)? You can't check her skill by hiding random days in last year's notebook and asking her to fill them in: with Tuesday and Thursday in view, guessing Wednesday is easy. The fair test is to give her everything up to Sunday and ask about next week. That's walk-forward validation.

🤔 Why this? Why lag features and walk-forward validation?
  1. The problem before. Classical forecasting fits one statistical model per series (Lesson 7.2), which is hard to mix with extra information such as prices, promotions or weather. Ordinary ML models can use any features, but they expect a table of independent rows, and a time series is neither a table nor independent: today is a lot like yesterday.
  2. What people tried. Feed the date in as a feature and validate with a random split or shuffled k-fold. It looks fine and is quietly wrong: each test day sits between training days, so the model interpolates instead of forecasting. In Playground 2 a k-NN model on the date scores MAE 4.3 in shuffled CV but 5.2 on the real next 8 weeks.
  3. Why this works. Rewrite the series as rows “features from the past → value now” and the problem becomes plain regression. Validate by rolling forward in time: train on days 1–100, test on 101–150; train on 1–150, test on 151–200; and so on. Every test mimics a real deployment. In Playground 2 an honest lag model scores 2.40 walk-forward and 2.13 on the real future: the estimate is honest, slightly cautious.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Early folds train on little history, so walk-forward scores are noisy and often pessimistic. Every horizon needs its own feature set (or a recursive loop). And no validation scheme survives a break in the world, such as a pandemic, a price change, a new competitor: the past then stops predicting the future (drift, Lesson 8.6).
  5. What came next. Dedicated forecasting models (exponential smoothing, ARIMA) and gradient boosting with careful features, plus global models trained across thousands of related series, which is how the M5 was won (Lesson 7.2). Deep forecasting models build on the same supervised framing (the sibling Deep Learning course).

Step 1: features, baselines and the forecast horizon

The forecast horizon h is how far ahead you must predict. A store ordering stock every morning for tomorrow needs h = 1; one ordering from a supplier with a two-week lead time needs h = 14. If you predict day t from data up to day t − h, then lag_1 is only legal when h = 1. In general you may use lags ≥ h and windows that end at t − h. Two baselines are free and you should always report them:

  • Naive: ŷ(t) = y(t − h). The last value you know.
  • Seasonal naive: ŷ(t) = y(t − 7k), the same weekday from the most recent week you know (k = ⌈h ⁄ 7⌉).

Your model's skill is how much smaller its error is than seasonal naive's: skill = 1 − MAEmodel ⁄ MAEseasonal naive. Zero means “all that work bought nothing”.

✏️ Worked example: features for a 7-day-ahead forecast

It's Saturday evening, day 369. You must forecast next Saturday, day 376, so h = 7.

  • Allowed: lag_7 = y(369) = 122 (this Saturday), lag_14 = y(362), the mean of days 363–369 = 105.8, weekday = Saturday, day index = 376. Weekday and day index are known for any future date.
  • Not allowed: lag_1 = y(375), Friday. On Saturday evening it hasn't happened yet. A model trained with lag_1 would need a value you don't have, and the code would quietly fill it with something wrong.
  • Seasonal naive says 122. If the model adds a little trend (+0.06 a day × 7 ≈ +0.4), it might say about 122.4. The difference between the two is the model's whole contribution: modest at h = 7, which is why the baseline matters.

Step 2: validation that respects time

Shuffled k-fold assumes rows are independent, like patients in a trial. Time-series rows are not: day 200 and day 201 are near-copies. If day 201 is in the training set, predicting day 200 is barely a forecast at all. Walk-forward validation (also “rolling-origin” or, in scikit-learn, TimeSeriesSplit) always trains on an initial stretch and tests on the block that follows, then grows the training stretch and repeats. A gap drops the last few training days before each test block, so validation can mimic an h-day horizon or a delay before data arrives.

🔮 Predict first

A k-NN model uses the date (day index and weekday) as its only features. Shuffled 5-fold CV says its MAE is 4.3. What do you expect on the real next 8 weeks?

It scores 5.2 on the real future, 20% worse than CV promised. In shuffled CV every test day has training neighbours a few days on either side; in the future there are only neighbours behind it. Walk-forward CV catches it (it even says 6.5, erring on the cautious side). Switch models and leak features below to see the other ways CV can lie.

Three lessons hide in that playground. (1) For the honest lag model, shuffled CV says 2.05, walk-forward 2.40, the future 2.13: lags already describe the recent past, so shuffling does little harm here. (2) For the date-based k-NN, shuffling flatters it by 20%: models that can interpolate in time are the ones shuffled CV fools. (3) The leaky feature makes both CVs report 0.00, a perfect score, while the real future gets 3.56, worse than the honest model. A 7-day mean that includes today, plus lags 1–6, lets the model solve for today exactly. No split strategy protects you from a leaky feature; a score that's too good to be true is your alarm.

With gap=7, fold 1 trains on rows 0–42 instead: the 7 rows just before each test block are dropped. Fold 1 has only 50 rows of history; that's why walk-forward scores are noisier and usually a bit worse than the final model trained on everything.

Step 3: error grows with the horizon

The further ahead you look, the less the recent past tells you. A chart of error against horizon is the single most useful forecasting picture: it tells you which features matter at which range, and whether your model is worth more than seasonal naive at the horizon the business actually needs.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

pandas (3.1) builds the features: shift(k) for lags, shift(1).rolling(7).mean() for windows (shift first), .dt.dayofweek for calendar. scikit-learn (1.9) supplies TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits, gap, test_size, max_train_size) and any regressor; HistGradientBoostingRegressor is a strong default. For whole forecasting workflows see Lesson 7.2 (statsforecast, sktime, Darts).

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
days = pd.date_range("2024-01-01", periods=730, freq="D")
weekly = np.array([-10, -6, -3, 0, 5, 16, 4])[days.day_of_week]
noise = np.zeros(730)
for t in range(1, 730):                          # persistent noise: busy days come in runs
    noise[t] = 0.9 * noise[t - 1] + rng.normal(0, 2.5)
df = pd.DataFrame({"y": 80 + 0.06 * np.arange(730) + weekly + noise}, index=days)

h = 7                                              # forecast 7 days ahead → only lags ≥ 7 are legal
for k in (7, 14, 28):
    df[f"lag_{k}"] = df["y"].shift(k)
df["mean_7"] = df["y"].shift(h).rolling(7).mean()  # shift FIRST, then roll: no peeking
df["dow"] = df.index.day_of_week
df["t"] = np.arange(len(df))
df = df.dropna()
X, y = df.drop(columns="y"), df["y"]

model = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(categorical_features=["dow"], random_state=0)
for name, cv in [("shuffled KFold", KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)),
                 ("TimeSeriesSplit", TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, test_size=56, gap=h - 1))]:
    mae = -cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error")
    print(f"{name:16s} MAE {mae.mean():.2f}  per fold {np.round(mae, 2)}")

snaive = df["lag_7"].iloc[-56:]                         # the baseline every forecast must beat
print("seasonal naive, last 56 days:", round((y.iloc[-56:] - snaive).abs().mean(), 2))

Run it: shuffled KFold reports MAE about 3.4, walk-forward about 5.4, and seasonal naive scores 3.1 on the last 56 days. So the boosted model that shuffled CV calls “close to the baseline” is in fact clearly worse than it. The culprit is t: trees can't extrapolate a trend past the training range (Lesson 7.2 fixes that), and shuffled folds hide it because every test day has training days on both sides. For R users, the fable and modeltime packages wrap the same ideas.

Choice✅ Gains❌ Costs

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Plot the series, compute seasonal naive, and ship a gradient-boosted model with lags, a few windows and calendar features, validated walk-forward at the horizon the business needs. Report skill vs seasonal naive. That is a real forecast in a day.

At scale

Thousands or millions of series (every product × store), backtests across many origins, a feature pipeline that produces exactly the same features in training and serving, holiday calendars per market, and monitoring for drift (Lessons 7.2, 8.5, 8.6). One global model across series usually beats one model per series.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you validate a demand-forecasting model?” A strong answer:

  • Says walk-forward / rolling-origin backtesting, never shuffled k-fold, and explains why: autocorrelation lets shuffled folds interpolate.
  • Matches validation to the horizon and data delays: only lags ≥ h, a gap if data arrives late, test blocks as long as the real forecast window.
  • Compares against naive and seasonal-naive baselines and reports skill, per horizon.
  • Hunts leakage: windows computed before shifting, features filled in later (final prices, returns), target encoding over the whole history.
  • Mentions breaks: retrain cadence, monitoring, and keeping a holdout period that is truly untouched.

Why this method: it is walk-forward validation with the horizon built in, run in parallel across series, so a team can compare models on thousands of products before trusting one.

Check yourself

1. You have 360 daily rows and use TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5) with default settings. How many rows does the first fold train on?
The default test size is 360 ÷ (5 + 1) = 60, and the first fold trains on everything before its test block: rows 0–59. Later folds grow to 120, 180, 240 and 300 rows. Small early folds are why walk-forward scores are noisy.
2. You must forecast sales 14 days ahead. A teammate's best model uses lag_1 and lag_7 and has a great validation score. What's wrong?
A feature must exist when the forecast is made. Use lags ≥ h, train one model per horizon (direct), or feed predictions back in (recursive). Validate with the same horizon, for example with gap=13, so the score reflects what you'll really get.
3. A daily model scores MAE 0.01 in walk-forward CV; seasonal naive scores 3.5. The features include lags 1–6 and df.y.rolling(7).mean(). What do you suspect?
Walk-forward splitting doesn't protect against a feature computed from the target itself. In Playground 2 the same bug gives a perfect 0.00 in both CVs and 3.56 on the real future. Scores far better than a strong baseline are a reason to audit features, not to celebrate.
📝 TL;DR
  • Turn a series into a table: past-only lags, rolling windows, calendar and trend features → the value now.
  • Only features known h days earlier are legal; different horizons want different features (often one model per horizon).
  • Always report naive and seasonal-naive baselines and your skill against them; seasonal naive is hard to beat.
  • Validate walk-forward (TimeSeriesSplit, with a gap for the horizon). Shuffled k-fold flatters models that interpolate in time.
  • No split protects against a leaky feature: shift before you roll, and distrust scores that are too good.
