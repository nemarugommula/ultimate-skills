Phase 7 · Lesson 4

Learning to rank

Search results, product listings, feeds, the reranked candidates of a recommender: whenever the output is an ordered list, what matters is the order, not the scores. Learning to rank trains models on exactly that, and gradient-boosted LambdaMART has been the workhorse for fifteen years.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Gradient boosting fits each new tree to the gradient of the loss: “which way should each prediction move?” (Lesson 4.3).
  • Logistic loss turns a score difference into a probability (Lesson 2.3).
  • A recommender retrieves a few hundred candidates; something must then order them (Lesson 7.3).
🎯 Hook: the top result gets the click, even when it's worse

In a 2005 eye-tracking study at Cornell, Thorsten Joachims and colleagues watched students use Google. Users looked at the first and second results about equally often, yet clicked the first one substantially more. Then the researchers secretly swapped the top two results for some users.

🔮 Predict first

With the top two swapped, so the less relevant page now sits at #1, what did users do?

Even in the swapped condition the authors still found “a strong bias to click on link one”, although the second abstract was more relevant. Users trust the ranking. Two consequences you'll handle below: the top few positions matter enormously (so the metric must weigh them most), and clicks are a biased label for training the next ranker.

Numbers first: grading a results page

Ranking data comes in query groups: for each query, a list of candidate documents, each with features (text match, freshness, popularity…) and a graded relevance label from human raters, say 0 = irrelevant, 1 = marginal, 2 = good, 3 = perfect. Here's one query and the order our current model shows:

Add up the contributions and you get the DCG (discounted cumulative gain) of the page: 6.71 for these four positions. The best possible order puts the 3 first, then the 2, then the 1: DCG = 7 + 1.893 + 0.5 = 9.39. Their ratio is NDCG (normalized DCG) = 6.71 ÷ 9.39 = 0.71. A perfect page scores 1.

NDCG@k = DCG@k ⁄ ideal DCG@k,   DCG@k = Σi=1..k (2reli − 1) ⁄ log₂(i + 1) “Score each of the top k results by how good it is, shrink the score the further down it sits, add them up, and divide by the score of the best possible order.”
💡 Big idea A ranker is judged by the order it produces within each query, with the top positions weighted most (NDCG@k). Pointwise models predict each document's grade separately; pairwise models learn which of two documents should come first; listwise models (LambdaRank, LambdaMART) push hardest on the swaps that change NDCG the most.
🧠 Analogy: judging a talent show

A judge who gives everyone scores out of 10 (pointwise) can be harsh on Monday and generous on Friday; all that matters is whether she puts the best act first. A judge who only compares two acts at a time (pairwise) never has a “harsh day” problem. And a judge who knows only the top three get prizes (listwise, NDCG@3) spends her attention on the close calls near the top, not on 17th versus 18th place.

🤔 Why this? Why LambdaMART?
  1. The problem before. Early search engines ranked by hand-tuned formulas: a text-match score such as BM25, plus link popularity, plus freshness, with weights someone adjusted by eye. With hundreds of signals, hand-tuning stops working.
  2. What people tried. Pointwise: regress the grade from the features. It wastes effort getting absolute scores right across queries, when only the order inside each query matters. Pairwise: RankNet (Burges et al., 2005) learns from pairs “A should beat B” with a logistic loss. Better, but every misordered pair costs the same, whether it's positions 1 and 2 or 50 and 51.
  3. Why this works. LambdaRank keeps the pairwise gradient but multiplies it by how much NDCG would change if the two swapped. Swapping a grade-3 and a grade-0 document at positions 1 and 2 changes DCG by 7 × (1 − 0.631) = 2.58; the same pair at positions 9 and 10 changes it by 7 × (0.301 − 0.289) = 0.084, 31 times less, so that pair gets 31 times less push. LambdaMART feeds these “lambda” gradients to boosted trees (MART, multiple additive regression trees). In Playground 2 it reaches NDCG@3 0.83 against 0.78 for plain pairwise.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need labelled query groups: human grades are expensive, clicks are cheap but biased by position (Playground 3). Training cost grows with the number of pairs per query. NDCG at a fixed k ignores everything below it. Features are query–document pairs you must engineer.
  5. What came next. Unbiased learning to rank from clicks, weighting each click by the inverse chance it was seen (Joachims et al., 2017); neural rerankers that read query and document together (cross-encoders, AI Engineer 3.4); and LLM-based reranking. LambdaMART stays the default for feature-based ranking.

    Step 2: pointwise, pairwise, listwise

    One trap catches pointwise models: features that shift per query. A popular query's documents all have high click counts; an obscure query's all have low ones. A pointwise model must explain those shifts in absolute grades, which it can't. A pairwise model compares documents inside the same query, so any shift shared by the whole query cancels out.

    Two results to notice. With no shift, LambdaRank gets fewer pairs right overall than pairwise (80.8% vs 83.8%) yet scores higher NDCG@3 (0.83 vs 0.78): it gives the “length” feature a negative weight because that is what matters at the top, and accepts more mistakes among the junk below.     Step 3: learning from clicks, and position bias

    Human grades are expensive, so most systems learn from clicks. But a click depends on two things: was the result seen, and was it relevant? The chance of being seen falls with position; that's position bias. Train on raw clicks and the new model learns “whatever was on top is good”, copies the old ranking, and the feedback loop locks in. The fix is inverse propensity scoring (IPS): estimate how often each position is examined (by occasionally swapping results, as Joachims did) and weight each click by 1 ⁄ that probability. A click at position 8, rarely seen, counts for much more than one at position 1.

    🧰 Tools you'll actually use

    XGBoost (3.4): XGBRanker, default objective rank:ndcg (LambdaMART), query ids passed as qid (rows sorted by query), pair sampling via lambdarank_pair_method ("mean" or "topk") and lambdarank_num_pair_per_sample, and click debiasing with lambdarank_unbiased=True. LightGBM (4.7): LGBMRanker, default objective lambdarank, query sizes passed as group (they must sum to the number of rows), metric positions via eval_at. scikit-learn: ndcg_score, dcg_score (linear gains). Search engines ship it too: Elasticsearch and OpenSearch have LTR plugins, Vespa and Solr have built-in support.

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import ndcg_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def make_queries(n_q, docs=20):
    X, y, qid = [], [], []
    for q in range(n_q):
        z = rng.normal(size=docs)                                 # hidden relevance
        shift = rng.normal() * 2                                  # per-query shift (e.g. query popularity)
        X.append(np.c_[z + rng.normal(0, 0.8, docs) + shift, rng.normal(size=(docs, 3))])
        y.append(np.digitize(z, [-0.2, 0.5, 1.2])); qid.append(np.full(docs, q))
    return np.vstack(X), np.concatenate(y), np.concatenate(qid)

X_tr, y_tr, q_tr = make_queries(300)
X_te, y_te, q_te = make_queries(100)

def mean_ndcg(scores, k=5):                                    # average NDCG@k over test queries
    return np.mean([ndcg_score([y_te[q_te == q]], [scores[q_te == q]], k=k) for q in np.unique(q_te)])

pointwise = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("pointwise NDCG@5:", round(mean_ndcg(pointwise.predict(X_te)), 3))

try:                                                              # LambdaMART (needs OpenMP on macOS: brew install libomp)
    import lightgbm as lgb, xgboost as xgb
    sizes = np.bincount(q_tr)                                     # LightGBM wants group sizes, rows grouped by query
    lgbm = lgb.LGBMRanker(objective="lambdarank", n_estimators=300, verbose=-1).fit(X_tr, y_tr, group=sizes)
    xgbr = xgb.XGBRanker(objective="rank:ndcg", lambdarank_pair_method="topk",
                         lambdarank_num_pair_per_sample=8, n_estimators=300).fit(X_tr, y_tr, qid=q_tr)
    print("LGBMRanker NDCG@5:", round(mean_ndcg(lgbm.predict(X_te)), 3))
    print("XGBRanker  NDCG@5:", round(mean_ndcg(xgbr.predict(X_te)), 3))
except Exception as e:
    print("LightGBM/XGBoost not available here:", type(e).__name__)

    Without the boosting libraries, the pointwise baseline prints NDCG@5 ≈ 0.69 (0.688 on scikit-learn 1.9); the per-query shift is what drags it down, and that is the gap the rankers close. The ranker calls follow the XGBoost learning-to-rank tutorial and the LightGBM LGBMRanker reference (checked November 2026). Keep each query's rows together and never split one query across train and test.

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Week one

    Use the search engine's text score (BM25) plus a couple of hand-weighted signals, and log every query, result list, position and click. Grade 200 queries by hand and compute NDCG@10 so you can measure every change. Then train an LGBMRanker on those features.

    At scale

    Hundreds of features per query–document pair, LambdaMART or neural rankers retrained daily on debiased clicks, small randomized “interleaving” experiments to estimate position bias and compare rankers, and a cross-encoder or LLM reranker on the top candidates.

    🎤 Interview angle

    “How would you improve the ranking of our product search?” A strong answer:

    • Defines the metric: NDCG@k (or MRR, mean reciprocal rank) on graded query groups, plus online conversion.
    • Frames it as learning to rank with query–document features, and explains pointwise vs pairwise vs LambdaMART and why NDCG-weighted gradients focus on the top.
    • Handles labels: human grades for evaluation, clicks for training, corrected for position bias (IPS, randomization).
    • Splits by query (and by time), never by row; watches for feedback loops.
    • Places it in a pipeline: cheap retrieval → feature-based ranker → expensive reranker on the top few.
    🏭 In the real world
    RankNet → LambdaRank → LambdaMART 🧪 Research · 2010 Burges: From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART: An Overview (PDF)

    How: a self-contained derivation of the three algorithms; LambdaMART is the boosted-tree version of LambdaRank.

    Why this method: per the overview, an ensemble of LambdaMART rankers won Track 1 of the 2010 Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge.

    Position bias, measured 🧪 Research · 2005 Joachims et al.: Accurately Interpreting Clickthrough Data as Implicit Feedback (PDF)

    How: eye-tracking plus “swapped” and “reversed” result orders on Google results.

    Why this method: showed clicks are relative signals shaped by trust in the ranking, which is why relative (pairwise) interpretations of clicks work better than absolute ones.

    Unbiased learning to rank 🧪 Research · 2017 Joachims, Swaminathan & Schnabel: Unbiased Learning-to-Rank with Biased Feedback

    How: inverse propensity weighting of clicks inside a ranking objective, with propensities estimated from randomized interventions.

    Why this method: learns from free click logs without copying the old ranker's position bias; XGBoost's lambdarank_unbiased builds on this line of work.

    LTR in the library 🧰 Open source XGBoost: Learning to Rank tutorial

    How: XGBRanker with qid, LambdaMART objectives for NDCG, MAP and pairwise, top-k pair construction and position debiasing.

    Why this method: gradient-boosted trees on hand-made features stay strong and fast for ranking with tabular signals.

    🤯 Fun fact · ranking by “lambdas” that aren't a loss

    LambdaRank's trick is that it never writes down a loss function: it simply declares what the gradient for each document should be (the pairwise push times |ΔNDCG|) and lets the optimizer follow it. Burges' overview reports that his team then showed empirically that following these gradients does optimize NDCG directly. Sometimes in ML you can skip the loss and go straight to the gradient.

    🌉 How it connects

    Learning to rank is the second stage after a recommender's retrieval (Lesson 7.3) or a search engine's keyword and vector retrieval (AI Engineer 2.4: hybrid search). LambdaMART is gradient boosting (Lessons 4.3–4.4) with a custom gradient; pairwise loss is logistic regression on score differences (Lesson 2.3). Splitting by query is group cross-validation (Lesson 5.1); position bias is a causal-inference problem (Lesson 8.4).

    Check yourself

    1. A results page has grades 0, 3, 1 at positions 1–3. Using gain 2grade − 1, what is DCG@3? (Discounts: 1, 0.631, 0.5.)
    Gains 0, 7, 1 → 0 × 1 + 7 × 0.631 + 1 × 0.5 = 4.417 + 0.5 = 4.92. The ideal order (3, 1, 0) has DCG 7 + 0.631 = 7.63, so NDCG@3 ≈ 0.64.
    2. You train a ranker on click logs from your current search engine. Offline, the new model agrees with the old ranking almost perfectly, and its NDCG on click labels is excellent. What's the most likely problem?
    Clicks mix relevance with exposure. Training on raw clicks rewards whatever the old ranker showed first, a feedback loop. IPS weighting (and evaluation on human grades or randomized traffic) breaks it.
    3. A teammate splits the ranking dataset randomly by row into train and test. Why is that a problem?
    Rankers are evaluated per query, and query-level quirks leak through shared queries. Keep each query's documents together (GroupKFold with the query id, Lesson 5.1), and split by time too if queries trend.
    📝 TL;DR
    • Ranking data = query groups of documents with graded labels; only the order inside each query matters.
    • NDCG@k: gains (2grade − 1) discounted by 1 ⁄ log₂(position + 1), divided by the ideal; the top positions dominate.
    • Pointwise predicts grades; pairwise learns which of two wins; LambdaRank/LambdaMART weight each pair's push by |ΔNDCG|.
    • Tools: XGBRanker(objective="rank:ndcg") with qid, LGBMRanker with group; split by query.
    • Clicks are position-biased: weight them by inverse examination probability (IPS) or you'll just copy the old ranker.
