Phase 7 · Lesson 3

Recommender systems: collaborative filtering & matrix factorization

“People who liked this also liked…” sells books, fills playlists and decides most of what gets watched on streaming services. You can build one from nothing but a table of who-liked-what: no descriptions of the items at all. Here's how, from neighbours to hidden tastes, and how to tell if it works.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors; 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated (Math 1.3).
  • k-nearest neighbours predicts from the most similar examples (Lesson 3.1).
  • Ridge regularization shrinks weights to stop a model fitting noise (Lesson 2.2); RMSE is root mean squared error (Lesson 5.2).
🎯 Hook: the million-dollar prize Netflix didn't ship

In October 2006 Netflix offered $1,000,000 to anyone who could predict movie ratings 10% more accurately than its own system, Cinematch (RMSE 0.9525 → 0.8572). It released 100,480,507 ratings from 480,189 users on 17,770 movies. Thousands of teams joined. In September 2009 BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos won with a 10.06% improvement; a rival team, The Ensemble, matched the score but had submitted 20 minutes later.

The winning solution blended hundreds of models. Two years earlier, the first-year Progress Prize team had already reported more than 2,000 hours of work on a blend of 107 algorithms.

🔮 Predict first

So did Netflix put the $1M grand-prize ensemble into production?

Netflix's engineering blog (2012): from the Progress Prize they took the two best single algorithms, matrix factorization (RMSE 0.8914 alone) and restricted Boltzmann machines (0.8990), blended to 0.88, adapted from 100 million to over 5 billion ratings, and put them in production. The grand-prize ensemble's extra accuracy “did not seem to justify the engineering effort”. Matrix factorization is the star of this lesson.

Numbers first: the ratings matrix is almost empty

Picture the Netflix data as a giant table: 480,189 rows (users) × 17,770 columns (movies) = about 8.5 billion cells. Only 100 million hold a rating: 1.2%. The job of a recommender is to fill in the other 98.8%, or at least the cells you'd most enjoy. Here is a tiny version you can edit:

You don't need to know anything about the films to guess that “You” will like Star Wars and not Love Actually: your ratings look like Ana's and Ben's, and they loved Star Wars. That guess has a name: collaborative filtering (CF), filtering items using the collaboration of everyone's ratings.

💡 Big idea Recommend from patterns in who liked what. Neighbourhood CF finds similar users (“people like you liked…”) or similar items (“people who liked this also liked…”). Matrix factorization goes further: it explains every rating as the match between a user's hidden tastes and a movie's hidden traits, r̂ ≈ puser · qmovie, learned from the filled cells and used to fill the empty ones.
🧠 Analogy: the friend with your taste

You ask a friend whose taste matches yours for a restaurant tip: that's user–user CF. You ask the waiter “people who order the curry, what else do they get?”: item–item CF. Matrix factorization is the food critic who has figured out that every dish can be described by a few dials (spicy, rich, adventurous), and so can every diner, so she can match a diner to a dish neither of them has ever met.

🤔 Why this? Why matrix factorization?
  1. The problem before. Hand-made rules (“bestsellers in your category”) are the same for everyone. Content-based recommendations need good item descriptions and can only suggest more of the same. And with 1.2% of cells filled, two users rarely share many rated movies, so their similarity is computed from a handful of numbers.
  2. What people tried. User–user CF (GroupLens, 1994) compares you with every other user: slow with millions of users and noisy with sparse overlaps. Item–item CF (Amazon, 2003) precomputes which items are bought together; it scales and still powers “customers also bought” (the paper won IEEE Internet Computing's test-of-time award in 2017). But neither learns a compact model of taste.
  3. Why this works. Assume a few hidden tastes explain most ratings. With k = 2, a user is two numbers, say (sci-fi 1.2, romance −0.5), and a movie too, say Star Wars (1.0, −0.2). Predicted rating = average 3.2 + 1.2 × 1.0 + (−0.5) × (−0.2) = 4.5. In Playground 2, 30 users and 12 movies need only 84 such numbers instead of 360 free cells, and those 84 cut the test RMSE from 1.19 (each movie's average) to 0.63. On Netflix, plain factorization alone took RMSE from 0.9525 to 0.8914.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A new user or item has no vector (the cold-start problem). Without regularization the factors explode on sparse data (Playground 2 with λ = 0). Ratings are rare and biased; what people actually click and watch (implicit feedback) is plentiful but has no “dislike”. And the lowest RMSE isn't the business goal: Netflix moved on to ranking what people actually watch.
  5. What came next. Implicit-feedback factorization (Hu, Koren & Volinsky, 2008), models that add content features for cold start (factorization machines, LightFM), and today multi-stage systems: neural candidate retrieval followed by a ranking model (YouTube, 2016), with two-tower retrievers (YouTube, 2019). Ranking itself is Lesson 7.4.

Step 1: neighbourhood collaborative filtering

User–user: measure how similar each user is to you on the movies you both rated, then predict a rating as your average plus the similarity-weighted opinions of your k nearest neighbours. Before comparing, subtract each user's average: a harsh critic's 3 and a generous fan's 5 can mean the same thing. Cosine similarity on those centred ratings is close to the Pearson correlation.

r̂you, item = r̄you + Σv ∈ neighbours sim(you, v) · (rv, item − r̄v) ⁄ Σ |sim(you, v)| “Start from your usual rating, then nudge it up or down by how much more than usual your look-alikes liked this item.”

Item–item flips it: compare columns. Two movies are similar if the same people rate them alike. To predict your rating for Star Wars, average your own ratings of the movies most similar to Star Wars. Item similarities change slowly and can be precomputed overnight, which is why big shops prefer them.

✏️ Worked example: one user–user prediction by hand

Predict You → Star Wars, using only Ana as the neighbour.

  • Your average: (4 + 5 + 1) ÷ 3 = 3.33. Ana's average: (5 + 4 + 5 + 1 + 2) ÷ 5 = 3.4.
  • Ana rated Star Wars 4, which is 4 − 3.4 = +0.6 above her usual.
  • With one neighbour the weights cancel: prediction = 3.33 + 0.6 = 3.93. With more neighbours, each one's “above usual” is weighted by how similar they are to you.

Step 2: matrix factorization (hidden tastes)

Give every user a vector pu and every movie a vector qi, each with k numbers (latent factors). Predict r̂ui = μ + pu · qi, where μ is the average rating. Learn the vectors by minimizing the squared error on the known ratings plus a penalty λ(‖p‖² + ‖q‖²) that keeps them small. Nobody tells the model what the factors mean; on movie data they often turn out to be readable axes like “action vs romance”.

Two standard training methods. SGD (stochastic gradient descent) loops over ratings and nudges p and q along the error. ALS (alternating least squares) freezes the movie vectors, which makes each user's vector a small ridge regression with an exact answer; then freezes users and solves for movies; and repeats. Each ALS step can only lower the training loss, and it parallelizes well, which is why Spark and the implicit library use it.

🔮 Predict first

You give the model more factors (k = 4 instead of 2) and remove the penalty (λ = 0). What happens to the error on held-out ratings?

It explodes. With λ = 0 the training error keeps falling while held-out RMSE shoots far past 5 stars: some users have only a few ratings, and an unpenalized vector can take any size that fits them. A little λ fixes it. Try it below.

Set λ = 0 and the training RMSE keeps falling while the test RMSE explodes to about 13 stars: classic overfitting (Lesson 5.8), fixed by the penalty. Too much penalty (λ = 1) squeezes every vector to zero and you are back to predicting the average.

Step 3: implicit feedback, cold start and honest evaluation

Most real signals are implicit: plays, clicks, purchases, watch time. There is no 1-star; a missing cell might mean “didn't like” or “never saw it”. Implicit-feedback models treat every observed interaction as a positive with some confidence (more plays, more confidence) and every missing one as a weak negative. The metric changes too: instead of RMSE on ratings, ask did the item the user actually chose next appear in our top k?

  • Leave-last-out: for each user, hide their most recent interaction, train on the rest, recommend k items they haven't seen, and check whether the hidden one is among them. It respects time, like Lesson 7.1's walk-forward.
  • Hit rate@k = share of users whose hidden item is in their top k. With one hidden item per user it equals recall@k; precision@k = hits ÷ k.
  • Cold start: a user with no history gets popular items (or items matching what they told you at sign-up); a new item gets shown using its content features (genre, text, price) until interactions arrive.
🔮 Predict first

“Recommend the 5 most popular items to everyone.” Against a personalized item–item recommender, how badly does that do on hit rate@5?

Popularity is a tough baseline: below, it reaches 46% hit rate@5 against 60% for item–item CF and 24% for random. Many published “improvements” were later found to lose against well-tuned simple baselines, so always report popularity.

Two towers: how big systems retrieve today

Modern recommenders at YouTube's scale work in stages. Retrieval narrows millions of items to a few hundred, then ranking orders those few hundred with a heavier model (Lesson 7.4). Retrieval is often a two-tower network: one tower turns the user (history, context) into a vector, the other turns an item (ID, text, category) into a vector, and the score is their dot product. That is matrix factorization with neural networks in place of lookup tables, and because the item tower reads features, new items get a vector on day one. Item vectors are precomputed and searched with an approximate nearest-neighbour index, the same machinery as vector databases (AI Engineer 2.2).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

implicit (0.7.3): ALS and BPR for implicit feedback on sparse matrices. scikit-surprise (1.1.5): SVD-style factorization and k-NN CF for explicit ratings, with cross-validation. LightFM (1.17, last released 2023): hybrid factorization with item and user features for cold start. scikit-learn: cosine_similarity, TruncatedSVD, NMF on sparse matrices. Two-tower models usually live in TensorFlow Recommenders or TorchRec.

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import csr_matrix
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_users, n_items = 500, 60
taste = rng.integers(0, 3, n_users)                       # 3 hidden genres
pop = np.exp(-np.arange(n_items) / 15)
histories = []
for u in range(n_users):
    w = pop * np.where(np.arange(n_items) % 3 == taste[u], 6.0, 1.0)
    histories.append(list(rng.choice(n_items, size=rng.integers(4, 15), replace=False, p=w / w.sum())))

# leave-last-out: hide each user's final interaction
train = [h[:-1] for h in histories]; held = np.array([h[-1] for h in histories])
rows = np.repeat(np.arange(n_users), [len(h) for h in train])
X = csr_matrix((np.ones(len(rows)), (rows, np.concatenate(train))), shape=(n_users, n_items))

item_sim = cosine_similarity(X.T)                          # item–item: columns that co-occur
np.fill_diagonal(item_sim, 0)
scores = {"item-item": X @ item_sim,                    # sum of similarities to my items
          "popularity": np.tile(np.asarray(X.sum(0)), (n_users, 1)).astype(float)}
k = 5
for name, S in scores.items():
    S = np.asarray(S, dtype=float); S[X.toarray() > 0] = -np.inf   # never recommend what they already have
    topk = np.argsort(-S, axis=1)[:, :k]
    hit = (topk == held[:, None]).any(axis=1).mean()
    print(f"{name:10s} hit rate@{k} = recall@{k} = {hit:.3f}   precision@{k} = {hit / k:.3f}")

# implicit ALS (pip install implicit):
#   model = implicit.als.AlternatingLeastSquares(factors=64); model.fit(X)
#   ids, scores = model.recommend(userid, X[userid], N=10)

Output (identical on scikit-learn 1.6 and 1.9): item–item hit rate@5 = 0.466 vs popularity 0.314. With millions of users, compute item similarities on a sample or with approximate neighbours, and always evaluate on interactions that happened after the training data.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Popularity (per category) as the baseline, then item–item co-occurrence on purchases or plays: a few lines of SQL or the snippet above. Evaluate leave-last-out, then A/B test. Many shops never need more for “customers also bought”.

At scale

Multi-stage systems: several candidate generators (two-tower retrieval, co-occurrence, trending) feed a learning-to-rank model (Lesson 7.4) with business rules for diversity and freshness, retrained daily, measured by online A/B tests on watch time or revenue rather than offline RMSE (Lesson 8.4).

🎤 Interview angle

“Design ‘you might also like’ for an e-commerce site.” A strong answer:

  • Starts from the data: mostly implicit feedback (views, carts, purchases), very sparse.
  • Proposes a popularity baseline, then item–item CF or implicit matrix factorization; mentions two-tower retrieval plus a ranker at scale.
  • Handles cold start: popular or content-based for new users and items.
  • Evaluates offline with time-based leave-last-out and recall@k / NDCG@k (Lesson 7.4), then online with an A/B test on conversion.
  • Mentions feedback loops and popularity bias: the system recommends what it already recommended, so add exploration and diversity.
🏭 In the real world
Netflix Prize to production 🏢 Netflix · 2012 Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 stars (Part 1)

How: matrix factorization and RBMs from the Progress Prize, re-engineered for billions of ratings and continuous updates.

Why this method: the two best single models gave most of the accuracy; by 2012 Netflix said 75% of what people watch comes from some sort of recommendation.

Customers who bought this… 🏢 Amazon · 2003 Amazon Science: the history of Amazon's recommendation algorithm

How: item-to-item CF: for each item, precompute related items from purchase histories; for a visitor, merge the lists for what they bought.

Why this method: Amazon found item-level analysis gave better recommendations than matching customers, and it is far cheaper than comparing every customer with every other.

Implicit feedback 🧪 Research · 2008 Hu, Koren & Volinsky: Collaborative Filtering for Implicit Feedback Datasets (PDF)

How: factorization where each interaction is a preference with a confidence that grows with its count, trained by ALS.

Why this method: TV viewing and clicks have no ratings; this model became the standard for implicit data (it's what implicit.als implements).

Two-tower retrieval at YouTube 🏢 Google · 2019 Yi et al.: Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations

How: a user tower and an item tower trained with in-batch negatives, corrected for how often popular items appear; deployed to retrieve from tens of millions of videos.

Why this method: the item tower encodes content features, which fights sparsity and the power-law of popularity; live A/B tests showed better recommendations.

🌉 How it connects

Neighbourhood CF is k-NN (Lesson 3.1) on rows or columns of a sparse matrix; matrix factorization is a low-rank approximation, the idea behind SVD and PCA (Lesson 6.5, Math 2.6: SVD & PCA), trained with ridge-style penalties (Lesson 2.2). User and item vectors are embeddings (Math 1.5, AI Engineer 2.1). Next, Lesson 7.4 orders the candidates a recommender retrieves; Lesson 8.4 covers the A/B tests that decide if it ships.

Check yourself

1. A user's hidden-taste vector is (1.0, −0.5) and a movie's is (0.8, 0.4). The average rating μ is 3.5. What does matrix factorization predict?
p · q = 1.0 × 0.8 + (−0.5) × 0.4 = 0.8 − 0.2 = 0.6, so r̂ = 3.5 + 0.6 = 4.1. The first factor matches strongly; the second pulls slightly the other way.
2. Your new recommender lowers RMSE on a random 20% of ratings, but in an A/B test users don't watch more. Which change to offline evaluation would most likely have warned you?
Users see a top-k list, not predicted stars, and they act in time order. A random split of ratings leaks the future and rewards accuracy on items nobody would be shown. Rank-based, time-respecting metrics match the product much better; the A/B test stays the final judge (Lesson 8.4).
3. A podcast app adds 300 new episodes a day. Item–item CF never recommends them in their first week. What's the standard fix?
New items have no interactions, so pure CF can't place them however often it retrains. Content features give every item a representation immediately; exploration (showing new items to some users) collects the interactions CF needs.
📝 TL;DR
  • Collaborative filtering recommends from patterns in a sparse user × item matrix; no item descriptions needed.
  • User–user: people like you liked it. Item–item: people who liked this liked that (precomputable, Amazon's workhorse).
  • Matrix factorization: r̂ = μ + pu · qi with k hidden factors, trained by ALS or SGD with a penalty λ; it was the Netflix Prize's most useful single model.
  • Real data is implicit; evaluate with time-based leave-last-out and recall@k / precision@k, against popularity.
  • Cold start needs content features; at scale, two-tower retrieval + a ranker (Lesson 7.4).
