Phase 7 · Lesson 2

Forecasting models: ARIMA & ETS vs gradient boosting

Exponential smoothing and ARIMA have forecast sales, inventory and electricity for sixty years with a handful of parameters. Gradient boosting wins competitions with thousands of features. Each fails in its own way, and knowing which failure you're facing is most of the job.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Lags, seasonal naive, the forecast horizon and walk-forward validation (Lesson 7.1).
  • Gradient boosting adds small trees one after another, each fixing the last ones' errors (Lesson 4.3); LightGBM is a fast implementation (Lesson 4.4).
  • A tree's leaf predicts an average of training targets, so trees never predict outside the range they saw (Lesson 4.2, quiz 3).
🎯 Hook: statistics won in 2018, machine learning won in 2020

M4 (2018) pitted 61 methods against 100,000 series, yearly to hourly, from every kind of domain. Uber's write-up of the results: pure machine-learning and neural-network methods did worse than standard statistical methods, and worse still than combinations of them. The winner, Uber's Slawek Smyl, glued exponential smoothing to a recurrent neural network.

M5 (2020), 42,840 Walmart sales series: the best statistical benchmark, exponential smoothing, scored 0.671 (WRMSSE, lower is better). The winner, built from LightGBM models, scored 0.520: 22.4% better. Second place also used LightGBM.

Same organizers, two years apart, opposite verdicts. What changed?

🔮 Predict first

Why did boosted trees win M5 but not M4?

The third. LightGBM dates from 2016, and many M5 series were intermittent: mostly days with zero sales. What M5 had was thousands of related series plus covariates (prices, promotions, holidays, SNAP days). A single global model trained on all of them learns patterns no single series could teach. M4's series were unrelated and had no covariates: the home ground of per-series statistical models. You'll build both kinds below.

💡 Big idea A series is roughly level + trend + seasonality + noise. Exponential smoothing (ETS) and ARIMA model those pieces directly, one series at a time, with a few parameters: great with short, unrelated series and no extra data. Gradient boosting models whatever features you hand it, across many series at once, but can't extrapolate: remove the trend (detrend or difference) before the trees see the target.
🧠 Analogy: a reputation with a fading memory

Your opinion of a restaurant is a running average of your visits, but last week's meal counts more than one from three years ago. One bad dinner nudges your opinion; a new chef (a level shift) changes it within a few visits. How quickly you forget is a single dial. That's exponential smoothing, and the dial is α.

🤔 Why this? Why exponential smoothing?
  1. The problem before. Naive forecasting trusts one noisy day completely. A plain moving average of the last k days gives a 6-day-old value the same weight as yesterday's and then drops it to zero on day 8. A straight-line regression on time can't adapt when the level shifts.
  2. What people tried. Moving averages of different lengths, fitted lines, hand adjustments. In the 1950s and 60s Brown, Holt and Winters (Winters' paper: Forecasting Sales by Exponentially Weighted Moving Averages, 1960) proposed weights that fade smoothly instead.
  3. Why this works. New level = α × today + (1 − α) × old level. Unrolled, the weights are α, α(1 − α), α(1 − α)², …: with α = 0.3 that's 0.30, 0.21, 0.15, 0.10… summing to 1. One number trades noise-resistance against speed. In Playground 1 the best α (0.30) gives a one-step error of 3.02 against 3.85 for naive (α = 1).
  4. What it costs, when it fails. One model per series and no covariates: promotions, prices and holidays need extensions or another model. It assumes the structure of the past continues. Linear trends extrapolate forever (hence “damped” trends). With thousands of related, short series, each fit learns alone from too little data.
  5. What came next. State-space ETS with likelihoods and prediction intervals, and automatic model selection (Hyndman & Khandakar, 2008); ARIMA for richer autocorrelation; hybrids like M4's ES-RNN; and global gradient boosting with engineered features, which won M5 (this lesson). Deep and foundation forecasting models continue the global idea (the sibling Deep Learning course).

Step 1: simple exponential smoothing (one dial)

ℓt = α·yt + (1 − α)·ℓt−1,    ŷt+h = ℓt “The new level is a blend of today's value and yesterday's level. The forecast for every future day is the current level: a flat line.”
🔮 Predict first

What does simple exponential smoothing turn into when α = 1?

Naive. With α = 1 the level is just today's value. At α → 0 it barely moves from its starting value: close to a long-run average. Slide between the two below.

✏️ Worked example: three days of smoothing by hand

Level ℓ = 50. Sales come in at 54, 51, 66 and α = 0.5.

  • Day 1: ℓ = 0.5 × 54 + 0.5 × 50 = 52. Forecast for tomorrow: 52.
  • Day 2: ℓ = 0.5 × 51 + 0.5 × 52 = 51.5. The miss was 51 − 52 = −1.
  • Day 3: ℓ = 0.5 × 66 + 0.5 × 51.5 = 58.75. A big jump moves the level halfway; if it's real, the next days pull it the rest of the way.

The same update in “error form”: ℓ = old ℓ + α × (miss). Every exponential-smoothing model is this idea applied to level, trend and season.

Step 2: add trend and seasonality (Holt and Holt-Winters)

Holt (1957) added a smoothed trend b with its own dial β; Winters (1960) added seasonal terms s with dial γ, one per weekday here. The forecast h days ahead is level + h × trend + the matching seasonal term. Modern libraries call these ETS models, after Error, Trend, Seasonal, each None, Additive or Multiplicative: ETS(A,A,A) is additive Holt-Winters. They fit the dials by maximum likelihood and pick the variant with an information criterion (AIC), so you rarely set them by hand.

ARIMA is the other classic family. In words: AR regresses today on the series' own lags, I (integrated) differences the series first to remove trend (yt − yt−1, or yt − yt−7 for weekly seasons), and MA regresses on recent forecast errors. ARIMA(p, d, q) says how many of each. ETS describes what the series looks like; ARIMA describes how it correlates with its past. Auto-selection (AutoARIMA, AutoETS) is the normal way to use both.

Level only gives a flat line through a rising, zig-zagging series (test MAE above 8). Adding the trend tilts it, adding the weekly season makes it zig-zag in step. Auto-fit settles on α = 0.7, β = 0.01, γ = 0.3, with a test MAE of 2.64, against 5.64 for seasonal naive, which misses the 28 days of growth. A big β is the classic way to break it: the trend chases the noise, and 28 days of extrapolation turn a small wobble into a big miss.

Step 3: why trees can't extrapolate a trend

Give gradient boosting the day index and the weekday, and it learns “days after 150 look like this”. But every leaf predicts an average of training targets, so no forecast can exceed the largest value it has seen. On a growing series the forecast goes flat exactly where you need it to rise. The fix is to let something else handle the trend and give the trees a target without one:

  • Detrend: fit a straight line (or ETS) for the trend, and let the trees predict the residuals.
  • Difference: predict the change yt − yt−7, then add it back to last week's value (the “I” of ARIMA).
  • Normalize: divide by a recent level (say, the last 28 days' mean), predict the ratio, multiply back. M5 teams used variants of all three.

Step 4: one global model for many series

A local model is fitted to one series alone: 30 stores, 30 models. A global model is one model trained on rows from all the series at once, with features scaled so a big store and a small store look alike (divide by each series' recent mean). It can learn “Saturdays are +20%” from 30 stores instead of one, and it works for a brand-new store with only two weeks of history. That's how the M5 winners used LightGBM: one model per store or department group, trained on thousands of products together.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

statsmodels (0.15): ExponentialSmoothing (Holt-Winters), ETSModel (state-space ETS with intervals), ARIMA with seasonal orders. statsforecast (Nixtla, 2.1): fast AutoETS, AutoARIMA, SeasonalNaive for thousands of series, plus cross_validation. sktime (1.2) and Darts (0.48) wrap statistical, ML and deep models behind one fit/predict interface. Prophet (1.5) fits trend + seasonality + holidays; since 1.4 its maintainers describe it as being in maintenance mode. LightGBM / XGBoost for global models, often via Nixtla's mlforecast.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
idx = pd.date_range("2025-01-06", periods=196, freq="D")        # starts on a Monday
weekly = np.array([-10, -6, -3, 0, 5, 16, 4])[idx.day_of_week]
y = pd.Series(40 + 0.25 * np.arange(196) + weekly + rng.normal(0, 3, 196), index=idx)
train, test = y[:-28], y[-28:]
mae = lambda f: np.abs(np.asarray(f) - test.to_numpy()).mean()

hw = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=7).fit()
arima = ARIMA(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 1, 7)).fit()
snaive = np.tile(train[-7:].to_numpy(), 4)

# Trees on the raw target flat-line; detrend first, then boost the residuals
t_tr, t_te = np.arange(168).reshape(-1, 1), np.arange(168, 196).reshape(-1, 1)
X = lambda t: np.c_[t, t % 7]
raw = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X(t_tr), train).predict(X(t_te))
line = LinearRegression().fit(t_tr, train)
gb = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X(t_tr) % 7, train - line.predict(t_tr))
detrended = line.predict(t_te) + gb.predict(X(t_te) % 7)

for name, f in [("seasonal naive", snaive), ("Holt-Winters", hw.forecast(28)), ("SARIMA", arima.forecast(28)),
                ("trees, raw", raw), ("trees, detrended", detrended),
                ("HW + trees average", (hw.forecast(28).to_numpy() + detrended) / 2)]:
    print(f"{name:20s} test MAE {mae(f):5.2f}")
print("raw trees never exceed the training max:", raw.max() <= train.max())

On this series (run on statsmodels 0.15 and scikit-learn 1.9): seasonal naive 3.71, Holt-Winters 2.58, SARIMA 2.60, raw trees 5.96, detrended trees 2.60, the average 2.58. The raw-tree forecast never exceeds the training maximum. For thousands of series, the statsforecast equivalent is StatsForecast(models=[AutoETS(season_length=7), AutoARIMA(season_length=7)], freq="D"), then .fit(df) and .predict(h=28) on a long table with columns unique_id, ds, y.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

A handful of series: AutoETS and AutoARIMA from statsforecast, seasonal naive, and their average, backtested with cross_validation. Ship the best, or the average. Twenty lines of code, minutes of compute.

At scale

Millions of product × location series: a global LightGBM per category with lag, window, price, promotion and calendar features, normalized targets, quantile outputs for safety stock, statistical models as fallbacks and baselines, and hierarchical reconciliation so store forecasts add up to region forecasts.

🎤 Interview angle

“Would you use ARIMA or XGBoost for demand forecasting?” A strong answer:

  • Asks how many series, how long, how related, and which covariates exist.
  • Explains ETS/ARIMA as level, trend, season (and autocorrelation) per series, and why they're strong on short unrelated series (M4).
  • Explains why trees can't extrapolate, and fixes it: detrend, difference or normalize the target.
  • Argues for a global boosted model when there are many related series with prices and promotions (M5), validated walk-forward against seasonal naive.
  • Mentions combinations and prediction intervals or quantiles, because inventory decisions need a range, not one number.
🏭 In the real world
M5: Walmart's 42,840 series 🧪 Research · 2022 IJF special issue on the M5 competition (editorial)

How: 5,507 teams in the Accuracy challenge; the winner combined multiple direct and recursive LightGBM models; 2nd place used LightGBM for the granular, intermittent series and N-BEATS (a neural network) for the higher levels.

Why this method: related series plus prices and calendar events reward one model trained across many series.

M4: 100,000 series, won by a hybrid 🏢 Uber · 2018 Uber Engineering: M4 Forecasting Competition

How: Slawek Smyl's ES-RNN mixes hand-coded exponential-smoothing formulas with a recurrent neural network trained across series.

Why this method: pure ML lost to statistics and to combinations of statistical methods; keeping the statistical structure and learning the rest won.

Statistical models at scale 🧰 Open source Nixtla statsforecast

How: AutoARIMA, AutoETS, Theta and seasonal naive compiled to run across thousands of series in parallel, with the same long-table format as Nixtla's mlforecast for global boosted models.

Why this method: the strongest baselines should be cheap to run, so every ML model gets compared against them.

Holt-Winters' origin 📜 History · 1960 statsmodels: ExponentialSmoothing

How: Peter Winters' 1960 paper in Management Science extended Holt's trend smoothing with seasonal factors, to forecast sales.

Why this method: three dials, computable by hand in 1960, still a top benchmark in 2020 (M5's ES_bu).

🤯 Fun fact · the oldest benchmark nearly won

In M5's published table, the bottom-up exponential-smoothing benchmark (0.671) beat every off-the-shelf machine-learning benchmark, including a global random forest (1.065) and per-series neural networks (0.977). The ML that won wasn't “ML instead of statistics”; it was ML plus a deep understanding of the data.

Check yourself

1. The smoothed level is 50 and today's sales are 60. With α = 0.2, what is the new level (and tomorrow's simple-exponential-smoothing forecast)?
0.2 × 60 + 0.8 × 50 = 52. In error form: 50 + 0.2 × (60 − 50). A small α moves the level only a fifth of the way toward each new value.
2. Subscriptions grow every month. Your LightGBM model with month-index features has a great validation score inside the training years, but its 12-month forecast is a flat line. What's the best fix?
Leaves average training targets, so no tree ensemble can forecast above the highest value it saw. Differencing, normalizing or detrending lets the trees model what they're good at (patterns around the trend) while something that can extrapolate handles the trend. Shuffling would only hide the problem in validation (Lesson 7.1).
3. 5,000 products, 6 months of daily sales each, with prices and promotion flags. Which first plan is strongest?
Related series with covariates are M5 territory: a global model learns promotion effects from thousands of products at once and handles new ones. Per-series ARIMA can't use the promotions easily and learns each product from 180 points. Keep statistical models as baselines and fallbacks.
📝 TL;DR
  • Exponential smoothing: level = α·today + (1 − α)·old level; Holt adds trend (β), Winters adds seasons (γ). ETS picks the variant and fits the dials for you.
  • ARIMA: regress on your own lags (AR), after differencing (I), plus recent errors (MA). Use AutoARIMA / AutoETS.
  • Trees can't extrapolate: detrend, difference or normalize the target before boosting.
  • Many related series + covariates → one global boosted model (M5). Few unrelated series → per-series statistical models (M4). Combinations are cheap insurance.
  • Untuned ML lost to seasonal naive in M5. Baselines first, always.
