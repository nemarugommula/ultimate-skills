Phase 4 · Lesson 5

Stacking & blending: when two models beat the best one

Different models make different mistakes. Combine their predictions with the right weights, learned without peeking, and the mix often beats every member. It's how competitions are won, and also how systems become too complicated to ship.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • k-fold cross-validation: split the training data into k parts; each part is predicted by a model trained on the other k − 1 (Lessons 0.6 and 5.1).
  • Averaging models helps most when their errors are not correlated (Lesson 4.2).
  • RMSE (root mean squared error) is the typical size of a miss, in the target's units (Lesson 5.2).
🎯 Hook: the million-dollar blend nobody shipped

In 2006 Netflix offered $1 million to anyone who could make its movie-rating predictions 10% more accurate. A year in, the first Progress Prize went to a team whose final answer combined 107 algorithms, after more than 2,000 hours of work. Netflix looked at the two best ingredients: matrix factorization (“SVD”) alone scored an RMSE of 0.8914, a Restricted Boltzmann Machine 0.8990, and a simple linear blend of the two 0.88, better than either. Netflix put those two into production.

The Grand Prize, won in 2009 by “BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos”, blended hundreds of predictors. In 2012 Netflix's tech blog explained that it evaluated some of the new methods offline, but the extra accuracy “did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into a production environment.” This lesson is about both halves of that story.

🔮 Predict first

Model A has RMSE 0.8914, model B 0.8990. You average their predictions with well-chosen weights. The blend's RMSE will be…

Lower than both: 0.88 in Netflix's case. A blend is never worse than its best member on the data its weights were chosen on (weight 1 on the best model is one of the options), and it's better whenever the two models' errors don't move in lockstep. Playground 1 shows exactly why.

Two models that are wrong in different places are worth more together than either alone. That's the whole idea; the rest of the lesson is about choosing the weights without fooling yourself.

💡 Big idea Blending combines several models' predictions with weights. Stacking learns how to combine them: a second-level meta-model takes the base models' predictions as its features. The catch: the meta-model must learn from predictions the base models made on rows they did not train on (out-of-fold predictions); otherwise it learns to trust whichever model memorized the training data.
🧠 Analogy: a panel of doctors

A radiologist, a lab specialist and a family doctor each give an opinion. A wise hospital director learns, from past cases, how much to trust each one, and in which situations. But imagine the director judged the doctors on cases they had already seen the answers to: the one who memorized old files would look perfect and get all the trust. The director must judge them on cases they saw fresh. That's out-of-fold stacking.

🤔 Why this? Why stacking?
  1. The problem before. You usually have several decent models (a GBM, a linear model, a k-NN, a neural net) and must pick one. Picking throws away what the others know, and different families are good in different regions of the data.
  2. What people tried. Simple averaging or voting (fine when models are equally good) and hand-tuned weights on a validation set (works, but easy to overfit when there are many models). Wolpert (1992, “Stacked generalization”) and Breiman (1996, “Stacked regressions”) proposed learning the combination from cross-validated predictions.
  3. Why this works. If two models' errors have spreads σA, σB and correlation ρ < 1, some weighted mix has a smaller spread than either. Here: 0.572 and 0.602 with ρ = 0.64 → best mix ≈ 0.534. A meta-model learns those weights, and can even learn “trust the tree when x is large”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Every base model must be trained k + 1 times, deployed, monitored and kept in sync; prediction latency is the sum (or the max, if parallel) of all of them. Gains are often small (fractions of a percent). If the models' errors are highly correlated (five GBMs with different seeds), there's little to gain. And if the meta-model sees leaked, in-sample predictions, it gets worse than the best base model (Playground 3).
  5. What came next. AutoML systems (AutoGluon, auto-sklearn) automated multi-layer stacking; scikit-learn ships StackingClassifier. In production, teams often distill a big ensemble into one smaller model, or simply ship the single best one (Netflix's choice).

Careful: we chose w on the same 200 validation points we're scoring. With one weight that's a mild sin; with fifty models and fifty weights it's overfitting the validation set. Stacking fixes that with out-of-fold predictions, below.

How much can a blend gain? It depends on ρ

For two models with error spreads σA and σB and error correlation ρ, the blend w·A + (1 − w)·B has error variance:

Var(error) = w²σA² + (1 − w)²σB² + 2w(1 − w)·ρσAσB “Each model's own error, scaled down by its weight, plus a shared part that only shrinks if the models disagree (ρ below 1).”

This is the same variance-of-a-sum rule as the forest formula in Lesson 4.2; derivation in Math 4.4.

🔮 Predict first

Two equally good models (σ = 1 each) whose errors are perfectly uncorrelated (ρ = 0). How much smaller is the 50/50 blend's RMSE?

0.71. Variance = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5, so RMSE = √0.5 ≈ 0.707: a 29% improvement for free. At ρ = 0.9 the same blend only reaches 0.975. Most real model pairs sit somewhere between 0.5 and 0.95.

Two lessons from the calculator. A weaker model can still help, if it's different enough (try σB = 1.5, ρ = 0.2). And a model that's just a near-copy of another (ρ ≥ 0.95) adds almost nothing, which is why stacking five GBMs that differ only in their seed is disappointing, while stacking a GBM with a k-NN or a linear model can pay.

Stacking without leaks: out-of-fold predictions

With many base models, you want a meta-model to learn the weights. It needs training data: for every training row, the base models' predictions. But if a deep tree predicts the rows it was trained on, it's always right, so the meta-model concludes “trust the tree completely”, and on new data the tree is just as wobbly as ever.

  1. Split the training set into k folds (k = 5 is typical).
  2. For each fold, train every base model on the other 4 folds and predict this fold. Every row now has an out-of-fold (OOF) prediction from each base model, made by a model that never saw it.
  3. Train the meta-model on the OOF predictions (often a simple logistic or linear regression).
  4. Refit each base model on the full training set; at prediction time, feed their predictions to the meta-model.

In-sample, the meta-model gives the tree a weight of about 4.5 and the others about 1: on training rows the tree's probabilities are always 0 or 1 and always right. The stack then scores exactly what the tree scores, 87%. With out-of-fold predictions, the tree's weight drops to about 0.45 and the stack reaches 92%, a point above the best single model. Small gain, big lesson: the leak can make stacking worse than not stacking.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): StackingClassifier / StackingRegressor (out-of-fold predictions built in, cv=5 by default), VotingClassifier / VotingRegressor, and cross_val_predict to make OOF predictions yourself. AutoGluon does multi-layer stacking automatically.

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier, RandomForestClassifier, StackingClassifier, VotingClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
base = [
    ("hgb", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)),
    ("rf", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0)),
    ("knn", make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15))),   # different family = different errors
]
stack = StackingClassifier(estimators=base,
                           final_estimator=LogisticRegression(),   # the meta-model: keep it simple
                           cv=5,                                  # meta-model trains on out-of-fold probabilities
                           stack_method="predict_proba")
vote = VotingClassifier(estimators=base, voting="soft")            # plain average of probabilities

for name, model in base + [("vote", vote), ("stack", stack)]:
    s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"{name:6s} AUC {s.mean():.4f}")

Output with scikit-learn 1.9: hgb 0.9930, rf 0.9913, knn 0.9927, vote 0.9939, stack 0.9939. Both combinations edge out the best single model (by about a thousandth of AUC, the area under the ROC curve, Lesson 5.4), at the cost of training 3 models × 6 fits. That's typical: real but small. Note the outer cross_val_score wraps the whole stack, so the inner OOF folds never see the outer test fold.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Don't stack yet. Ship one well-validated GBM. If you later have two good models from different families, a VotingClassifier(voting="soft") is a cheap, safe win; measure it before keeping it.

At scale

Each extra model is a pipeline, a feature dependency, a monitor and a retraining job. Teams stack where a fraction of a percent is worth millions (ads, fraud) and accept the cost, or they distill the ensemble into one model that mimics it. Netflix publicly chose not to ship the full prize-winning blend.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's stacking, and what's the classic mistake?” A strong answer:

  • Base models' predictions become features for a meta-model, which learns how to combine them.
  • The meta-model must train on out-of-fold predictions; in-sample predictions leak (overfit models look perfect) and the stack overtrusts them.
  • It helps when base models are diverse (low error correlation); variance of a blend = w²σA² + (1 − w)²σB² + 2w(1 − w)ρσAσB.
  • Names the costs: latency, complexity, maintenance; mentions Netflix's decision not to ship the full Grand Prize blend.
🏭 In the real world
What Netflix shipped 🏢 Netflix · 2012 Netflix Tech Blog: Netflix Recommendations, Beyond the 5 stars (Part 1)

How: took the two strongest algorithms from the Progress Prize blend (SVD and RBMs), adapted them to billions of ratings and online updates, and put them in production.

Why this method: a linear blend of two diverse models captured most of the gain; the Grand Prize's hundreds of models were judged not worth the engineering, and the business had moved on to streaming.

The Grand Prize blend 🏢 Netflix Prize · 2009 Koren: The BellKor Solution to the Netflix Grand Prize (PDF)

How: blended sets of 454, 75 and 24 predictors, using gradient-boosted trees as one of the blenders, trained on the held-out Probe set; the author notes the accurate term is “stacked generalization”.

Why this method: in the final 30 days, a better blend touched hundreds of predictors at once, a faster path to improvement than inventing a new model.

Kaggle winners mix families 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen & Guestrin: XGBoost paper, introduction

How: of 17 winning 2015 Kaggle solutions that used XGBoost, eight used it alone; most of the others combined it with neural nets in ensembles.

Why this method: trees and neural nets make different errors. The paper also reports KDD Cup 2015 winners saying ensembles beat a well-configured XGBoost “by only a small amount”.

Stacking as AutoML 🧰 Open source · 2020 Erickson et al.: AutoGluon-Tabular

How: trains many model families and stacks them in multiple layers, automatically, from one line of Python.

Why this method: the authors find a multi-layer combination uses the time budget better than searching for the single best model; in two popular Kaggle competitions it beat 99% of participating data scientists after 4 hours of training on the raw data.

🤯 Fun fact · 107 algorithms in, two came out

The 2007 Progress Prize answer combined 107 algorithms and more than 2,000 hours of work. When Netflix engineers took it apart, almost all of the value they chose to ship came from just two of them, SVD and RBMs, blended linearly (0.8914 and 0.8990 alone → 0.88 together). The team also handed over its source code, which is part of what made that analysis possible.

🌉 How it connects

Stacking generalizes the averaging of random forests (4.2) and uses boosted trees (4.3–4.4) as base models and as blenders. Out-of-fold predictions are cross-validation (5.1) put to work; the same trick makes target encoding safe (1.3). Diverse models matter for the same reason as in forests: correlated errors don't cancel. Matrix factorization, the Netflix Prize's star, is Lesson 7.3; AutoML's automated stacking is 8.7; the serving cost of many models is 8.5. Variance of a weighted sum: Math 4.4.

Check yourself

1. Two models, each with RMSE 2.0, have errors correlated at ρ = 0.5. What's the RMSE of their 50/50 average?
Variance = 0.25 × 4 + 0.25 × 4 + 2 × 0.25 × 0.5 × 2 × 2 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3, so RMSE = √3 ≈ 1.73. Uncorrelated would give √2 ≈ 1.41; perfectly correlated, still 2.0.
2. A colleague trains five base models on all the training data, predicts the same training rows, and fits a logistic meta-model on those predictions. The stack does worse on test than the best base model. Why?
In-sample predictions of flexible models (deep trees, 1-NN, big GBMs) look nearly perfect, so the meta-model puts its weight on them. Out-of-fold predictions show each model's honest error; StackingClassifier does this by default with cv=5.
3. Your 12-model stack beats your single LightGBM by 0.3% AUC in an offline test. The product serves 2,000 predictions per second with a 30 ms budget. What's the sensible next step?
This is Netflix's 2012 lesson: offline gains must justify the engineering and serving cost. Often two diverse models capture most of the gain; sometimes a single model is the right call.
📝 TL;DR
  • Blending/stacking combine models' predictions; it helps when their errors are not strongly correlated.
  • Blend error variance = w²σA² + (1 − w)²σB² + 2w(1 − w)ρσAσB: diverse models help most, near-copies barely at all.
  • Train the meta-model on out-of-fold predictions; in-sample predictions leak and make the stack overtrust overfit models.
  • Voting (fixed weights), blending (one hold-out), stacking (k-fold OOF), multi-layer (AutoML): increasing power and cost.
  • Gains are usually small; latency and maintenance are real. Netflix shipped two models, not the prize-winning hundreds.
