Why boosted trees still win on tabular data (and when they don't)

Deep learning conquered images, text and audio. On spreadsheets, gradient-boosted trees kept winning for years, and researchers worked out why. Then foundation models for tables arrived. Here's what the evidence says, as of late 2026, and how to choose.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Trees split on one feature at a time, so their boundaries are axis-aligned staircases (Lesson 4.1).
  • Gradient-boosted trees (GBDTs) are the state-of-the-art tree ensembles (Lessons 4.3–4.4).
  • k-NN predicts from nearby points by Euclidean distance, so every feature counts equally (Lesson 3.1).
🎯 Hook: 20,000 compute hours per model

By 2022 dozens of papers had proposed deep networks for tables, many claiming to beat XGBoost. Léo Grinsztajn, Edouard Oyallon and Gaël Varoquaux built a fair fight: 45 datasets with clear tabular characteristics, every model tuned with the same budget, and a hyperparameter search of about 20,000 compute hours for each learner. Their verdict: tree-based models “remain state-of-the-art on medium-sized data (~10K samples)”, even before counting their much faster training.

Then they asked the more interesting question: why? They found three properties of real tables that trees handle and standard neural networks don't. You'll break both kinds of model on purpose below.

🔮 Predict first

Take a table and replace its two columns, “age” and “income”, by two mixtures of them: 0.7·age + 0.7·income and −0.7·age + 0.7·income (a 45° rotation). No information is lost. What happens to a gradient-boosted tree model?

It gets worse: in Playground 1 test accuracy falls from 97% to 86%. Trees ask questions about one column at a time; “age > 60” becomes a diagonal line in the rotated columns, which a tree can only approximate with a staircase. A k-NN model (or a standard neural network) doesn't care about rotation at all, which sounds like a strength and, on tables, isn't.

Rotation hurts the trees; useless columns wreck the distance-based model. Real tables almost never come rotated, and they very often contain columns that don't matter. Both facts favour trees.

💡 Big idea Tabular data has a natural orientation: each column means something (age, price, count), and the target often changes abruptly along a single column (thresholds, rules, exceptions), while many columns are irrelevant. Trees are built for exactly that: one-column questions, piecewise-constant outputs, and splits that simply ignore useless columns. Standard neural networks assume smooth functions and treat all directions alike. That's why GBDTs win on typical tables, and why the gap closes on images, text, audio and huge datasets, where those assumptions flip.
🧠 Analogy: a tax form vs a photograph

A tax form is full of sharp rules on single boxes: “if income is above 50,000, add line 12”. Mixing boxes together (“0.7 × income + 0.7 × number of children”) makes the rules harder to read, and half the boxes don't apply to you. A photograph is the opposite: no single pixel means anything, patterns are smooth and can appear anywhere, and rotating the picture shouldn't change what's in it. Trees are tax accountants; convolutional networks are photographers.

🤔 Why this? Why do trees still win on tables?
  1. The problem before. After deep learning's successes on images and text, it was natural to expect it to win on tables too. Many papers reported new tabular architectures beating XGBoost, each on its own choice of datasets and tuning budget, so nobody knew what to believe.
  2. What people tried. Careful benchmarks. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon (2021) re-ran several deep tabular models on 11 datasets, including the ones their own papers used: XGBoost generally won and needed far less tuning, though an ensemble of XGBoost and deep models did best. Grinsztajn et al. (2022) built a 45-dataset benchmark with equal budgets and reached the same verdict for medium-sized data.
  3. Why this works. Grinsztajn et al. found three reasons. (1) Neural networks are biased toward smooth functions, while real tabular targets are irregular: smoothing the target hurt trees but barely changed NNs. (2) Tables contain many uninformative features, which MLP-like networks handle badly. (3) Data is not rotation invariant: random rotations reversed the ranking, putting NNs above trees. In our toy: 20 useless columns take k-NN from 92% to 47%, while the GBDT stays at 90%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Trees can't learn from raw pixels, words or waveforms, can't share representations across tasks, can't extrapolate, and give piecewise-constant predictions. When the signal lives in combinations of many columns (a rotated pattern), or in unstructured data, deep learning wins.
  5. What came next. Tabular foundation models: TabPFN (Nature, 2025) is a transformer pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets that predicts a new table in one forward pass, and it beat tuned GBDTs on datasets up to 10,000 rows. Later versions push the size limits up, and the living benchmark TabArena (2025) reports that GBDTs remain strong contenders while deep learning catches up with big budgets and ensembling, and foundation models excel on smaller datasets.

Reason 2: uninformative features

🔮 Predict first

You add 8 columns of pure random noise to the table. The GBDT drops from 97% to about 94%. What happens to k-NN (92% to start)?

59%. k-NN measures distance across all columns equally, so 8 noise columns swamp the 2 that matter: “nearest neighbours” become random. A tree just never splits on a column that doesn't help. Grinsztajn et al. found MLP-like networks are similarly hurt by uninformative features, and that real tabular datasets contain many of them.

Reason 3: irregular targets

Real tabular targets jump: a loan becomes risky the day a payment is 90 days late; a price changes at a size threshold; one product category behaves differently. Trees produce exactly that, piecewise-constant functions. Neural networks trained by gradient descent are known to learn low-frequency (smooth) functions first, so they round off sharp corners. Grinsztajn et al. tested it directly: they smoothed the training targets with a Gaussian kernel; for small smoothing lengths, “smoothing the target function on the train set decreases markedly the accuracy of tree-based models, but barely impacts that of NNs”. In other words, the NNs were already ignoring the irregular part, the part trees were using.

The new chapter: tabular foundation models

A foundation model learns from many datasets and is then applied to a new one. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., Nature, January 2025) is a transformer pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a generator of plausible tables. Given your training rows and test rows together, it predicts the test labels in a single forward pass (in-context learning): no gradient training on your data, no hyperparameter search.

What the Nature paper claimsNumber
Data sizes evaluatedup to 10,000 samples and 500 features (and 10 classes)

The field moved fast. Prior Labs' TabPFN-2.5 report (November 2025) targets up to 50,000 rows and 2,000 features and reports a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on small and medium classification datasets. As of November 2026, Prior Labs' documentation lists TabPFN-3 (May 2026) and TabPFN-3.5 (September 2026) with limits of up to 1,000,000 training rows, with model weights under non-commercial licenses (commercial use through a paid license or the hosted API). These are the vendor's own claims; the independent, continuously maintained TabArena benchmark is the place to check how they hold up.

✏️ Worked example: does TabPFN's speed matter for your use case?
  • A churn model is retrained weekly on 8,000 customers and scores 200,000 customers in a nightly batch.
  • Training: GBDT with a 1-hour Optuna search vs TabPFN's few seconds. TabPFN wins the “training” time by far.
  • Scoring: at the paper's 0.2 s per prediction, done one at a time, 200,000 predictions would take 40,000 s ≈ 11 hours; at CatBoost's 0.0002 s, about 40 seconds. In practice you'd score in large batches, which is much faster per row, so measure it, but the gap in serving cost is real.
  • A real-time fraud check with a 10 ms budget per request rules out the 0.2 s figure entirely; the GBDT fits easily. Prior Labs' newer versions advertise faster inference and distillation into small MLPs or tree ensembles for exactly this reason.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Try both experiments on a real dataset in a few lines with scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026). For foundation models, the tabpfn package follows the same fit/predict API (check its license and hardware notes first); for benchmarks, see TabArena.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)                       # unsupervised scaling: no labels, no leak
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(13, 13)))               # a random rotation of the 13 columns
variants = {"original": Z, "rotated": Z @ Q,
            "+100 noise cols": np.hstack([Z, rng.normal(size=(len(Z), 100))])}
gbdt = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)
mlp = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64, 64), max_iter=2000, random_state=0)
for name, data in variants.items():
    g = cross_val_score(gbdt, data, y, cv=5).mean()
    m = cross_val_score(mlp, data, y, cv=5).mean()
    print(f"{name:16s} GBDT {g:.3f}   MLP {m:.3f}")

Output with scikit-learn 1.9: original GBDT 0.955, MLP 0.978 · rotated GBDT 0.967, MLP 0.972 · +100 noise columns GBDT 0.961, MLP 0.815. An honest result: on this small (178 rows), smooth dataset the MLP is better at first, and one random rotation barely matters to either model. Effects vary by dataset, which is why Grinsztajn et al. averaged over 45 of them. But the noise effect is huge and clear: 100 useless columns cost the MLP 16 points, while the GBDT doesn't notice.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Tabular problem? GBDT first (HistGradientBoosting, LightGBM or CatBoost), with early stopping. If you have under ~10,000 rows and accuracy matters more than serving cost, also try a tabular foundation model and compare on the same folds. Don't start with a custom neural network for a plain table.

At scale

Large companies run both: GBDTs for most tabular decisions (risk, fraud, pricing, ETA), large neural networks where data is huge and multimodal (feeds, ads, search), and often hybrids, such as neural embeddings of text or images fed into a GBDT. The choice is re-made with benchmarks on their own data, not on papers.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do gradient-boosted trees often beat neural networks on tabular data?” A strong answer:

  • Cites evidence: Grinsztajn et al. 2022 (45 datasets, equal tuning budgets, ~10k samples) and Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2021 (XGBoost beat deep models, with less tuning).
  • Gives the three reasons: irregular targets (NNs biased to smooth), uninformative features, meaningful column orientation (NNs rotation-invariant).
  • Knows when DL wins: unstructured data, very large data, multimodal, end-to-end systems.
  • Mentions the update: tabular foundation models (TabPFN, Nature 2025) beat tuned GBDTs on small data; check living benchmarks (TabArena) and weigh inference cost and licensing.
🏭 In the real world
The benchmark that explained it 🧪 Research · 2022 Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux: Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on tabular data?

How: 45 datasets, a benchmarking method that accounts for hyperparameter search, and controlled transformations (smoothing targets, removing or adding features, rotating data).

Why this method: it turned “trees seem to win” into three testable inductive biases, and published every point of its search as a baseline for future models.

“Deep learning is not all you need” 🧪 Research · 2021 Shwartz-Ziv & Armon: Tabular Data, Deep Learning is Not All You Need

How: compared recent deep tabular models with XGBoost on 11 datasets, including those used in the deep models' own papers, and measured tuning effort.

Why this method: XGBoost generally outperformed the deep models and needed much less tuning; an ensemble of deep models and XGBoost did better than XGBoost alone.

A foundation model for tables 🧪 Research · 2025 Hollmann et al.: Accurate predictions on small data with a tabular foundation model (Nature)

How: a transformer pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets performs in-context learning on a new table; evaluated on datasets up to 10,000 samples and 500 features.

Why this method: small datasets are the norm (the paper notes 76% of datasets on OpenML had under 10,000 rows when written), and there it outperformed GBDTs tuned for hours.

A benchmark that keeps up 🧪 Research · 2025 TabArena: A Living Benchmark for Machine Learning on Tabular Data

How: a curated, continuously maintained benchmark with a public leaderboard (tabarena.ai) that is updated as models and versions change.

Why this method: its launch results: GBDTs are still strong contenders, deep learning catches up under larger time budgets with ensembling, foundation models excel on smaller datasets, and ensembles across model families advance the state of the art.

The vendor's roadmap 🏢 Prior Labs · 2025–2026 TabPFN model versions and limits (docs)

How: TabPFN-2.5 (Nov 2025, report arXiv 2511.08667), 2.6, 3 and 3.5 (Sep 2026), with larger row and column limits, text features, a hosted API and distillation into small models for fast serving.

Why this method: the 2025 model's limits (10,000 rows, slow inference) were the main reasons to choose a GBDT instead; each release targets them. Weights are under non-commercial licenses: check before production use.

🤯 Fun fact · a model that never saw real data

TabPFN's pre-training used only synthetic data: millions of random tables generated from simple causal mechanisms, each with some target values hidden. It learned “how to learn from a table” in general, so when it meets your real spreadsheet, fitting is just reading it. The Nature paper's own description: TabPFN “is a learning algorithm that is itself learned across millions of synthetic datasets”.

Check yourself

1. You'll score 5 million customers every night, and you'll also need a real-time check under 10 ms per request. With the TabPFN paper's figure of 0.2 s per prediction, how long would scoring one at a time take for the nightly batch?
5,000,000 × 0.2 s = 1,000,000 s ≈ 278 hours ≈ 11.6 days. Batching speeds this up a lot, but the 10 ms real-time check is still out of reach at that figure, while a GBDT (≈ 0.0002 s) does it easily. Serving cost is part of the model choice.
2. A teammate runs PCA on 40 tabular features (keeping all 40 components) “to decorrelate them” before XGBoost, and accuracy drops. Most likely why?
Keeping all components loses nothing, but it's a rotation. Tree splits are axis-aligned, so rules like “age > 60” turn into staircases. Grinsztajn et al. showed random rotations hurt trees and leave rotation-invariant networks unchanged.
3. Which project is the best fit for deep learning rather than gradient-boosted trees?
Images are unstructured: no single pixel means anything and patterns can appear anywhere, which is what convolutional networks are built for. The other two are classic tables, where GBDTs are the strong default.
📝 TL;DR
  • On medium-sized tables (~10k rows) tuned GBDTs beat deep networks in careful benchmarks (Grinsztajn et al. 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2021).
  • Three reasons: targets are irregular (NNs prefer smooth), many features are useless (NNs and k-NN get diluted), columns are meaningful (trees use the orientation; rotation hurts them).
  • Deep learning wins on images, text, audio, very large or multimodal data.
  • Tabular foundation models (TabPFN, Nature 2025) beat tuned GBDTs on small tables in seconds; newer versions push limits; check TabArena, inference cost and licenses.
  • Default: GBDT first, compare a foundation model on small data, ensemble if the gain is worth it.
