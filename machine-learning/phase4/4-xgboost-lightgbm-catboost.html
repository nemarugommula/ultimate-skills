Phase 4 · Lesson 4

XGBoost, LightGBM & CatBoost: what each one changed

All three are gradient boosting (Lesson 4.3), the same algorithm. What made them famous is a handful of clever engineering and statistical fixes. Knowing which fix each one introduced tells you which library to pick and which knobs matter.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Gradient boosting adds small trees one by one, each fitted to the residuals (negative gradients) of the current sum, scaled by a learning rate (Lesson 4.3).
  • A tree split is chosen by scanning candidate thresholds and keeping the one with the biggest gain (Lesson 4.1).
  • Target encoding replaces a category by the average target of its rows, and leaks if done naively (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook: 17 of 29

When Tianqi Chen and Carlos Guestrin published the XGBoost paper in 2016, they counted the winning solutions that Kaggle had featured on its blog during 2015: 17 of the 29 used XGBoost, eight of them using XGBoost alone. At the KDD Cup 2015, every one of the top-10 teams used it.

A year later Microsoft's LightGBM paper reported training up to over 20 times faster than conventional gradient boosting with almost the same accuracy. Then Yandex's CatBoost showed that the standard recipe has a hidden target leak, and fixed it. Three libraries, three different problems.

🔮 Predict first

Which single change gives LightGBM (and scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting) most of its speed on large data?

Bucketing (histograms). Instead of trying every distinct value as a threshold, each feature is pre-sorted once into ≤ 255 bins, and a split search scans 255 bin totals instead of millions of values. In Playground 3, 255 bins try 3.4× fewer thresholds than the exact search, run about 10× faster in this page's JavaScript, and lose almost nothing (test RMSE 0.340 vs 0.334).

💡 Big idea Every modern GBM library is the same loop: compute gradients → grow a tree on them → add it with a learning rate. They differ in how a tree is grown: how candidate splits are found (exact, quantiles, histograms), in which order leaves are split (level-wise, leaf-wise, symmetric), how each leaf's value is computed (Newton step with L2 penalty), and how they avoid leaking the target (ordered statistics). Those choices decide speed, memory and how much tuning you need.
🧠 Analogy: three restaurant kitchens, one recipe

Three kitchens cook the same dish. XGBoost's chef measures carefully and adds a pinch of restraint to every portion (regularization), and has a plan for missing ingredients. LightGBM's chef pre-chops everything into 255 standard sizes so nothing needs measuring during the rush, and spends effort on the plates that need the most fixing. CatBoost's chef refuses to taste the dish they're about to serve to the critic, because tasting it would bias them. Same dish; different kitchen rules.

🤔 Why this? Why these three libraries?
  1. The problem before. Classic gradient boosting (Friedman 2001, scikit-learn's GradientBoosting*) sorts every feature at every node and only uses first-order gradients. It was accurate but slow on millions of rows, memory hungry on wide sparse data, needed hand-encoded categoricals, and had few built-in brakes against overfitting.
  2. What people tried. Random subsampling of rows and columns (stochastic boosting), coarse pre-binning by hand, one-hot encoding every category, and simply buying bigger machines. Each helped a little, at a cost in accuracy, memory or engineering time.
  3. Why this works. XGBoost scores a split with gradient and curvature, plus a penalty: a leaf with residuals 4, 6 and 5 gets the value 15 / (3 + λ) = 3.75 with λ = 1, not the plain mean 5, so small, noisy leaves are shrunk automatically. LightGBM replaces “try every value” with “try 255 bins”, turning each split search from millions of candidates into hundreds. CatBoost computes each row's category statistic only from rows that came before it in a random order, so a row never sees its own label.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Many knobs, with different names in each library (min_child_weight, min_child_samples, min_data_in_leaf…). Leaf-wise trees overfit small data unless you cap leaves or depth. Histograms can miss a precise threshold in a feature with very fine structure. All of them are still tree ensembles: no extrapolation, no learning from raw images or text.
  5. What came next. The three borrowed each other's best ideas: XGBoost added histogram (tree_method="hist", now the default), leaf-wise growth and native categoricals; LightGBM and CatBoost added GPU training; scikit-learn shipped a LightGBM-style booster. The open question became whether trees still win at all (Lesson 4.6).

XGBoost: a regularized objective and second-order steps

XGBoost writes down what each new tree should minimize: the training loss plus a penalty on the tree's complexity. With T leaves and leaf values w:

Obj = Σᵢ loss(yᵢ, ŷᵢ) + γ·T + ½ λ Σⱼ wⱼ² “Fit the data, but every extra leaf costs γ and every large leaf value costs λ.”

It then approximates the loss around the current prediction with its gradient g (slope) and hessian h (curvature) for every row, a Newton step. Summing them in a leaf (G, H) gives a closed form for the best leaf value and for the gain of any split:

w* = − G ⁄ (H + λ)     gain = ½ [ GL²⁄(HL+λ) + GR²⁄(HR+λ) − G²⁄(H+λ) ] − γ “A leaf's value is its total gradient divided by its total curvature plus λ. A split is worth making only if it improves the score by more than γ.”

For squared error g = ŷ − y and h = 1, so w* is a shrunken mean of the residuals. For log-loss h = p(1 − p): rows the model is already sure about barely count. The Taylor expansion behind it: Math 8.1b.

✏️ Worked example: leaf values and a split's gain (squared error, λ = 1, γ = 0)
  • A candidate split sends rows with residuals 4, 6, 5 left and −2, −3 right. Gradients are minus the residuals: GL = −15, HL = 3; GR = +5, HR = 2; parent G = −10, H = 5.
  • Left leaf value: 15 / (3 + 1) = 3.75 (the plain mean would be 5). Right: −5 / (2 + 1) = −1.67 (plain mean −2.5).
  • Gain = ½ [ 225/4 + 25/3 − 100/6 ] = ½ [ 56.25 + 8.33 − 16.67 ] = 23.96. With gamma (γ) = 30 this split would be refused.
  • A leaf holding a single row with residual 10 gets 10 / (1 + 1) = 5: half its residual. λ protects you from trusting tiny leaves.

Sparsity-aware splits. Real tables are full of blanks and zeros. At every split XGBoost tries sending all rows with a missing value left, then right, and keeps the better default direction; it only scans the rows that are present, which is what made it fast on sparse data. Weighted quantile sketch. For huge data it proposes candidate thresholds at quantiles of each feature, weighting rows by their hessian h, so candidates are dense where the loss is sensitive. Today's default, tree_method="hist", goes further and uses fixed histograms, like LightGBM.

LightGBM: histograms and leaf-wise growth

Histograms. Before training, each feature is cut into at most max_bin = 255 bins. To evaluate splits in a node, LightGBM adds up the gradients falling in each bin (one pass over the rows), then scans 255 bin totals. A further trick: a child's histogram is the parent's minus its sibling's, so only the smaller child is ever counted.

Leaf-wise growth. Most trees grow level by level: split every leaf at depth 1, then every leaf at depth 2… LightGBM instead always splits the single leaf with the largest gain, anywhere in the tree, until it has num_leaves (default 31). With the same number of leaves it usually reaches a lower training loss, and the documentation warns it can overfit small data, which is why max_depth and min_data_in_leaf (alias min_child_samples, default 20) exist.

🔮 Predict first

Both growth orders get the same budget of 6 leaves on data with a diagonal boundary and two small “islands” of the opposite class. Which reaches the higher test accuracy?

Leaf-wise: 85.0% vs 81.7%. Level-wise must split every depth-2 leaf before going deeper, so it wastes splits on regions that are already clean. Leaf-wise goes straight for the islands. With 32 leaves both end at the same tree; the difference is how fast you get there, and how deep (leaf-wise reaches depth 3 at 6 leaves here, and depth 7 at 16).

GOSS (Gradient-based One-Side Sampling): rows with small gradients are already well fitted. GOSS keeps the top a × 100% rows by gradient size, randomly samples b × 100% of the rest, and multiplies the sampled small-gradient rows by (1 − a)/b so the totals stay unbiased. EFB (Exclusive Feature Bundling): one-hot and other sparse columns are rarely non-zero at the same time, so LightGBM packs them into a single column with offset bins; fewer columns, same splits. In LightGBM 4.x GOSS is chosen with data_sample_strategy="goss"; EFB is on by default (enable_bundle=True).

CatBoost: never let a row see its own label

Categorical features with many levels (city, product ID, user ID) are where boosting used to struggle. One-hot encoding explodes the number of columns; plain target encoding leaks.

✏️ Worked example: the leak, and the ordered fix

City “Zug” appears once in training, and that customer churned (y = 1). Plain target encoding gives Zug the value 1.0, computed from that very row. The tree happily learns “encoding = 1.0 → churn”: perfect on training, meaningless on new Zug customers.

Ordered target statistics (prior 0.5, smoothing weight 1): shuffle the rows once, and give each row the average target of the earlier rows of its category. City “Aarau” has 4 rows, in shuffled order with labels 1, 0, 1, 1:

Aarau row (shuffled order)yearlier rowsordered encodingplain encoding
1st1none(0 + 0.5) / (0 + 1) = 0.500.75
2nd01(1 + 0.5) / (1 + 1) = 0.750.75
3rd11, 0(1 + 0.5) / (2 + 1) = 0.500.75
4th11, 0, 1(2 + 0.5) / (3 + 1) = 0.6250.75

No row's encoding uses its own label, and Zug's single row gets the prior 0.5: honest uncertainty. CatBoost applies the same “only the past” rule to the boosting residuals themselves (ordered boosting), using several random orders. The paper shows that standard boosting's residuals are slightly biased on training rows (a “prediction shift”), because each tree was fitted on the same rows it's then evaluated on.

Symmetric (oblivious) trees. CatBoost's default grow_policy="SymmetricTree" uses the same question for every node at a given depth. A depth-6 tree is then just 6 yes/no answers forming a 6-bit index into 64 leaf values: very fast to evaluate, and the paper notes such trees are balanced and less prone to overfitting. Default depth is 6. Note that on CPU the default boosting_type is "Plain"; set boosting_type="Ordered" to get ordered boosting (the docs say it's usually better on small datasets, but slower). Ordered target statistics for categorical features are used either way.

    🚀 Startup vs big tech

    Week one

    Start with HistGradientBoostingClassifier: no extra dependency, NaN and categories handled. If your data has many high-cardinality categoricals, try CatBoost with defaults next; it often wins with almost no tuning. Pick one library and stick with it until a measured need says otherwise.

    At scale

    Hundreds of millions of rows on GPUs or Spark: XGBoost and LightGBM both have mature distributed and GPU training. Teams standardize on one library across the company, tune with Optuna (Lesson 5.7), export to a portable format, and watch prediction latency: the number of trees × depth is your serving cost (Lesson 8.5).

    🎤 Interview angle

    “What's the difference between XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost?” A strong answer:

    • Same algorithm (gradient-boosted trees); differences are in tree growth and statistics.
    • XGBoost: regularized objective (γ per leaf, λ on leaf weights), second-order gain formula, learned default direction for missing values, quantile sketch / histograms.
    • LightGBM: histogram binning, leaf-wise growth (watch num_leaves), GOSS, EFB; very fast on big, wide data.
    • CatBoost: ordered target statistics for categoricals, ordered boosting against prediction shift, symmetric trees; strong defaults.
    • Picks by data: lots of categoricals → CatBoost; huge or sparse → LightGBM; distributed/GPU ecosystem → XGBoost; no extra dependencies → scikit-learn HGB. And always: early stopping on a validation set.
    🏭 In the real world
    XGBoost 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen & Guestrin: XGBoost, A Scalable Tree Boosting System

    How: regularized second-order boosting with sparsity-aware split finding, a weighted quantile sketch, column blocks for parallel and cache-aware scans, and out-of-core training.

    Why this method: the paper reports running more than ten times faster than popular existing solutions on one machine and scaling to billions of examples; that combination is why it dominated 2015-era competitions.

    LightGBM 🏢 Microsoft · 2017 Ke et al.: LightGBM, A Highly Efficient Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (NeurIPS)

    How: histogram-based splits, GOSS to skip well-fitted rows, EFB to bundle exclusive sparse features, leaf-wise growth.

    Why this method: when both rows and features are many, scanning everything for every split is the bottleneck; the paper reports up to over 20× faster training with almost the same accuracy.

    CatBoost 🏢 Yandex · 2018 Prokhorenkova et al.: CatBoost, unbiased boosting with categorical features

    How: ordered target statistics and ordered boosting driven by random permutations; oblivious trees as base learners.

    Why this method: it identifies a target leak (prediction shift) in standard boosting and in naive target encoding, and removes it, which matters most for high-cardinality categorical data.

    scikit-learn's booster 🧰 Open source HistGradientBoostingClassifier docs

    How: 255-bin histograms, learned direction for missing values at each split, categorical_features="from_dtype" by default, early stopping automatic above 10,000 rows.

    Why this method: the docs call it much faster than GradientBoostingClassifier for big datasets (10,000+ rows) and say it's inspired by LightGBM: the same ideas, without leaving scikit-learn.

    🤯 Fun fact · the 6-bit tree

    Because every level of a CatBoost symmetric tree asks one shared question, evaluating a depth-6 tree needs only 6 comparisons, whose yes/no answers are literally the bits of the leaf number (0 to 63). No branching down different paths, no pointer chasing, which suits modern CPUs and vectorized code. The CatBoost paper cites this speed-up at test time as one reason for choosing oblivious trees.

    Check yourself

    1. An XGBoost leaf holds 4 rows (squared error) whose residuals add up to 12. With reg_lambda = 2, what value does the leaf get?
    w* = −G / (H + λ). The gradients add up to −12 and each row's hessian is 1, so H = 4: w* = 12 / (4 + 2) = 2, shrunk from the plain mean of 3. 6 would forget the rows' curvature (H).
    2. You have 200,000 rows with 40 columns, 12 of them categorical with thousands of levels (store IDs, zip codes). Little time to tune. Good first choice?
    High-cardinality categoricals are exactly what ordered target statistics were built for, and CatBoost's defaults are strong. One-hot would create tens of thousands of sparse columns; naive target encoding on the full training set leaks each row's own label.
    3. Your LightGBM model with num_leaves = 255 hits 99.9% training accuracy and does worse than a random forest on validation (5,000 rows). What do you change first?
    Leaf-wise growth with 255 leaves on 5,000 rows makes deep, narrow trees that memorize; the LightGBM docs warn about exactly this on small data. Limit capacity and stop early. More bins add precision (and overfitting); GOSS is about speed.
    📝 TL;DR
    • XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are all gradient-boosted trees; they differ in how trees are grown and how statistics are computed.
    • XGBoost: penalty γ per leaf and λ on leaf values, Newton-step gain, learned default direction for missing values.
    • LightGBM: ≤ 255-bin histograms, leaf-wise growth (cap num_leaves), GOSS, EFB. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting copies the histogram idea.
    • CatBoost: ordered target statistics and ordered boosting so no row sees its own label; symmetric trees for fast prediction.
    • Pick by data and constraints; always use early stopping on a validation set and compare at least two on your own data.
