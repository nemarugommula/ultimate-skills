Phase 4 · Lesson 2

Bagging & random forests: many shaky trees, one steady answer

One deep decision tree memorizes noise. A hundred of them, each trained a little differently, vote their mistakes away. It's one of the most reliable “just works” models in all of machine learning.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A decision tree asks yes/no questions about the features (“is x₁ ≤ 0.4?”) until each leaf is nearly pure; deeper trees fit more detail (Lesson 4.1).
  • Overfitting: a model that scores 100% on training data and much worse on new data has learned the noise (Lesson 0.6).
  • Variance measures how much a number jumps around from sample to sample (Math 4.4).
🎯 Hook: the Xbox that knew where your elbow was

In 2010 Microsoft's Kinect let you play games by waving your arms. Its camera saw only a depth image; something had to decide, for every pixel, “is this a hand, an elbow, a knee?”, many times a second on an Xbox 360.

That something was a randomized decision forest. The research team trained 3 trees, each 20 levels deep, on 1 million synthetic images (about a day on a 1,000-core cluster), and the finished system ran in under 5 ms per frame, about 200 frames per second, on the Xbox 360's GPU.

A single tree that deep should overfit horribly. So why did a handful of them, averaged, work well enough to ship in millions of living rooms? That's this lesson.

🔮 Predict first

You have one deep tree that gets 80% right on new data. You train 99 more, just as deep, each on a slightly different random sample of the same data, and let all 100 vote. Roughly what will the vote get?

On this lesson's dataset the vote goes from 80% → 89% with nothing else changed. The trees make different mistakes, and different mistakes cancel when you average. You'll watch it happen below.

Numbers first: one tree is a coin you flipped once

Here is the dataset you'll play with: 300 points shaped like two interlocking moons, with lots of noise. Half train, half test. A tree grown until every leaf is pure gets every training point right. Then we redraw the training set ten times (same data, resampled at random) and grow ten such trees:

Two things jump out. A deep tree's score depends on which sample it happened to see: that is high variance. And the forest beats even the carefully pruned tree, while keeping every tree deep and detailed. Averaging didn't make the trees simpler; it made their errors cancel.

💡 Big idea Train many low-bias, high-variance models (deep trees) so that their mistakes are as different from each other as possible, then average. Bagging makes them different by giving each tree a random resample of the rows. Random forests also give each split a random subset of the columns. Averaging keeps the detail and cancels the noise.
🧠 Analogy: guessing the weight of an ox

At a 1907 livestock fair in Plymouth, 787 visitors paid to guess the weight of an ox. Francis Galton collected the tickets: the middle guess was 1,207 lb; the ox weighed 1,198 lb. Hardly anyone guessed that well alone. Butchers guessed high, farmers low, tourists wildly; their errors pointed in different directions and cancelled.

Now imagine everyone had first listened to the same loud expert. Their guesses would agree with each other, and averaging 787 copies of one opinion gives you one opinion. A crowd is only wise when its mistakes are independent. Bagging and random features are tricks for making a crowd of trees disagree.

🤔 Why this? Why random forests?
  1. The problem before. Decision trees are wonderful: no scaling, handle mixed features, capture interactions, explainable. But a deep tree memorizes noise: move a few training points and the whole tree changes (here, test accuracy swings between 75% and 81% from resampling alone). That's high variance.
  2. What people tried. Pruning (stop the tree early, Lesson 4.1) lowers variance but throws away detail: the depth-2 tree is steady but blunt (high bias). Bagging (Breiman, 1996: train trees on bootstrap resamples, then vote) helped a lot, but the trees stayed too similar: the strongest feature wins the top split in almost every tree, so their mistakes are correlated and don't cancel.
  3. Why this works. If each tree's error has variance σ² and any two trees' errors correlate by ρ, the average of B trees has variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. More trees shrink the second term; only less correlation shrinks the first. With 100 trees at ρ = 0.3, variance drops to 0.3 + 0.7/100 = 0.307 of one tree's. Breiman's 2001 trick, a random subset of features at every split, lowers ρ: in Playground 4, using 1 of 10 features per split takes tree-to-tree correlation from 0.38 down to 0.11.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You lose the single readable tree (100 trees don't fit on a slide), memory and prediction time grow with the number of trees, and a forest can't extrapolate: it predicts within the range of targets it saw, so it fails on trends that continue beyond the training data (forecasting, Lesson 7.2). Very noisy features can also dilute it, and impurity-based importance is biased toward many-valued features (Lesson 8.1).
  5. What came next. Extremely randomized trees (2006) randomize the thresholds too. Boosting took the opposite route: instead of averaging independent trees, each new tree fixes the previous ones' mistakes (Lessons 4.3 and 4.4). Gradient boosting usually wins a bit more accuracy after tuning; random forests remain the strong default that needs almost none.

Step 1: bagging = bootstrap + aggregate

Bootstrap means: from your n training rows, draw n rows with replacement. Some rows appear twice or three times; some don't appear at all. Each tree gets its own bootstrap sample, so each tree sees a slightly different world. Aggregate means: average their predictions (or let them vote). Together: bootstrap aggregating, “bagging”.

🔮 Predict first

Draw 20 marbles from a bag of 20, putting each one back after you draw it. About how many of the 20 marbles never get drawn?

About 7 (37%). Each draw misses a given marble with probability 19/20, so it's missed all 20 times with probability (19/20)²⁰ ≈ 0.358. As n grows this tends to 1/e ≈ 0.368. Try it below.

✏️ Worked example: a free test set inside your training set

Every row is out-of-bag for about 37% of the trees. With 100 trees, row 7 was unseen by roughly 37 of them.

  • Ask only those ~37 trees to vote on row 7. Say 30 say “class 1” and 7 say “class 0”: the OOB prediction is class 1.
  • Do that for every row and compare with the true labels: that's the OOB accuracy, an estimate of test accuracy that cost no extra data.
  • On this lesson's moons it reads 85% against a true test accuracy of 89%: close, and with only 150 rows a few points of noise either way is normal. It is a quick check, not a replacement for proper cross-validation (Lesson 5.1).

Why averaging works (and where it stops working)

Pretend each tree's error on some new point is a random number with variance 1. If the trees were independent, the average of 100 of them would have variance 1/100: a ten-times smaller spread. But trees trained on overlapping samples of the same data are correlated. With correlation ρ between any two, the variance of the average is:

Var(average of B trees) = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ² ⁄ B “A floor of ρ that no number of trees can remove, plus a part that shrinks with every tree you add.”

This is equation 15.1 in The Elements of Statistical Learning; the variance rules behind it are in Math 4.4.

🔮 Predict first

Trees correlate at ρ = 0.3. You can afford infinitely many trees. What's the smallest variance you can reach, as a share of one tree's?

0.3. The (1 − ρ)/B part vanishes, the ρ part never does. After a few hundred trees, adding more is wasted compute; only making trees less alike helps. Check it with the sliders.

Step 2: random features, the “random” in random forest

Bagging alone leaves trees too alike. Suppose one feature (say, a customer's number of late payments) is far more predictive than the rest. Every bootstrap tree will split on it first, and their top halves will look nearly identical. Correlated trees, high ρ, a high floor.

Breiman's fix: at every split, let the tree consider only a random handful of the features (max_features). Sometimes the star feature isn't on the menu, so the tree has to find another good question. Each tree gets a bit worse alone, but the trees become much more different from each other. The common defaults: about √d features per split for classification (4 of 16), and in scikit-learn all features for regression (max_features=1.0), where the original papers suggested about d/3.

What you should see: the single deep tree carves little boxes around every noisy point (100% train, about 80% test), and 🎲 moves those boxes around. Bagging smooths it. The random forest's boundary barely moves when you reseed, and test accuracy sits around 89%. Add a single mislabelled point deep inside the other class: the lone tree builds an island for it; the forest mostly shrugs, because only the trees whose bootstrap sample contains that point can see it, and they're outvoted.

With only 2 features, “random features” can only mean 1 of 2 per split, so bagging and the forest look similar here. The difference grows with the number of features: that's the next playground.

Fewer features per split → trees less alike (ρ falls from about 0.38 at 10 features to about 0.11 at 1) but each tree is weaker (about 70% → 61%). Breiman's paper says it in one line: a forest's error depends on the strength of the trees and the correlation between them. The sweet spot is in between, and √10 ≈ 3 is usually close to it. Notice too that the curves flatten instead of turning down: adding trees doesn't overfit; it just stops helping.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): RandomForestClassifier / RandomForestRegressor, ExtraTreesClassifier, and BaggingClassifier to bag any model. R users have ranger. XGBoost and LightGBM also offer a random-forest mode, but most teams reach for scikit-learn here.

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=500,        # more trees never overfit; they just cost time and memory
    max_features="sqrt",    # the default for classifiers: √30 ≈ 5 features per split
    min_samples_leaf=1,     # deep trees are fine: averaging handles the variance
    oob_score=True,        # free validation estimate from the out-of-bag rows
    n_jobs=-1,              # trees are independent: train them on every CPU core
    random_state=0,
).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

print(f"OOB {rf.oob_score_:.3f}  test {rf.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")

# Importance you can trust: shuffle one column of held-out data and see how much the score drops (Lesson 8.1)
imp = permutation_importance(rf, X_te, y_te, n_repeats=10, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)
for name, m in sorted(zip(X.columns, imp.importances_mean), key=lambda t: -t[1])[:5]:
    print(f"{name:25s} {m:.3f}")

Knobs worth tuning, in order: max_features, then min_samples_leaf (1–10; larger = smoother, smaller files), then n_estimators (just “enough”: watch OOB flatten). For imbalanced classes try class_weight="balanced_subsample" (Lesson 5.5). The built-in feature_importances_ is impurity-based and can favour high-cardinality columns: scikit-learn's own example adds a column of pure random numbers and still sees it ranked as important; permutation importance on held-out data doesn't fall for it.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

A random forest with defaults is the best first model for a tabular problem: one line, no scaling, little tuning, honest OOB number. It also gives you a quick feature ranking and a baseline the fancy model must beat. Many products never need more.

At scale

Teams usually move the headline model to gradient boosting (LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost) for the last few points of accuracy and smaller, faster models. Forests stay around as baselines, for anomaly detection (isolation forests, Lesson 6.7), for quick feature screening, and where robustness matters more than the last 0.5%.

🎤 Interview angle

“How does a random forest work, and why is it better than one tree?” A strong answer:

  • Names the problem: deep trees are low bias, high variance.
  • Explains the two sources of randomness, bootstrap rows and random features per split, and why: to decorrelate the trees.
  • Quotes the variance of an average, ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B, to explain why more trees help but plateau, and why lowering ρ matters.
  • Mentions OOB error, that more trees don't overfit, and the limits: can't extrapolate, biased impurity importance, model size.
  • Compares with boosting: forests reduce variance by averaging independent trees; boosting reduces bias by adding trees that fix earlier mistakes (Lesson 4.3).
🏭 In the real world
Body-part recognition in Kinect 🏢 Microsoft · 2011 Real-Time Human Pose Recognition in Parts from Single Depth Images

How: a randomized decision forest labels every depth pixel with a body part, using simple depth-difference features; the per-pixel answers are pooled into 3D joint positions.

Why this method: each pixel's prediction is a few tree look-ups, which runs in parallel on a games-console GPU at about 200 frames per second, and the hundreds of thousands of synthetic training images kept even 20-level trees from overfitting.

179 classifiers on 121 datasets 🧪 Research · 2014 Do we Need Hundreds of Classifiers to Solve Real World Classification Problems? (JMLR)

How: compared 179 classifiers from 17 families (neural nets, SVMs, boosting, forests…) on 121 datasets.

Why this method: random forests came out as the family most likely to be the best; the top one reached 94.1% of the maximum accuracy on average. That's why “try a random forest first” became standard advice.

Tabular benchmark: trees vs deep learning 🧪 Research · 2022 Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on tabular data? (Grinsztajn et al.)

How: benchmarked random forests, gradient-boosted trees and deep tabular models on 45 medium-sized datasets with equal tuning budgets.

Why this method: tree-based models stayed state of the art on medium-sized data (around 10,000 rows), partly because they cope well with uninformative features and irregular patterns. Lesson 4.6 unpacks it.

The original paper 📜 History · 2001 Leo Breiman: Random Forests (PDF)

How: combined his 1996 bagging with random feature selection at each split, plus out-of-bag estimates of error, strength, correlation and variable importance.

Why this method: it matched AdaBoost's accuracy while being more robust to noisy labels, and showed the error converges (no overfitting) as trees are added.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn ensemble guide

How: RandomForestClassifier, ExtraTreesClassifier and BaggingClassifier, with parallel training (n_jobs) and OOB scores.

Why this method: one consistent fit/predict API (Lesson 0.5), so a forest drops into any Pipeline and cross-validation loop.

🤯 Fun fact · a trademarked algorithm

“Random Forests” is a trademark of Leo Breiman and Adele Cutler, licensed to Salford Systems, which is why some commercial tools call theirs “decision forests” or “random decision forests”. Check yourself

1. Your trees' errors correlate at ρ = 0.5. Compared with one tree, roughly how much variance is left with 10 trees, and with 1,000?
ρ + (1 − ρ)/B: 0.5 + 0.5/10 = 0.55; 0.5 + 0.5/1000 ≈ 0.50. Going from 10 to 1,000 trees barely helps. To do better, lower ρ (smaller max_features, more randomness), not B.
2. A teammate's random forest scores 100% on training data and 91% on test. They want to cut n_estimators from 500 to 50 “to reduce overfitting”. What do you tell them?
Adding trees only lowers the variance of the average; the error converges as B grows (Breiman 2001). Each deep tree fits its training sample perfectly, so 100% train accuracy says nothing. Removing the bootstrap would make trees more alike, the opposite of what helps.
3. You must predict next year's house prices, and prices have risen every year. Why might a random forest disappoint here?
Every leaf predicts an average of training targets, so every forest prediction lies inside the training range. For trends, model the change (e.g. predict growth rates), add a linear part, or use a forecasting model (Lessons 7.1–7.2). Forests do regression fine and never need scaling.
📝 TL;DR
  • Deep trees are low bias but high variance: their answers depend on the luck of the sample.
  • Bagging trains each tree on a bootstrap sample and averages; a random forest also picks random features at each split, to make the trees disagree.
  • Variance of the average = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B: more trees help until the ρ floor; lowering ρ is what max_features is for.
  • About 37% of rows are out-of-bag for each tree, giving a free OOB estimate. More trees never overfit.
  • Costs: no single readable tree, bigger and slower models, no extrapolation. Next: boosting, which fixes mistakes instead of averaging them (4.3).
