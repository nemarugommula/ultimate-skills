Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Decision trees: twenty questions, Gini & entropy

A decision tree is the game of twenty questions, learned from data: ask the yes/no question that best separates the answers, then repeat inside each half. It's the building block of random forests, XGBoost and LightGBM, so almost every tabular model you'll ship is made of these.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Classification: predict a label (churn / stay) from features (support calls, tenure…) (Lesson 0.1).
  • Overfitting: 100% on training data and much worse on new data means the model learned the noise (Lesson 0.6).
  • Entropy measures surprise: a 50/50 coin has 1 bit, a sure thing has 0 (Math 6.2).
🎯 Hook: one million things in twenty questions

“Is it alive? Is it bigger than a breadbox? Does it live in water?” In the game of twenty questions, a good player can pin down one thing out of a huge number of possibilities. The trick isn't knowledge; it's choosing questions that split the remaining options as evenly as possible.

Machine learning turned that trick into an algorithm. In 2006 the IEEE data-mining conference (ICDM) polled researchers for the ten most influential data-mining algorithms; the published list (Wu et al., 2008) includes two decision-tree learners, C4.5 and CART, plus AdaBoost, which boosts tiny trees (Lesson 4.3). Two decades later, trees are still inside most winning tabular models.

🔮 Predict first

Each yes/no question can at best halve the possibilities. How many questions do you need to single out one item among 1,000,000?

20. Halving 20 times leaves 1,000,000 ÷ 2²⁰ = 1,000,000 ÷ 1,048,576 < 1. Good questions shrink the problem exponentially. A tree that asks 20 questions in a row can carve the data into about a million boxes; that's exactly why deep trees can memorize anything, including noise.

Your gut says B: its “yes” side is perfectly pure, and its “no” side is nearly pure. A tree needs a number for “how mixed is this group?” so it can compare thousands of questions automatically. That number is called impurity.

💡 Big idea A decision tree is built greedily: try every feature and every threshold, pick the question whose two halves are purest (largest drop in impurity), then repeat inside each half until a stopping rule says “enough”. Each final box (a leaf) predicts the majority label (or the average, for regression) of the training rows that land in it.
🧠 Analogy: sorting laundry

You sort a pile of laundry with a series of questions: whites or colours? Then, inside the colours: delicate or not? You don't plan the whole procedure up front; at each pile you ask the question that splits that pile most cleanly, and you stop when each pile is “one kind” or too small to bother. You also never ask “is 0.7 × redness + 0.3 × fluffiness > 2?”: every question looks at one property. Both habits are exactly how CART grows a tree.

🤔 Why this? Why decision trees?
  1. The problem before. Linear models (Phase 2) add up feature effects, so “many support calls and a monthly contract” only matters if you hand-craft that interaction. Before learned trees, rules like “if calls ≥ 3 and monthly then risky” were written by hand, by experts, and went stale.
  2. What people tried. Hand-written rule systems (brittle, expensive to maintain) and linear models with hand-made interaction and threshold features (you must guess the right ones). Both needed a human to find the structure.
  3. Why this works. A tree searches for the rules itself, one greedy question at a time, using impurity to score each question. On the table above, the Gini impurity of the whole group is 0.5; question A only lowers it to 0.417, question B lowers it to 0.167, so B wins. Thresholds and interactions come for free, and the result reads like a flowchart.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A deep tree memorizes noise (high variance: move a few rows and the tree changes). Boundaries are axis-aligned staircases, so a diagonal boundary needs many steps. A tree can't extrapolate: it never predicts outside the range of targets it saw. And greedy choices can miss patterns like XOR, where no single first question looks useful.
  5. What came next. Pruning and stopping rules (below) tame variance a bit. Much better: average many trees (random forests, Lesson 4.2) or add trees that fix each other's mistakes (boosting, Lessons 4.3–4.4). Single trees remain the readable building block.

Measuring “mixed”: Gini impurity and entropy

Take a group where a share p of the people churn. Two popular ways to score how mixed it is:

  • Gini impurity: pick two people at random (with replacement). How often do they disagree? A 50/50 group: 0.5. A pure group: 0.
  • Entropy: how many bits of surprise per person, on average? A 50/50 group: 1 bit. A pure group: 0.
Gini = 1 − Σₖ pₖ²     Entropy = − Σₖ pₖ log₂ pₖ “Gini: one minus the sum of squared class shares. Entropy: the average number of bits needed to say which class a random member is.”

A question splits a group of n into a left part (nL) and a right part (nR). Its score is the size-weighted impurity of the two parts, and the gain is how much that is below the parent's impurity:

gain = impurity(parent) − ( nL/n · impurity(left) + nR/n · impurity(right) ) “How much purer, on average, are the children than the parent?” With entropy this gain is called information gain.

Where the entropy formula comes from, and why it's measured in bits: Math 6.2. How information gain chooses splits, derived: Math 8.1b.

✏️ Worked example: score both questions by hand

Parent (5 churn, 5 stay): Gini = 1 − 0.5² − 0.5² = 0.5; entropy = 1 bit.

Question A (contract = monthly?):

  • Yes side 4 of 6 churn: Gini = 1 − (4/6)² − (2/6)² = 1 − 0.444 − 0.111 = 0.444.
  • No side 1 of 4 churn: Gini = 1 − (1/4)² − (3/4)² = 1 − 0.0625 − 0.5625 = 0.375.
  • Weighted: 6/10 × 0.444 + 4/10 × 0.375 = 0.267 + 0.150 = 0.417. Gain = 0.5 − 0.417 = 0.083.

Question B (support calls ≥ 3?):

  • Yes side 4 of 4 churn: Gini = 1 − 1² − 0² = 0.
  • No side 1 of 6 churn: Gini = 1 − (1/6)² − (5/6)² = 1 − 0.028 − 0.694 = 0.278.
  • Weighted: 4/10 × 0 + 6/10 × 0.278 = 0.167. Gain = 0.5 − 0.167 = 0.333: four times question A's.

With entropy the verdict is the same: A's children average 0.875 bits (information gain 0.125), B's average 0.390 bits (information gain 0.610). The tree asks B first. Then it repeats inside the “no” side: Fay (monthly, 0 calls, churned) is the only churner left, and a second question would try to isolate her.

Growing the whole tree: when to stop?

After the first question, the tree recurses: each side gets its own best question, and so on. If nothing stops it, it keeps going until every leaf is pure, which on noisy data means one leaf per stubborn noisy point. The main knob is max_depth: how many questions in a row the tree may ask.

🔮 Predict first

On the noisy data below (two classes in a checkerboard of four quadrants, 10% of labels flipped), which depth gives the best test accuracy?

Depth 3 wins here: 84% on test. Depth 1 can't express a checkerboard at all (50%: a coin flip). Unlimited depth reaches 100% on training data and drops to 72% on test, because it builds little boxes around the flipped labels. Slide the depth and watch both numbers.

Three lessons hide in this playground. Overfitting: deeper is better on training data forever, but test accuracy peaks early. Greedy myopia: the first split's Gini gain is only 0.026 (almost nothing, because one cut through a checkerboard leaves both halves 50/50), yet the two splits below it gain 0.29 and 0.13. A tree that only looked one step ahead would have stopped. No scaling needed: tick “× 1000” and nothing changes, because a tree only compares a feature with a threshold; multiplying a column by 1000 just multiplies the threshold by 1000. Distance-based models like k-NN (Lesson 3.1) would be thrown off completely (Lesson 1.4).

Pruning: three ways to say “enough”

Knob (scikit-learn)What it doesTypical values
max_depthAt most this many questions in a row3–10 for a single tree
min_samples_leafEvery leaf must hold at least this many training rows, so no leaf can be built for one noisy point5–50, or 1% of rows
ccp_alphaCost-complexity pruning: grow the full tree, then cut back branches that don't earn their keep. Each leaf costs α; a branch survives only if it lowers the total weighted impurity by more than α per extra leafchoose by cross-validation (Lesson 5.1)

The first two stop the tree early (pre-pruning). ccp_alpha grows everything and then removes (post-pruning), which can keep a weak-looking split if strong splits sit below it: exactly the XOR situation above.

minimize   total leaf impurity + α × (number of leaves) “Every leaf has a price α. Keep a branch only if the impurity it removes is worth the leaves it adds.”

Regression trees: predictions as staircases

For a numeric target, the recipe barely changes: impurity becomes the variance (mean squared error) of the targets in a node, and each leaf predicts the average of its training targets. The prediction is a step function: flat inside each box, jumping at each threshold.

🔮 Predict first

A regression tree is trained on x between 0 and 6, where y climbs steadily from about 1 to about 6.7. What does it predict at x = 8, where the true value is about 8?

The rightmost leaf's average: 5.5 for a depth-2 tree, 6.3–6.4 for any depth from 3 up. Every point beyond the last threshold falls into the rightmost leaf, which predicts the average of the training targets it holds. Trees (and forests and boosted trees) are flat outside the training range. The straight line says 8.2: worse inside the range, much better outside.

RMSE is the root mean squared error: the typical size of a miss, in the target's units (Lesson 5.2). Depth 2 is best on test here (0.58); depth 12 gets 0.00 on training data and 0.74 on test. And no depth fixes x = 8: that's a property of the model family, not a tuning problem. For trends, predict the change instead of the level, add a linear part, or use a forecasting model (Lessons 7.1–7.2).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): DecisionTreeClassifier / DecisionTreeRegressor implement an optimized CART (always binary splits). criterion="gini" (default), "entropy" or "log_loss" (the same as entropy). export_text and plot_tree show the learned rules; cost_complexity_pruning_path lists the α values worth trying.

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

deep = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)       # grows until every leaf is pure
print("deep:", deep.get_depth(), "levels,", deep.get_n_leaves(), "leaves, test", round(deep.score(X_te, y_te), 3))

# post-pruning: try each alpha on the pruning path, keep the best by 5-fold cross-validation
path = deep.cost_complexity_pruning_path(X_tr, y_tr)
best = max(path.ccp_alphas[:-1], key=lambda a: cross_val_score(
    DecisionTreeClassifier(ccp_alpha=a, random_state=0), X_tr, y_tr, cv=5).mean())
pruned = DecisionTreeClassifier(ccp_alpha=best, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("pruned:", pruned.get_n_leaves(), "leaves, test", round(pruned.score(X_te, y_te), 3))
print(export_text(pruned, feature_names=list(X.columns), max_depth=2))   # the rules, readable

On this split the unpruned tree has 5 levels and 18 leaves; the pruned one keeps just 5 leaves with the same test accuracy (0.902), and prints as five readable rules (“worst perimeter ≤ 106.1 and worst concave points ≤ 0.13 → benign”…). Every column was used raw: no scaling, no one-hot needed for numeric data. Current scikit-learn trees also accept missing values (NaN) directly with the default splitter="best"; categorical columns, though, still have to be encoded first (Lesson 1.3).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

A depth-3 or depth-4 tree is a great explainer: fit it, print it with export_text, and show the business “churn is mostly people with 3+ support calls on monthly plans”. It's also a quick sanity check for leakage: if the first split is on cancellation_date, you've leaked the answer (Lesson 0.6).

At scale

Single trees rarely ship as the main model; ensembles of them do (forests, gradient boosting). Where regulation demands a model a person can audit line by line, a small tree or a rule list is still used, and teams accept some accuracy loss for it. Trees also make compact surrogates that approximate a complex model to explain it (Lesson 8.1).

🎤 Interview angle

“How does a decision tree choose its splits, and how do you stop it overfitting?” A strong answer:

  • Greedy search over every feature and threshold, scoring each split by the weighted impurity of the children (Gini or entropy; variance for regression); pick the largest gain; recurse.
  • Can compute one by hand: a 50/50 node has Gini 0.5, a pure node 0; Gini = 1 − Σp².
  • Overfitting control: max_depth, min_samples_leaf, ccp_alpha chosen by cross-validation, and above all ensembles (4.2, 4.3).
  • Knows the quirks: no scaling needed (monotone transforms don't change the splits), axis-aligned boundaries, no extrapolation, greedy (XOR), unstable to small data changes.
🏭 In the real world
Top 10 algorithms in data mining 🧪 Research · 2008 Wu et al., Knowledge and Information Systems (PDF)

How: award winners nominated algorithms; programme-committee members of KDD, ICDM and SDM 2006 voted; the 145 attendees of the ICDM '06 panel confirmed the same list. C4.5 and CART both made it.

Why this method: trees handle mixed data types and missing values, need little preparation, and produce rules people can read. The paper also lists CART's differences from C4.5: always binary splits, Gini instead of information-based criteria, and cost-complexity pruning chosen by cross-validation.

Information gain is born 📜 History · 1986 Quinlan: Induction of Decision Trees (Machine Learning, 1986)

How: ID3 grows a tree top-down, choosing at each node the attribute with the largest information gain (entropy drop); its successor C4.5 added numeric thresholds, missing-value handling and pruning.

Why this method: it turned expert-system “knowledge acquisition” (interviewing experts for rules) into learning rules from examples.

Every tree inside XGBoost 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen & Guestrin: XGBoost, A Scalable Tree Boosting System

How: the paper calls its weak learners regression trees (CART); the split search is this lesson's algorithm with a different gain formula (Lesson 4.4).

Why this method: trees are cheap to fit, handle any feature scale, and capture interactions, which is what you want from each small step of boosting.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn user guide: Decision Trees

How: an optimized CART with Gini, entropy and squared-error criteria, cost-complexity pruning and readable exports.

Why this method: the guide lists the trade-offs honestly: simple to understand and visualize, little data preparation, but prone to overfitting and unstable to small data changes, which is why it recommends ensembles.

🤯 Fun fact · half the code was for missing values

The “Top 10” paper notes that, according to Friedman and colleagues, 50% of the original CART code was devoted to handling missing values. CART's trick, surrogate splits, keeps a ranked list of backup questions that mimic the main one, so a row with a blank in the main feature can still go left or right. Modern boosting libraries use a simpler idea: learn a default direction for missing values at every split (Lesson 4.4).

Check yourself

1. A node holds 8 rows: 6 of class A and 2 of class B. What is its Gini impurity?
1 − (6/8)² − (2/8)² = 1 − 0.5625 − 0.0625 = 0.375. A 50/50 node would be 0.5 and a pure node 0, so this one is in between, closer to mixed than pure.
2. Your unpruned tree scores 100% on training data and 71% on validation. What's the most sensible first fix?
100% train vs 71% validation is classic high variance: the tree memorized noise. Pruning or averaging trees fixes that. Scaling never changes a tree's splits, and Gini vs entropy rarely matters.
3. A product team wants to predict next quarter's revenue, which has grown every quarter, with a gradient-boosted tree model on past quarters. What will likely go wrong?
Every leaf predicts an average of training targets, and a sum of trees is still bounded by what they saw. Model the growth rate (a target that stays in range), add a linear trend, or use a forecasting method (Lessons 7.1–7.2).
📝 TL;DR
  • A decision tree asks greedy yes/no questions on one feature at a time, choosing the split that most reduces impurity.
  • Gini = 1 − Σp² (0.5 for a 50/50 node); entropy = −Σp log₂p (1 bit); for regression, variance. Gain = parent − weighted children.
  • Unlimited depth memorizes noise; control it with max_depth, min_samples_leaf or ccp_alpha, chosen by validation.
  • No scaling needed; axis-aligned staircase boundaries; flat outside the training range (no extrapolation); greedy, so it can miss XOR-like patterns.
  • Single trees are readable but unstable; forests (4.2) and boosting (4.3) turn them into the strongest tabular models.
