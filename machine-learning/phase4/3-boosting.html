Phase 4 · Lesson 3

Boosting: from AdaBoost to gradient boosting

A random forest averages trees that never talk to each other. Boosting builds them in a line: each new tree studies the mistakes of the ones before it. That simple idea powers XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, the models that win most tabular-data competitions.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A decision stump is a tree with one question; a shallow tree has a few (Lesson 4.1).
  • Random forests lower variance by averaging many deep trees (Lesson 4.2).
  • A gradient points uphill on the loss; stepping against it lowers the loss (Math 3.4).
🎯 Hook: the camera that found faces in 2001

Learning theorists once asked a strange question: if you have a learner that's only slightly better than guessing, can you combine copies of it into one that's almost always right? Robert Schapire proved the answer is yes (“The strength of weak learnability”, 1990). Yoav Freund and Schapire then turned the proof into a practical algorithm, AdaBoost; their paper won the 2003 Gödel Prize, one of theoretical computer science's top awards.

In 2001 Paul Viola and Michael Jones used AdaBoost to pick a handful of simple light-and-dark rectangle features out of a huge pool. Their detector found faces in 384 × 288 images at 15 frames per second on a 700 MHz Pentium III, real-time face detection on an ordinary desktop PC. Each weak feature alone was poor. Hundreds of them, boosted, were excellent.

🔮 Predict first

A single decision stump (one yes/no question) gets 65% on a ring-shaped dataset (one class inside a circle, the other around it). What do you get from 100 stumps, each trained to fix the previous ones' mistakes?

95% on test. No single stump can draw a circle, but a weighted vote of 100 axis-aligned cuts can approximate one as a many-sided box. Averaging 100 stumps without boosting (a forest of stumps) only reaches 77%. Watch it in Playground 1.

Read it as a rule of thumb: forests fix variance, boosting fixes bias. Forests average strong, wobbly learners; boosting adds up weak, steady ones.

💡 Big idea Boosting trains models one after another. Each new weak model (usually a small tree) is trained to fix what the current ensemble still gets wrong, and is then added to it with a small weight. AdaBoost does this by re-weighting the training points it misclassified. Gradient boosting does it by fitting each new tree to the residuals (more generally, the negative gradient of the loss). The final prediction is a sum of many small trees.
🧠 Analogy: a relay of tutors

A student takes a practice exam. Tutor 1 teaches the broad topics. The student retakes the exam; tutor 2 looks only at the questions still wrong and drills those. Tutor 3 drills whatever is still wrong after that. No tutor is brilliant, but each one spends all their time on the remaining mistakes. A forest is the opposite: twenty tutors teach the whole syllabus independently and the student averages their advice.

The danger is obvious too: by tutor 50, the only questions left wrong may be ones with a typo in the answer key. A tutor who drills those is teaching noise. That's boosting's overfitting, and why we stop early.

🤔 Why this? Why boosting?
  1. The problem before. Simple models (stumps, shallow trees, linear models) are stable but biased: they can't draw complex shapes. Deep trees can, but they're noisy, and averaging them (forests) leaves the bias of each tree untouched.
  2. What people tried. Bigger single models (deeper trees, more features) traded bias for variance. Averaging (bagging, Lesson 4.2) cut variance but doesn't help when every member is too simple: our forest of stumps is stuck at 77%.
  3. Why this works. Each round looks only at what's still wrong. In AdaBoost, if a stump misses 3 of 10 points (weighted error 0.3), those 3 points are up-weighted until they carry exactly half of the total weight, so the next stump must pay attention to them. In gradient boosting, if a house is predicted at 300 but sold for 450, the next tree is trained to predict that +150. Add the corrections up and the bias melts away.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Trees are built in sequence, so training is harder to parallelize than a forest. Boosting can overfit: keep adding trees and it starts fitting noise, so you need a learning rate, a validation set and early stopping. AdaBoost is sensitive to mislabelled points (they keep getting heavier). More knobs to tune than a forest.
  5. What came next. Friedman's gradient boosting (2001) generalized the idea to any differentiable loss. Then engineering: XGBoost (2016) added regularization and second-order steps, LightGBM (2017) histograms and leaf-wise growth, CatBoost (2018) ordered boosting for categorical data (Lesson 4.4).

AdaBoost: make the mistakes heavier

Start with every training point equally important (weight 1/n). Then repeat:

  1. Fit a stump that minimizes the weighted error.
  2. Give that stump a vote α: big if its weighted error is small, zero if it's a coin flip.
  3. Multiply the weights of the points it got wrong by eα and of the ones it got right by e−α; renormalize to sum to 1.
α = ½ ln( (1 − err) / err )    final vote: sign( Σₘ αₘ · stumpₘ(x) ) “A stump that's right 70% of the time gets a vote of 0.42; one that's right 50% gets 0. The ensemble is a weighted vote of all stumps.”
✏️ Worked example: one round of re-weighting
  • 10 points, each weight 0.1. The best stump misses 3 of them: weighted error = 0.3.
  • Its vote: α = ½ ln(0.7 / 0.3) = ½ ln(2.333) = 0.424.
  • Missed points: 0.1 × e0.424 = 0.1 × 1.528 = 0.153. Correct points: 0.1 × e−0.424 = 0.1 × 0.655 = 0.065.
  • Totals before normalizing: 3 × 0.153 = 0.458 and 7 × 0.065 = 0.458. Equal! After dividing by 0.917: each missed point weighs 0.167, each correct one 0.071.
  • The 3 mistakes now carry 50% of the weight. That's always true after an AdaBoost update: the last stump is exactly a coin flip on the new weights, so the next stump has to find something new.

Gradient boosting: fit the leftovers

AdaBoost's re-weighting only makes sense for its own exponential loss. In 2001 Jerome Friedman found the general recipe, which works for squared error, log-loss, absolute error, ranking losses, anything you can differentiate:

  1. Start with a constant guess F₀ (the mean target, or the log-odds of the positive class).
  2. Compute each row's residual r = y − F(x). (In general: the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the prediction. For squared error that is exactly the residual.)
  3. Fit a small regression tree to the residuals.
  4. Add it, shrunk by a learning rate η: F ← F + η · tree. Repeat.
FM(x) = F₀ + η · tree₁(x) + η · tree₂(x) + … + η · treeM(x) “Start from the average, then add M small corrections, each one a tree trained on what the sum so far still gets wrong.”

Why the negative gradient is the right target, and boosting as gradient descent in function space: Math 8.1b.

  • F₀ = mean price = 300. The stump that best fits the residuals splits at size ≤ 100: left leaf = mean of (−90, −60, 0) = −50; right leaf = +150.
  • With η = 0.1, each prediction moves only a tenth of the way. Mean squared error: (90² + 60² + 0² + 150²)/4 = 8,550 → (85² + 55² + 5² + 135²)/4 = 7,125.

Learning rate × rounds, and early stopping

The learning rate η (also called shrinkage) and the number of rounds M trade off against each other: halve η and you need roughly twice the rounds to get to the same place. Smaller steps usually generalize a little better and are easier to stop at the right moment, but cost more trees. Nobody guesses M. You hold out a validation set, watch its loss after every round, and stop when it hasn't improved for a while (the patience), keeping the best round. That's early stopping.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): HistGradientBoostingClassifier/Regressor (fast, histogram-based, the one to use; Lesson 4.4), GradientBoostingClassifier/Regressor (the classic, fine for small data), AdaBoostClassifier. For big or competitive work: XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost (Lesson 4.4).

from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer, make_friedman1
from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier, GradientBoostingRegressor, HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

# Gradient boosting for regression, with early stopping on an internal 10% validation split
X, y = make_friedman1(n_samples=2000, noise=1.0, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)
gbr = GradientBoostingRegressor(
    learning_rate=0.05, n_estimators=2000,   # small steps, generous maximum
    max_depth=3, subsample=0.8,                  # shallow trees; 80% of rows per tree (stochastic boosting)
    validation_fraction=0.1, n_iter_no_change=20, # early stopping: patience 20 rounds
    random_state=0,
).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("trees used:", gbr.n_estimators_, " test R²:", round(gbr.score(X_te, y_te), 3))

# Classification: the histogram version is much faster on big data and stops early by default above 10,000 rows
Xc, yc = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
Xc_tr, Xc_te, yc_tr, yc_te = train_test_split(Xc, yc, stratify=yc, random_state=0)
hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.1, max_iter=500, early_stopping=True,
                                     n_iter_no_change=20, random_state=0).fit(Xc_tr, yc_tr)
ada = AdaBoostClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=0).fit(Xc_tr, yc_tr)   # default weak learner: a stump
print("HGB rounds:", hgb.n_iter_, " test acc:", round(hgb.score(Xc_te, yc_te), 3), "| AdaBoost:", round(ada.score(Xc_te, yc_te), 3))

Output with scikit-learn 1.9: the regressor stops after 419 of its 2,000 allowed trees (test R² 0.933); the histogram booster stops after 71 rounds and both classifiers score 0.944 on this split (other versions differ in the last digit). staged_predict on the classic estimators gives the prediction after every round, which is how you draw Playground 2's curve on real data.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

HistGradientBoostingClassifier() with defaults, early stopping on, is a strong tabular baseline in one line: it handles missing values and needs no scaling. Compare it with a random forest and a logistic regression; if boosting doesn't win clearly, ship the simplest.

At scale

Teams use XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost for speed on millions of rows, GPUs, distributed training and fine control (Lesson 4.4). They tune η, depth or leaves, rounds, row and column subsampling with a search tool (Lesson 5.7), monitor validation loss in every training run, and keep the early-stopping round in the model registry (Lesson 8.5).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain gradient boosting, and how it differs from a random forest.” A strong answer:

  • Additive model of small trees built sequentially; each tree fits the negative gradient of the loss (the residuals for squared error), added with a learning rate.
  • Forests: deep trees, built independently, averaged → reduce variance. Boosting: shallow trees, built sequentially, summed → reduce bias.
  • Knows boosting overfits with too many rounds; uses a learning rate, early stopping on validation, subsampling, depth limits.
  • Can describe AdaBoost's re-weighting and its weakness with label noise, and name XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost as engineered gradient boosting.
🏭 In the real world
Real-time face detection 🧪 Research · 2001 Viola & Jones: Rapid Object Detection using a Boosted Cascade of Simple Features (PDF)

How: AdaBoost selects a few rectangle features at a time (each weak learner uses one feature); boosted classifiers are chained in a cascade that throws away easy non-face windows early.

Why this method: boosting doubles as feature selection, picking a handful of useful features from a pool of over 180,000 candidates per window, and the result was fast enough for 15 frames per second on 2001 hardware.

AdaBoost wins the Gödel Prize 📜 History · 2003 ACM SIGACT: 2003 Gödel Prize

How: awarded to Freund and Schapire for their 1997 paper introducing AdaBoost.

Why this method: the citation praises its elegance, simplicity, wide applicability and “striking success in reducing errors in benchmark applications”.

Gradient boosting, defined 📜 History · 2001 Friedman: Greedy Function Approximation, A Gradient Boosting Machine (Annals of Statistics)

How: fit each new tree to the negative gradient of an arbitrary loss, then set leaf values to minimize that loss; shrinkage (the learning rate) as regularization.

Why this method: one framework for regression, classification and robust losses, and the basis of every modern GBM library.

The Netflix Prize blend 🏢 Netflix Prize · 2009 Koren: The BellKor Solution to the Netflix Grand Prize (PDF)

How: in the last 30 days of the competition the winning team used gradient-boosted trees (200 trees, shrinkage 0.18) to blend hundreds of predictors, adding features such as how many movies each user had rated.

Why this method: GBDTs handle skewed inputs without transformations and highly correlated inputs without trouble. Lesson 4.5 is about this kind of blending.

Kaggle's workhorse 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen & Guestrin: XGBoost

How: of the 29 winning solutions published on Kaggle's blog in 2015, 17 used XGBoost; every top-10 team in KDD Cup 2015 used it.

Why this method: boosted trees are accurate on tabular data and, engineered well, fast. Lesson 4.4 shows what XGBoost changed.

🤯 Fun fact · 6,000 features, about 10 looks per window

The full Viola–Jones detector was a cascade of 38 boosted stages with over 6,000 features, yet on a test set of 75 million sub-windows it evaluated only about 10 features per window on average: its first stage, built from just two boosted features, already rejects most non-face windows, so the expensive stages rarely run. Boosting plus “quit early when it's obviously not a face” is what made it real time.

Check yourself

1. An AdaBoost stump has weighted error 0.2. Roughly what vote α does it get?
α = ½ ln(0.8 / 0.2) = ½ ln 4 = ½ × 1.386 ≈ 0.69. Error 0.5 would give α = 0 (a coin flip gets no vote); error near 0 gives a huge vote. 1.39 forgot the ½.
2. Your GBM's training loss keeps falling, but validation loss has risen for the last 150 of 500 rounds. What do you do?
Rising validation loss while training loss falls is overfitting by rounds. Early stopping keeps the best round automatically. Deeper trees would overfit even faster.
3. A team's single deep tree overfits badly, and their depth-2 tree underfits badly. A colleague suggests a random forest of depth-2 trees. What's better, and why?
Averaging reduces variance, not bias: a forest of shallow trees stays shallow-ish (77% vs 65% for one stump on the ring data). Boosting the shallow trees attacks the bias (95%); a forest of deep trees attacks the deep tree's variance (95%).
📝 TL;DR
  • Boosting builds small models in sequence; each one targets what the ensemble still gets wrong; the prediction is their (weighted) sum.
  • AdaBoost re-weights mistakes (α = ½ ln((1 − err)/err)); gradient boosting fits each tree to the residuals / negative gradient of any loss.
  • Forests reduce variance by averaging; boosting reduces bias by adding.
  • Boosting can overfit by adding rounds: use a learning rate (0.03–0.1), a validation set and early stopping. Halving η roughly doubles the rounds needed.
