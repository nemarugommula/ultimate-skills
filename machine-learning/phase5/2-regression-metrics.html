Phase 5 · Lesson 2

Regression metrics: MAE, RMSE, R², MAPE

“How wrong is the model, on average?” sounds like one question. It's at least four, and each metric quietly rewards a different model. Pick the one whose mistakes cost what your business's mistakes cost.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A residual (error) is prediction − actual; linear regression picks the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals (Lesson 2.1).
  • The mean is pulled by extreme values; the median (middle value) isn't (Math 5.1).
  • Cross-validation gives a steady estimate of any metric on new data (Lesson 5.1).
🎯 Hook: which delivery-time model would you ship?

You're building the “arrives in 25 min” estimate for a food-delivery app. On 10 test orders:

  • Model A is off by 2 minutes on every order.
  • Model B is exactly right on 9 orders and 20 minutes late on one.

Both have the same average miss: 2 minutes. One of them produces an angry customer, a refund and a one-star review. Which number would have warned you?

🔮 Predict first

Root mean squared error (RMSE) squares each miss, averages, then takes the square root. What does it say about A and B?

A: √(10 × 2² ÷ 10) = 2. B: √(20² ÷ 10) = √40 ≈ 6.3. Squaring makes one 20-minute miss count like a hundred 2-minute ones. Mean absolute error (MAE) says “tie”; RMSE says “B has a dangerous tail”. Neither is wrong: they answer different questions.

✏️ Worked example: all four by hand
  • MAE (mean absolute error) = (1 + 2 + 3 + 0 + 2) ÷ 5 = 1.6. “Typically off by 1.6.” Same units as y.
  • MSE (mean squared error) = (1 + 4 + 9 + 0 + 4) ÷ 5 = 3.6; RMSE = √3.6 = 1.90. Same units as y, but leans toward the big misses (the +3 contributes half of the total).
  • R² (“R squared”): compare with the dumbest model, always predicting the mean (12). Its squared errors sum to 4 + 0 + 9 + 9 + 4 = 26; ours to 18. R² = 1 − 18 ÷ 26 = 0.31: we removed 31% of the squared error the mean-guesser makes.
  • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) = (1/10 + 2/12 + 3/15 + 0/9 + 2/14) ÷ 5 = 12.2%. Unit-free, easy to explain to a manager… with traps you'll see below.
MAE = mean |ŷ − y|  ·  RMSE = √ mean (ŷ − y)²  ·  R² = 1 − Σ(ŷ − y)² ⁄ Σ(y − ȳ)² “MAE: the typical miss. RMSE: the typical miss, with big misses counted extra. R²: what share of the mean-guesser's squared error we got rid of.”
💡 Big idea Every metric is a price list for mistakes. MAE charges by the minute; RMSE charges by the minute squared, so big misses are expensive; MAPE charges by the percent, so misses on small values are expensive. A model trained or chosen on one price list will behave differently from one chosen on another: squared error leads to predicting the mean, absolute error to predicting the median.
🧠 Analogy: parking fines

City A fines you $10 per minute you overstay. City B fines you the minutes squared: 2 minutes costs $4, 20 minutes costs $400. In City A you don't mind the occasional long overstay; in City B you'd rather be a little late every day than very late once. MAE is City A, RMSE is City B. Your business is one of the two (or a third city with its own rules): pick the metric that matches.

🤔 Why this? Why more than one regression metric?
  1. The problem before. “Average error” sounds obvious, but plain average of ŷ − y lets +10 and −10 cancel to 0. You need to remove the sign, and how you remove it (absolute value, square, percent) changes which model wins.
  2. What people tried. MSE came first (Gauss and Legendre, around 1800): it's smooth, easy to minimise with calculus, and gives the mean. But one outlier can dominate it. MAE is robust, but its optimum is the median and its gradient has a kink. MAPE was adopted because managers think in percent.
  3. Why this works. Each metric's best constant guess reveals its taste. For prices 310–410 plus one $1.5M mansion (in $k), the mean is 473 and the median 360: MSE picks 473 (wrong for every normal house), MAE picks 360. You'll drag the mansion in Playground 1.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. RMSE is hijacked by outliers and grows with them; MAE ignores whether the misses are rare-and-huge; R² depends on how spread out y is, so it can't be compared across datasets; MAPE explodes when actual values near zero appear and favours forecasts that run low.
  5. What came next. sMAPE and scaled errors like MASE for forecasting competitions, pinball (quantile) loss when over- and under-prediction cost different amounts, and full prediction intervals (Lesson 7.2). For classification, the story repeats with accuracy, precision and recall (Lesson 5.3).

Outliers: RMSE flinches, MAE shrugs

Predict the price of the next house on a street with a single number. Nine houses sold for $310k to $410k; the tenth is a mansion. Which single guess is “best”? It depends on the price list.

🔮 Predict first

The mansion goes from $420k to $3M. What happens to the guess that minimises MAE?

It stays at the median, $360k, no matter how extreme the mansion gets: the median only cares that the mansion is “above the middle”, not by how much. The RMSE-minimising guess, the mean, climbs from $365k to $623k. Drag it below.

Two lessons hide in that chart. First, RMSE is minimised by the mean and MAE by the median, which is why training with squared error (the default almost everywhere) predicts averages and training with absolute error predicts typical values. Second, look at R²: it's exactly 0 at the mean and negative anywhere else. R² = 0 means “no better than always guessing the average”; a negative R² on test data means your model is worse than that, which happens more often than people admit.

⚠️ R² traps
  • R² depends on the spread of y. The same model, with the same RMSE, scores a higher R² on a dataset with a wider range of y. Don't compare R² across datasets; compare RMSE or MAE.
  • Training R² always rises as you add features (even random ones). Judge it on validation folds.
  • “R² = 0.9” can still be useless if the remaining error is too big for the decision (±$50k on a house).

MAPE: friendly percentages, nasty surprises

MAPE divides each miss by the actual value. That's what makes it unit-free (“we're off by 8%”), and also what breaks it: if the actual value is tiny, a small miss becomes a huge percentage, and if it's 0, the formula divides by zero.

🔮 Predict first

A store sells about 100 units a day, and the forecast is always 10 units too high. One quiet day it sells only 1 unit. What does MAPE over the 8 days become?

133%. That one day's error is 10 ÷ 1 = 1,000%, and the average of 8 days swallows it. The MAE hasn't moved: still exactly 10 units. sMAPE stays at 29% because it divides by the average of forecast and actual. Try it below.

Notice the asymmetry. A forecast that's too low can be off by at most 100% (forecasting 0), while a forecast that's too high has no ceiling. So a model chosen to minimise MAPE learns to forecast low: on demand data, that means empty shelves. sMAPE (symmetric MAPE) divides by the average of |forecast| and |actual|, capping each term at 200%. The M4 forecasting competition (100,000 series) ranked methods by sMAPE together with MASE (mean absolute scaled error: your MAE divided by the MAE of a naive “same as last period” forecast), precisely to avoid MAPE's problems.

When mistakes have different prices: quantile (pinball) loss

A bakery bakes loaves each morning. An unsold loaf costs $1 (ingredients, thrown away). A customer who finds the shelf empty costs $3 in lost profit. Neither MAE nor RMSE knows that running out is three times worse than overbaking. What should the bakery predict?

The answer: not the average demand, but a quantile, the level that demand stays under on a certain share of days. The right share is cost of running short ÷ (cost of running short + cost of leftovers) = 3 ÷ (3 + 1) = 75%. The loss that rewards predicting that quantile is the pinball loss: it charges τ per unit of under-prediction and (1 − τ) per unit of over-prediction, with τ = 0.75 here. MAE is the special case τ = 0.5, which gives the median.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): all of these live in sklearn.metrics, and as CV scorers they're negated (scikit-learn maximises scores): scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error", "neg_mean_absolute_error", "r2". root_mean_squared_error has its own function since 1.4.

from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import (mean_absolute_error, root_mean_squared_error, r2_score,
                             mean_absolute_percentage_error, mean_pinball_loss)
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, train_test_split

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True)                 # disease progression after one year (25 to 346)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

for loss in ["squared_error", "absolute_error"]:          # train for the mean vs for the median
    m = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss=loss, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    p = m.predict(X_te)
    print(f"{loss:15s} MAE {mean_absolute_error(y_te, p):5.1f}  RMSE {root_mean_squared_error(y_te, p):5.1f}  "
          f"R² {r2_score(y_te, p):.2f}  MAPE {mean_absolute_percentage_error(y_te, p):.0%}")

# Asymmetric costs: predict the 75th percentile, score with the matching pinball loss
q75 = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=0.75, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
p75 = q75.predict(X_te)
print(mean_pinball_loss(y_te, p75, alpha=0.75), (y_te <= p75).mean())   # ≈ 72% of actuals fall below

print(cross_val_score(HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0), X, y, cv=5,
                      scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error").mean())       # about −60

mean_absolute_percentage_error returns a fraction (0.39, not 39) and doesn't protect you from zeros: it divides by a tiny epsilon and returns an enormous number. LightGBM (objective="quantile", alpha=) and XGBoost (objective="reg:quantileerror") train quantile models too; for forecasting metrics such as MASE see the sibling packages sktime and statsforecast (Lesson 7.2).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Report MAE in real units plus RMSE, next to a dumb baseline (predict the mean, or “same as last week”). If your model doesn't beat the baseline clearly, nothing else matters. Ask the business which mistakes hurt before you pick a loss.

At scale

Forecasting teams predict quantiles (P10, P50, P90) instead of one number, because inventory, staffing and capacity decisions each need a different quantile. Amazon's MQ-RNN forecaster, for example, is trained on several quantile losses at once. Metrics are weighted by revenue so the important items count most.

🎤 Interview angle

“When would you use MAE instead of RMSE?” A strong answer:

  • RMSE squares errors, so it penalises big misses more and is minimised by the mean; MAE treats misses linearly, is robust to outliers and is minimised by the median.
  • Choose by the cost of mistakes: if a 20-minute miss is much worse than ten 2-minute ones, RMSE; if outliers are noise you shouldn't chase, MAE.
  • Mentions R²'s meaning (vs the mean-guesser, can be negative) and MAPE's zero problem and low bias.
  • For asymmetric costs, proposes a quantile/pinball loss with τ from the real costs, and always compares against a baseline.
🏭 In the real world
M4 forecasting competition 🧪 Research · 2018 M4-methods (official repository)

How: 100,000 time series, 61 methods, ranked by sMAPE, MASE and their combination (OWA, the overall weighted average relative to a benchmark).

Why this method: scale-free metrics let series of wildly different sizes be averaged, and MASE avoids MAPE's division by near-zero actuals.

M5: Walmart sales 🧪 Research · 2020 M5-methods (official repository)

How: around 42,000 hierarchical daily series from Walmart; point forecasts scored by a weighted, scaled RMSE (WRMSSE) and the uncertainty track by a weighted scaled pinball loss (WSPL) over several quantiles.

Why this method: squared error for the expected sales, pinball loss for the quantiles that drive stocking decisions, and revenue weights so best-sellers count more.

Quantile forecasts for Amazon demand 🏢 Amazon · 2017 Wen et al.: A Multi-Horizon Quantile Recurrent Forecaster

How: one network outputs several quantiles of future demand for every product, trained with the sum of their quantile losses.

Why this method: stocking decisions need a high quantile, not the mean; a quantile loss lets the model learn each directly.

🤯 Fun fact · the 200% ceiling

“Symmetric” MAPE isn't really symmetric: because it divides by the average of forecast and actual, a forecast of 0 for an actual of 100 scores exactly 200%, the worst possible, while a forecast of 200 scores only 67%. The fix for one asymmetry created another, which is part of why scaled errors like MASE became popular.

Check yourself

1. Errors on four predictions: +2, −2, +2, −6. What are MAE and RMSE?
MAE = (2 + 2 + 2 + 6) ÷ 4 = 3. MSE = (4 + 4 + 4 + 36) ÷ 4 = 12, RMSE = √12 ≈ 3.46. RMSE > MAE whenever the misses are uneven; the gap grows with the one big −6. (The plain mean of the errors is −1: signed errors cancel, which is why nobody uses it as a metric.)
2. Your model's R² on the test set is −0.2. What does that mean?
R² = 1 − SSE ÷ SST. If your squared errors exceed those of the mean-guesser, the ratio is above 1 and R² is negative. On training data least squares can't go below 0, but on new data anything goes: it's a sign of overfitting, leakage in reverse, or a broken pipeline.
3. A warehouse loses $9 of profit when an item is out of stock and $1 per unit of excess stock. What should your demand model predict?
τ = cost of running short ÷ (short + excess) = 9 ÷ (9 + 1) = 0.9. Stocking the 90th percentile means running out on only about 1 day in 10, which is the cheapest balance given these prices. MSE and MAE assume both mistakes cost the same.
📝 TL;DR
  • MAE = typical miss (robust, gives the median); RMSE = typical miss with big ones counted extra (gives the mean).
  • R² compares with always guessing the mean: 0 = no better, negative = worse. Don't compare it across datasets.
  • MAPE explodes near zero and rewards forecasting low; prefer MASE/sMAPE or MAE in units.
  • Different costs for over and under? Predict a quantile: τ = costunder ÷ (costunder + costover), scored by pinball loss.
  • Always report a baseline next to your metric. Next: the same game for classification (5.3).
