Phase 5 · Lesson 4

ROC & precision–recall curves

Every threshold gives a different confusion matrix. Sweep them all and you get a curve that shows the whole model at once, and one number, the area under it, that means something surprisingly concrete.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Recall (true-positive rate, TPR) = caught ÷ all positives; false-positive rate (FPR) = false alarms ÷ all negatives = 1 − specificity (Lesson 5.3).
  • Precision = real positives ÷ all flagged, and it falls when positives get rarer (Lesson 5.3).
  • The threshold turns a score into yes/no; lowering it raises recall and false alarms together (Lesson 5.3).
🎯 Hook: from Pearl Harbor radar screens to medical AI

After the attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, the US military studied how well radar operators could tell enemy aircraft from noise on their screens. An eager operator raised more alarms and caught more planes, but also cried wolf at flocks of birds. To compare operators fairly, regardless of how eager each one was, they plotted hits against false alarms at every level of eagerness: the receiver operating characteristic, the ROC curve.

Seventy-five years later, Google's 2016 diabetic-eye-disease model was summarised the same way: an area under the ROC curve of 0.991. But here's a puzzle you'll solve in this lesson: two fraud models with the very same ROC curve can be excellent or almost useless, depending on one thing that the ROC curve never shows.

🔮 Predict first

A model has a ROC AUC of 0.86. You pick one positive case and one negative case at random. What's the chance the model gives the positive the higher score?

86%. That's the cleanest meaning of ROC AUC: the probability that a random positive is ranked above a random negative. 0.5 is coin-flipping, 1.0 is a perfect ranking. You'll check it by drawing random pairs in Playground 2.

Plot TPR against FPR for every threshold and you have the ROC curve; plot precision against recall and you have the PR curve. Each point is one threshold; the curve is the whole menu of trade-offs the model offers.

💡 Big idea A curve shows a model at every threshold at once, so you can judge the model before choosing where to operate it. ROC (TPR vs FPR) measures how well the model ranks positives above negatives and ignores how rare positives are. PR (precision vs recall) shows what you'd actually experience: how many of your alarms are real. When positives are rare, PR is the honest one.
🧠 Analogy: lining up job candidates

A recruiter ranks 200 applicants from most to least promising. ROC asks: “how often does a good candidate end up above a weak one?”, a property of the ranking. PR asks: “if I interview my top 20, how many will be good?”. If 100 of the 200 are good, the top 20 will be nearly all good. If only 2 are good, even a great ranking fills your interview day with weak candidates. Same ranking skill, very different day.

🤔 Why this? Why ROC and PR curves?
  1. The problem before. Precision, recall and F1 describe a model at one threshold. Compare two models at 0.5 and you may be comparing one at a sensible operating point with another at a silly one; the threshold choice is mixed up with the model's skill.
  2. What people tried. Reporting several thresholds in a table (tedious, and which ones?), or accuracy at the “best” threshold (still one point, and chosen on the test set).
  3. Why this works. Sweep every threshold and plot the trade-off: the curve separates how good the ranking is from where you'll operate. Its area summarises the ranking in one number with a plain meaning: ROC AUC = P(random positive scores above random negative). AUC 0.86 → the positive wins 86 of 100 random pairs.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. ROC uses FPR = FP ÷ all negatives; with 99% negatives, even thousands of false alarms make a tiny FPR, so ROC looks great while precision is terrible (here, the same curve gives AP 0.85 or 0.12). And no curve tells you the costs: you still pick a threshold (Lesson 5.3).
  5. What came next. PR curves and average precision for rare positives, partial AUC and “recall at FPR ≤ 1%” for operating constraints, and calibration (Lesson 5.6) when the scores must be probabilities, which AUC never checks.

What AUC really means: a ranking contest

ROC AUC = P( score(random positive) > score(random negative) ) “Pick one positive and one negative at random: AUC is how often the model ranks them the right way round (ties count half).”

That's why AUC doesn't care about the threshold, or even whether the scores are probabilities: multiply every score by 10, or square it, and the ranking, and so the AUC, stays the same. It's also why AUC is the same as the Mann–Whitney U statistic from classical statistics, divided by (number of positives × number of negatives).

✏️ Worked example: AUC by counting pairs

Three positives scored 0.9, 0.6, 0.4; three negatives scored 0.7, 0.3, 0.2. There are 3 × 3 = 9 positive–negative pairs:

  • 0.9 beats 0.7, 0.3, 0.2 → 3 wins. 0.6 beats 0.3, 0.2 but loses to 0.7 → 2 wins. 0.4 beats 0.3, 0.2 → 2 wins.
  • AUC = (3 + 2 + 2) ÷ 9 = 7 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.78. Draw the ROC curve through these six scores and the area under it is the same 0.78.
  • The one mistake (negative 0.7 above positives 0.6 and 0.4) costs 2 of 9 pairs. Fix it by scoring that negative 0.35 and AUC becomes 1.0, even though no score is anywhere near 0 or 1.

Why PR is more honest when positives are rare

🔮 Predict first

You keep the model fixed (ROC AUC 0.86) and make positives rarer: from 50% of cases to 1%. What happens to the ROC curve and to the PR curve?

ROC AUC stays at 0.86: TPR is computed only among positives and FPR only among negatives, so the class ratio cancels out. Average precision falls from 0.85 to 0.12: at recall 50%, precision drops from 88% to 8%. Same ranking skill; many more negatives to make false alarms from. Slide it below.

This is the point of Saito and Rehmsmeier's widely cited 2015 paper: on imbalanced data the ROC plot “can be deceptive”, because people read a low FPR as “few false alarms”, while the PR plot shows the fraction of true positives among your positive predictions, which is what you'll live with. A rule of thumb: report ROC AUC for ranking quality and comparability; use PR / average precision when positives are rare and false alarms cost something.

Average precision (AP)

Average precision summarises the PR curve: walk down the ranked list, and every time you hit a positive, note the precision so far; average those numbers. A perfect ranking scores 1.0; a random one scores about the share of positives (so AP 0.12 at 1% positives is still twelve times better than random). scikit-learn's average_precision_score computes exactly this step-wise sum, Σ (Rn − Rn−1) Pn; it's preferred over the trapezoid area under the PR curve, which can be overly optimistic.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): roc_curve, roc_auc_score, precision_recall_curve, average_precision_score, and the plotting helpers RocCurveDisplay / PrecisionRecallDisplay. Pass scores or probabilities, never hard 0/1 predictions.

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import (average_precision_score, precision_recall_curve,
                             roc_auc_score, roc_curve, RocCurveDisplay, PrecisionRecallDisplay)
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

for pos in [0.5, 0.01]:                              # balanced vs 1% positives
    X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20000, weights=[1 - pos], class_sep=0.7,
                               flip_y=0, random_state=0)
    X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, random_state=0)
    s = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
    print(f"positives {pos:.0%}: ROC AUC {roc_auc_score(y_te, s):.3f}  AP {average_precision_score(y_te, s):.3f}")

fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y_te, s)                   # one (FPR, TPR) point per distinct threshold
prec, rec, thr_pr = precision_recall_curve(y_te, s)
print("recall at FPR ≤ 1%:", tpr[fpr <= 0.01].max())    # an operating-point metric
RocCurveDisplay.from_predictions(y_te, s)            # or .from_estimator(model, X_te, y_te)
PrecisionRecallDisplay.from_predictions(y_te, s)

For multiclass, roc_auc_score(y, P, multi_class="ovr") averages one-vs-rest AUCs. As a CV scorer use scoring="roc_auc" or "average_precision". Here the balanced run gives ROC AUC ≈ 0.91 and AP ≈ 0.92; the 1% run still has ROC AUC above 0.8 but AP around 0.43: that gap is this lesson.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Report ROC AUC and average precision on cross-validation, plus the precision and recall at the threshold you'll ship. Plot the PR curve once: it usually ends the argument about whether the model is “good enough”.

At scale

Teams pick an operating constraint (“block at most 0.1% of good payments”, “review 500 cases a day”) and track recall at that point, often per segment. Curves are recomputed on fresh labelled data each week, since prevalence drifts and PR moves with it (Lesson 9.4).

🎤 Interview angle

“What does an AUC of 0.9 mean, and when would you not trust it?” A strong answer:

  • ROC AUC = probability that a random positive scores above a random negative; 0.5 = random, 1 = perfect ranking. Threshold-free.
  • It's insensitive to the class ratio, which is a strength for comparison and a weakness under heavy imbalance: a tiny FPR can still mean most alarms are false.
  • Under imbalance, report the PR curve / average precision and precision at the operating point.
  • AUC says nothing about calibration (scores as probabilities, Lesson 5.6) or costs; you still choose a threshold.

🤯 Fun fact · a curve named after radio sets

“Receiver operating characteristic” describes the radar receiver and its operator: how the pair behaved at different sensitivity settings. The idea moved into psychology as signal detection theory in the 1950s and 60s, then into medicine and finally machine learning, which is why a curve for spam filters is still named after World War II radio equipment.

Check yourself

1. Positives scored 0.8 and 0.3; negatives scored 0.5 and 0.1. What is the ROC AUC?
4 pairs: 0.8 beats 0.5 and 0.1 (2 wins); 0.3 beats 0.1 but loses to 0.5 (1 win). AUC = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75.
2. A defect detector has ROC AUC 0.97 on a factory line where 1 part in 2,000 is defective. Inspectors complain nearly every alarm is a false one. What's going on?
AUC ignores the class ratio. Say recall is 90% at FPR 1%: per 2,000,000 parts that's 900 defects caught and about 20,000 false alarms, a precision of about 4%. The PR curve and average precision show it immediately.
3. You multiply all your model's scores by 0.5 (so every score is half as big). What happens to the ROC AUC and to the predictions at threshold 0.5?
AUC only depends on the order of the scores, which doesn't change. The threshold is an absolute number, so the predictions do change. That's also why AUC can't tell you whether the scores are calibrated probabilities (Lesson 5.6).
📝 TL;DR
  • Sweep the threshold: ROC plots TPR vs FPR, PR plots precision vs recall. Each point is one confusion matrix.
  • ROC AUC = probability a random positive outranks a random negative; 0.5 random, 1 perfect. It ignores class ratio, thresholds and calibration.
  • With rare positives, the same ROC can hide terrible precision (here AP 0.85 → 0.12 at AUC 0.86). Use PR / average precision.
  • AP ≈ prevalence for a random model, 1 for a perfect one; report it alongside precision and recall at your operating point.
