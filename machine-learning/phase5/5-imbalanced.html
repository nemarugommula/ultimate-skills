Phase 5 · Lesson 5

Imbalanced data: class weights, SMOTE, threshold moving

When 1 row in 30 is the one you care about, models seem to ignore it. The classic fixes reshape the training data. The modern view is simpler: a good model plus the right threshold usually does just as well, and keeps its probabilities honest.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • With rare positives, accuracy lies; use precision, recall, F1 and the PR curve (Lessons 5.3–5.4).
  • The threshold on a score trades recall for precision, and the best one depends on costs (Lesson 5.3).
  • Every preprocessing step must be fitted inside the training folds, or CV leaks (Lesson 5.1).
🎯 Hook: to SMOTE, or not to SMOTE?

For twenty years, the standard advice for imbalanced data was “balance it first”: duplicate the rare rows, or invent synthetic ones with SMOTE (the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling TEchnique, 2002). Thousands of tutorials do it by reflex.

In 2022, Yotam Elor (Amazon) and Hadar Averbuch-Elor (Cornell) tested that advice on 73 imbalanced datasets, including strong gradient-boosting models (XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM). Their findings: balancing helped weak classifiers, but for the strong ones (XGBoost, CatBoost) it did not improve the ranking quality (AUC). And once the decision threshold was tuned, balancing and threshold tuning gave similar F1, so they recommend the threshold, for “simplicity and lower compute cost”.

So what is imbalance really doing to a model, and when do the fixes help? Let's find out.

🔮 Predict first

You train logistic regression on data with 7% positives, then train it again after oversampling the positives to 50%. What happens to its ROC AUC (how well it ranks positives above negatives)?

It barely changes: 0.912 → 0.912 on this lesson's data. Rebalancing mostly shifts the scores up for everyone, which moves the effective threshold, but the ordering of who's more suspicious than whom hardly moves. The real side effect is on the probabilities: the average predicted risk rises from 7% to 28%. Playground 2 shows both.

💡 Big idea Imbalance mostly causes a threshold problem, not a learning problem: the model ranks fine, but 0.5 is the wrong place to cut when positives are rare. Class weights and resampling move the cut by distorting the training data (and the probabilities); threshold moving moves it directly. Resample only when the model genuinely can't learn the rare class, and then only inside the training folds.
🧠 Analogy: the airport security line

Out of 10,000 bags, 3 hold something dangerous. A screener who opens a bag only when they're more than 50% sure it's dangerous will almost never open one. You could retrain them by showing them a training set with half dangerous bags (resampling): they'd become jumpy everywhere, and their “I'm 60% sure” would no longer mean 60%. Or you could keep their judgement as it is and simply tell them: “open any bag you rate above 5%” (threshold moving).

🤔 Why this? Why class weights, SMOTE or threshold moving?
  1. The problem before. A model minimising average loss on 97% negatives can score well by leaning “negative”: at threshold 0.5 our plain model catches 17% of positives. Weak learners (one decision tree, k-NN, naive Bayes) can also genuinely underfit a rare class with only a few dozen examples.
  2. What people tried. Undersampling the majority (throws data away), random oversampling (copies rows; trees can memorise them), and SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002): new minority points on the line segment between a minority point and one of its k nearest minority neighbours. Class weights do the same as oversampling without copying: each positive's loss counts Nneg ÷ Npos times more.
  3. Why this works. All of them make positives count more, which pushes scores up and the boundary toward the negatives. With weights of 400 ÷ 30 ≈ 13, a positive's mistake costs 13 negatives' worth, so recall jumps from 17% to 87%. Threshold moving gets the same effect after training: flag at 0.27 instead of 0.5.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Resampling and weights miscalibrate the probabilities (average predicted risk 27% when the truth is 7%; Brier score 0.048 → 0.123). Resampling before splitting leaks copies into the test folds (Playground 3: CV F1 0.97 vs 0.25 for real). SMOTE invents points in nonsense places when the minority is noisy or the features are categorical.
  5. What came next. The 2022 re-evaluation above, and a 2022 simulation study on logistic regression concluding that imbalance corrections “led to poor calibration” without improving AUC. Current practice: strong model, tune the threshold (TunedThresholdClassifierCV), report PR metrics, calibrate if needed (Lesson 5.6).

SMOTE: how synthetic points are made

SMOTE picks a minority point, finds its k nearest minority neighbours (k = 5 by default), picks one of them at random, and places a new point at a random spot on the segment between the two. Repeat until you have as many as you want. It's “oversampling with jitter”: the new points fill in the space between real ones instead of stacking copies.

🔮 Predict first

One minority point is mislabelled and sits deep inside the majority class. You run SMOTE with k = 5. What happens around that point?

It becomes a seed of nonsense: every time it's picked, its neighbours are far away, so the segments cross majority territory and new “positives” land among the negatives. Turn on the noisy point below and watch. Variants like Borderline-SMOTE and SMOTE + Tomek links try to clean this up.

Notice that SMOTE only ever looks at the minority class: it has no idea where the majority lives. That's its main weakness. It also needs numeric features where “halfway between” makes sense (halfway between “Paris” and “Tokyo” is meaningless; SMOTE-NC and SMOTEN exist for categorical columns), and it gets less useful in high dimensions where nearest neighbours are far apart (Lesson 3.1).

✏️ Worked example: what class weights do to the math

Training set: 400 negatives, 30 positives. class_weight="balanced" gives class c the weight n ÷ (2 × nc):

  • negatives: 430 ÷ (2 × 400) = 0.54; positives: 430 ÷ (2 × 30) = 7.2. A positive counts about 13× as much as a negative.
  • That's the same as duplicating each positive 13 times, without the copies (and without the leak risk).
  • For logistic regression, it shifts the intercept by about log 13 ≈ 2.6 in log-odds, so every predicted probability rises. A row with true risk 7% gets scored roughly 50%, which is precisely why the “balanced” model flags it at 0.5.

Threshold moving does the opposite bookkeeping: keep the honest 7%, and flag it because 7% is above your tuned threshold.

Break it: resampling before you split

🔮 Predict first

You randomly oversample the positives in the whole dataset, then run 5-fold CV with a 1-nearest-neighbour model. What does CV say about F1?

Near perfect: 0.97, while the honest number is 0.25. The copies of each positive land in different folds, so every test positive has an identical twin in the training folds, and 1-NN finds it at distance 0. Run it below.

The rule is absolute: resample only the training part, never the validation or test part, and never before splitting. The imbalanced-learn library's Pipeline enforces it: its samplers run during fit and are skipped during predict and scoring, so cross_validate stays honest.

So when do the fixes help?

SituationTry firstWhy
Strong model (gradient boosting, well-tuned logistic regression), probabilities matterNothing; tune the threshold; report PR/APRanking is already good; keeps calibration

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): class_weight="balanced" on most classifiers ("balanced_subsample" for forests), sample_weight= in fit, and TunedThresholdClassifierCV. imbalanced-learn (0.14 as of late 2026): SMOTE, RandomOverSampler, RandomUnderSampler, BalancedRandomForestClassifier, and a leak-proof Pipeline.

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, TunedThresholdClassifierCV, cross_validate

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=6000, weights=[0.97], class_sep=0.8, flip_y=0, random_state=0)  # 3% positives
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
scoring = ["f1", "roc_auc", "average_precision", "neg_brier_score"]

candidates = {
    "plain": LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
    "class weights": LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, class_weight="balanced"),
    "threshold tuned": TunedThresholdClassifierCV(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), scoring="f1"),
    "boosted trees + tuned threshold": TunedThresholdClassifierCV(HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0), scoring="f1"),
}
for name, model in candidates.items():                  # about a minute: the tuned models run an inner CV
    r = cross_validate(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring=scoring)
    print(name, {s: round(r["test_" + s].mean(), 3) for s in scoring})

# If you do resample: imbalanced-learn's Pipeline runs SMOTE inside each training fold only
from imblearn.over_sampling import SMOTE
from imblearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
smote_lr = make_pipeline(SMOTE(k_neighbors=5, random_state=0), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
r = cross_validate(smote_lr, X, y, cv=cv, scoring=scoring)       # honest: test folds are never resampled

On this run (scikit-learn 1.9), plain logistic regression scored F1 0.14 at 0.5; class weights 0.22 but a Brier score five times worse (0.124 vs 0.025); the tuned threshold 0.30 with the Brier score untouched; and boosted trees with a tuned threshold 0.43. ROC AUC and AP were identical for the three logistic models: they rank the same way. pip install imbalanced-learn; its samplers must go in its Pipeline, not scikit-learn's.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Train a gradient-boosted model on the data as it is, evaluate with average precision and the PR curve, and choose the threshold from costs or a review-capacity limit (“we can check 200 alerts a day”). Only reach for SMOTE if a weak model is a hard requirement.

At scale

With billions of negatives (ads, payments), teams undersample negatives to make training affordable and then correct the probabilities back (by the sampling rate) because bids and risk scores need true probabilities. Thresholds are set per segment and re-tuned as base rates drift.

🎤 Interview angle

“You have 1% positives. How do you handle the imbalance?” A strong answer:

  • Starts with evaluation: stratified CV, PR curve / average precision, precision and recall at an operating point, not accuracy.
  • Says imbalance is often a threshold problem: train a strong model, tune the threshold on validation data from costs.
  • Knows the tools (class weights, over/undersampling, SMOTE) and their costs: miscalibration, memorising duplicates, noisy synthetic points.
  • Insists resampling happens only inside training folds (imbalanced-learn Pipeline), never before the split.
  • Cites the evidence: balancing helps weak learners more than strong boosted trees (Elor & Averbuch-Elor, 2022).
🏭 In the real world
To SMOTE, or not to SMOTE? 🧪 Research · 2022 Elor & Averbuch-Elor (arXiv)

How: 73 imbalanced datasets; weak learners (MLP, SVM, tree, AdaBoost) and strong ones (XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM); several oversamplers; fixed vs tuned thresholds.

Why this method: balancing helped weak classifiers but not strong ones in AUC; with a tuned threshold, balancing and threshold tuning were similar, and SMOTE-like methods weren't significantly better than random oversampling.

The harm of imbalance corrections 🧪 Research · 2022 van den Goorbergh et al.: logistic-regression risk models (arXiv)

How: simulations and an ovarian-cancer case study with random undersampling, oversampling and SMOTE.

Why this method: every correction gave poorly calibrated risks (overestimating the minority class) without better AUC; shifting the threshold gave similar sensitivity and specificity.

The original SMOTE 📜 History · 2002 Chawla et al.: SMOTE (Journal of AI Research)

How: synthetic minority points along segments to k nearest minority neighbours, combined with undersampling the majority; tested with C4.5, Ripper and naive Bayes.

Why this method: the weak learners of the time improved in ROC terms, and it became the default fix for two decades.

Resampling done safely 🧰 Open source imbalanced-learn: Common pitfalls and recommended practices

How: samplers with fit_resample, used inside a Pipeline so they only touch training data.

Why this method: the docs explain the exact leak in Playground 3: resampling the whole dataset before splitting gives over-optimistic results.

🤯 Fun fact · the card-fraud dataset was about this

The famous credit-card fraud dataset from Lesson 5.3 (492 frauds in 284,807) was released with a paper titled Calibrating Probability with Undersampling for Unbalanced Classification (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015): it's literally about how undersampling distorts probabilities and how to undo it. The dataset outlived the paper's message.

Check yourself

1. Training data: 9,000 negatives, 1,000 positives. With class_weight="balanced" (weight = n ÷ (2 × nc)), what weight does each positive get, and how many times a negative's?
Positives: 10,000 ÷ (2 × 1,000) = 5.0. Negatives: 10,000 ÷ (2 × 9,000) ≈ 0.56. Ratio 5.0 ÷ 0.56 = 9 = 9,000 ÷ 1,000: as if each positive were copied 9 times.
2. A teammate applies SMOTE to the full dataset, then splits train/test, and reports recall 0.95. What's the problem?
Each synthetic point lies between two real minority points; if one is in train and the synthetic one in test, the model has effectively seen the test point. Also, the test set should keep the real class ratio. Split first, then resample the training part only.
3. A hospital uses your model's probability to tell patients their risk (“you have a 12% risk”). Positives are 3% of cases. Which imbalance strategy fits best?
Resampling and weights inflate predicted risks (here, average risk 27% for a 7% truth), so a “12%” would really mean much less. When the probabilities themselves are the product, keep them honest; handle decisions with a threshold, and check calibration (Lesson 5.6).
📝 TL;DR
  • Imbalance mostly breaks the 0.5 threshold, not the model's ranking: AUC barely moves when you rebalance.
  • Class weights, random oversampling and SMOTE all push scores up (more recall) and miscalibrate probabilities.
  • Threshold moving (tuned by CV, from costs) usually matches them and keeps probabilities honest; resampling helps weak learners more than strong ones.
  • SMOTE interpolates between minority neighbours: noisy points and categorical features break it.
  • Resample only inside training folds (imbalanced-learn Pipeline). Next: making probabilities trustworthy (5.6).
