Phase 5 · Lesson 8

Learning curves & bias–variance in practice

Your model isn't good enough. Should you get more data, a bigger model, more regularisation, or better features? Two simple plots answer that question before you waste a week, and they're the most useful diagnostic in this course.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Overfitting: great on training data, worse on new data; underfitting: poor on both (Lesson 0.6, Math 5.6).
  • Validation scores come from cross-validation or a held-out set (Lesson 5.1); RMSE measures regression error (Lesson 5.2).
  • Polynomial features let a linear model bend; a higher degree means a more flexible model (Lesson 2.5).
🎯 Hook: a billion words of training data

In 2001, Michele Banko and Eric Brill at Microsoft Research took a humble task, choosing the right word from confusable sets like {then, than} or {principle, principal}, and asked what would happen with far more training text than anyone had used. They trained four standard learners on up to one billion words.

Their learning curves kept climbing: accuracy grew roughly log-linearly even out to one billion words, and none of the learners was “close to asymptoting”. They suggested the field might reconsider spending time and money on algorithms versus on more data.

But more data doesn't always help: for some models, the curve is already flat. How do you know which situation you're in? You plot the curve.

🔮 Predict first

A model scores 99.9% on training data and 82% on validation. You can double the training data or try a fancier model. Which is more likely to help?

More data, or a simpler/more regularised model. A huge train–validation gap is high variance: the model memorises its sample. A bigger model memorises even better. On scikit-learn's digits data (the Tools box below), an SVM's validation accuracy climbs from 82% to 97% as the training set grows from 143 to 1,437 rows, with training accuracy stuck near 99.9%. That's a learning curve; you'll draw your own below.

💡 Big idea Plot training error and validation error together. Both high and close together → high bias: make the model more flexible or add features; more data won't help. Training low, validation much higher → high variance: more data, more regularisation, a simpler model. Both low and close → done. Validation suspiciously good, or better than training → check for leakage. The curves tell you what to do next.
🧠 Analogy: the student and the exam

A student who scores 50% on both homework and the exam hasn't understood the material (bias): more practice questions of the same kind won't fix it; a better method of study might. A student who aces every homework problem but scores 60% on the exam memorised the homework answers (variance): more and different practice problems are exactly what helps. A student who scores higher on the exam than on homework probably saw the exam in advance (leakage).

🤔 Why this? Why learning and validation curves?
  1. The problem before. One validation score (“82%”) says the model isn't good enough but not why. Teams guess: collect more data (weeks), try a bigger model (days), add features. A wrong guess wastes the most expensive resource you have: time.
  2. What people tried. Trial and error on the final metric. Or theory: the bias–variance decomposition says expected test error = bias² + variance + noise, but you can't compute bias and variance directly on real data, because you never see the true function.
  3. Why this works. The curves are measurable stand-ins. The training error approximates what the model can fit; the gap to validation error approximates variance; where both flatten tells you the floor. In Playground 1, degree 3 reaches validation RMSE 0.31 against a noise floor of 0.30, while degree 12 fits training data to 0.25 and validation at 0.62.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. A learning curve means training many models (one per size per fold), so use a sample of the data for big models. Curves are noisy with small data (plot the spread across folds). They can't detect a validation set that leaks or doesn't look like production; that needs the splits of Lesson 5.1.
  5. What came next. The same reading carries over to deep learning (training-loss vs validation-loss curves, early stopping), and to “scaling laws” that predict how error falls with data and model size. You'll meet those in the sibling Deep Learning course.

Validation curve: turn the complexity knob

expected test error = bias² + variance + noise “How far the average model is from the truth, plus how much a model jumps around from one training sample to another, plus the randomness no model can predict.”

The derivation (for squared error) is in Math 5.6. In the playground we know the true curve, so we can measure all three by retraining on 60 fresh training sets.

Learning curve: add training data

🔮 Predict first

A straight line (degree 1) is fitted to this curvy data. You give it 10× more training points. What happens to its validation error?

Almost nothing: from about 0.43 at 40 rows to 0.42 at 320. Training and validation error meet at a level well above the noise floor (0.30): the signature of high bias. More data is wasted on it; a more flexible model or better features are not. The degree-12 model is the opposite: 2.03 at 10 rows, 0.30 at 320.

✏️ Worked example: reading a learning curve by hand

Validation error at 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 rows: 0.30, 0.26, 0.23. Training error: 0.05, 0.07, 0.09. The business needs 0.15.

  • The gap is large (0.23 − 0.09 = 0.14) and still shrinking: variance is the main problem.
  • Each doubling cut validation error by about 0.035 (0.04, then 0.03). If that continues, 8,000 rows ≈ 0.20, 16,000 ≈ 0.18, 32,000 ≈ 0.16. Getting to 0.15 by data alone needs something like 30,000+ rows.
  • Cheaper first: regularise (smaller trees, larger min_samples_leaf, stronger λ) or reduce features, then re-plot. If the training error is already 0.09, the model can't get below that without more capacity either, so watch both.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): validation_curve and learning_curve return train and validation scores per fold; ValidationCurveDisplay and LearningCurveDisplay plot them with ±1 std bands. Pass scoring= to use any metric from Lessons 5.2–5.4.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import LearningCurveDisplay, ValidationCurveDisplay, learning_curve, validation_curve
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)

# Validation curve: one knob from simple to complex (the table at the top of this lesson)
depths = [1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16]
tr, va = validation_curve(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), X, y, param_name="max_depth",
                          param_range=depths, cv=5)
for d, a, b in zip(depths, tr.mean(1), va.mean(1)):
    print(f"depth {d:2d}: train {a:.3f}  validation {b:.3f}  gap {a - b:.3f}")

# Learning curve: the same model with more and more training rows
sizes, tr, va = learning_curve(SVC(gamma=0.001), X, y, train_sizes=np.linspace(0.1, 1.0, 5), cv=5)
for n, a, b in zip(sizes, tr.mean(1), va.mean(1)):
    print(f"n={n:4d}: train {a:.3f}  validation {b:.3f}")       # 143 rows: 0.994 / 0.818 … 1,437: 0.999 / 0.972

# One-liners that draw the plots (with ±1 std bands)
LearningCurveDisplay.from_estimator(SVC(gamma=0.001), X, y, cv=5)
ValidationCurveDisplay.from_estimator(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), X, y,
                                      param_name="max_depth", param_range=depths, cv=5)

For big data, compute learning curves on a sample (say up to 10% of rows) with n_jobs=-1. For gradient boosting, the per-round training and validation loss (evals_result() in XGBoost/LightGBM, validation_fraction + n_iter_ in scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting) is a validation curve over the number of trees, and early stopping picks its minimum automatically.

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Before asking for budget to label more data, plot a learning curve on what you have: if validation error is flat over the last doublings, more labels won't help; spend the money on features. It takes an afternoon and can save a quarter.

At scale

Large teams fit curves of error vs data size and model size to forecast what the next 10× of data or compute will buy, and decide budgets from them. The scaling-law papers behind modern language models are this lesson's learning curves, drawn on log axes over many orders of magnitude.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your model's validation accuracy is disappointing. What do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Compares training and validation scores first, then plots learning and validation curves instead of guessing.
  • Maps patterns to fixes: high bias → more capacity or features; high variance → more data, regularisation, simpler model; both at the floor → new information.
  • Explains bias² + variance + noise in words and that training error alone always prefers the most complex model.
  • Treats “validation better than training” or “too good to be true” as a leakage alarm.
  • Mentions cost: curves on a sample; early stopping as an automatic validation curve for boosting and neural nets.

🤯 Fun fact · the curve that bends back down

Classical theory says validation error is U-shaped in model size. In modern deep learning, people observed that error can go up near the point where the model just barely fits the training data, and then come down again as models grow far bigger: “double descent” (named by Belkin and colleagues in 2019). The bias–variance trade-off isn't wrong, but “bigger always overfits” turned out to be too simple. The sibling Deep Learning course picks up that story.

Check yourself

1. Training RMSE 4.1, validation RMSE 4.3, and both have been flat for the last three doublings of data. The business needs 2.5. What next?
A small gap with both errors flat and far above the target is high bias: the model can't express what it needs. More data won't move a flat curve, and regularisation would make bias worse. Try a more flexible model or new features, then re-plot.
2. Your validation accuracy (96%) is higher than your training accuracy (91%) on a hard medical task, and much higher than published work. What's the first thing to check?
Validation beating training on a hard task, by a lot, is a classic leakage alarm (strong regularisation or dropout can make it happen honestly, but only by small amounts). Re-split by patient, refit preprocessing inside folds, and look for features that wouldn't exist at prediction time (Lesson 5.1).
3. A model's expected test MSE is 0.40: bias² 0.05, variance 0.25, noise 0.10. If bagging cuts the variance by 60%, what's the new expected MSE?
New variance = 0.25 × 0.4 = 0.10. MSE = 0.05 + 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.25. The noise (0.10) is a floor no model can remove, and bagging doesn't touch the bias.
📝 TL;DR
  • Expected test error = bias² + variance + noise. Training error alone always favours the most complex model.
  • Validation curve (error vs a knob) finds the complexity sweet spot; learning curve (error vs data size) tells you whether more data will help.
  • Both high and close → bias: more capacity, better features. Big gap → variance: more data, regularisation, simpler model.
  • Validation better than training, or too good to be true → suspect leakage.
