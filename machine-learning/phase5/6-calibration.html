Phase 5 · Lesson 6

Calibration: when 70% should mean 70%

A model can rank cases perfectly and still lie about probabilities. If you price loans, bid on ads or tell a patient their risk, the number itself has to be honest. Calibration checks it and fixes it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • ROC AUC only measures ranking: it doesn't change if you squash or stretch the scores (Lesson 5.4).
  • Log loss and the Brier score (mean squared error of probabilities) grade the probabilities themselves (Lesson 5.3).
  • Resampling and class weights inflate predicted probabilities (Lesson 5.5).
🎯 Hook: better networks, worse probabilities

In 2017 a Cornell team compared a 1998-style 5-layer LeNet with a modern 110-layer ResNet on the CIFAR-100 image dataset. The ResNet was far more accurate: 30.6% error against LeNet's 44.9%.

But LeNet's confidence matched its accuracy, while the ResNet's average confidence was substantially higher than its accuracy: when it said “90% sure”, it was right much less often. Their title says it plainly: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks. The fix they recommended was a single number, a “temperature”, fitted after training.

This isn't just a deep-learning problem. Naive Bayes, boosted trees and support vector machines all distort probabilities, each in its own direction. How do you see it, and how do you fix it?

🔮 Predict first

A weather forecaster said “70% chance of rain” on 100 days. If the forecaster is well calibrated, on how many of those days did it rain?

About 70. That's what calibration means: among all the times you say p, the event happens about p of the time. Not more, not less. The reliability diagram below checks exactly this, bucket by bucket.

Numbers first: what naive Bayes really means

We trained scikit-learn's Gaussian naive Bayes on a synthetic dataset with 20 correlated features (code in the Tools box), then grouped its test predictions into 10 buckets and counted how often the positive class really happened in each:

Naive Bayes said (bucket average)4%14%35%55%75%85%99%
Really positive in that bucket23%34%51%60%57%61%95%

When it says 4%, the truth is 23%; when it says 85%, the truth is 61%. Its probabilities are pushed toward 0 and 1: overconfident. Yet its ROC AUC is a respectable 0.82, so it ranks reasonably. Recalibrating it (isotonic regression) cut the Brier score from 0.185 to 0.158 and the log loss from 0.68 to 0.48.

💡 Big idea A model is calibrated if, among all cases where it predicts p, the event happens a fraction p of the time. Ranking (AUC) and calibration are different skills: you can have one without the other. Check calibration with a reliability diagram; fix it by fitting a small monotone map from scores to probabilities on held-out data (Platt scaling or isotonic regression). The ranking stays the same; the numbers become honest.
🧠 Analogy: the friend who's “100% sure”

You have a friend who says “I'm 100% sure” about everything and is right two times in three. Their opinions are still useful: when they're “100% sure” they're more often right than when they're “pretty sure”. You've simply learned to translate: “100% sure” from them means about 67%. Calibration is building that translation table for a model.

🤔 Why this? Why calibrate probabilities?
  1. The problem before. Many decisions use the probability, not just the ranking: expected cost (p × loss), prices and bids, “your risk is 12%”, combining models. A model trained for accuracy or ranking can output scores that sort well but aren't probabilities: naive Bayes says 85% for things that happen 61% of the time.
  2. What people tried. Trust predict_proba because it sums to 1 (it can still be wrong). Use only well-calibrated model families like logistic regression (gives up the accuracy of boosting or forests). Ignore it and tune a threshold (fine for yes/no decisions, useless when the number itself is the product).
  3. Why this works. Learn the translation on data the model didn't train on. Platt scaling (Platt, 1999): fit a tiny logistic regression on the model's score, p = σ(a·score + b): two numbers. Isotonic regression: fit the best non-decreasing step function. Both are monotone, so the ranking is preserved (AUC unchanged, except isotonic ties). In Playground 2, Platt fixes a sigmoid-shaped distortion with only 100 calibration rows.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. You need held-out calibration data (or cross-validation). Isotonic overfits with few rows (scikit-learn's guide: it matches or beats sigmoid with more than about 1,000). Platt can't fix shapes that aren't sigmoid-like. Calibration also drifts: fixed today, wrong after the base rate changes (Lesson 9.4).
  5. What came next. Temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017), one parameter for neural networks, now method="temperature" in scikit-learn's CalibratedClassifierCV; beta calibration; and conformal prediction for honest prediction sets and intervals (Lesson 9.5).

The reliability diagram

Sort predictions into buckets (0–10%, 10–20%, …). For each bucket, plot the average prediction (x) against the fraction that really happened (y). A calibrated model lies on the diagonal. Below the diagonal = overconfident on the high side (says 80%, happens 60%); an S-shape the other way = underconfident (says 60%, happens 80%).

Two summary numbers: the Brier score, mean (p − y)², which rewards both calibration and sharpness (lower is better; Glenn Brier proposed it for weather forecasts in 1950), and the expected calibration error (ECE), the average gap between the bucket points and the diagonal, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bucket.

🔮 Predict first

You make a model more and more overconfident (every probability pushed toward 0 or 1, keeping their order). What happens to its ROC AUC?

It doesn't change: 0.811 at every setting below, because pushing probabilities toward the ends keeps their order. Brier score and log loss, which grade the numbers, get worse; ECE grows. That's why AUC alone can't tell you whether probabilities are usable.

Which models do what? From Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana's classic 2005 study of ten model families: naive Bayes pushes probabilities toward 0 and 1 (its independence assumption double-counts correlated evidence). Boosted trees, boosted stumps and SVMs push them away from 0 and 1, giving a characteristic sigmoid-shaped reliability curve. Neural nets and bagged trees of the time had little or no bias, and random forests were among the best before calibration, though averaging many trees means they rarely reach 0 or 1. Logistic regression is usually close to calibrated because it directly minimises log loss. Modern deep networks swing back to overconfident (the hook). After calibration, boosted trees, random forests and SVMs gave the best probabilities in that study.

Fixing it: Platt scaling vs isotonic regression

✏️ Worked example: Platt scaling by hand

An SVM outputs a raw score s (any real number). Platt fits p = σ(a·s + b) on held-out data. Suppose the fit gives a = 1.5, b = −0.5:

  • s = 0 → σ(−0.5) = 0.38; s = 1 → σ(1.0) = 0.73; s = 2 → σ(2.5) = 0.92.
  • The order is kept (higher s, higher p), so AUC is unchanged. Only two numbers were learned, so 100 to 200 held-out rows are often enough.
  • Isotonic regression would instead learn a staircase: “scores in this range → 0.41, the next range → 0.47, …”, as many steps as the data supports.

The pattern to remember: Platt is a 2-parameter curve, steady with little data, and perfect when the distortion is sigmoid-shaped, but stuck when it isn't. Isotonic can fix any monotone distortion but needs data: with 30 rows its staircase is jagged and it does worse than doing nothing; with a few thousand it wins on the irregular shape. scikit-learn's advice matches: isotonic when you have more than about 1,000 calibration rows.

⚠️ Calibrate on data the model never saw

Fit the calibrator on the training rows and it learns the model's (overfit) training confidence: useless. Either let CalibratedClassifierCV(cv=5) do cross-fitting for you, or calibrate an already-trained model on a separate held-out set by wrapping it in FrozenEstimator (the older cv="prefit" option is gone in scikit-learn 1.9). And calibrate on data with the real class ratio, never on resampled data.

When calibration matters (and when it doesn't)

🔮 Predict first

A lender approves every loan whose predicted default probability is below 1/6 (that's where expected profit turns positive: gain $100 if repaid, lose $500 if not). The model ranks perfectly well but is underconfident: all its probabilities are pulled toward the middle. What happens?

It approves only 61 of 1,000 (the honest model approves 704), and profit falls from $44,000 to $5,500. An absolute rule like “p < 1/6” only works if p means what it says. But if the lender instead approved “the safest 70%”, a pure ranking rule, the distortion wouldn't matter at all. Try both below.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): calibration_curve and CalibrationDisplay for reliability diagrams, brier_score_loss and log_loss to score, and CalibratedClassifierCV with method="sigmoid" (Platt), "isotonic" or, since 1.8, "temperature". Wrap an already-fitted model in sklearn.frozen.FrozenEstimator to calibrate it on held-out data.

from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV, calibration_curve
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.frozen import FrozenEstimator
from sklearn.metrics import brier_score_loss, log_loss, roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20000, n_features=20, n_informative=4, n_redundant=10, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.5, random_state=0)

models = {
    "naive Bayes (raw)": GaussianNB().fit(X_tr, y_tr),
    "+ Platt (sigmoid)": CalibratedClassifierCV(GaussianNB(), method="sigmoid", cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr),
    "+ isotonic": CalibratedClassifierCV(GaussianNB(), method="isotonic", cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr),
}
for name, m in models.items():                     # Brier 0.185 → 0.164 → 0.158; AUC ≈ 0.82 for all
    p = m.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:20s} Brier {brier_score_loss(y_te, p):.3f}  log loss {log_loss(y_te, p):.3f}  AUC {roc_auc_score(y_te, p):.3f}")

frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_te, models["naive Bayes (raw)"].predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1], n_bins=10)

# Already-trained model: freeze it, calibrate on separate held-out rows (the model is not refitted)
X_fit, X_cal, y_fit, y_cal = train_test_split(X_tr, y_tr, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
nb = GaussianNB().fit(X_fit, y_fit)
cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(FrozenEstimator(nb), method="isotonic").fit(X_cal, y_cal)

CalibrationDisplay.from_estimator(model, X_te, y_te, n_bins=10) draws the reliability diagram in one line. With cv=5 (and the default ensemble=True), you get 5 model + calibrator pairs whose probabilities are averaged. Scikit-learn's guide notes calibration usually leaves ROC AUC unchanged, though isotonic can shift it slightly by creating ties (here 0.816 → 0.824).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Plot one reliability diagram on validation data and print the Brier score next to AUC. If you only rank, move on. If anyone will read the number as a probability, wrap the model in CalibratedClassifierCV and plot again.

At scale

Ads, credit and insurance systems multiply predicted probabilities by money, so calibration is monitored like uptime: predicted vs observed rates per segment, every day, with recalibration jobs when they drift. Undersampled training data is corrected back to the real base rate before any bid is placed.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your model has AUC 0.92. Can the business use its outputs as probabilities?” A strong answer:

  • AUC measures ranking only; it's unchanged by any monotone distortion, so it says nothing about calibration.
  • Check with a reliability diagram, the Brier score and log loss on held-out data with the real class ratio.
  • Fix with Platt (little data, sigmoid shape) or isotonic (lots of data), or temperature scaling for neural nets, fitted on data the model didn't train on.
  • Knows the usual suspects: naive Bayes overconfident, SVMs and boosted stumps underconfident, resampling inflates, deep nets overconfident.
  • Explains when it matters: absolute thresholds, pricing, expected value, showing risks to people; not for top-k ranking.
🏭 In the real world
Ten model families, calibrated 🧪 Research · 2005 Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana: Predicting Good Probabilities With Supervised Learning (PDF)

How: compared SVMs, neural nets, trees, bagging, random forests, boosting, naive Bayes and logistic regression, before and after Platt and isotonic calibration.

Why this method: max-margin methods push probabilities away from 0 and 1, naive Bayes toward them; after calibration, boosted trees, random forests and SVMs predicted the best probabilities.

Overconfident deep networks 🧪 Research · 2017 Guo et al.: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

How: measured calibration across architectures; depth, width, weight decay and batch normalisation all affected it.

Why this method: temperature scaling, a one-parameter version of Platt scaling, was “surprisingly effective” on most datasets.

Undersampling and probabilities in fraud 🧪 Research · 2015 Dal Pozzolo et al.'s credit-card dataset (OpenML)

How: released with the paper “Calibrating Probability with Undersampling for Unbalanced Classification”.

Why this method: fraud teams undersample legit payments to train faster, which inflates fraud probabilities; the paper shows how to correct them.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Probability calibration

How: CalibratedClassifierCV (sigmoid, isotonic, temperature), FrozenEstimator, reliability diagrams with CalibrationDisplay.

Why this method: the guide's comparison of logistic regression, naive Bayes, random forest and linear SVM reproduces the 2005 findings on synthetic data.

🤯 Fun fact · forecasters invented this

The Brier score was proposed by Glenn Brier of the US Weather Bureau in 1950, in a three-page paper titled Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability. Weather forecasting was the first field to publish probabilities every day and get graded on them every day, which is why reliability diagrams are still standard in meteorology.

🌉 How it connects

Calibration is the missing half of the metrics in 5.3–5.4 (AUC ranks; Brier and log loss grade the numbers), and the repair kit for 5.5's resampled models. Platt scaling is logistic regression (2.3) on one feature; naive Bayes' overconfidence comes from its independence assumption (3.2). Conformal prediction (9.5) gives distribution-free guarantees on top. For log loss as cross-entropy, see Math 6.3; why bucket averages settle down, Math 4.7.

Check yourself

1. Across 200 cases where the model predicted 0.8, the event happened 120 times. Is it calibrated there, and in which direction is it off?
120 ÷ 200 = 60%. It claimed 80%, so it's overconfident in that bucket; on the reliability diagram the point (0.8, 0.6) sits below the diagonal. A calibrator would map 0.8 to about 0.6.
2. You have an SVM and only 300 labelled rows to spare for calibration. Which method?
Platt scaling was designed for SVM scores and fits only a slope and an intercept, so 300 rows are plenty. Isotonic can overfit with fewer than about 1,000 rows (Playground 2). A plain SVM outputs margins, not probabilities.
3. After calibrating a model with Platt scaling, what happens to its ROC AUC and its Brier score?
Platt applies a monotone function, so the ranking, and the AUC, stay put. The Brier score and log loss judge the probability values, so they improve when the values become honest (naive Bayes: 0.185 → 0.164).
📝 TL;DR
  • Calibrated = among predictions of p, a fraction p happen. Ranking (AUC) and calibration are separate skills.
  • Check with a reliability diagram, Brier score, log loss and ECE, on held-out data with the real class ratio.
  • Naive Bayes and deep nets: overconfident. SVMs and boosted stumps: underconfident (sigmoid-shaped). Resampling inflates.
  • Fix with Platt (2 parameters, small data), isotonic (any monotone shape, 1,000+ rows) or temperature; CalibratedClassifierCV, FrozenEstimator.
  • Matters for absolute thresholds, pricing, risks shown to people; not for top-k ranking. Next: tuning hyperparameters (5.7).
