Phase 5 · Lesson 3

Classification metrics & the confusion matrix

A fraud model that never catches a single fraud can be 99.8% accurate. To know whether a classifier is any good, count its four kinds of answers, then weigh them by what each mistake costs.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A classifier like logistic regression outputs a score (often a probability); a threshold turns it into yes/no (Lesson 2.3).
  • Validation scores come from data the model didn't train on, ideally via cross-validation (Lesson 5.1).
  • Cross-entropy measures how surprised a probability forecast is by what happened (Math 6.3).
🎯 Hook: the 99.83% accurate fraud detector

A public dataset from a research collaboration between the Université Libre de Bruxelles and the payments company Worldline (published 2015) holds two days of European card transactions from September 2013: 284,807 transactions, of which 492 are fraud (0.172%). It's a classic for learning fraud detection.

Here's a “model” that needs no training: return "legit". It's right on 284,315 of 284,807 transactions: 99.83% accuracy. It also catches zero frauds. Any metric that calls this model excellent is the wrong metric. Which ones see through it?

🔮 Predict first

For the “always legit” model, what's its recall: the share of real frauds it catches?

0%: 0 of 492. Accuracy mixes the huge legit class with the tiny fraud class, so the legit class drowns everything. Recall looks only at the frauds. You'll build both from the four cells of the confusion matrix next.

Read them together: “the model catches half the frauds, and 4 in 5 of its alarms are real”. That's a description you can act on; “91% accurate” isn't (always saying “legit” already gets 86% on this data).

💡 Big idea A classifier makes two kinds of mistakes: false alarms (FP) and misses (FN). Accuracy adds them up as if they cost the same and as if the classes were equally common; usually neither is true. Precision asks about the alarms, recall about the cases you care about, and the threshold trades one for the other. Choose the trade by what each mistake costs.
🧠 Analogy: the smoke alarm

A very sensitive smoke alarm (high recall) never misses a fire, but goes off every time you make toast (low precision). A lazy one (high precision) only rings when the kitchen is ablaze, and sleeps through the small fire that starts at night (low recall). You can't buy one that's perfect at both; you choose the sensitivity by asking which is worse in your house: burnt toast alarms or an unnoticed fire.

🤔 Why this? Why precision, recall and the confusion matrix?
  1. The problem before. Accuracy is one number that hides two kinds of mistakes. When one class is rare (fraud 0.17%, disease 1%, spam 10%), predicting “never” scores sky-high accuracy and is useless.
  2. What people tried. Error rate (1 − accuracy) has the same blind spot. Medicine had long used sensitivity and specificity to describe tests; information retrieval (search engines) used precision and recall to describe result lists. Machine learning borrowed both.
  3. Why this works. Splitting by what really happened (rows) and what was predicted (columns) gives four counts, and each ratio isolates one question. For the “always legit” model: accuracy 99.83%, recall 0 of 492 = 0%. The bad model is exposed instantly.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. Now you have several numbers and a threshold to choose, so you need to know the business costs. Precision depends on how rare positives are (it falls when they get rarer, see Playground 2), and F1 assumes misses and false alarms matter equally, which is rarely true.
  5. What came next. Fβ to weight recall more (or less), cost-based thresholds (below), log loss to judge the probabilities themselves, and threshold-free curves, ROC and PR (Lesson 5.4).

The threshold: one dial, every metric moves

🔮 Predict first

You lower the threshold from 0.5 to 0.2, so more transactions get flagged. What happens?

Recall rises from 51% to 80% (more frauds caught) while precision falls from 78% to 51% (more false alarms among the flags). That trade-off is unavoidable for a fixed model; only a better model moves both. Slide it below.

F1 and Fβ: one number, if you must

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Unlike the ordinary average, it collapses when either one is small: precision 1.0 with recall 0.02 averages to 0.51 but has F1 = 0.04. Fβ tilts it: β = 2 treats recall as twice as important (cancer screening, fraud), β = 0.5 favours precision (a spam filter that must not eat real mail).

Fβ = (1 + β²) · P · R ⁄ (β² · P + R) “A precision–recall blend where recall counts β times as much; β = 1 is plain F1.”

Two more you'll meet: balanced accuracy (the average of recall and specificity; “always legit” gets exactly 50%) and MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient, a correlation between predicted and true labels that uses all four cells and stays near 0 for useless models).

The accuracy paradox, and why precision depends on rarity

Keep a model fixed, with recall 90% and a false-alarm rate of 5% on legit cases, and change only how rare positives are. Accuracy barely moves. Precision falls off a cliff: when positives are rare, even a small false-alarm rate on the huge negative class produces more false alarms than there are positives.

🔮 Predict first

That model screens for a disease that 1 in 1,000 people have. A patient tests positive. Roughly how likely is it that they really have the disease?

About 1.8%. Out of 100,000 people, 100 are sick and 90 are caught; but 5% of the 99,900 healthy people also test positive: 4,995 false alarms. 90 ÷ (90 + 4,995) ≈ 1.8%. That's Bayes' rule in action (Math 4.2). Slide the rarity below.

That's why fraud teams talk about the false-positive rate on legit traffic with many decimal places. Stripe, for example, describes fraud as roughly 1 in every 1,000 payments and reports that its Radar system wrongly blocks about 0.1% of legitimate ones. At 1 in 1,000, a 5% false-alarm rate would bury every real fraud under fifty false alarms.

Pick the threshold by what mistakes cost

Suppose a manual review of a flagged payment costs $10 (a false alarm wastes that), and a missed fraud costs $100. The total cost is 10 × FP + 100 × FN. Instead of arguing about F1, minimise that. If the scores are calibrated probabilities (Lesson 5.6), the math even tells you where to look: flag when p × $100 > (1 − p) × $10, that is, when

p > t* = CFP ⁄ (CFP + CFN) = 10 ⁄ 110 ≈ 0.09 “Flag whenever the expected loss of letting it through is bigger than the cost of a review.”

Not 0.5! The 0.5 default is only right when both mistakes cost the same.

With uncalibrated scores (boosted trees, SVMs, Lesson 5.6) the formula misleads, but you can still pick the cheapest threshold on validation data. scikit-learn's TunedThresholdClassifierCV does exactly that with cross-validation.

Log loss: grading the probabilities themselves

Precision and recall only look at which side of the threshold a score lands. Log loss (cross-entropy) grades the probability: for each row it charges −log(probability given to what really happened), then averages. Saying 0.9 for a fraud costs 0.11; saying 0.1 costs 2.30; saying 0.01 costs 4.61. Being confidently wrong is punished hard.

Our model's average log loss is 0.214; a model that ignores the features and says “13.75% fraud” to everyone (the base rate) gets 0.400. Log loss is what logistic regression and gradient boosting minimise during training, and it's the right metric when you'll use the probabilities themselves, for pricing, ranking or expected-cost decisions. It can be fooled by nothing except good, honest probabilities.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9): confusion_matrix, classification_report (precision, recall, F1 per class), fbeta_score, balanced_accuracy_score, matthews_corrcoef, log_loss, and TunedThresholdClassifierCV / FixedThresholdClassifier (since 1.5) to stop living with 0.5.

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import (classification_report, confusion_matrix, fbeta_score,
                             log_loss, make_scorer)
from sklearn.model_selection import TunedThresholdClassifierCV, train_test_split

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=5000, weights=[0.95], class_sep=0.8, random_state=0)  # 5% positives
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, random_state=0)
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

print(confusion_matrix(y_te, clf.predict(X_te)))          # rows = truth, columns = prediction: [[TN FP] [FN TP]]
print(classification_report(y_te, clf.predict(X_te), digits=3))
print("log loss", log_loss(y_te, clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]))

# Choose the threshold by cross-validation to maximise F2 (recall counts double) instead of using 0.5
tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
                                   scoring=make_scorer(fbeta_score, beta=2), cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"best threshold {tuned.best_threshold_:.2f}, test F2 {fbeta_score(y_te, tuned.predict(X_te), beta=2):.3f}")

For money-based thresholds, write a scorer that returns minus the cost (e.g. -(10 * fp + 100 * fn) from confusion_matrix) and hand it to TunedThresholdClassifierCV. Note scikit-learn's confusion matrix puts the negative class first: tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y, yhat).ravel().

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Week one

Show the confusion matrix and precision/recall at the threshold you'd actually use, next to the “always majority class” baseline. Ask the business two questions: what does a miss cost, and what does a false alarm cost? Then set the threshold from those numbers.

At scale

Teams track a dashboard of metrics per segment (country, merchant type, device), set thresholds per segment, and guard a business constraint such as “block at most 0.1% of good payments” while maximising recall. Thresholds are re-tuned as fraud rates and costs drift (Lesson 9.4).

🎤 Interview angle

“Your fraud model has 99% accuracy. Is it good?” A strong answer:

  • Asks for the base rate: if fraud is 1%, “always legit” also scores 99%, so accuracy says nothing.
  • Asks for the confusion matrix, then precision (how many alarms are real) and recall (how many frauds are caught), at the operating threshold.
  • Explains the threshold trade-off and chooses it from costs (t* = CFP ÷ (CFP + CFN) if calibrated) or from a constraint (“FPR ≤ 0.1%”).
  • Mentions threshold-free views (PR curve, average precision, Lesson 5.4) and log loss if probabilities are used downstream.
🏭 In the real world
Card fraud with 0.172% positives 🧪 Research · 2015 The creditcard dataset (OpenML, from Dal Pozzolo et al.)

How: 284,807 anonymised card transactions (features PCA-transformed for confidentiality, plus time and amount), 492 frauds; collected by Worldline and ULB's Machine Learning Group for research on fraud detection and on calibrating probabilities after undersampling.

Why this method: the standard teaching example of the accuracy paradox and of imbalanced evaluation (Lessons 5.4–5.5).

Stripe Radar 🏢 Stripe · 2023 How we built it: Stripe Radar

How: a model scores every payment in milliseconds; fraud is about 1 in 1,000 payments, and Radar wrongly blocks about 0.1% of legitimate ones.

Why this method: the business is described in exactly these terms: recall on fraud (a 1.5% recall drop was “unacceptably large”) against false positives that block good customers.

Diabetic eye disease screening 🏢 Google · 2016 Gulshan et al., JAMA: deep learning for diabetic retinopathy

How: the same model was reported at two thresholds: sensitivity 90.3% / specificity 98.1%, or sensitivity 97.5% / specificity 93.4% (EyePACS-1 set).

Why this method: medicine reports sensitivity and specificity, and the operating point is a clinical choice: a screening program favours sensitivity so that few sick patients are missed.

The everyday reference 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Tuning the decision threshold

How: TunedThresholdClassifierCV searches the threshold that maximises any scorer (including a cost) with cross-validation.

Why this method: “predict uses 0.5” was a silent default for years; this makes the threshold a tuned, validated part of the model.

🤯 Fun fact · precision and recall came from libraries

Precision and recall were measures for document retrieval long before machine learning: of the documents a search returned, how many were relevant (precision), and of all relevant documents in the library, how many it found (recall). The F-measure came from the same field. Sensitivity and specificity, meanwhile, came from medical testing. Same four boxes, two vocabularies.

Check yourself

1. A model flags 50 emails as spam; 40 of them really are. There were 80 spam emails in total. Precision and recall?
Precision = TP ÷ flagged = 40 ÷ 50 = 80%. Recall = TP ÷ all real spam = 40 ÷ 80 = 50%. F1 = 2 × 0.8 × 0.5 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 0.62.
2. A missed cancer costs far more than a follow-up scan for a healthy patient. Your calibrated model outputs P(cancer). What do you do with the 0.5 threshold?
When a miss costs, say, 50 times a follow-up, t* = 1 ÷ 51 ≈ 0.02: refer anyone with more than a 2% risk. Accuracy would fall (many false alarms) and that's the right call. Raising the threshold buys precision at the cost of missed cancers.
3. Two spam filters: A has precision 0.99, recall 0.40; B has precision 0.90, recall 0.90. Which has the higher F1, and why does F1 use a harmonic mean?
F1(A) = 2 × 0.99 × 0.40 ÷ 1.39 ≈ 0.57; F1(B) = 0.90. The plain averages are 0.695 and 0.90. The harmonic mean punishes imbalance, so you can't buy a high F1 by maxing one side. Whether A is better depends on costs: a filter that must never eat real mail might still prefer A.
📝 TL;DR
  • Count TP, FP, FN, TN. Accuracy hides rare classes: “always legit” is 99.83% accurate on the card-fraud data and catches nothing.
  • Precision = how many alarms are real; recall = how many positives you catch. The threshold trades one for the other.
  • F1 is their harmonic mean; Fβ weights recall β times more. Precision falls as positives get rarer.
  • Choose the threshold by cost: t* = CFP ÷ (CFP + CFN) for calibrated scores, or tune it on validation data.
  • Log loss grades the probabilities; confident mistakes cost the most. Next: all thresholds at once (5.4).
