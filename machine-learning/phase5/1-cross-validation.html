Phase 5 · Lesson 1

Cross-validation: k-fold, stratified, group, time series

One train/test split is one roll of the dice. Cross-validation rolls k times and averages, and, done right, it tests your model the way the real world will: on new patients, new customers, next month.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A test set is data the model never touched while being built; its score estimates how the model does on new data (Lesson 0.6).
  • Leakage: information from the test set sneaks into training, so the score looks better than reality (Lesson 0.6).
  • An average of k noisy numbers jumps around less than one of them (Math 4.7).
🎯 Hook: half the papers graded themselves on their own patients

In 2017 a team at Northwestern University reviewed 62 studies that used smartphones and wearable sensors to predict health outcomes. Each study had many recordings per person, and each had to split them into training and test data.

28 of the 62 (45%) split record by record, so recordings of the same person landed on both sides. Those papers reported a median error of 5.6%. The papers that kept each person entirely on one side reported 13.0%, more than twice as much. Same kind of data, same kind of models; the only big difference was how they cut the data.

Why does the cut matter so much, and how do you cut so your number tells the truth? That's this lesson.

🔮 Predict first

A model diagnoses disease from wrist-sensor data: 30 patients, 8 recordings each. You shuffle all 240 recordings and run 5-fold cross-validation. Later it's tested on brand-new patients. What happens to its accuracy?

On this lesson's toy version, shuffled CV says 92%; on 300 new patients the model gets 73%. Shuffled CV let it recognise people (each person's recordings look alike) instead of disease. Keeping patients together (GroupKFold) says 75%: honest. You'll break and fix it in Playground 3.

Numbers first: one split is a coin you flipped once

Take 100 rows of noisy data and a k-nearest-neighbours model. Hold out 20 rows as a test set and score it. Now do it again with a different random 20, and again, 200 times:

How you estimate accuracyAverage estimateSpread (std) across 200 triesRange you might see
One 80/20 split84%8.1 points60% to 100%
5-fold cross-validation (average of 5 splits)84%2.0 pointsmuch tighter

Same model, same data. With one split, you could report 60% or 100% depending on luck. Cross-validation gives every row one turn in the test set and averages, so most of that luck cancels. That's the whole idea; the rest of the lesson is about cutting correctly.

💡 Big idea Split the data into k folds. Train on k − 1 of them, test on the one left out, and rotate until each fold has been the test set once. Report the mean and spread of the k scores. And make every split look like the real future: new groups stay unseen, the future stays in the future.
🧠 Analogy: one exam vs a semester of quizzes

A single final exam can be unlucky: the three questions you didn't revise all show up. Five quizzes spread over the semester, each covering different material, give a fairer picture of what you know.

But the quizzes only count if the student hasn't seen the answers. If one quiz reuses last week's questions word for word, a student who memorised them looks brilliant. That's what happens when the same patient (or customer, or day) sits in both train and test.

🤔 Why this? Why cross-validation?
  1. The problem before. You need a number for “how good is this model on new data?”, but you only have one dataset. Score on the training data and you reward memorising. One held-out test set is honest, but with small data it's noisy (60% to 100% above) and it wastes rows you could train on.
  2. What people tried. A bigger test set: less noise, but less training data. Reusing the training score with a penalty (AIC-style formulas): works for some models, not for trees or k-NN. Repeating random splits: better, but some rows get tested often and others never.
  3. Why this works. Every row is tested exactly once, by a model that never saw it, and you average k scores. Averaging 5 noisy estimates cuts the spread: here from 8.1 points to 2.0. With 10 folds each model trains on 90% of the data, so the estimate is close to what a model trained on all of it would get.
  4. What it costs, when it fails. k times the training time. And it's only honest if the folds mimic reality: if rows are grouped (patients, users, stores) or ordered in time, a random shuffle leaks and the score lies. If you tune on the same folds you report, the score is optimistic too.
  5. What came next. Stratified, group and time-series splitters (this lesson), nested CV for honest tuning (below), repeated CV for small data, and cheaper search strategies built on CV (Lesson 5.7).

Stratified: keep the rare class in every fold

With 5 rare rows in 20 and plain shuffling, some test folds get two ■, some get none. A fold with no positives can't measure recall at all, and the per-fold scores swing for no good reason. Stratified k-fold deals the rare rows out like cards, one per fold. scikit-learn's cross_val_score already uses stratified folds by default for classifiers when you pass cv=5.

Group: the same patient can't be on both sides

When rows come in groups (recordings of one patient, sessions of one user, photos of one product), the rows inside a group are near-copies. Shuffle them and the model can “cheat”: it finds the test row's sibling in the training set. GroupKFold puts whole groups into folds. The question it answers is the one you care about: “how well does it do on a new patient?”

Time series: no peeking into the future

For forecasts, the real world always asks “given the past, predict what comes next”. TimeSeriesSplit trains on an expanding window of the past and tests on the block right after it. A gap skips a few rows between them, for when features use recent history (a 7-day average would otherwise overlap the test days). Shuffled k-fold on time series trains on Thursday to predict Wednesday: easy, and useless. Lesson 7.2 builds on this for forecasting.

✏️ Worked example: reading a CV result

5-fold accuracies: 0.82, 0.86, 0.79, 0.88, 0.85.

  • Mean = (0.82 + 0.86 + 0.79 + 0.88 + 0.85) ÷ 5 = 0.840.
  • Spread: the standard deviation of the five is 0.032. A rough uncertainty for the mean is 0.032 ÷ √5 ≈ 0.014 (rough because the folds share training rows, so they're not independent).
  • So a rival model scoring 0.845 is not clearly better; one scoring 0.90 probably is. Compare models on the same folds, and look at fold-by-fold differences, not just the two means.

Notice two things. The k-fold mean is far steadier than one split. And a bigger k makes a single fold noisier (each test fold is tiny: 10 rows at k = 10) even though the k-fold mean gets steadier. In practice k = 5 or 10 is the standard choice: k = 10 trains on more data, k = 5 costs half as much. For tiny data, repeated k-fold (RepeatedStratifiedKFold) averages over several shuffles too.

With tight clusters (each person's recordings nearly identical) shuffled CV says about 92% while new patients get about 73%: a 19-point lie, the same pattern the 2017 review found in real papers. GroupKFold lands within a couple of points. Slide “how alike” to the right: when recordings vary as much within a person as between people, the leak shrinks, because a sibling recording is no longer a giveaway. Real data is usually on the left.

⚠️ Leaks that survive even group splits
  • Preprocessing outside the folds: fitting a scaler, an imputer, target encoding, feature selection or SMOTE on all rows before CV. Put every step in a Pipeline so it refits inside each fold (Lesson 1.6; SMOTE in Lesson 5.5).
  • Duplicates: the same customer under two IDs, the same image resized. Deduplicate or group by a smarter key.
  • Time hiding inside features: “days until cancellation” in a churn model. No splitter can fix a feature from the future.

Tuning on your CV score: nested cross-validation

Say you try 50 settings of a model and pick the one with the best 5-fold score. That best score is optimistic: among 50 noisy numbers, the top one got lucky. Cawley and Talbot (2010) showed this “selection bias” can be as large as the differences between learning algorithms people write papers about.

Nested CV fixes it with two loops. The inner loop (on the training part only) does the tuning and picks a setting. The outer loop scores that whole tune-then-train procedure on folds the tuning never saw. In scikit-learn it's one line: pass a GridSearchCV to cross_val_score. Its number estimates “how well does my tuning process do on new data”; for the final model you still tune once on all the data. The simpler everyday version: tune with CV on the training set, then touch a held-out test set once at the end (Lesson 5.7 shows the cost of peeking).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

scikit-learn (1.9 as of late 2026): splitters in sklearn.model_selection, plus cross_val_score (one metric) and cross_validate (several metrics, train scores, fit times). Pass groups= for group splitters.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import (KFold, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit,
                                     GridSearchCV, cross_val_score)
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())  # the scaler refits inside every fold

for cv in [KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)]:
    s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv)
    print(f"{type(cv).__name__:16s} {s.mean():.3f} ± {s.std():.3f}")

# Grouped rows: pass the group id of every row; a group never sits in train and test at once
patients = np.random.default_rng(0).integers(0, 50, size=len(y))   # pretend: 50 patients
s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=GroupKFold(n_splits=5), groups=patients)

# Time order: train on the past, test on the block after it, skipping `gap` rows in between
for tr, te in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=3, gap=10).split(X):
    print("train", tr.min(), "-", tr.max(), " test", te.min(), "-", te.max())   # 0-132 | 143-284 ...

# Nested CV: the inner GridSearchCV tunes k, the outer loop scores the whole tuning procedure
inner = GridSearchCV(model, {"kneighborsclassifier__n_neighbors": [1, 3, 5, 11, 21]}, cv=5)
outer = cross_val_score(inner, X, y, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=1))
print(f"nested CV {outer.mean():.3f} ± {outer.std():.3f}")          # about 0.97

Also worth knowing: StratifiedGroupKFold (groups and class balance at once), LeaveOneGroupOut (one fold per group, e.g. per hospital), RepeatedStratifiedKFold for small data, and cross_validate(..., return_train_score=True) to see the train/validation gap (Lesson 5.8).

🚀 Startup vs big tech

Small data

Every row is precious, so 5- or 10-fold CV (stratified, grouped by customer) is your main tool, plus a small held-out test set you look at once. Make the split mimic production: if you'll score new customers, group by customer.

Lots of data

With millions of rows one big split is already steady, and training k times is expensive, so teams use a time-based holdout (“train through June, validate on July”) and backtests over several months. The real final exam is an online A/B test (Lesson 9.3).

🎤 Interview angle

“Your model gets 95% in cross-validation and 70% in production. What happened?” A strong answer:

  • Asks how the folds were made: grouped rows (same user, patient, device) split across folds, or time shuffled, so CV measured memorisation.
  • Checks preprocessing leakage: scaling, encoding, feature selection or resampling done before splitting instead of inside a Pipeline.
  • Checks selection bias: many models tuned and picked on the same folds; fix with nested CV or an untouched test set.
  • Mentions distribution shift (production data differs, Lesson 9.4) and that the fix is to make validation mimic deployment.
🏭 In the real world
Subject-wise vs record-wise validation 🧪 Research · 2017 Saeb et al.: The need to approximate the use-case in clinical machine learning (GigaScience)

How: compared splitting by recording vs by person on an activity-recognition dataset and in simulation, then reviewed 62 wearable-sensor papers.

Why this method: record-wise CV “massively overestimates” accuracy; 45% of the papers used it, and their reported error (5.6%) was less than half that of subject-wise papers (13.0%).

Leakage across 17 fields 🧪 Research · 2022 Kapoor & Narayanan: Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis in ML-based Science

How: surveyed published errors in ML-based science and re-ran civil-war prediction studies.

Why this method: leakage affected 329 papers in 17 fields; every paper claiming complex models beat logistic regression for civil-war prediction failed to reproduce once the leaks were fixed.

Selection bias in model selection 🧪 Research · 2010 Cawley & Talbot: On Over-fitting in Model Selection (JMLR, PDF)

How: showed that tuning hyperparameters on a CV score overfits that score, and that reporting it as the performance is biased.

Why this method: nested CV (tune inside, evaluate outside) gives an unbiased estimate of the whole procedure.

The everyday implementation 🧰 Open source scikit-learn: Cross-validation user guide

How: splitters (KFold, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit…) that plug into cross_validate and every search class.

Why this method: the guide's diagrams of each splitter are the clearest one-page summary of “which rows go where”.

🤯 Fun fact · the civil-war models

Kapoor and Narayanan's reproduction found that the headline claims of fancy models beating plain logistic regression at predicting civil wars all came from leakage. Fixed, the complex models did no better than the decades-old logistic regression. The most impressive accuracy in a paper is often a splitting bug.

Check yourself

1. 5-fold CV gives 0.70, 0.74, 0.72, 0.78, 0.76. What's the mean, and is a rival model at 0.745 clearly better?
(0.70 + 0.74 + 0.72 + 0.78 + 0.76) ÷ 5 = 0.74. The folds range over 8 points (std ≈ 0.028), so 0.005 is noise. Compare both models on the same folds and look at the per-fold differences.
2. You predict whether a customer will churn. Each customer has 12 monthly rows. You'll use the model on new sign-ups. Which split?
A customer's months look alike, so shuffled rows let the model recognise the customer. Grouping by customer asks the real question: how well does it do on someone it has never seen? If time also matters (you predict next month), split by time too.
3. You tried 200 hyperparameter settings and report the best 5-fold score, 0.91, as your model's accuracy. What's wrong?
Each CV score has noise; picking the maximum of 200 picks the luckiest. That's selection bias (Cawley & Talbot, 2010). Nested CV scores the whole tuning procedure on outer folds the tuning never saw; or keep a final test set and look at it once.
📝 TL;DR
  • k-fold CV: train on k − 1 folds, test on the last, rotate, report mean ± spread. Far steadier than one split (here 2.0 vs 8.1 points).
  • Stratify for classification; group when rows share a patient/user/store; split by time when the future matters.
  • Shuffled CV on grouped data measures memorisation: 92% in CV, 73% on new patients in the toy, and 5.6% vs 13.0% error in real papers.
  • Keep every preprocessing step inside the folds (Pipeline). Tuning on CV makes CV optimistic: use nested CV or an untouched test set.
  • k = 5 or 10 is standard; next, choosing what to measure (5.2–5.4).
