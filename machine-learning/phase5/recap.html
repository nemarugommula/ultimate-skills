Phase 5 · Recap

Recap: Evaluation & tuning

How to know a model is good before your users find out: folds that mimic the real world, a metric that prices mistakes the way the business does, honest curves and probabilities, a fair search, and a diagnosis of what to fix next. Here's the phase on one page, a game, and a system to design yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🔁

5.1 Cross-validation

k = 5 or 10; stratify classes; group by patient/user; split by time for forecasts; preprocessing inside the folds.

One split is one dice roll (8.1 vs 2.0 points of spread). Shuffled CV on grouped data said 92% for 73% reality. Tuning on CV makes it optimistic: nested CV or an untouched test set.

🧰 KFold, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit, cross_validate

📏

5.2 Regression metrics

RMSE → mean, punishes big misses. MAE → median, robust. R² vs the mean-guesser. Pinball at τ = Cunder ÷ (Cunder + Cover).

MAPE explodes near zero and rewards forecasting low; prefer MASE/sMAPE for many series. Always show a baseline.

🧰 root_mean_squared_error, mean_absolute_error, r2_score, mean_pinball_loss

🧮

5.3 Classification metrics

Precision = TP ÷ flagged; recall = TP ÷ positives; F1 = harmonic mean. Threshold t* = CFP ÷ (CFP + CFN) if calibrated.

“Always legit” is 99.83% accurate on card fraud and catches nothing. Precision falls as positives get rarer. Log loss grades probabilities.

🧰 confusion_matrix, classification_report, TunedThresholdClassifierCV

📈

5.4 ROC & PR

ROC AUC = P(random positive outranks random negative). Rare positives → PR curve / average precision.

Same ROC (AUC 0.86) gave AP 0.85 at 50% positives and 0.12 at 1%. AUC ignores thresholds, class ratio and calibration.

🧰 roc_auc_score, average_precision_score, RocCurveDisplay, PrecisionRecallDisplay

⚖️

5.5 Imbalanced data

Strong model + tuned threshold first. Weights / resampling / SMOTE only if needed, and only inside training folds.

Rebalancing left AUC at 0.912 but inflated risks (7% → 27%). Resampling before splitting: CV F1 0.97 for a real 0.25.

🧰 class_weight, imblearn Pipeline + SMOTE, TunedThresholdClassifierCV

🌡️

5.6 Calibration

Reliability diagram + Brier. Platt for little data / sigmoid shape; isotonic with 1,000+ rows; fit on held-out data.

Naive Bayes said 85% for 61% reality. Ranking rules don't need calibration; absolute thresholds, prices and shown risks do.

🧰 CalibratedClassifierCV, FrozenEstimator, calibration_curve, brier_score_loss

🎛️

5.7 Hyperparameter search

Random > grid (few knobs matter). TPE/Optuna for expensive trials; halving and pruning for cheap early signals. Log scales.

60 random trials find a top-5% setting with ~95% chance. 100 coin-flip models “score” 59% on one test set: never tune on it.

🧰 RandomizedSearchCV, HalvingRandomSearchCV, Optuna (study, trial.suggest_*, pruners)

🩺

5.8 Learning curves

Both errors high and close → bias (capacity, features). Big gap → variance (data, regularisation). Validation > train → leakage?

Test error = bias² + variance + noise. Training error always prefers the most complex model; the curves tell you what to do next.

🧰 validation_curve, learning_curve, LearningCurveDisplay

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “You've built a model that predicts which customers will cancel next month. Your notebook says 94% accuracy. Before the business uses it to decide who gets a discount, walk me through how you'd convince yourself, and them, that it really works. What would you measure, how would you split the data, and how would you pick the cut-off?”

A strong answer covers: the base rate (if 6% churn, “always stays” is 94% accurate); splitting by customer and by time (train on earlier months, validate on later ones) with preprocessing inside a Pipeline; precision/recall and the PR curve or average precision instead of accuracy; a threshold from the cost of a discount vs the value of a saved customer, or from campaign capacity; checking calibration if expected value is computed from the probabilities; tuning with CV on the training period and one final look at a held-out month; learning curves to decide between more data and more features; and a live A/B test as the real final exam.

🏗️ Build it yourself: the hospital readmission model

✏️ The problem (a made-up but realistic scenario)

A hospital group wants to predict, at discharge, which patients will be readmitted within 30 days, so a nurse can phone them in the first week. You have 3 years of records: 120,000 discharges from 45,000 patients at 8 hospitals; about 11% were readmitted. The nurses can make 300 calls a week. The risk will also be shown on the discharge screen as a percentage (“risk 18%”). Next year a 9th hospital joins.

Using this phase's ideas, design the evaluation: splits, metrics, threshold, imbalance handling, calibration, tuning and the checks you'd run. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Requirements

  • Rank discharges so the weekly 300 calls reach as many future readmissions as possible.
  • Show an honest percentage: among patients shown “18%”, about 18% should come back.
  • Work on patients and a hospital the model has never seen.

Numbers

  • 120,000 discharges ÷ 156 weeks ≈ 770 discharges a week; at 11% that's about 85 readmissions a week.
  • 300 calls cover 300 ÷ 770 ≈ 39% of discharges. A random 300 would reach about 0.11 × 300 ≈ 33 readmissions (recall 39%). If the model's precision in its top 300 were, say, 20%, it would reach 60 (recall 70%): that's the number to beat and to report.
  • The “always no readmission” baseline is 89% accurate, which is why accuracy is off the table.

Validation plan

  1. Group by patient: 45,000 patients have 2.7 discharges each on average, and a patient's visits look alike (Lesson 5.1).
  2. Split by time too: train on years 1–2, validate on year 3, because practice, coding and populations drift. Leave the last 3 months as a final test set, opened once.
  3. Leave-one-hospital-out CV as a second view, to estimate performance at the new 9th hospital.
  4. Every step (imputation, encoding, scaling, any resampling) inside a Pipeline so it refits per fold.

Metrics

  • Headline: recall and precision at the top 300 per week (the real operating point), plus average precision for threshold-free comparison (Lessons 5.3–5.4). ROC AUC reported for comparability with published work.
  • Calibration: reliability diagram, Brier score and log loss on the validation year, per hospital (Lesson 5.6).

Model and threshold

  • Gradient-boosted trees (HistGradientBoosting or LightGBM) as the main model; logistic regression as the transparent baseline.
  • 11% positives is not extreme: no resampling; the “threshold” is simply “the 300 highest risks this week”, a ranking rule (Lesson 5.5).
  • Because a percentage is shown on screen, calibrate with CalibratedClassifierCV (isotonic: tens of thousands of rows) on data with the real 11% rate.

Tuning and diagnosis

  • Optuna, 50–100 trials, scored on the time-based validation folds, with early stopping on boosting rounds (Lesson 5.7). Report the final test set once.
  • Learning curve on 10%, 20%, 40%, 80% of patients: if validation AP is still rising, more history helps; if flat, invest in features such as previous admissions, medications and follow-up appointments (Lesson 5.8).

Classic mistakes

  • Shuffling discharges so the same patient's earlier and later visits land on both sides.
  • Features recorded after discharge (a follow-up visit that only happens if the patient got worse).
  • Choosing the threshold, the model and the calibration on the final test set.
  • Showing uncalibrated boosted-tree scores as percentages to clinicians.
  • Reporting one overall AUC and never checking each hospital separately.

What this design doesn't cover yet: explaining individual predictions to nurses (Lesson 8.2), fairness across patient groups (8.4), and monitoring drift after launch (9.4).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A churn model is validated with shuffled 5-fold CV over monthly rows (12 per customer) and scores 0.95 AUC; in production it scores 0.78. What's the most likely cause?
Grouped rows leak through shuffled folds, the same pattern as Lesson 5.1's patients (92% in CV, 73% for real). Group by customer, and since the model predicts the future, validate on later months too.
2. A model's validation scores: ROC AUC 0.93, average precision 0.18, with 1% positives. Your manager asks if it's “93% accurate”. What do you say?
ROC AUC is a ranking probability, not accuracy, and it ignores the class ratio. AP for a random model ≈ the positive rate (0.01), so 0.18 is a real signal, but precision at any useful recall will be low. Report precision and recall at the operating point (Lesson 5.4).
3. A false alarm costs $5 and a miss costs $45. Your probabilities are calibrated. What threshold minimises expected cost?
t* = CFP ÷ (CFP + CFN) = 5 ÷ 50 = 0.10: flag whenever p × $45 > (1 − p) × $5. If the probabilities weren't calibrated, you'd pick the cheapest threshold on validation data instead (Lessons 5.3, 5.6).
4. You oversample the minority class to 50/50, retrain, and your ROC AUC is unchanged while the average predicted probability jumps from 5% to 30%. Which statement is right?
Resampling mostly shifts scores up for everyone (Lesson 5.5), which moves the effective threshold but not the order. Monotone changes leave AUC alone (5.4). If the probabilities matter, recalibrate on data with the true class ratio (5.6).
5. Training accuracy 0.99, validation 0.81, and the validation curve is still rising as you add data. You have budget for either a model twice as large or 3× more labelled data. Which?
A large gap with a still-rising validation curve is high variance: more data closes the gap, a bigger model widens it. While waiting for labels, regularise or simplify (Lesson 5.8).
🌉 What's next: Phase 6, unsupervised learning

So far every dataset came with labels to grade against. Phase 6 takes the labels away: grouping customers with k-means and choosing k (6.1), hierarchical clustering and dendrograms (6.2), clusters of any shape with DBSCAN and HDBSCAN (6.3), soft clusters with Gaussian mixtures (6.4), squeezing many columns into a few with PCA (6.5), maps with t-SNE and UMAP and what they really preserve (6.6), and catching the weird ones with anomaly detection (6.7). Evaluation gets harder without labels, so this phase's habits (baselines, honest splits, the right metric) matter even more.
