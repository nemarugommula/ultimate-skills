Phase 2 · Lesson 4

Regularization: dropout, weight decay, early stopping

A big network can memorize anything, including the mistakes in your labels. Three cheap tricks stop it: switch off random neurons, pull weights toward zero, and stop at the right moment. You'll watch a net score 100% on its training data and 71% on new data, then fix it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Overfitting: a model fits the noise of its training set and does worse on new data; the validation set is held-out data that measures it (math 5.6).
  • L2 regularization adds λ‖w‖² to the loss, so big weights cost something (math 7.4).
  • AdamW applies weight decay separately from the gradient (Lesson 1.5); model.eval() switches layers to their test-time behaviour (Lesson 2.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2012 Geoffrey Hinton's group published a strange idea: during training, randomly switch off half the neurons on every example. AlexNet used it in its two big fully connected layers that year; its authors wrote that without it the network “exhibits substantial overfitting”. On the MNIST digits, the dropout paper cut a standard network's test error from 1.60% to 1.35%.

Ten years later, Google's 540-billion-parameter PaLM was “trained without dropout” at all, and Karpathy's nanoGPT ships with dropout = 0.0 # for pretraining 0 is good. Yet GPT-3 and Llama 2 both use weight decay of 0.1.

Why does breaking your own network on purpose make it better, and why did the biggest models stop doing it? You'll overfit a net on noisy points, rescue it with each trick, look inside a dropout mask, and meet two results that broke the textbook picture of overfitting.

Numbers first: 100% right, and wrong

Our playground's training set has 40 points, and each label had a 20% chance of being deliberately flipped (made wrong): 6 of the 40 were. The network has 2 hidden layers of 48 units: about 2,500 parameters for 40 points. That's 60 knobs per point.

A model that's “right” about the world can get at most 34 of these 40 training labels right, 85%, because 6 are wrong. So if you see 100% training accuracy, the network has memorized all 6 wrong labels. Each memorized mistake is a little island in the decision boundary that misclassifies new points near it.

💡 Big idea Regularization means anything that makes a model prefer simpler explanations even when it could fit the training data perfectly. Dropout makes no single neuron indispensable; weight decay makes big weights expensive; early stopping stops before the network has time to memorize. All three trade a little training accuracy for a lot of validation accuracy.
🧠 Analogy: studying for an exam

Memorizing last year's answer key gets 100% on last year's paper and fails this year's. Dropout is studying in a group where random members are absent each day, so everyone has to understand everything. Weight decay is a teacher who deducts points for overly complicated answers. Early stopping is closing the books when your practice-test score stops improving, before you start memorizing the practice test itself.

Dropout: break it on purpose

On every training example, each hidden unit is switched off with probability p (its output set to 0). A different random set each time. At test time, nothing is dropped. One catch: with p = 0.5, a unit downstream receives on average only half its usual input during training. Inverted dropout, what PyTorch does, fixes that during training: the survivors are multiplied by 1/(1 − p), so the expected value stays the same and test time needs no change at all.

✏️ Worked example: keeping the average
  • A unit outputs 2.0. With p = 0.1, it's kept with probability 0.9 and scaled by 1/0.9 ≈ 1.111 → 2.222, or dropped → 0.
  • Expected value: 0.9 × 2.222 + 0.1 × 0 = 2.0. Same as test time, where it's simply 2.0.
  • A layer of 1,000 units with p = 0.1 keeps about 900 per example. Without the 1/(1 − p) scaling, the next layer would see 10% less input in training than at test: a systematic shift.
  • How many different “thinned” networks? Each unit is in or out: 2¹⁰⁰⁰ for that one layer. Each example trains a different one, and they all share the same weights.

Careful with the letter p: the 2014 dropout paper uses p for the probability of keeping a unit (0.5 for hidden units, 0.8 for inputs). PyTorch's nn.Dropout(p) uses p for dropping. Same idea, opposite number.

What you should see: each random mask gives a different output (the histogram shows all 64). Averaged over all of them, with inverted scaling, you get exactly the full network's output: test time with all units on is the ensemble average of the 64 thinned networks. Switch scaling off and the average is only (1 − p) of the full output, half at p = 0.5: the train/test mismatch that the 1/(1 − p) factor removes. (With dropout in several stacked ReLU layers the match becomes an approximation, which the dropout paper found “works well in practice”.)

🤔 Why this? Dropout (Hinton et al. 2012; Srivastava et al. 2014)
  1. The problem before Big networks overfit small datasets. Averaging many separately trained models fixes that, but training and running 10 large networks costs 10×. Units also co-adapt: one unit learns to fix another's specific mistakes, a fragile arrangement that doesn't transfer to new data.
  2. What people tried L2 weight penalties, early stopping, smaller networks, ensembles of separately trained models.
  3. Why this works Every step trains a random thinned network, and they share weights, so dropout is “a way of approximately combining exponentially many different neural network architectures efficiently”. No unit can rely on any particular partner, so each has to be useful on its own. At test time, keep all units: with inverted scaling, that's the ensemble average (exactly, for one layer: you just checked 64 masks). MNIST: 1.60% → 1.35% test error.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Slower convergence: AlexNet's authors note it “roughly doubles the number of iterations required to converge”. Noisy training loss. Train and eval behave differently (forget model.eval() and predictions become random). It interacts badly with BatchNorm statistics when placed before BN. And with enough data it's unnecessary: LLM pretraining usually sees most text only once or a few times, so memorizing isn't the bottleneck, and PaLM used none.
  5. What refined it DropConnect (drop weights instead of units), spatial dropout for CNN feature maps, DropPath/stochastic depth (drop whole residual blocks, Lesson 2.2), attention dropout in transformers; and in fine-tuning, small rates such as 0.1 (PaLM's fine-tuning; nanoGPT's suggestion). Data augmentation (2.5) does a similar job by adding noise to the inputs instead.

Weight decay: big weights cost extra

Add a penalty to the loss: loss + (λ/2)·Σw². Its gradient is just λ·w, so every step also pulls each weight a little toward 0, which is why it's called decay. A weight survives only if the data keeps pushing back. Smaller weights make smoother functions: a boundary can't make sharp little islands around single noisy points without some large weights.

✏️ Worked example: how fast does a weight decay?

λ = 0.01, learning rate 0.3. Each SGD step subtracts 0.3 × 0.01 × w = 0.003w, so an unused weight is multiplied by 0.997 per step. After 800 steps: 0.997⁸⁰⁰ ≈ 0.09. A weight of 2.0 that the data doesn't need shrinks to about 0.18. A weight the data does need settles where the data's pull balances the decay.

🤔 Why this? Weight decay (L2), and decoupled weight decay in AdamW
  1. The problem before Nothing stops a network from growing huge weights to fit every noisy point exactly; large weights mean sharp, wiggly functions and confident wrong predictions.
  2. What people tried Fewer parameters, early stopping, max-norm constraints on each unit's weights (used together with dropout in the 2014 paper).
  3. Why this works One extra term, λ‖w‖², whose gradient λw shrinks every weight a bit each step; only weights the data actively supports stay large. It's the oldest regularizer in statistics (ridge regression, math 7.4), and it costs one multiply-add per weight.
  4. What it costs, when it fails λ is a hyperparameter: too big and the model underfits (λ = 0.03 above). With Adam, adding λw to the gradient is not the same as decaying the weights, because Adam rescales gradients per parameter: Loshchilov & Hutter (2017) showed “L2 regularization and weight decay are not identical” for adaptive methods. Decaying biases and normalization γ/β usually doesn't help.
  5. What refined it AdamW: apply the decay directly to the weights, separately from the Adam step (Lesson 1.5). The common LLM recipe: AdamW with weight decay 0.1 (GPT-3, Llama 2) on weight matrices only, none on biases and norm parameters.

Early stopping: quit while you're ahead

Watch the validation loss while training. When it hasn't improved for a while (the patience, here 150 steps), stop and restore the best checkpoint. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's textbook calls it “probably the most commonly used form of regularization in deep learning”, because it's nearly free: you were going to evaluate on validation data anyway.

🤔 Why this? Early stopping
  1. The problem before Train long enough and a big network memorizes noise: in our playground the validation loss is lowest near step 75 and rises after it, while training loss keeps falling.
  2. What people tried Picking the number of epochs by hand, or by trial runs.
  3. Why this works Networks tend to learn broad, simple patterns first and memorize individual noisy examples later. Stopping at the validation minimum keeps the first part. It acts like a limit on how far the weights can travel from their small initial values, similar in spirit to weight decay. Cost: one validation pass every so often, plus saving the best weights.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It uses the validation set to choose, so you need a separate test set for an honest final number. Noisy validation curves trigger it too early (patience helps). Learning-rate schedules that decay late (Lesson 1.6) can produce late improvements it would miss. And the curve isn't always U-shaped: see double descent below.
  5. What refined it Checkpointing on the best validation score (every training framework has it), patience and minimum-improvement thresholds, and for LLMs, picking the training length up front from scaling laws (Lesson 6.8).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

nn.Dropout(p) (p = drop probability), weight decay through the optimizer (torch.optim.AdamW(..., weight_decay=0.1)), and early stopping as a few lines in your loop (or EarlyStopping callbacks in Lightning and Hugging Face). This snippet shows inverted dropout and the common “no decay on biases and norms” parameter groups, the same split nanoGPT uses.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
drop = nn.Dropout(p=0.5)               # PyTorch's p = probability of DROPPING
x = torch.ones(1, 8)
print("train:", drop(x))               # survivors scaled by 1/(1-p) = 2
drop.eval()
print("eval: ", drop(x))               # identity at eval time

# AdamW: decoupled weight decay; skip biases and norm layers (a common recipe)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(16, 64), nn.LayerNorm(64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.1), nn.Linear(64, 2))
decay = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.dim() >= 2]       # weight matrices
no_decay = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.dim() < 2]     # biases, LayerNorm γ/β
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": decay, "weight_decay": 0.1},
                         {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0}], lr=3e-4)
print(len(decay), "tensors with decay,", len(no_decay), "without")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# train: tensor([[0., 0., 2., 0., 0., 0., 2., 2.]])
# eval:  tensor([[1., 1., 1., 1., 1., 1., 1., 1.]])
# 2 tensors with decay, 4 without

Note that only 3 of 8 survived this time: dropout keeps each unit independently, so the count varies (the expected number is 4). Early stopping in plain PyTorch: track best_val, save copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict()) when it improves, break after patience evaluations without improvement, then model.load_state_dict(best). Gotcha: weight_decay= on plain torch.optim.Adam is L2-in-the-gradient, not decoupled; use AdamW.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your model has 99% training accuracy and 70% validation accuracy. What do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Diagnoses overfitting from the gap, and first checks it isn't a bug or a split problem (leakage, different distributions).
  • Reaches for more data or augmentation first (Lesson 2.5), then weight decay (AdamW), dropout, early stopping with checkpoints, a smaller model.
  • Explains dropout precisely: random masks per example, inverted 1/(1 − p) scaling, all units at test time ≈ ensemble average; model.eval() matters.
  • Knows why LLM pretraining uses little or no dropout (data seen about once; underfitting, not overfitting, is the regime) but keeps weight decay, and that double descent means “bigger overfits more” isn't always true.
🏭 In the real world
Dropout, the full study 🧪 Research · 2014 Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Salakhutdinov: Dropout (JMLR)

How: drop hidden units with retain probability 0.5 and inputs with 0.8, scale at test time, often combined with max-norm weight constraints; tested on vision, speech, text and genetics.

Why this method: an approximate average over exponentially many thinned networks at the price of one; it improved results across all of those domains.

Dropout where it overfit 📜 History · 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton: AlexNet

How: dropout with probability 0.5 in the first two fully connected layers, which hold most of the 60 million parameters.

Why this method: without it the network “exhibits substantial overfitting”; the price was roughly twice as many iterations.

No dropout for a 540B model 🏢 Google · 2022 Chowdhery et al.: PaLM

How: “trained without dropout, although dropout of 0.1 is used for finetuning in most cases”.

Why this method: pretraining on a huge corpus is far from memorizing it, so dropout's slowdown buys nothing; small fine-tuning datasets are where it pays again.

Weight decay 0.1 with AdamW 🏢 OpenAI · 2020 Brown et al.: GPT-3

How: “All models use weight decay of 0.1 to provide a small amount of regularization”, citing Loshchilov & Hutter's decoupled weight decay; Llama 2 reports the same 0.1.

Why this method: it keeps weights from growing without the slowdown or noise of dropout, and decoupling makes it behave predictably under Adam.

🤯 Fun facts · overfitting is weirder than the textbook says

Double descent. The classic picture says: make a model bigger and test error eventually goes up. Nakkiran et al. (2019, with OpenAI) showed that for ResNets, CNNs and transformers, test error can go up and then come back down as models get even bigger, a “double-descent” curve, and that in some settings “more data actually hurts”. The worst point sits where the model is just big enough to fit the training data exactly.

Grokking. Power et al. (2022) trained small transformers on puzzles like division modulo 97 and found that “long after severely overfitting, validation accuracy sometimes suddenly begins to increase from chance level toward perfect generalization”. Training accuracy had been 100% for ages, and the network then suddenly figured out the rule. Weight decay was the most effective intervention they tried for getting there with less data. Early stopping would have quit too soon.

Check yourself

1. Inverted dropout with p = 0.2 (drop probability). A unit's output is 5.0. What does the next layer receive if the unit is kept, and what's the expected value?
Kept units are scaled by 1/(1 − 0.2) = 1.25 → 6.25. Expected value: 0.8 × 6.25 + 0.2 × 0 = 5.0, the same as at test time, where nothing is dropped or scaled.
2. Training loss keeps falling; validation loss fell until epoch 12 and has risen for 8 epochs since. Training accuracy is 100%. What's going on and what's the quickest fix?
Diverging train and validation curves are the signature of overfitting. The best weights you have are from epoch 12; early stopping keeps them. Then regularize (or get more data) so the next run can go further before the curves split.
3. You add weight_decay=0.1 to torch.optim.Adam and it hurts. A colleague says “use AdamW”. Why would that differ?
For plain SGD, L2 in the loss and weight decay are the same thing. For Adam they're not: the λw term gets divided by each parameter's running gradient size, so weights with big gradients barely decay. Loshchilov & Hutter's AdamW decouples the two (Lesson 1.5).
📝 TL;DR
  • Overfitting shows as a train/val gap: 100% train, 71% validation in our playground, with validation loss rising while training loss falls.
  • Dropout zeroes each unit with probability p while training and scales survivors by 1/(1 − p); test time uses all units ≈ an average of 2ⁿ thinned networks. Costs: slower convergence, noisy training, model.eval() matters.
  • Weight decay pulls every weight toward 0 each step (penalty λ‖w‖²); use AdamW (decoupled), typically 0.1 for LLMs, not on biases and norms.
  • Early stopping: keep the checkpoint with the best validation score and stop after some patience. Nearly free.
  • LLM pretraining often uses no dropout (huge data, seen about once) but keeps weight decay. Double descent and grokking show that overfitting curves can surprise you.
