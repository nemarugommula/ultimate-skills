Phase 2 · Lesson 1

Vanishing and exploding gradients (and gradient clipping)

Multiply thirty numbers together and you almost never get something near 1. That one fact explains why deep networks used to be untrainable, why training runs suddenly print NaN, and why every big language model clips its gradients.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Backprop sends the gradient from the loss backwards, layer by layer, multiplying by each layer's local slope (the chain rule, Lesson 1.2 and math 3.5).
  • An activation's slope is at most 0.25 for sigmoid and exactly 1 for ReLU when z > 0 (Lesson 0.4).
  • A training step moves each weight by learning rate × gradient (Lessons 1.5 and 1.6).
🎯 Hook

Google trained PaLM, a 540-billion-parameter language model, in 2022. Its paper reports that the loss spiked roughly 20 times during training, “despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled”. Their fix was brute force: rewind to a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skip 200 to 500 batches of data.

Meanwhile GPT-3, LLaMA and Llama 2 all list the same line in their training recipe: clip the gradient norm at 1.0. So does Karpathy's nanoGPT (grad_clip = 1.0). Thirty years earlier, in 1991, a student named Sepp Hochreiter showed in his diploma thesis why deep and recurrent networks were so hard to train in the first place.

What makes gradients either shrink to nothing or blow up to infinity, and almost never stay “just right”? In this lesson you'll make both happen on purpose, beat a depth-30 challenge, crash a training run to NaN and rescue it with one line of code.

Numbers first: multiply thirty numbers

During backprop, the gradient that reaches layer 1 is the gradient at the output multiplied by one factor per layer. Here's what thirty factors do:

Factor per layerAfter 30 layersWhat layer 1 feels
0.50.5³⁰ ≈ 0.000000001A billionth of the signal: it never learns
0.90.9³⁰ ≈ 0.042Only 4%, from a factor that looked harmless
1.01³⁰ = 1The signal arrives intact
1.11.1³⁰ ≈ 1717× too loud: early layers take giant steps
2.02³⁰ ≈ 1,000,000,000A billion times too loud: NaN is coming

A 10% error per layer becomes a 24× swing (from 0.042 to 17) after thirty layers. Being exactly 1 is a knife edge. Anything else, compounded, either vanishes or explodes.

💡 Big idea The gradient at an early layer is a product of one factor per layer above it, and each factor is roughly (how big the weights are) × (the activation's slope). Products of many numbers drift exponentially. Every technique in this phase is a way to keep those factors near 1, or to stop the damage when they aren't.
🧠 Analogy: photocopying a photocopy

Copy a page, then copy the copy, thirty times. If the machine makes each copy 10% lighter, the last one is blank. If it makes each 10% darker, the last one is solid black. Only a perfectly calibrated machine keeps the page readable. Backprop is that photocopier, run once per layer, on the learning signal.

Where the factor comes from

Look at one layer: z = Wa, then a′ = f(z). Going backwards, the gradient passes through two things:

  • The activation: multiplied by its slope f′(z). Sigmoid: at most 0.25. Tanh: up to 1, falling fast. ReLU: 1 or 0.
  • The weights: multiplied by Wᵀ. If the weights are typically too big, the signal grows; too small, it shrinks. For a layer of width n with weights of spread σ, the size factor is about σ·√n.

So the per-layer factor is roughly weight scale × average slope. Initialization (Lesson 1.4) chooses the weight scale so that the product is 1: Xavier for tanh, He (σ = √(2/n), the extra 2 making up for ReLU switching off half its units) for ReLU.

✏️ Worked example: three layers, one neuron each

A chain of three neurons. Each has weight w and sits where its activation's slope is s. The output sends back a gradient of 1. Each layer multiplies by w × s:

ReLU, on (s = 1), w = 1.51.5 × 1 = 1.51.5³ ≈ 3.4
ReLU, on (s = 1), w = 11 × 1 = 11³ = 1

Three layers already make a 1,700× difference between the first and second rows (3.4 ÷ 0.002). Real layers have many neurons, so the factor is an average over them, but the arithmetic is the same.

A flat loss can also be a bug (Lesson 2.6), and a spike can be one bad batch of data. Checking per-layer gradient norms tells you which story you're in.

The extreme case: recurrent networks

A recurrent network (RNN, Lesson 5.2) reads a sequence one step at a time and reuses the same weight at every step. Backprop through 100 time steps multiplies by that same factor 100 times. There's no averaging over different layers to save you:

0.9¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.00003   ·   1.0¹⁰⁰ = 1   ·   1.1¹⁰⁰ ≈ 13,781

Notice what clipping is not: it does nothing for vanishing gradients. You can't clip a tiny number into a useful one. It's a seatbelt, not an engine.

Gradient clipping: by value or by norm

Think of all the gradients of all the weights as one long arrow g. Its length ‖g‖ (the global norm: square every gradient, add them all up, take the square root) says how big the step will be. There are two ways to cap it at a threshold c:

  • Clip by value: clamp every single number into [−c, c]. Simple, but it bends the arrow: big components get cut, small ones don't.
  • Clip by norm: if ‖g‖ > c, shrink the whole arrow: g ← g × c / ‖g‖. Same direction, shorter length. If ‖g‖ ≤ c, nothing happens.

What you should see: with g = (4, 1) and c = 2, clipping by value gives (2, 1): the angle jumps from 14° to 27°, a 12.5° turn, because only the big component was cut. Clipping by norm gives (1.94, 0.49): same direction, length 2. Drag the arrow inside the circle and norm clipping leaves it alone. That's why norm clipping is the default: in normal steps it does nothing, and in a bad step it keeps the direction the gradient asked for and only limits how far you go.

🤔 Why this? Gradient clipping by global norm
  1. The problem before One bad batch or one steep “cliff” in the loss surface produces a gradient 100× to 10⁶⁰× larger than usual (you'll see 2.4 × 10⁶⁰ below). The step lr × g throws the weights somewhere useless: the loss spikes, ReLUs die, or the numbers overflow to inf and NaN. Hours of training gone in one step.
  2. What people tried A lower learning rate for the whole run (slow everywhere, to survive a few bad steps); penalties on the recurrent weights; clipping each gradient entry separately (Mikolov's element-wise clipping, which Pascanu et al. (2013) describe as the backbone of their approach).
  3. Why this works Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013) proposed rescaling the gradient whenever its norm goes over a threshold: if ‖g‖ > c, g ← c·g/‖g‖. The direction is kept, only the step length is capped. Example: g = (30, 40) has norm 50; with c = 1 it becomes (0.6, 0.8). Normal steps (‖g‖ ≤ c) are untouched, so it costs nothing when training is healthy.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One more hyperparameter. Too tight (c = 0.1 in our playground) and every step is clipped, which is just a smaller learning rate: 229 steps instead of 41. It can hide a real bug (bad data, a too-high LR). It doesn't help vanishing gradients at all. With mixed precision you must unscale gradients first (Lesson 10.2). And it isn't a guarantee: PaLM still spiked with it on.
  5. What refined it Adam's per-parameter scaling (Lesson 1.5) already tames gradient size; learning-rate warmup (1.6) avoids early explosions; adaptive gradient clipping (AGC, Brock et al. 2021) clips each unit by the ratio of its gradient norm to its weight norm and let them train ResNets without normalization; and for giant runs, monitoring plus rewind-and-skip (PaLM). In LLM pretraining, c = 1.0 is the common default.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In PyTorch, clipping is one line between loss.backward() and optimizer.step(): torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_ (by norm, the default choice) or clip_grad_value_ (by value). clip_grad_norm_ returns the norm before clipping: log it, it's the best early-warning signal you have. This snippet measures vanishing in a 30-layer tanh stack and then clips.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
net = nn.Sequential(*[m for _ in range(30) for m in (nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.Tanh())], nn.Linear(64, 1))
x, y = torch.randn(256, 64), 100 * torch.randn(256, 1)   # big targets → big gradients

loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(net(x), y)
loss.backward()
linears = [m for m in net if isinstance(m, nn.Linear)]
print(f"layer 1 grad norm:  {linears[0].weight.grad.norm():.1e}")
print(f"layer 31 grad norm: {linears[-1].weight.grad.norm():.1e}")

# gradient clipping: one line between backward() and step()
total = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(net.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
print(f"global norm before clipping: {total:.3f}")
after = torch.sqrt(sum(p.grad.pow(2).sum() for p in net.parameters()))
print(f"global norm after clipping:  {after:.3f}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# layer 1 grad norm:  9.6e-07
# layer 31 grad norm: 4.9e+00
# global norm before clipping: 10.158
# global norm after clipping:  1.000

Layer 1 gets about 5 × 10⁶ times less gradient than the last layer with PyTorch's default init and tanh: vanishing, live. Gotchas: clip after backward() and before step(); with torch.amp's GradScaler call scaler.unscale_(optimizer) first, or you clip the scaled numbers. In Hugging Face's Trainer the setting is max_grad_norm (default 1.0).

🎤 Interview angle

“Why are deep networks hard to train, and what does gradient clipping fix?” A strong answer:

  • Backprop multiplies one factor per layer (weights × activation slope); a product of many factors shrinks or grows exponentially unless each is ≈ 1. RNNs are the worst case because they reuse one weight at every time step.
  • Names symptoms: vanishing = flat loss, tiny early-layer gradient norms; exploding = spikes, inf/NaN, dead ReLUs.
  • Maps fixes to causes: ReLU, Xavier/He init, residuals, normalization, LSTM gates for vanishing; lower LR, warmup and clipping for exploding.
  • Explains clipping by norm (rescale if ‖g‖ > c, direction kept) vs by value, places it after backward(), and knows the LLM default of 1.0 and that it doesn't fix vanishing.
🏭 In the real world
Clipping, as introduced for RNNs 🧪 Research · 2013 Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio: On the difficulty of training recurrent neural networks

How: analyses exploding and vanishing gradients in RNNs and proposes rescaling the gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold.

Why this method: exploding gradients come from sharp “cliffs” in the loss surface; capping the step length keeps the optimizer from being thrown off the cliff while leaving normal steps alone.

The LLM default: clip at 1.0 🏢 OpenAI · 2020 Brown et al.: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)

How: “we clip the global norm of the gradient at 1.0”, alongside Adam and a cosine learning-rate schedule. LLaMA and Llama 2 (Meta, 2023) report the same “gradient clipping of 1.0”.

Why this method: large transformers occasionally see huge gradients; a fixed norm cap costs nothing in normal steps and limits the damage of rare bad ones.

When clipping isn't enough 🏢 Google · 2022 Chowdhery et al.: PaLM

How: the 540B model's loss spiked about 20 times with clipping on; they restarted from a checkpoint about 100 steps earlier and skipped roughly 200 to 500 batches.

Why this method: no principled fix was affordable at that scale, and the spikes didn't appear in smaller models: monitoring the loss and gradient norm, then rewinding, was the practical answer.

One line in a popular training script 🧰 Open source Karpathy: nanoGPT

How: grad_clip = 1.0 in the config and torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), grad_clip) right after unscaling the gradients.

Why this method: it's the cheapest insurance in the whole training loop, and the script is the template many people learn GPT training from.

🤯 Fun fact · the problem was found in a student thesis

The first formal analysis of vanishing and exploding gradients is Sepp Hochreiter's 1991 diploma thesis at TU Munich, written in German (“Untersuchungen zu dynamischen neuronalen Netzen”). His advisor Jürgen Schmidhuber later called it “a first milestone of Deep Learning research”. Six years later the two of them published the LSTM, built specifically to give the gradient a path with factor 1 through time.

Check yourself

1. Each of 20 layers multiplies the gradient by about 0.8 on the way back. Roughly what fraction of the output gradient reaches layer 1?
Factors multiply: 0.8²⁰ ≈ 0.0115. A 20% loss per layer leaves about 1% after 20 layers. That compounding is the whole problem: a factor that looks nearly harmless becomes a vanishing gradient with depth.
2. Training a transformer, the loss is fine for 3,000 steps, then jumps to NaN. Your logged gradient norm went from about 0.8 to 900 one step before. What's the best first fix?
A sudden 1,000× jump in the norm followed by NaN is an exploding gradient. Clipping by norm caps the step at a safe length; then look at the cause (LR too high, missing warmup, a corrupted batch). Sigmoid would bring back vanishing gradients, and a higher LR makes explosions worse.
3. A gradient is (6, 8) and you clip by norm with c = 5. What do you get?
‖(6, 8)‖ = 10 > 5, so multiply by 5/10 = 0.5 → (3, 4): same direction, length 5. (5, 5) is clipping by value, which changes the direction from 53° to 45°.
📝 TL;DR
  • The gradient at layer 1 is a product of one factor per layer (≈ weight scale × activation slope). Products drift exponentially: 0.9³⁰ ≈ 0.04, 1.1³⁰ ≈ 17.
  • Vanishing: flat loss, tiny early-layer gradients. Exploding: spikes, inf/NaN, dead ReLUs. RNNs are the extreme case (one weight, reused every step).
  • Fixes for the cause: ReLU (0.4), Xavier/He init (1.4), residuals (2.2), normalization (2.3), LSTM gates (5.3), a sane learning rate (1.6).
  • Gradient clipping limits the damage of exploding steps: by norm (g ← c·g/‖g‖ if ‖g‖ > c) keeps the direction; by value bends it. LLMs commonly clip at 1.0.
  • Log the norm that clip_grad_norm_ returns: it's your early warning. Clipping is a seatbelt, not a fix for bad init or a too-high learning rate.
