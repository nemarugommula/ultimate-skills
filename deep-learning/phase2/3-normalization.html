Phase 2 · Lesson 3

Normalization: BatchNorm → LayerNorm → RMSNorm

Subtract a mean, divide by a spread: the oldest trick in statistics, applied inside the network at every layer. It let image models train 14× faster, and its descendant runs at every layer of today's LLMs. The whole story is which numbers you average over.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Mean and standard deviation (spread) of a list of numbers; “standardizing” = (x − mean) / std (math 5.1).
  • Activations that drift too big or too small make gradients explode or vanish (Lesson 2.1); a residual sum grows with depth unless something rescales it (Lesson 2.2).
  • A mini-batch is the handful of examples processed together in one training step (Lesson 1.7).
🎯 Hook

In 2015 Sergey Ioffe and Christian Szegedy at Google added one operation after the layers of their Inception image classifier: normalize each feature using the mean and variance of the current mini-batch. The new network reached the same accuracy in 14 times fewer training steps, and an ensemble hit 4.9% top-5 error on ImageNet, which they reported as “exceeding the accuracy of human raters”.

Their explanation (“internal covariate shift”) was later challenged: a 2018 MIT paper argued that stable layer inputs have “little to do with” why it works. And for transformers, the batch version was replaced by LayerNorm, then by the even simpler RMSNorm that LLaMA uses.

Why normalize at all, why did the field switch from averaging over the batch to averaging over each example, and what bug does BatchNorm hide in your eval code? You'll see exactly which cells get averaged, watch batch statistics fall apart at batch size 2, and catch a classic train/eval bug.

The next layer sees one input that barely moves and one in the hundreds. A learning rate small enough for unit 2 is useless for unit 1. If a sigmoid or tanh follows, unit 2 is saturated (slope ≈ 0). And as the earlier layers train, these scales keep changing, so the next layer chases a moving target.

The fix is the same one you'd use on a spreadsheet column: standardize. Subtract the mean, divide by the spread, and every unit lands around 0 with spread 1.

x̂ = (x − mean) / √(variance + ε)   then   y = γ·x̂ + β

ε (about 10⁻⁵) avoids dividing by zero. γ (scale) and β (shift) are learned, one pair per feature, starting at 1 and 0, so the network can undo the normalization if that's what it needs. The only real question is: mean and variance of which numbers?

💡 Big idea Every normalization layer computes the same thing, (x − mean)/std then γ, β. They differ only in which cells they average over: BatchNorm averages one feature across the batch; LayerNorm averages one example across its features; RMSNorm does LayerNorm's job but skips the mean; GroupNorm averages one example across a group of channels. That one choice decides batch-size sensitivity, train/eval behaviour and where each one is used.
🧠 Analogy: grading on a curve

BatchNorm grades each exam question on a curve using this week's class: your score depends on who else sat the exam that day. A class of two and the curve is meaningless. LayerNorm grades each student against their own scores on all questions: no classmates needed, and a class of one works fine. RMSNorm does the same but only asks “how big are your scores overall?”, not “what's your average?”.

BatchNorm: average over the batch

✏️ Worked example: one feature, a batch of 4

A feature takes the values 2, 4, 6, 8 across the batch.

  • Mean = (2 + 4 + 6 + 8)/4 = 5. Variance = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5, std = √5 ≈ 2.236.
  • x̂ = (x − 5)/2.236 → −1.34, −0.45, 0.45, 1.34: mean 0, spread 1.
  • With learned γ = 2, β = 1: y = 2x̂ + 1 → −1.68, 0.11, 1.89, 3.68.
  • Parameters: 2 per feature (γ, β). A 512-feature BatchNorm has 1,024 learned numbers, plus 1,024 running statistics (mean and variance) that are not learned by gradients.

Running statistics. At test time you may predict one image at a time, so there's no batch to average. During training BatchNorm keeps a moving average of each feature's mean and variance (PyTorch: running = 0.9 × running + 0.1 × batch, momentum 0.1). model.eval() switches to those stored numbers; model.train() switches back to batch statistics. Forget the switch and you get the bug at the end of this lesson.

🤔 Why this? Batch normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015)
  1. The problem before Deep networks needed small learning rates and careful init; as earlier layers learned, later layers' inputs kept changing scale, and saturating activations got stuck. Ioffe & Szegedy named this internal covariate shift: “the change in the distribution of network activations due to the change in network parameters during training”.
  2. What people tried Careful initialization (Lesson 1.4), standardizing only the input data, lower learning rates, ReLU (0.4).
  3. Why this works Standardize every feature with the batch's mean and variance, then let γ, β rescale. Activations stay in a good range however the earlier weights move, so you can use “much higher learning rates and be less careful about initialization”: 14× fewer steps for Inception. The batch noise also acts as a mild regularizer. Later, Santurkar et al. (2018) showed BN works even when they add distribution shift back, and argued the real effect is a smoother loss landscape with more predictable gradients. Both stories agree on what it does to training; they disagree on why.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each example's output depends on its batch-mates. Small batches make the statistics noisy (next playground: at batch 2 the output can only be ±1). Train and eval behave differently (running stats), a classic source of bugs. Batch size 1 is impossible in training. Variable-length sequences and RNNs are awkward (statistics per time step), and multi-GPU training needs synchronized statistics (SyncBatchNorm) when per-GPU batches are small.
  5. What refined it LayerNorm (2016) for sequences and transformers; GroupNorm (2018) for small-batch vision; RMSNorm (2019) for speed; and normalizer-free ResNets with adaptive gradient clipping (2021, Lesson 2.1) for people who wanted BN gone entirely. BN is still standard in CNNs trained with decent batch sizes (Phase 3).

LayerNorm: average over each example's features

Ba, Kiros and Hinton (2016) simply turned the averaging 90°. Each example is normalized using the mean and variance of its own features. No batch involved: the same computation at training and test time, any batch size, and in a transformer, each token is normalized on its own. Same numbers 2, 4, 6, 8, but now they're the 4 features of one token: x̂ = −1.34, −0.45, 0.45, 1.34, computed without looking at any other token.

🤔 Why this? Layer normalization (Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016)
  1. The problem before BatchNorm “is dependent on the mini-batch size and it is not obvious how to apply it to recurrent neural networks”. An RNN applying BN naively would need separate statistics for every time step, which fails for a test sequence longer than any training sequence. Language models also predict one sequence at a time.
  2. What people tried BN per time step, or no normalization at all in RNNs (with careful init and gradient clipping, Lesson 2.1).
  3. Why this works Average over the features of one example (one token). It “performs exactly the same computation at training and test times”, works at batch size 1, needs no running statistics and doesn't leak information between examples. In transformers, every token at every layer gets its scale reset, which keeps the residual stream (Lesson 2.2) in range.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It assumes the features of a layer are comparable enough to share one mean and variance. In convolutional networks that's often false (edge pixels' units behave very differently), and the LayerNorm authors themselves found BN better for CNNs: on ResNet-50 ImageNet with batch 32 per GPU, Wu & He measured 25.3% error for LN vs 23.6% for BN. It needs two passes over each token's features (mean, then variance).
  5. What refined it RMSNorm (drop the mean, next), and moving it from after the residual add (post-norm, the 2017 transformer) to inside the branch (pre-norm, which the LLaMA paper credits to GPT-3), which trains deep transformers far more stably (Lesson 6.4).

RMSNorm: skip the mean

Zhang and Sennrich (2019) asked whether LayerNorm's re-centering (subtracting the mean) matters, or only its re-scaling. RMSNorm keeps only the scaling: divide by the root mean square, then multiply by γ. No mean, usually no β.

RMS(x) = √(mean(x²) + ε)   ·   y = γ · x / RMS(x)

For 2, 4, 6, 8: mean of squares = (4 + 16 + 36 + 64)/4 = 30, RMS = √30 ≈ 5.48, so y = 0.37, 0.73, 1.10, 1.46. Not centered at 0 any more, but the overall size is fixed at 1.

🤔 Why this? RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019)
  1. The problem before LayerNorm runs at every layer, for every token, and its extra work “significantly slows the underlying network”, especially RNNs, in their measurements.
  2. What people tried LayerNorm everywhere; fused GPU kernels to hide its cost.
  3. Why this works Their hypothesis: “re-centering invariance in LayerNorm is dispensable”; what stabilizes training is controlling the scale. Dropping the mean removes one reduction and one subtraction per value. In their experiments RMSNorm matched LayerNorm's quality while cutting running time by 7% to 64% depending on the model.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Outputs aren't centered, so if a zero mean matters, the network has to learn it elsewhere. The speedup is smaller in modern fused kernels than in the paper's 2019 setups. It's still a per-token reduction that must happen in higher precision for stability.
  5. What refined it LLaMA (2023): “we normalize the input of each transformer sub-layer… We use the RMSNorm normalizing function”. Llama 2 kept the same recipe, and many open LLMs followed. It's built into PyTorch as nn.RMSNorm.

GroupNorm: the small-batch vision fix

CNN features are channels × height × width. GroupNorm (Wu & He, 2018) splits each example's channels into groups (32 by default) and normalizes within each group, over the channels and all pixels. Like LayerNorm it ignores the batch; unlike LayerNorm it doesn't force all channels to share one mean. With batches of 2 images per GPU on ImageNet ResNet-50, GN had 24.1% error vs BatchNorm's 34.7%; at the usual 32 per GPU they're close (24.1% vs 23.6%). GroupNorm with 1 group is LayerNorm; with one channel per group it's instance norm.

The train/eval bug

A BatchNorm model trained on house sizes, deciding “bigger than average?”. During training it learned running statistics: mean 120 m², spread 40 m². The answer is “big” when the normalized size is above 0. We score it on 8 houses, the 4 largest of which really are big.

In real code this bug looks like “validation accuracy is great in the training loop and terrible in production”.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

nn.BatchNorm1d/2d (features / image channels), nn.LayerNorm(d), nn.RMSNorm(d), nn.GroupNorm(groups, channels). This snippet checks each one against the formula by hand, then shows the train/eval switch.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
x = torch.randn(4, 6) * 3 + 5          # a batch of 4 examples, 6 features

# BatchNorm: average each FEATURE over the batch (dim 0)
bn = nn.BatchNorm1d(6)                 # γ = 1, β = 0 at init
y = bn(x)                              # train mode: uses this batch's mean and variance
mine = (x - x.mean(0)) / torch.sqrt(x.var(0, unbiased=False) + bn.eps)
print("BatchNorm matches:", torch.allclose(y, mine, atol=1e-5))

# LayerNorm: average each EXAMPLE over its features (last dim)
ln = nn.LayerNorm(6)
mine = (x - x.mean(-1, keepdim=True)) / torch.sqrt(x.var(-1, unbiased=False, keepdim=True) + ln.eps)
print("LayerNorm matches:", torch.allclose(ln(x), mine, atol=1e-5))

# RMSNorm: no mean subtraction, divide by the root mean square
rms = nn.RMSNorm(6, eps=1e-6)
mine = x / torch.sqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + 1e-6)
print("RMSNorm matches:  ", torch.allclose(rms(x), mine, atol=1e-5))

# The train/eval switch only matters for BatchNorm
print("running mean after 1 batch:", bn.running_mean[:3])   # 0.9·0 + 0.1·batch mean
print("train output, example 0:", y[0, :3].detach())
bn.eval()                                                    # uses running stats now
print("eval output,  example 0:", bn(x)[0, :3].detach())

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# BatchNorm matches: True
# LayerNorm matches: True

# train output, example 0: tensor([-1.1385, -0.4977, -1.1274])
# eval output,  example 0: tensor([0.6774, 0.7767, 3.5744])

After one batch the running mean has moved only 10% of the way to the true mean (about 5), so the eval output is badly off. It converges over hundreds of steps. Gotchas: call model.eval() before validation and inference (and model.train() after); BatchNorm uses the biased variance in the forward pass; set bias=False on a linear/conv layer right before a BatchNorm (β already shifts it); for multi-GPU training with small per-GPU batches, convert with nn.SyncBatchNorm.convert_sync_batchnorm(model).

🎤 Interview angle

“BatchNorm vs LayerNorm: what's the difference, and why do transformers use LayerNorm or RMSNorm?” A strong answer:

  • Draws the tensor: BN averages each feature over the batch (and spatial positions); LN averages each example over its features; both then apply learned γ, β.
  • Consequences: BN depends on batch size and batch-mates, needs running statistics for eval (train/eval bug), and struggles with small batches and variable-length sequences; LN is per token, identical at train and test.
  • RMSNorm drops the mean (only rescales), is cheaper and is what LLaMA-style models use, in pre-norm position (6.4). GroupNorm is the small-batch vision answer.
  • Is honest about why normalization helps: the original “internal covariate shift” story vs the smoother-landscape view (Santurkar et al., 2018). Both agree it allows higher learning rates.
🏭 In the real world
14× fewer steps for Inception 🏢 Google · 2015 Ioffe & Szegedy: Batch Normalization

How: normalize every layer's inputs per mini-batch, with learned γ, β and running statistics for inference; raise the learning rate and remove dropout.

Why this method: the same accuracy in 14 times fewer steps, and an ensemble reached 4.9% top-5 validation error on ImageNet.

Why does BN really work? 🧪 Research · 2018 Santurkar, Tsipras, Ilyas & Madry: How Does Batch Normalization Help Optimization?

How: injected noise after BN layers to re-create distribution shift, then measured the loss landscape's smoothness and gradient predictability.

Why this method: it separated two explanations: BN still worked with the shift added back, pointing to a smoother optimization landscape rather than stable input distributions.

RMSNorm in LLaMA 🏢 Meta · 2023 Touvron et al.: LLaMA

How: pre-normalization: the input of each attention and feed-forward sub-layer goes through RMSNorm, instead of normalizing the output.

Why this method: “to improve the training stability”, with RMSNorm as the cheaper normalizer; the recipe became the template for many open LLMs.

Small batches in detection 🧪 Research · 2018 Wu & He: Group Normalization

How: normalize over groups of channels within each image; also tested on COCO detection and segmentation and video classification.

Why this method: these tasks fit only a few high-resolution images per GPU, exactly where batch statistics fall apart (10.6 points better than BN at batch 2 on ImageNet).

🤯 Fun fact · batch size 2 is a coin

Standardize any two different numbers and you always get exactly −1 and +1. So a BatchNorm layer trained with batch size 2 turns every feature of every example into a binary ±1 signal (before γ and β), throwing away how far apart the values were. With B examples, no normalized value can ever exceed √(B − 1) in size: you saw that limit in the noise playground.

Check yourself

1. A feature has values 1, 3, 5, 7 across a batch of 4. What is the BatchNorm output (γ = 1, β = 0, ignore ε) for the example with value 7?
Mean = 4; variance = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5; std ≈ 2.236. (7 − 4)/2.236 ≈ 1.34. Same as 2, 4, 6, 8: shifting all values doesn't change the normalized output.
2. Your image classifier with BatchNorm scores 92% in the training script's validation loop but 60% in the serving code, which predicts one image at a time. What's the likely bug?
In train mode BN uses the current batch's statistics; with one image (or a batch of similar images) those are meaningless. model.eval() switches to running statistics (and turns off dropout too, Lesson 2.4). Check mode first whenever train-time and deploy-time numbers disagree.
3. You're building a language model that will generate text one token at a time for a single user. Which normalization fits best?
Generation runs at batch size 1 with sequences of any length; batch statistics don't exist there. Per-token normalization behaves identically in training and generation, which is why transformers use LayerNorm and LLaMA-style models use RMSNorm (Lesson 6.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Normalization = (x − mean)/√(var + ε), then learned γ, β. It keeps every layer's inputs in a good range, so you can use higher learning rates.
  • BatchNorm averages each feature over the batch: 14× faster Inception training, but depends on batch-mates, is noisy at small batches (±1 at batch 2), and needs running statistics, so model.eval() matters.
  • Why it helps is debated: “internal covariate shift” (2015) vs a smoother loss landscape (2018).
  • LayerNorm averages each example over its features: batch-independent, same at train and test, the transformer default. RMSNorm drops the mean: cheaper, equally good, used in LLaMA-style LLMs.
  • GroupNorm normalizes groups of channels per image: the fix for 1 or 2 images per GPU (24.1% vs BN's 34.7% error at batch 2).
