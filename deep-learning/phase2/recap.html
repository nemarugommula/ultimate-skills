Recap: Making it train

Why deep networks fail to train and the tools that fixed it: gradient products and clipping, residual highways, normalization, regularization, augmentation, and a debugging routine. Here's the phase on one page, a symptom-to-fix game, and a broken 40-layer network to repair yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

📉

2.1 Vanishing & exploding

Gradient at layer 1 = product of per-layer factors (weight scale × slope). 0.9³⁰ ≈ 0.04, 1.1³⁰ ≈ 17. Clip the global norm at 1.0.

Vanishing: flat loss, tiny early gradients. Exploding: spikes, NaN, dead ReLUs. RNNs are the extreme case. Clipping by norm keeps the direction; it doesn't fix vanishing.

🧰 clip_grad_norm_ (returns the norm: log it), clip_grad_value_

🛣️

2.2 Residual connections

y = x + F(x): gradient × (1 + F′) per block, 2ⁿ paths, “do nothing” = F → 0.

Fixed the degradation problem (plain-56 worse than plain-20 on training error). Zero parameters with matching shapes. In every ResNet and transformer.

🧰 return x + self.f(x), torchvision resnet50

⚖️

2.3 Normalization

(x − mean)/√(var + ε) · γ + β. BN: over the batch. LN: over features. RMSNorm: no mean. GN: groups of channels.

BN: 14× fewer steps, but batch-dependent, noisy at batch 2 (±1 only), needs model.eval(). LN/RMSNorm per token for transformers (LLaMA: pre-norm RMSNorm).

🧰 nn.BatchNorm2d, nn.LayerNorm, nn.RMSNorm, nn.GroupNorm

🎲

2.4 Regularization

Dropout: zero with prob p, scale survivors 1/(1 − p). Weight decay 0.1 with AdamW. Early stopping on val loss.

Overfitting = train/val gap (100% vs 71% in the playground). LLM pretraining often uses no dropout but keeps weight decay. Double descent and grokking break the simple picture.

🧰 nn.Dropout, torch.optim.AdamW, best-checkpoint saving

🔄

2.5 Data augmentation

Only label-preserving transforms. AlexNet: crops + flips = 2,048 variants per image. CutMix: labels by area.

Flips, crops, color, rotation; mixup/CutMix (ResNet-50 23.68% → 21.40%); RandAugment (2 knobs), TrivialAugment (0); SpecAugment for audio. 6 ↔ 9 is the classic trap.

🧰 torchvision.transforms.v2: RandomResizedCrop, TrivialAugmentWide, MixUp, CutMix

🩺

2.6 Debugging

Initial loss ≈ ln K (0.693, 2.303, 6.908). Overfit one batch to ≈ 0. Look at the data first.

Curve shapes: flat at ln K, slow line, jagged → NaN, U-shaped val, val bad from epoch 1, sawtooth, too good to be true. LR range test: ÷10 from the minimum.

🧰 per-layer grad norms, set_detect_anomaly, TensorBoard / W&B

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “Why couldn't people train 100-layer networks in 2014, and what changed? Then: our 24-layer transformer's loss goes NaN after 2,000 steps. What do you check, in what order?”

A strong answer covers: backprop multiplies one factor per layer, so gradients vanish or explode exponentially with depth; ReLU and He init fixed step 0; residual connections gave a factor-1 path (and 2ⁿ short paths); normalization kept every layer's inputs in range so learning rates could go up. For the NaN: look at the gradient-norm log for a sudden jump, check the learning rate and warmup, make sure gradients are clipped (max_norm 1.0) after unscaling, look for inf/NaN in the offending batch, prefer bf16 over fp16, check pre-norm placement; if it's rare and late, rewind to a checkpoint and skip the batch (PaLM). And before any of that, an overfit-one-batch test and an initial-loss check would have ruled out wiring bugs.

🏗️ Build it yourself: rescue a 40-layer network

✏️ The problem

A colleague's image classifier won't learn. It's an MLP on 28 × 28 grayscale images (784 inputs), 40 hidden layers of 256 units with sigmoid, weights drawn from N(0, 1) (standard deviation 1), no normalization, no skips, 10 classes, plain SGD with learning rate 0.1, batch size 128, 60,000 training images. The loss sits at about 2.30 for hours, then sometimes prints NaN.

Using this phase's tools, diagnose it with numbers, then fix it one change at a time, saying what each change fixes and how you'd verify it. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Size and first checks

  • Parameters: input layer 784 × 256 + 256 = 200,960; 39 hidden-to-hidden layers × (256 × 256 + 256) = 39 × 65,792 = 2,565,888; output 256 × 10 + 10 = 2,570. Total ≈ 2.77 million.
  • Loss stuck at 2.30 = ln 10: the network outputs nearly uniform probabilities. Not learning at all (Lesson 2.6).
  • Gradient product: sigmoid's slope ≤ 0.25, so even at its best 0.25³⁹ ≈ 3 × 10⁻²⁴. And with std 1 weights, each unit's pre-activation has spread √256 = 16: almost every sigmoid is saturated (slope ≈ 0) (Lessons 0.4, 2.1).
  • The occasional NaN: with std-1 weights, any unit that does land in its steep region gets huge updates; there's no clipping (2.1).

The fix, one step at a time

  1. Sanity checks first. Visualize a batch with labels; check the initial loss ≈ 2.30; overfit one batch of 16 images. This one will fail, which tells you the problem is the model and optimizer, not the data (2.6).
  2. ReLU (or GELU) instead of sigmoid. Slope 1 when active instead of ≤ 0.25 (0.4).
  3. He init, std = √(2/256) ≈ 0.088 instead of 1: per-layer factor ≈ 1 at step 0 (1.4). Verify: per-layer gradient norms within 10× of each other at step 1 (the 2.1 playground).
  4. Residual blocks: 20 blocks of x + F(x), F = Linear → ReLU → Linear. A factor-1 path through all 40 layers (2.2).
  5. Normalization inside each block: LayerNorm (or BatchNorm1d with batch 128), pre-norm style: x + F(Norm(x)) (2.3). Remember model.eval() if you use BatchNorm.
  6. Optimizer: AdamW, learning rate from an LR range test (likely around 1e-3), a short warmup and cosine decay, weight decay 0.05 on weight matrices only (1.5, 1.6, 2.4).
  7. Clip the gradient norm at 1.0 and log the returned norm: the NaNs stop, and spikes become visible (2.1).
  8. Now regularize, only if there's a train/val gap: small random shifts and rotations (no flips: digits aren't symmetric), dropout 0.1, early stopping on validation loss (2.4, 2.5).

Verify each step

  • After steps 2–5: the overfit-one-batch test reaches ≈ 0 in a few hundred steps; per-layer gradient norms stay within an order of magnitude; dead-unit fraction below ~50%.
  • After 6–7: training loss falls smoothly from 2.30; no NaN; the gradient norm log has no spikes.
  • After 8: the train/val gap shrinks; keep the best checkpoint.

Classic mistakes

  • Changing five things at once, so you never learn which one mattered. Fix the seed and change one thing at a time.
  • Adding dropout to a network that can't fit the training data (it makes underfitting worse).
  • Clipping gradients and declaring victory while the cause (bad init, sigmoid) is still there.
  • Horizontal flips or 180° rotations on digits (6 ↔ 9).
  • Honestly: for images, a small CNN (Phase 3) would beat a 40-layer MLP with a fraction of the parameters.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A 50-layer plain network where each layer multiplies the backward signal by about 0.85. Roughly what fraction of the output gradient reaches layer 1?
Factors multiply: 0.85⁴⁹ ≈ 0.00035 (49 layers above layer 1). A residual connection would make each factor about 1 + F′ instead, and normalization would keep it near 1 (Lessons 2.1, 2.2).
2. A segmentation model fits only 2 high-resolution images per GPU. Training with BatchNorm is unstable and accuracy is poor. Best change?
With 2 examples, standardizing gives ±1 only, and the statistics jump around. GroupNorm normalizes within each image and was designed for this: 24.1% vs 34.7% error at batch 2 on ImageNet (Lesson 2.3).
3. With inverted dropout p = 0.25, a kept unit's output of 3.0 becomes what during training, and what at test time?
Survivors are scaled by 1/(1 − 0.25) = 4/3: 3.0 → 4.0. The expected value is 0.75 × 4.0 = 3.0, so test time (all units, no scaling) matches (Lesson 2.4).
4. A new 1,000-class model's first logged loss is 0.4. What do you conclude?
Before training, the model can't know the answers: uniform guessing gives −ln(1/1000) = 6.91. A starting loss of 0.4 means the answer is reaching the model by another route, or the loss isn't measuring what you think (Lesson 2.6).
5. Gradient (0.3, 0.4, 1.2) and you clip by global norm with max_norm 0.65. What comes out?
‖g‖ = √(0.09 + 0.16 + 1.44) = √1.69 = 1.3 > 0.65, so scale by 0.65/1.3 = 0.5 → (0.15, 0.2, 0.6): same direction, length 0.65. (0.3, 0.4, 0.65) is clip-by-value, which bends the direction (Lesson 2.1).
