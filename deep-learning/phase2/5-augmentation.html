Phase 2 · Lesson 5

Data augmentation: more data for free

A cat flipped left to right is still a cat. Encode that one fact in your data loader and your training set gets twice as big for free. Do it to the digit 6 upside down and you've just taught your model that 6s look like 9s.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Overfitting shows as a gap between training and validation accuracy; more data is the best cure (Lesson 2.4, math 5.6).
  • An image is a grid of numbers: height × width × color channels (math 2.3).
  • A soft label like “70% cat, 30% dog” works fine with cross-entropy loss (Lesson 1.1).
🎯 Hook

AlexNet (2012) trained on 1.2 million labeled images, and its authors still wrote that without data augmentation “our network suffers from substantial overfitting”. Their fix: train on random 224 × 224 crops of each 256 × 256 image, randomly mirrored. That “increases the size of our training set by a factor of 2048”, computed on the CPU while the GPU trained, “in effect, computationally free”. A second trick, jittering the colors, cut the top-1 error by over 1%.

Today, every image model's training script starts with an augmentation pipeline. Speech recognizers blank out stripes of spectrograms; classifiers learn from images blended 70/30 with other images, with labels blended to match.

Which transformations are “free data” and which secretly change the answer? You'll augment tiny pictures and catch a 6 turning into a 9, then watch augmentation shrink a train/validation gap live.

Numbers first: where AlexNet's 2048 comes from

A 256 × 256 image has (256 − 224) × (256 − 224) = 32 × 32 = 1,024 possible positions for a 224 × 224 crop. Each can be mirrored or not: × 2 = 2,048 versions of every photo. They overlap heavily (“highly interdependent”, as the authors say), so this isn't 2,048 times the information. But every epoch the network sees a slightly different copy, so it can't memorize exact pixels.

💡 Big idea Data augmentation is knowledge you already have about your problem, written as transformations that change the input but not the label. Each one teaches the network an invariance (“position doesn't matter”, “lighting doesn't matter”) without a single new label. The rule that matters: only use transformations under which the label truly stays the same, for your task.
🧠 Analogy: flashcards in different handwriting

Learning a language from one set of flashcards, you might memorize the coffee stain on card 12. Rewrite the cards in different handwriting, sizes and ink colors every week, and the only thing left to memorize is the word itself. But don't rewrite “b” upside down: that's “q”.

✏️ Worked example: how much data did you just make?
  • 10,000 training images, horizontal flip (× 2) and a random crop with 4 pixels of padding on a 32 × 32 image, the classic CIFAR-10 recipe (the one the ResNet paper uses): 9 × 9 = 81 positions × 2 = 162 variants each, about 1.6 million distinct inputs.
  • Training for 100 epochs shows each image 100 times. Without augmentation, 100 identical copies. With it, 100 draws out of 162 variants: most epochs see a version never seen before.
  • Cost: no new labels, no extra storage (variants are made on the fly), a little CPU time per batch in the data loader.
🤔 Why this? Data augmentation
  1. The problem before Labeled data is expensive, and big networks memorize small datasets. Even with 1.2 million ImageNet images, AlexNet “suffers from substantial overfitting” without augmentation.
  2. What people tried Collecting and labeling more data (slow, costly), smaller models, dropout and weight decay (Lesson 2.4), hand-engineering invariant features.
  3. Why this works You often know transformations that can't change the answer. Applying them randomly during training gives the network many versions of each example (2,048 per photo for AlexNet) and forces it to rely on what survives the transformation: shape rather than exact pixel position, object rather than lighting. It's regularization through the data, and the knowledge is yours, not learned.
  4. What it costs, when it fails A transformation that changes the label teaches a lie (6 ↔ 9, mirrored text, a left-turn arrow flipped into a right-turn one, left vs right in medical scans). Too strong and the model underfits (you'll see it below). It adds CPU work to the data pipeline, which can become the bottleneck on fast GPUs. And it can't create information that isn't in the data: no amount of flipping teaches a new breed of dog.
  5. What refined it Learned and random policies: AutoAugment (2018) searched for the best policy (expensive), RandAugment (2019) cut that to two numbers N and M, TrivialAugment (2021) to zero (one random op per image). Label-mixing methods: mixup and CutMix (next). Domain versions: SpecAugment for speech, back-translation for text. In self-supervised learning (Lesson 9.1) augmentations become the training task itself.

Mixing images: mixup and CutMix

A stranger idea: blend two training images and blend their labels to match. mixup (Zhang et al., 2017) takes a weighted average of two whole images, x̃ = λ·x₁ + (1 − λ)·x₂, with label ỹ = λ·y₁ + (1 − λ)·y₂, where λ is drawn from a Beta(α, α) distribution. CutMix (Yun et al., 2019) instead cuts a rectangle out of one image, pastes in the same region of another, and mixes the labels by area.

What you should see: mixup at λ = 0.7 gives a ghostly overlay of both digits with the label “70% six, 30% two”. CutMix with a 4 × 4 patch keeps 48 of 64 pixels from the 6, so the label is 75% six, 25% two (16 ÷ 64 = 25% of the area came from the 2). Every pixel of a CutMix image is a real pixel from some image, which is why it holds up better for tasks that need to find objects.

🤔 Why this? mixup and CutMix
  1. The problem before Networks trained the normal way become extremely confident, memorize even random labels, and behave erratically between training examples. Cutout (erasing a random square, 2017) helped regularize but throws pixels away: “information loss and inefficiency”, in the CutMix authors' words.
  2. What people tried Label smoothing, dropout, cutout, stronger single-image augmentation.
  3. Why this works mixup trains on “convex combinations of pairs of examples and their labels”, so the network learns to behave linearly between examples and can't be 100% sure about blended inputs. CutMix keeps every pixel real, swapping regions instead of blending, and mixes labels by area, so the model must recognize objects from partial views. On ImageNet ResNet-50, top-1 error went from 23.68% (baseline) to 22.58% with mixup and 21.40% with CutMix.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Training takes longer to converge and training accuracy looks worse (the labels are soft). Large α in mixup underfits (the paper found α between 0.1 and 0.4 worked on ImageNet). Blended images can hurt localization tasks: mixup lowered detection scores in the CutMix paper's transfer tests. It needs a loss that accepts soft labels.
  5. What refined it Using them together or alternately (RandomChoice([MixUp, CutMix])), part of modern image-classifier recipes alongside RandAugment or TrivialAugment, label smoothing and long training (vision transformers, Lesson 4.5).

What you should see (seeded): with no augmentation, 100% training accuracy and about 85% validation: a 15-point gap, with the boundary bending around flipped points. Jitter σ = 0.3: training accuracy drops to about 87% (it stopped memorizing the wrong labels) and validation rises to about 97%: the gap is gone, even reversed. Beyond images, and at test time

Audio: SpecAugment (Park et al., 2019) works directly on the spectrogram (Lesson 8.1): it warps it in time and masks random blocks of consecutive time steps and frequency channels, so a speech recognizer can't rely on any one slice. Text is harder, because changing a word can change the meaning: EDA (2019) uses four simple edits (synonym replacement, random insertion, random swap, random deletion) and reported gains on five classification tasks; back-translation (Sennrich et al., 2016) translates sentences to another language and back to create paraphrases, introduced to create extra training data for machine translation. For LLMs, the usual “augmentation” is simply more and more varied data.

Test-time augmentation (TTA): run the trained model on several augmented copies of a test image and average the predictions. AlexNet already did it: five 224 × 224 crops (four corners and the center) plus their mirror images, ten in all, with the softmax outputs averaged. It costs N× inference compute for a small accuracy gain, so it's common in competitions and medical imaging, rarer in latency-sensitive products.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In PyTorch, image augmentation lives in torchvision.transforms.v2, which works on plain tensors and also transforms bounding boxes and masks consistently (handy for detection, Phase 4). Augment the training set only; validation and test get just resize and normalize.

import torch
from torchvision.transforms import v2

torch.manual_seed(0)
train_tf = v2.Compose([
    v2.RandomResizedCrop(224, scale=(0.08, 1.0), antialias=True),   # random crop + resize
    v2.RandomHorizontalFlip(p=0.5),
    v2.TrivialAugmentWide(),                       # one random op, random strength
    v2.ToDtype(torch.float32, scale=True),         # uint8 0..255 → float 0..1
])
img = torch.randint(0, 256, (3, 256, 320), dtype=torch.uint8)   # a fake RGB image
print(train_tf(img).shape, train_tf(img).dtype)

# mixup / CutMix work on whole batches (images + integer labels)
images = torch.rand(8, 3, 224, 224)
labels = torch.randint(0, 10, (8,))
mix = v2.RandomChoice([v2.MixUp(num_classes=10, alpha=0.2), v2.CutMix(num_classes=10)])
images, soft = mix(images, labels)
print(soft.shape, soft[0].sum().item(), (soft[0] > 0).sum().item(), "classes in label 0")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, torchvision 0.23):
# torch.Size([3, 224, 224]) torch.float32
# torch.Size([8, 10]) 1.0 2 classes in label 0

The soft labels are rows of 10 probabilities summing to 1, with two non-zero classes: pass them straight to nn.CrossEntropyLoss, which accepts probabilities as targets. Apply MixUp/CutMix after batching (in the training loop or a collate_fn), since they need pairs of images. Gotcha: spatial transforms must move boxes, masks and keypoints too; v2 does that if you pass them as tv_tensors.

🎤 Interview angle

“You have 5,000 labeled images and your model overfits. How do you use augmentation?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from the task's invariances: which transformations keep the label (crops, flips, color, small rotations) and which don't (vertical flips for scenes, horizontal flips for text or left/right-sensitive classes, 6 vs 9).
  • Augments training data only, on the fly, and checks the effect on the train/val gap.
  • Knows the modern menu: RandAugment/TrivialAugment, mixup/CutMix with soft labels, SpecAugment for audio, back-translation for text; transfer learning from a pretrained model (Lesson 3.5) as the other big lever.
  • Mentions the pitfalls: label-breaking ops, too-strong augmentation underfitting, the data loader becoming the bottleneck, and TTA's inference cost.
🏭 In the real world
Crops, flips and color for AlexNet 📜 History · 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton: AlexNet

How: random 224 × 224 crops plus mirroring (2,048 variants per image), PCA-based color jitter, and 10-crop averaging at test time.

Why this method: generated on the CPU while the GPU trained, so “computationally free”; without it the 60-million-parameter network overfit badly.

CutMix on ImageNet 🏢 NAVER Clova · 2019 Yun et al.: CutMix

How: paste a patch from one training image into another and mix labels by area.

Why this method: keeps every pixel informative; ResNet-50 top-1 error 23.68% → 21.40%, and unlike mixup and Cutout it also improved transfer to detection.

Masking spectrograms for speech 🏢 Google · 2019 Park et al.: SpecAugment

How: time warping plus masking blocks of consecutive time steps and frequency channels of the log mel spectrogram, applied online during training.

Why this method: it needs no extra data and makes the recognizer robust to missing slices of sound; it's applied to the spectrogram the network already reads, so it's cheap.

One random op, zero knobs 🧪 Research · 2021 Müller & Hutter: TrivialAugment

How: for each image, pick one augmentation at random and a random strength. Built into torchvision as TrivialAugmentWide.

Why this method: “parameter-free”, nothing to search or tune; its authors report that it “outperforms previous methods for almost free”.

🤯 Fun fact · the 6 and the 9 are the same picture

In this course's 8 × 8 digit set, rotating the 6 by 180° produces exactly the 9, shifted down by one row: the template matcher scores it 1.00, a perfect match. Rotation is a perfectly reasonable augmentation for photos of cells under a microscope and a disastrous one for house numbers. The data doesn't know which task you're solving; you do.

🌉 How it connects

Augmentation is regularization through the data, next to dropout and weight decay (2.4). Convolutions build in some invariance to shifts by design (Lesson 3.2); transfer learning (3.5) is the other answer to small datasets. Detection and segmentation need box- and mask-aware augmentation (Phase 4); SpecAugment appears with spectrograms and speech (8.1, 8.2); self-supervised methods like SimCLR turn “two augmentations of the same image should match” into the whole training signal (9.1); adversarial examples (9.5) are the worst-case version of a small perturbation. Images as tensors: math 2.3.

Check yourself

1. CutMix pastes a 112 × 112 patch from a dog photo into a 224 × 224 cat photo. What label does the mixed image get?
Labels are mixed by area: 112² ÷ 224² = 12,544 ÷ 50,176 = 25% of the pixels came from the dog, so 0.75 cat + 0.25 dog. A 112-pixel side is half the width, but only a quarter of the area.
2. You're training a model to read street-sign text. A teammate adds random horizontal flips “because they always help”. What do you say?
An augmentation is only free data if the label survives it. Mirrored letters aren't the same letters, and a left arrow flipped is a right arrow. Pick transformations that match the task's real invariances.
3. With augmentation, your training accuracy dropped from 100% to 88% and validation rose from 80% to 87%. Is that good?
Augmented training examples are harder, so training accuracy falling is expected. Validation improved and the gap closed: classic regularization. If validation had fallen too, then suspect too-strong or label-breaking augmentation.
📝 TL;DR
  • Augmentation applies random, label-preserving transformations during training: crops, flips, color, small rotations, noise, cutout. AlexNet's crops + flips gave 2,048 variants per image.
  • It's your knowledge of the task's invariances. Label-breaking transforms (6 ↔ 9, mirrored text, x-flipping the moons) are worse than none.
  • mixup blends two images and their labels; CutMix pastes a patch and mixes labels by area (ResNet-50: 23.68% → 21.40% top-1 error).
  • RandAugment (2 knobs) and TrivialAugment (0 knobs) replace hand-tuned pipelines. SpecAugment for audio; EDA and back-translation for text.
  • Augment the training set only; test-time augmentation averages predictions over copies at N× cost. In PyTorch: torchvision.transforms.v2.
