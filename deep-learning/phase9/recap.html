Recap: Frontier & robustness

Learning without labels, networks for graphs, attention's fixed-memory rival, looking inside models, attacking them, and knowing when not to trust them. Here's the phase on one page, a game where you diagnose why a model failed, and a medical model to design so a hospital can trust it.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🪞

9.1 Self-supervised learning

InfoNCE = cross-entropy over similarities ÷ τ. Negatives (or a momentum teacher) prevent collapse. MAE masks 75% (BERT: 15%).

Make the data label itself, pretrain on huge unlabeled sets, score with a linear probe or kNN. SimCLR matched supervised ResNet-50 (76.5%); DINOv3: 7B params, 1.7B images.

🧰 DINOv2 / DINOv3 weights, timm, Hugging Face

🕸️

9.2 Graph neural networks

Aggregate neighbours (sum / mean / max) + shared update. k layers = k hops. Params don't grow with the graph.

GCN = normalized averaging; GraphSAGE samples (25 × 10); GAT learns neighbour weights. Deep GNNs over-smooth: stay at 2–4 layers. A transformer is a GNN on a complete graph.

🧰 PyTorch Geometric, DGL

🐍

9.3 State-space models

KV cache per token = 2 × KV heads × head dim × bytes × layers. An SSM's state is fixed.

Linear recurrence = loop (generate) or convolution / scan (train). Mamba is selective (Δ, B, C from the token). Weak at exact copying; hybrids keep a little attention (Jamba 1 : 7, Granite 4.0 9 : 1).

🧰 mamba_ssm, Hugging Face transformers

🔬

9.4 Interpretability

IG credits sum to output − baseline. Randomize the weights: a real explanation must change.

Saliency is local and saturates; probes show what's decodable (not what's used); superposition makes neurons polysemantic; SAEs (overcomplete + L1) recover monosemantic features.

🧰 Captum, TransformerLens, SAE viewers

🎭

9.5 Adversarial examples

Δscore = ε × m × n (random noise: ε × m × √n). FGSM = x + ε·sign(∇ₓloss); PGD = many steps + projection.

Panda 57.7% → gibbon 99.3% at ε = 0.007. Adversarial training: CIFAR-10 PGD accuracy 3.5% → 45.8%, clean 95.2% → 87.3%, only for the trained threat model.

🧰 Adversarial Robustness Toolbox, AutoAttack, torchattacks

🎲

9.6 Uncertainty & OOD

ECE = Σ (bin share) × |acc − conf|. Temperature: softmax(logits / T), fitted on validation NLL.

ResNet-110 / CIFAR-100: ECE 16.53% → 1.26%, same accuracy. Ensembles (M = 5) and MC dropout measure epistemic uncertainty; OOD = score + threshold on normal data + fallback.

🧰 torchmetrics CalibrationError, netcal, OpenOOD

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “We have a vision model with great test accuracy. Before it goes into a hospital, what can still go wrong, and for each failure, how would you detect it and what would you do about it?”

A strong answer covers: spurious features (audit with integrated gradients or Grad-CAM, run the weight-randomization sanity check, occlude and re-test); miscalibration (reliability diagram, ECE, temperature scaling on a validation set; it won't survive shift); out-of-distribution inputs (a score such as energy or ensemble disagreement, a threshold that keeps 95% of normal data, a human fallback); distribution shift over time (name it: covariate, label or concept; monitor flag rates and confidence; label samples; recalibrate or retrain); and adversarial inputs if anyone has a reason to attack (define the threat model, evaluate with PGD, adversarially train if needed). Bonus: self-supervised pretraining on the hospital's unlabeled scans to make the most of few labels.

🏗️ Build it yourself: a chest X-ray triage model a hospital can trust

✏️ The problem

A hospital wants a model that sorts incoming chest X-rays into “likely normal: routine queue” and “needs a radiologist soon”. Suppose they have 300,000 unlabeled past X-rays, 6,000 labelled by radiologists (normal plus 4 findings), and about 2,000 new scans a day, some from a second site with a different scanner. Requirements: a wrong “likely normal” on a sick patient is the costly error; the system must say “not sure” rather than guess; radiologists want to see why; and it must keep working as scanners and patient mix change.

Using this phase's ideas, design the pipeline: pretraining, the classifier, calibration, the OOD gate, the explanation audit and monitoring, with numbers. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

1. Representation: self-supervised pretraining (9.1)

  • Start from a general SSL backbone such as DINOv2 ViT-L/14 (1,024-dimensional features); natural photos aren't X-rays, so continue DINO-style or MAE-style pretraining on the 300,000 unlabeled hospital scans. No labels needed, and it narrows the domain gap.
  • Check with a linear probe on the 6,000 labels: 1,024 × 5 + 5 = 5,125 parameters, trained in seconds. If it's already strong, fine-tune the top blocks; if not, the pretraining isn't doing its job.
  • Split by patient (never the same patient in train and test) and keep 1,000 labelled scans for calibration and 1,000 for final testing, including some from the second site.

2. Calibration (9.6)

  • Fit a temperature T on the 1,000-scan calibration split by minimizing NLL; report the reliability diagram and ECE on the test split (target: a few percent or less). Accuracy doesn't change; the confidences become usable.
  • Decision rule from calibrated numbers: routine queue only if p(normal) ≥ 0.98 and the scan passes the OOD gate; everything else goes to a radiologist. Choose 0.98 from the test split so the miss rate on sick patients meets the clinical target, and recheck it per site.

3. The OOD gate (9.6)

  • Train an ensemble of 5 classifier heads (different seeds) on the shared backbone: almost free compared with 5 full backbones. Score each scan by ensemble disagreement and by the energy score.
  • Set the threshold to keep 95% of normal in-distribution scans. By construction about 5% of ordinary scans (≈ 100 a day out of 2,000) are flagged too: that's the price of the safety net, and it goes into the radiologists' workload estimate.
  • Test the gate on true OOD inputs you can collect: lateral views, paediatric scans, other body parts, the second site's scans.

4. Explanation audit (9.4)

  • Integrated gradients or Grad-CAM on 200 random scans and on every test error. Look for spurious evidence: text markers, rulers, the portable-scanner tag, tubes and lines that correlate with sickness. Mask or crop them and retrain if found.
  • Run the weight-randomization sanity check on your attribution method once, and confirm important regions by occluding them and watching the score drop.

5. Robustness (9.5)

  • Natural robustness first: evaluate under realistic changes (noise, contrast, rotation, scanner differences). Worst-case adversarial robustness matters only if someone has a reason to attack (for example, insurance fraud); then define the threat model and evaluate with PGD before trusting the model.

6. Monitoring (9.6)

  • Daily: OOD-flag rate (expect ~5%; alert if it doubles), confidence histogram, predicted class mix (label shift, for example a flu season), per site.
  • Monthly: radiologists label a random sample of 200 routine-queue scans to measure the real miss rate and ECE; recalibrate T when ECE drifts, retrain when accuracy drops or a new scanner arrives.

Classic mistakes

  • Fitting T on the test set, then reporting the same set's ECE.
  • Assuming calibration from the first site holds at the second (it usually doesn't under shift).
  • Treating raw softmax as an OOD detector: networks give high confidence to noise.
  • Splitting by image instead of patient, so the test set leaks.
  • Shipping saliency maps without a sanity check, so radiologists trust pictures of edges.

Not covered here: regulation and clinical validation, fairness across patient groups, and serving at scale (Phase 10 covers efficiency and deployment; AI Engineer 8.5 covers responsible-AI practice).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. A 32-layer hybrid keeps 4 attention layers. Each attention layer stores 4 KiB per token. How much KV cache does one 1,048,576-token (1M) context need?
4 layers × 4 KiB × 1,048,576 tokens = 16,777,216 KiB = 16 GiB. All 32 layers as attention would need 128 GiB; the SSM layers' fixed states add only megabytes (Lesson 9.3).
2. A grayscale 224 × 224 input (50,176 pixels) feeds a roughly linear score with average |weight| 0.005. An attacker may change each pixel by ε = 2/255 ≈ 0.0078. About how much can the score move?
ε × m × n = 0.0078 × 0.005 × 50,176 ≈ 1.96. Random ±ε noise would move it about ε × m × √n = 0.0078 × 0.005 × 224 ≈ 0.009: the aligned attack is 224× stronger (Lesson 9.5).
3. Your SimCLR-style run has a loss that falls to near zero, but kNN accuracy on the features is at chance. You discover the two “augmentations” of each image were accidentally identical. What happened?
Negatives were still there, so it didn't collapse to one point. But with identical views, the twin is found by low-level details, so the features carry no semantic structure. The augmentations (crop, colour jitter, blur) are what force content-level features (Lesson 9.1).
4. Which kind of uncertainty shrinks if you collect much more training data of the same kind, and which method estimates it?
Aleatoric uncertainty is noise in the data itself (an ambiguous image stays ambiguous). Epistemic uncertainty is the model's ignorance where it has seen little data; more data removes it, and models trained differently disagree there (Lesson 9.6).
5. A linear probe on layer 6 of a language model predicts “the sentence is in French” with 99% accuracy. A colleague concludes “layer 6 uses language identity to make its predictions”. What's the right response?
Probes are correlational; in Lesson 9.4 a probe decoded a pure noise input the task never needed with 97% accuracy. Causal tests (activation patching, ablating or steering along the probe direction) show whether the model relies on it.
