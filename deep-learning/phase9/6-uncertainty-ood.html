Uncertainty, calibration and out-of-distribution data

A model that says “90% sure” should be right 90% of the time, and should say “I don't know” when shown something it has never seen. Most deep networks do neither out of the box. Here's how to measure that, fix the first with one number, and catch the second.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Softmax turns logits into probabilities; dividing the logits by a temperature T > 1 flattens them (Lesson 0.6; math 8.2).
  • Cross-entropy / negative log-likelihood (NLL) punishes confident wrong answers hardest (Lesson 1.1; math 6.3).
  • Dropout randomly zeroes units during training (Lesson 2.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2017, Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger compared a 1998 LeNet with a 2016 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100. The ResNet was far more accurate, yet its confidence no longer matched reality: they wrote that “modern neural networks are no longer well-calibrated”. Its expected calibration error was 16.53%. Then they divided its logits by one learned number, and the error fell to 1.26%, with exactly the same predictions.

A year earlier, Hendrycks and Gimpel noted that pure random noise fed into an MNIST digit classifier gets a predicted class probability of 91%.

When should you believe a model's confidence, and how can it tell you “this input is unlike anything I was trained on”? You'll calibrate an overconfident model to under 2% error, then build an ensemble that notices when it's out of its depth.

Numbers first: confidence is not correctness

Take 1,000 predictions where a model said “90% sure”. If it's calibrated, about 900 are right. Real deep networks often get 700 right: overconfident. That matters whenever a number drives a decision:

SituationWhat goes wrong with miscalibration
A triage model sends cases below 80% confidence to a doctorIf “95%” really means 75%, the riskiest cases skip review
A self-driving stack fuses camera and radar by confidenceThe overconfident sensor wins arguments it should lose
An LLM pipeline answers only when confident, else retrievesIt answers wrongly without retrieving

Two separate problems hide here. Calibration: on the kind of data you trained on, do confidences match accuracy? Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection: can the model tell that an input is a different kind of data altogether (a cat photo sent to a chest X-ray model)?

💡 Big idea A probability is a promise. Calibration checks the promise on familiar data (reliability diagram, ECE) and fixes it cheaply with temperature scaling. Uncertainty comes in two kinds: noise in the data itself (aleatoric) and the model's own ignorance (epistemic). Epistemic uncertainty is what flags out-of-distribution inputs, and you estimate it by asking several plausible models and seeing if they disagree (ensembles, MC dropout).
🧠 Analogy: weather forecasters

A good forecaster who says “70% chance of rain” is right on about 70% of those days: calibrated. An overconfident one says 95% every time. Now send the forecaster to a planet they've never studied. A single forecaster happily says “95% sunny”. Ask five forecasters who trained differently: on Earth they agree; on the new planet their guesses scatter. The scatter is the warning.

Reliability diagrams and ECE

Sort predictions into bins by confidence (0–10%, 10–20%, …). In each bin, compare the average confidence with the actual accuracy. Plot accuracy against confidence: a calibrated model sits on the diagonal. That's a reliability diagram. Bars below the diagonal mean overconfident.

Expected calibration error (ECE) boils it down to one number: the average gap |accuracy − confidence| across bins, weighted by how many predictions fall in each.

ECE = Σbins (bin size ÷ N) × | accuracy(bin) − confidence(bin) |
✏️ Worked example: ECE with two bins

100 predictions. Bin A: 60 predictions with average confidence 0.95, of which 45 are right (accuracy 0.75). Bin B: 40 predictions, average confidence 0.60, 22 right (accuracy 0.55).

ECE = 0.60 × |0.75 − 0.95| + 0.40 × |0.55 − 0.60| = 0.60 × 0.20 + 0.40 × 0.05 = 0.12 + 0.02 = 0.14

A 14% calibration error, mostly from the confident bin: “95%” actually means 75%. Overall accuracy is 67%, while the average confidence is 0.81 (0.6 × 0.95 + 0.4 × 0.60). Guo et al. used 15 bins.

Temperature scaling: one number fixes a lot

Why are modern nets overconfident? Guo et al. linked it to depth, width, Batch Normalization and less weight decay: training keeps pushing the logits bigger to shrink the NLL even after accuracy stops improving. Their fix: after training, divide all logits by a single temperature T, chosen to minimize NLL on a held-out validation set.

calibrated probabilities = softmax( logits ÷ T )    T > 1 softens overconfidence; T < 1 sharpens underconfidence

🤔 Why this? Temperature scaling
  1. The problem before Modern networks are accurate but overconfident: Guo et al. measured ECE of 16.53% for a ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100. Decisions that threshold on confidence inherit the error.
  2. What people tried Histogram binning, isotonic regression, Bayesian binning (BBQ), and Platt scaling (fit a logistic curve to the scores); vector and matrix scaling (a learned linear map on the logits).
  3. Why this works Overconfidence in these models is mostly a uniform “logits too big” effect, so one shared T undoes most of it. It's fitted on held-out data by minimizing NLL, a smooth 1D problem (the paper: “by far the fastest” method). Because T > 0 rescales every logit equally, the argmax and therefore accuracy are untouched. On ResNet-110 / CIFAR-100: 16.53% → 1.26% ECE.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It needs a validation set from the same distribution. One T can't fix a model that's overconfident on some classes and underconfident on others. Most importantly, it calibrates in-distribution only: Ovadia et al. (2019) found that calibration from i.i.d. test data “does not usually translate” to shifted data, and it does nothing for OOD inputs.
  5. What refined it Per-class or vector scaling, label smoothing and focal loss during training, ensembles (better calibrated under shift), and conformal prediction (prediction sets with a coverage guarantee).

Deep ensembles and MC dropout

Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017): train M copies of the network from different random initializations (and data order), average their predictions, and use their disagreement as uncertainty. The paper recommends M = 5. MC dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016): keep dropout switched on at test time, run T forward passes, and treat the spread as uncertainty, a cheap approximation to a Bayesian network with a single trained model.

🤔 Why this? Deep ensembles (and MC dropout)
  1. The problem before One network gives one confident answer everywhere, including where it has no data (39% of far OOD points at ≥ 99% confidence in your playground). Proper Bayesian neural networks, which keep a distribution over weights, were hard to scale and tune.
  2. What people tried Variational Bayesian networks (SVI), Laplace approximations, MC dropout as a cheap Bayesian approximation (Gal and Ghahramani: “T stochastic forward passes through the network and averaging the results”).
  3. Why this works Near the data, every well-trained network is pinned to the same answers, so they agree. Away from it, nothing pins them, and different random starts lead to different guesses: disagreement measures epistemic uncertainty. It needs no change to the model or loss. Ovadia et al.'s large study under dataset shift: “Deep ensembles seem to perform the best across most metrics”, with M = 5 often enough.
  4. What it costs, when it fails M× the training and inference compute and memory (5 models = 5× the bill). Members can agree on the same wrong extrapolation (you saw dark patches far from the data). MC dropout is cheaper (one model, T passes) but usually less reliable than a real ensemble.
  5. What refined it Cheaper ensembles (snapshot ensembles from one run, BatchEnsemble, ensembling only the last layers or LoRA adapters), distance-aware single models, and evaluation of uncertainty under shift as standard practice.

Out-of-distribution detection with a score and a threshold

Whatever score you use, OOD detection in production looks the same: compute a score per input, pick a threshold on normal validation data (for example, “keep 95% of normal inputs”), and route anything past the threshold to a fallback: a human, a refusal, a simpler rule.

  • Max softmax probability (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016): the baseline. Low top probability → suspicious. Free, and better than nothing, but the 91% confidence on pure noise shows its limit.
  • Energy score (Liu et al., 2020): E(x) = −T · log Σi elogiti/T, computed from the logits before softmax. In words, it looks at how large the logits are overall, which softmax throws away. On a CIFAR-10 WideResNet it cut the false-positive rate (at 95% true-positive rate) by 18.03% compared with max softmax.
  • Ensemble disagreement or feature-space distance (how far an input's embedding is from the training embeddings).
🤔 Why this? OOD detection with a score threshold
  1. The problem before A classifier must pick one of its known classes, so it labels a cat photo sent to an X-ray model as some disease, often confidently. Nguyen et al. (2014) generated images “completely unrecognizable to humans” that networks labelled with 99.99% confidence.
  2. What people tried An extra “other” class (but you can't collect examples of everything that isn't your data), reconstruction error from autoencoders, density models.
  3. Why this works You don't need examples of every possible OOD input, only of normal ones: set the threshold so 95% of normal validation inputs pass, and anything stranger than that is flagged. Scores that use more information than the top softmax probability (energy, ensemble spread) separate the two groups better: 52% → 81% caught in your playground.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Every threshold trades false alarms (normal inputs sent to review) against misses. “Near-OOD” inputs (a new disease that looks like a known one) are much harder than far-OOD (a cat). Scores computed from the model can share its blind spots; and adversarial inputs (Lesson 9.5) can be crafted to look normal.
  5. What refined it Energy-based fine-tuning, outlier exposure (train with a generic pile of “other” data), feature-distance methods, and monitoring the rate of flags over time in production.

Distribution shift in production

Even without single weird inputs, the whole stream of data drifts. Name the kind of shift and you know what to monitor:

ShiftWhat changesExampleMonitor
Covariate shiftthe inputs p(x); the rule p(y | x) staysa hospital buys a new scanner; images get sharperinput statistics, embedding drift, OOD-flag rate

In practice: log confidences and OOD scores, alert when their averages move, sample flagged and random cases for human labels, and recalibrate or retrain when accuracy on fresh labels drops. LLM-specific monitoring is in AI Engineer 8.1 (LLMOps).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Calibration and uncertainty need very little code. Libraries help with evaluation: torchmetrics (CalibrationError), netcal, and the PyTorch-OOD and OpenOOD benchmarks. ECE, temperature scaling (fitted with L-BFGS) and MC dropout in plain PyTorch:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

def ece(probs, labels, n_bins=15):
    conf, pred = probs.max(1)
    edges = torch.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1)
    total = torch.tensor(0.0)
    for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
        inb = (conf > lo) & (conf <= hi)
        if inb.any():                                   # |accuracy − confidence|, weighted by bin size
            total += inb.float().mean() * ((pred[inb] == labels[inb]).float().mean() - conf[inb].mean()).abs()
    return total.item()

# simulated overconfident classifier: true logits z, labels drawn from softmax(z), model reports 2.5·z
torch.manual_seed(0)
z = 1.2 * torch.randn(6000, 10); c = torch.randint(0, 10, (6000,)); z[torch.arange(6000), c] += 4.0
labels = torch.multinomial(F.softmax(z, 1), 1).squeeze(1)
logits = 2.5 * z
val, test = slice(0, 3000), slice(3000, 6000)

log_T = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)             # T = exp(log_T) stays positive
opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([log_T], lr=0.1, max_iter=100)
def closure():
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[val] / log_T.exp(), labels[val])   # fit T on the VALIDATION split
    loss.backward()
    return loss.detach()
opt.step(closure)
T = log_T.exp().item()

for name, t in [("before (T = 1)", 1.0), (f"after  (T = {T:.2f})", T)]:
    p = F.softmax(logits[test] / t, 1)
    acc = (p.argmax(1) == labels[test]).float().mean().item()
    print(f"{name}: accuracy {acc:.3f}  mean confidence {p.max(1).values.mean():.3f}  ECE {100 * ece(p, labels[test]):.1f}%")

# MC dropout: keep dropout ON at test time and look at the spread of predictions
torch.manual_seed(0)
X = torch.rand(400, 1) * 2 - 1; y = torch.sin(3 * X) + 0.1 * torch.randn_like(X)   # data only in [−1, 1]
net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 1))
o = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-2)
for _ in range(1500):
    o.zero_grad(); F.mse_loss(net(X), y).backward(); o.step()
net.train()                                              # dropout stays active
with torch.no_grad():
    for x0 in [0.0, 3.0]:
        samples = torch.stack([net(torch.tensor([[x0]])) for _ in range(100)])
        print(f"x = {x0}: MC-dropout std of 100 predictions = {samples.std().item():.3f}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# before (T = 1): accuracy 0.728  mean confidence 0.938  ECE 21.0%
# after  (T = 2.49): accuracy 0.728  mean confidence 0.734  ECE 2.1%
# x = 0.0: MC-dropout std of 100 predictions = 0.061
# x = 3.0: MC-dropout std of 100 predictions = 0.541

Fit T on a validation split, report ECE on a separate test split, and refit whenever the model or the data changes. MC dropout's spread is 9× larger at x = 3, far outside the training range [−1, 1]: epistemic uncertainty showing up.

🎤 Interview angle

“Your model says 95% on cases it gets wrong, and sometimes it sees inputs from a new hospital. What do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Measure: reliability diagram and ECE on held-out data; fix with temperature scaling fitted on validation NLL (accuracy unchanged).
  • Separate aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty; use a deep ensemble (M = 5) or MC dropout for epistemic uncertainty.
  • OOD: a score (max softmax baseline, energy score, ensemble disagreement), a threshold set on normal data (e.g. keep 95%), and a fallback path.
  • Production: name the shift (covariate, label, concept), monitor score distributions and flag rates, label samples, recalibrate or retrain.
🏭 In the real world
The calibration wake-up call 🧪 Research · 2017 Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

How: reliability diagrams and ECE (15 bins) across vision and NLP models; compared binning methods, Platt, vector, matrix and temperature scaling.

Why this method: temperature scaling, one parameter, was the simplest and often the best: ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100 went from 16.53% to 1.26% ECE.

Ensembles for uncertainty 🧪 DeepMind · 2017 Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel & Blundell: Simple and Scalable Predictive Uncertainty Estimation using Deep Ensembles

How: M independently initialized networks trained with a proper scoring rule, averaged; M = 5 recommended.

Why this method: a non-Bayesian method that's simple, parallel and needs no tuning, and it beat approximate Bayesian methods on uncertainty quality.

Uncertainty under dataset shift 🧪 Google · 2019 Ovadia et al.: Can You Trust Your Model's Uncertainty?

How: benchmarked temperature scaling, MC dropout, ensembles, variational methods and more as images and text were progressively corrupted or shifted.

Why this method: found that uncertainty quality degrades with shift for every method, that i.i.d. calibration doesn't transfer, and that deep ensembles held up best.

OOD from the logits 🧪 Research · 2020 Liu, Wang, Owens & Li: Energy-based Out-of-distribution Detection

How: score each input by its energy, −T·logsumexp(logits/T), instead of the top softmax probability; optionally fine-tune to widen the gap.

Why this method: a drop-in replacement for the baseline that uses the logits' scale; it reduced FPR at 95% TPR by 18.03% on CIFAR-10.

🤯 Fun fact · 99.99% sure it's a lion

Nguyen, Yosinski and Clune (2014) used an evolutionary algorithm to create images that look like TV static or abstract stripes. State-of-the-art networks labelled them as specific objects with 99.99% confidence, “labeling with certainty that white noise static is a lion”. That paper is why “the softmax is not a confidence you can trust off-distribution” became folk wisdom.

Check yourself

1. 200 predictions fall into two confidence bins. Bin 1: 150 predictions, average confidence 0.90, accuracy 0.80. Bin 2: 50 predictions, average confidence 0.50, accuracy 0.50. What's the ECE?
ECE = (150/200) × |0.80 − 0.90| + (50/200) × |0.50 − 0.50| = 0.75 × 0.10 + 0.25 × 0 = 0.075, a 7.5% calibration error, all from the confident bin.
2. After temperature scaling with T = 2.3 fitted on validation data, a colleague says “accuracy went down because the model is less confident now”. Are they right?
Temperature scaling is monotonic per input: logits/T keeps their order. The argmax doesn't change, so neither does accuracy. What changes is how confident each prediction claims to be, which is exactly what ECE and NLL measure.
3. A calibrated chest X-ray classifier (ECE 1.5% on the test set) is deployed at a new hospital whose scanner produces different-looking images. Confidence stays high but accuracy drops. What's the best first step?
Calibration measured on in-distribution data does not survive a shift (Ovadia et al.). The new scanner changed p(x): covariate shift. Detect it with an OOD score and a threshold set on the original data, keep humans in the loop for flagged cases, and adapt the model with labelled data from the new hospital.
📝 TL;DR
  • Calibrated = “p% confident” is right p% of the time. Check with a reliability diagram; summarize with ECE.
  • Modern nets are overconfident (ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100: ECE 16.53%). Temperature scaling, one T fitted on validation NLL, fixed it to 1.26% without changing accuracy.
  • Aleatoric uncertainty = noisy data; epistemic = the model's ignorance. Ensembles (M = 5) and MC dropout estimate epistemic uncertainty by disagreement.
  • OOD detection = a score (max softmax, energy, ensemble spread) + a threshold set on normal data + a fallback. Single softmax is overconfident off-distribution (91% on noise, 99.99% on static).
  • Shifts: covariate (inputs), label (class mix), concept (the rule). Monitor scores and flag rates; calibration doesn't survive shift.
