Self-supervised learning: SimCLR, MAE, DINO

Labels are the expensive part of deep learning. Self-supervised learning gets a network to teach itself from raw, unlabeled data, and today it produces the vision backbones that everyone else fine-tunes.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An embedding is a vector a network produces for an input; similar inputs should get nearby vectors, measured by cosine similarity (math 1.5).
  • Softmax with a temperature turns scores into probabilities; a small temperature makes it sharper (Lesson 0.6).
  • Transfer learning: reuse a pretrained backbone and train a small head on your task (Lesson 3.5); ViTs cut an image into patches (Lesson 4.5).
🎯 Hook

In 2020, Google researchers trained a ResNet-50 on ImageNet without using a single label. Then they froze it and trained just one linear layer on top. It scored 76.5% top-1, matching the same network trained the normal, fully supervised way. That method is SimCLR.

In 2025, Meta trained DINOv3, a 7-billion-parameter vision model, on 1.7 billion images, again with no labels. Meta reports that the World Resources Institute uses it on satellite images to detect tree loss and deforestation.

How can a network learn what a dog is if nobody ever tells it “dog”? By the end of this lesson you'll have trained a tiny one that sorts images into groups it was never told about, watched it collapse into uselessness, and fixed it.

Numbers first: why labels are the bottleneck

A supervised model needs a human to say “cat” for every training picture. Unlabeled pictures are nearly free: phones, the web and cameras make billions of them. So the gap looks like this:

The trick: invent a task whose answer is already in the data, so the data labels itself. That's a pretext task. You don't care about the task itself; you care that solving it forces the network to learn useful features. Later you throw the task away and keep the features.

Language models do exactly this: “predict the next word” needs no labeller, because the next word is in the text (Lesson 6.7). This lesson is how vision found its own versions.

💡 Big idea Self-supervised learning (SSL) = make up a question the data can answer about itself, train a network to answer it on huge unlabeled data, then reuse the network's features for the tasks you actually care about with very few labels. Three families: contrastive (SimCLR, MoCo), teacher–student without negatives (BYOL, DINO) and masked modelling (MAE).
🧠 Analogy: spot-the-same-thing cards

A child gets a deck of photo cards. Some cards are two shots of the same object: one zoomed in, one in black and white. Nobody tells the child what the objects are, only “find the pairs”. To match a zoomed-in, grey photo of a dog with a colour photo of the same dog, the child has to notice ears, fur and snout, and ignore colour and zoom. After a thousand rounds the child has a pretty good notion of “dog”, without the word.

Early pretext tasks (and why they weren't enough)

The first ideas were puzzles: rotate an image by 0°, 90°, 180° or 270° and ask the net which; cut it into a 3×3 jigsaw and ask it to reorder the pieces; remove the colour and ask it to paint it back. Each works a bit. Each also has shortcuts: a net can learn “sky is usually at the top” to solve rotation without learning objects. The breakthrough came from a more general question: are these two views the same image?

Contrastive learning: pull pairs together, push the rest apart

SimCLR's recipe, in four steps:

  1. Take a batch of N images. Make two random augmentations of each (random crop, colour jitter, blur, flip). That's 2N views.
  2. Run every view through the same encoder (a ResNet) plus a small projection head, and normalize the output to length 1: an embedding on a sphere.
  3. For each view, its twin is the positive; the other 2N − 2 views are negatives.
  4. Loss: “pick your twin out of the line-up”. A softmax over similarities, where the right answer is the twin.

That loss is called InfoNCE (SimCLR calls its version NT-Xent). In words: take the similarity to your twin, compare it with the similarities to everyone else, and push the twin's share of the softmax towards 100%. The temperature τ divides every similarity before the softmax:

loss = −log [ esim(z, z⁺)/τ ÷ ( esim(z, z⁺)/τ + Σnegatives esim(z, z⁻)/τ ) ]

It is exactly a cross-entropy loss for a classification problem whose “classes” are the other items in the batch (math 6.3, softmax in math 8.2).

🤔 Why this? Contrastive loss (InfoNCE / SimCLR)
  1. The problem before Supervised pretraining needs a label for every image (ImageNet-1K: about 1.3 million hand-labelled images). Puzzle pretext tasks (rotation, jigsaw, colouring) needed no labels but left a large accuracy gap and had shortcuts.
  2. What people tried Predicting rotations or patch positions; clustering features; memory banks of old embeddings as negatives.
  3. Why this works To pick its twin out of thousands, an embedding must keep what survives augmentation (shape, parts, texture of the object) and drop what doesn't (colour, crop position). The augmentations define what “the same” means. Negatives stop the cheap answer “map everything to one point” (you'll see that answer in the next playground). SimCLR's linear probe reached 76.5% top-1 on ImageNet, matching a supervised ResNet-50.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It needs many negatives: SimCLR's best runs used batches of 4,096 to 8,192 (16,382 negatives per pair) on 128 TPU v3 cores. Two different photos of the same breed of dog are treated as negatives anyway (“false negatives”). Results depend heavily on picking the right augmentations.
  5. What refined it MoCo keeps a queue of 65,536 recent embeddings as negatives, from a slowly updated “momentum” copy of the encoder, so batches can stay small. BYOL and DINO then dropped negatives altogether (below). CLIP uses the same loss between images and their captions (Lesson 4.6).

No negatives at all: BYOL and DINO

Negatives are expensive and sometimes wrong (two photos of the same breed are not really “different”). In 2020, BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent, DeepMind) showed you can drop them and still avoid collapse, which surprised many people. The trick is a teacher:

  • A student network sees one view and tries to predict the teacher's output for the other view.
  • The teacher gets no gradient. Its weights are a slow moving average of the student's (BYOL: 0.996 old + 0.004 new at each step, rising towards 1).
  • The student also has an extra small predictor on top, so it can't just copy.

BYOL reached 74.3% linear-probe accuracy on ImageNet with a ResNet-50, without a single negative pair. DINO (Meta, 2021) applied the idea to Vision Transformers: teacher and student each output a probability distribution over many “prototype” slots, and the student is trained with cross-entropy to match the teacher.

🤔 Why this? Non-contrastive SSL with a momentum teacher (BYOL, DINO)
  1. The problem before Contrastive methods need thousands of negatives per step (huge batches or a 65,536-entry queue) and treat same-class images as enemies.
  2. What people tried Bigger batches, memory banks, MoCo's queue, removing likely false negatives.
  3. Why this works The target comes from a slowly moving teacher, so the student chases a target that changes only a little each step; the collapse solution is not a stable place to land when the target lags behind. DINO adds two cheap guards: centering (subtract a running average from the teacher's outputs, so no single slot can win for every image) and sharpening (a low teacher temperature, so its outputs can't be flat and uniform). The paper notes that centering alone encourages collapse to a uniform output and sharpening alone to a single slot; together they balance. Bonus: DINO's ViT attention maps outline objects with no segmentation labels.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two networks in memory; extra hyperparameters (moving-average rate, two temperatures, the centering rate). Get them wrong and it still collapses, often silently: always monitor the spread of embeddings or a kNN score during training.
  5. What refined it DINOv2 (2023) combined DINO with masked-patch prediction on 142 million curated images; DINOv3 (2025) scaled to 7 billion parameters and 1.7 billion images and added “Gram anchoring” to keep its patch features clean in long training.

Masked image modelling: hide 75% and redraw it

BERT learned language by hiding 15% of the words and guessing them (Lesson 6.7). MAE (Masked Autoencoders, Meta, 2021) does the same for images: cut the image into patches, hide 75% of them, and train a ViT to repaint the missing pixels. Two design choices make it work:

  • The big encoder only processes the visible 25% of patches, so pretraining is cheap; a small decoder gets placeholder tokens for the missing ones and does the repainting. The paper reports training 3× faster or more.
  • The masking ratio is much higher than BERT's. Why? The MAE authors' answer: words are dense with meaning, while images are full of spatial redundancy: a missing patch can often be filled in from its neighbours with no understanding at all.

What you should see: at 15% the lazy fill's average error on the smile is 0.075 (pixels run 0 to 1) and 0% of hidden pixels are cut off from their neighbours. Blurring the neighbours basically solves the task, so a network trained on it would learn little more than blurring. At 75% the error doubles to 0.148 and 15.6% of hidden pixels have nothing visible within 2 steps; at 90% it's 54%. To fill those, a model has to know what eyes and mouths look like. MAE's own sweep agrees: 75% was best for both linear probing and fine-tuning.

🤔 Why this? Masked autoencoding (MAE)
  1. The problem before Contrastive and teacher methods depend on hand-picked augmentations and big batches, and their global “whole image” embedding can ignore fine detail.
  2. What people tried Denoising autoencoders, inpainting, and BERT-style masking with low ratios (20–50% in earlier vision work), which was slow and gave modest gains.
  3. Why this works Reconstructing 75% of an image from 25% can't be done by local copying (you just measured it), so the model must learn objects and parts. Skipping masked patches in the encoder makes it 4× fewer tokens: cheap enough to pretrain big ViTs on ImageNet-1K alone. A plain ViT-Huge pretrained this way and fine-tuned reached 87.8% on ImageNet-1K, the best result among methods using only that data at the time.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Raw pixel reconstruction wastes effort on unpredictable detail (exact texture), and MAE features are weaker under a linear probe than contrastive or DINO features: they usually need fine-tuning to shine.
  5. What refined it Predicting features instead of pixels: I-JEPA (2023) predicts the embeddings of hidden blocks from a context block, and DINOv2 mixes masked prediction with DINO's teacher. Video versions like V-JEPA 2 (2025) predict missing parts of videos.

How do you score features nobody labelled?

The standard test is the linear probe: freeze the pretrained encoder, then train only one linear layer on top with the labels of a benchmark. If a straight line (a hyperplane) can separate the classes in feature space, the features already “know” the classes. The other tests are k-nearest-neighbour accuracy (what your playground showed) and fine-tuning everything with few labels.

✏️ Worked example: how small is a linear probe?

A ResNet-50 has 25,557,032 parameters (counted with torchvision). Its last feature vector has 2,048 numbers. A linear probe for 1,000 ImageNet classes has 2,048 × 1,000 weights + 1,000 biases = 2,049,000 parameters, all of them in one layer that can't learn new features. If that tiny layer scores 76.5%, as with SimCLR, the other 23.5 million frozen parameters already did the work. With only 1% of the ImageNet labels and fine-tuning, SimCLR reached 85.8% top-5, beating AlexNet trained on 100× more labels.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In practice you rarely pretrain SSL yourself: you download a backbone (DINOv2/DINOv3 from Meta's GitHub or Hugging Face, MAE and DINO weights through timm) and fine-tune or probe it. When you do train one, the loss is a few lines. This is SimCLR's NT-Xent on random embeddings, plus MAE-style random masking:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

def info_nce(z1, z2, tau=0.5):
    """z1[i] and z2[i] are two views of image i. Every other view is a negative."""
    z = F.normalize(torch.cat([z1, z2]), dim=1)          # 2N × d, length 1
    sim = z @ z.T / tau                                     # cosine similarity / τ
    sim.fill_diagonal_(float("-inf"))                      # a view is not its own twin
    n = z1.shape[0]
    twin = torch.cat([torch.arange(n, 2 * n), torch.arange(0, n)])
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, twin)                       # “pick your twin”

torch.manual_seed(0)
a = torch.randn(256, 128)
b = a + 0.1 * torch.randn(256, 128)                   # 256 near-perfect twins
print("random pairs:      ", round(info_nce(a, torch.randn(256, 128)).item(), 3))
print("log(2N − 1) =      ", round(torch.log(torch.tensor(511.)).item(), 3))
print("near-copies, τ=0.5:", round(info_nce(a, b).item(), 3))
print("near-copies, τ=0.1:", round(info_nce(a, b, tau=0.1).item(), 3))

# MAE-style masking: keep a random 25% of 196 patch tokens (a 14 × 14 ViT grid)
tokens = torch.randn(8, 196, 768)
keep = torch.rand(8, 196).argsort(dim=1)[:, :49]
visible = torch.gather(tokens, 1, keep[..., None].expand(-1, -1, 768))
print("encoder input:    ", tuple(visible.shape))

Random embeddings score about log(2N − 1), the “guessing” loss: with 512 views that's 6.24. Notice the near-perfect twins still score 4.3 at τ = 0.5: cosine similarity can't exceed 1, so 0.99 ÷ 0.5 = 2 is the biggest score a twin can get, and 510 negatives at score 0 still outweigh e² ≈ 7.4. That's another reason SimCLR-style training uses small temperatures. If your SSL loss sits at that value for long, nothing is being learned. If the loss is near 0 but kNN accuracy is bad, check for collapse or a leak (for example, both views accidentally identical).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain contrastive learning, and what stops it collapsing.” A strong answer:

  • Two augmentations of one image are a positive pair; other images in the batch are negatives; InfoNCE is a softmax cross-entropy over similarities with a temperature.
  • Collapse = every input maps to the same vector, which trivially makes positives similar. Negatives prevent it; BYOL/DINO prevent it with a momentum teacher (+ a predictor, or centering and sharpening).
  • Why MAE masks 75% (spatial redundancy) vs BERT's 15%, and that SSL features are judged by linear probe, kNN, and fine-tuning with few labels.
  • Practical: monitor embedding spread or kNN during training; the augmentations decide what the model learns to ignore.
🏭 In the real world
Contrastive pretraining matches supervised 🧪 Research · 2020 Chen et al.: A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations (SimCLR)

How: two augmentations per image, a ResNet plus a small projection head, NT-Xent loss over batches of up to 8,192 images.

Why this method: no labels, no memory bank, no special architecture; the linear probe reached 76.5%, the same as a supervised ResNet-50.

A queue of negatives 🧪 Research · 2019 He et al.: Momentum Contrast (MoCo)

How: negatives come from a queue of K = 65,536 past embeddings, produced by a momentum encoder updated as 0.999 old + 0.001 new.

Why this method: lots of consistent negatives without huge batches, so it runs on ordinary GPU servers.

Masked autoencoders scale ViTs cheaply 🧪 Research · 2021 He et al.: Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners (MAE)

How: mask 75% of patches, encode only the visible 25%, reconstruct pixels with a light decoder.

Why this method: 3× or faster pretraining and 87.8% ImageNet-1K accuracy for a plain ViT-Huge using only ImageNet-1K data.

Universal vision backbones, used on satellite images 🏢 Meta · 2025 Meta AI: DINOv3

How: DINO-style self-distillation scaled to 7B parameters and 1.7B unlabeled images, then distilled into smaller ViT and ConvNeXt models.

Why this method: labels are scarce or impossible for satellite and scientific images; Meta reports WRI uses DINOv3 to detect tree loss, and NASA JPL uses DINOv2 for Mars exploration robots.

🤯 Fun fact · segmentation for free

The DINO paper found that a self-supervised ViT's attention maps outline the main object in the picture, a dog or a bird, crisply enough to look like a segmentation mask, though the model never saw a mask or a label. The authors write that such features “contain explicit information about the semantic segmentation of an image”, which supervised ViTs show far less clearly. You'll peek at attention maps in Lesson 4.5.

Check yourself

1. A SimCLR batch has N = 1,024 images. How many negatives does each view get?
Two views per image gives 2N = 2,048 views. Remove the view itself and its twin: 2,048 − 2 = 2,046 negatives. (SimCLR's batch of 8,192 gives 16,382, as the paper says.)
2. You train a BYOL-style model. The loss drops to almost 0 in an hour, but the linear probe is at chance and every embedding has cosine similarity 0.999 with every other. What happened?
Matching two views is perfectly solved by a constant output. Non-contrastive methods rely on the slowly moving teacher, the predictor (BYOL) or centering plus sharpening (DINO) to make collapse unstable. A near-zero loss with identical embeddings is the classic signature, which is why you monitor embedding spread or kNN accuracy during training.
3. Why does MAE hide 75% of image patches when BERT hides only 15% of words?
Language is dense with meaning: guessing 15% of words is already hard. Neighbouring pixels are highly correlated, so a low masking ratio is a blurring task (your lazy fill nearly solved 15%). At 75% local copying fails, and the encoder only processes the visible quarter of the tokens.
📝 TL;DR
  • SSL invents a task the data answers itself (a pretext task), trains on huge unlabeled data, then reuses the features with few labels.
  • Contrastive (SimCLR, MoCo): two augmentations are positives, the rest are negatives; InfoNCE = softmax cross-entropy over similarities ÷ τ. More negatives = harder question; MoCo keeps a 65,536 queue.
  • Without negatives, everything can collapse to one point. BYOL/DINO avoid it with a slow momentum teacher (DINO: centering + sharpening).
  • MAE hides 75% of patches (images are redundant; BERT hides 15% of words) and encodes only the visible quarter.
  • Score SSL with a linear probe or kNN; in 2026 DINOv2/DINOv3 and CLIP-style models are the go-to pretrained vision backbones.
