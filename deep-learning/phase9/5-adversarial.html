Phase 9 · Lesson 5

Adversarial examples and robustness

Add a pattern too faint for you to see, and a network that was sure it saw a panda becomes 99.3% sure it sees a gibbon. Why tiny nudges add up to big mistakes, how attackers compute them in one line, and what it costs to defend.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The gradient of the loss with respect to the input says which way to nudge each pixel to make the loss bigger (Lesson 9.4 used the same gradient to explain; here we use it to attack).
  • A dot product w·x adds up one term per input (math 1.3); the max-norm ‖η‖∞ is the biggest single entry (math 1.4).
  • Training = minimizing the loss with gradient descent (Lesson 1.5).
🎯 Hook

In 2014, Ian Goodfellow and colleagues took a photo that GoogLeNet labelled “panda”, 57.7% confident. They added a noise-like pattern scaled by 0.007, which in their words “corresponds to the magnitude of the smallest bit of an 8 bit image encoding”. To a person the two photos are identical. GoogLeNet now said “gibbon”, 99.3% confident.

It isn't only digital. In 2018, researchers put a few black and white stickers on a real stop sign; a road-sign classifier read it as a 45 mph speed-limit sign in 100% of lab photos and 84.8% of video frames taken from a moving car.

How can a change you can't even see flip a confident answer? You'll attack a network yourself, calculate why high-dimensional inputs are so fragile, and train a tougher model to see what robustness costs.

Numbers first: tiny changes, everywhere at once

Take a linear score s = w·x, and change every input by at most ε, in the direction that hurts most: ηi = ε × sign(wi). Each term changes by ε × |wi|. With n inputs whose weights average m in size:

change in score = ε × m × n
Inputnε = 1/255 per pixel, m = 0.01
MNIST digit (28 × 28)7840.031
CIFAR image (32 × 32 × 3)3,0720.12
ImageNet image (224 × 224 × 3)150,5285.9, enough to flip a confident logit gap

No single pixel moves by more than one brightness step, but 150,528 tiny pushes all in the harmful direction add up. Goodfellow et al.'s explanation: “we can make many infinitesimal changes to the input that add up to one large change to the output”. Networks with ReLUs are piecewise linear, so they inherit the problem.

💡 Big idea An adversarial example is an input changed by a small, deliberately chosen perturbation (each pixel by at most ε) so that the model gets it wrong. It works because, in high dimensions, a model's output is sensitive to coordinated small changes across thousands of inputs, even though it's robust to random ones. Attacks follow the input gradient; defenses train on attacks.
🧠 Analogy: a crowd of whispers

Ask 150,000 people to each nudge a heavy cart by one millimetre. If they push in random directions, the pushes cancel and the cart barely moves. If an attacker hands each of them a note saying which way to push, the cart rolls. Random noise is the first crowd; an adversarial perturbation is the second.

FGSM: the one-line attack

Goodfellow et al. turned the idea into the fast gradient sign method. In words: compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input, keep only its sign (+1 or −1 per input), and step ε in that direction. One backward pass.

xadv = x + ε · sign( ∇x loss(x, y) )

The sign makes every input move by exactly ε: the largest change allowed in the max-norm (‖η‖∞ ≤ ε) box. Gradients: math 3.3.

✏️ Worked example: FGSM by hand

A logistic-regression model with w = [2, −1, 0.5], bias 0, input x = [0.2, 0.1, 0.4], true class 1. Score s = 0.4 − 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.5, so p(class 1) = σ(0.5) = 0.62: correct.

For cross-entropy, ∂loss/∂x = (p − y) · w = −0.38 × [2, −1, 0.5], whose signs are [−, +, −]. FGSM with ε = 0.2: xadv = [0.0, 0.3, 0.2].

New score: 0 − 0.3 + 0.1 = −0.2, p = σ(−0.2) = 0.45: now wrong. The score dropped by exactly ε × (|2| + |−1| + |0.5|) = 0.2 × 3.5 = 0.7, the ε × m × n formula with n = 3. With 150,528 inputs of the same average weight size, the same ε buys a drop about 50,000× bigger (150,528 ÷ 3).

🤔 Why this? FGSM (fast gradient sign method)
  1. The problem before Szegedy et al. (2013) found adversarial examples with an expensive optimizer (box-constrained L-BFGS) per image. Too slow to study at scale, or to train against.
  2. What people tried Explanations blaming extreme non-linearity or overfitting; defenses based on that guess.
  3. Why this works If the model is roughly linear near x, the best perturbation inside an ε-box is ε × sign(gradient): every input moves the full ε in the harmful direction, so the damage is ε × m × n (the worked example: 0.7 from three inputs). One backward pass per image, so you can generate millions and train on them.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It takes one straight step. If the model curves near x, one step can overshoot or miss, so FGSM underestimates how vulnerable a model is. Models trained only against FGSM can learn to mask the gradient and still fall to stronger attacks.
  5. What refined it Iterative attacks (BIM, then PGD, below), optimization-based attacks like Carlini–Wagner, and black-box attacks that work without gradients.

Why high dimensions are the problem

What you should see: with ε = 1/255 and m = 0.01, the attack shifts an ImageNet-size score by 5.9: it beats a margin of 5. Random noise of exactly the same size shifts it by about 0.015, 388× less (√150,528 ≈ 388). That's why models are robust to random noise but not to adversarial noise, and why bigger inputs are easier to attack: at MNIST size even 8/255 only shifts the score by 0.25. Real networks aren't one linear score, but the ε × m × n arithmetic is the core of Goodfellow et al.'s explanation.

PGD: the attack defenses are measured against

Madry et al. (2017) framed robustness as a game: the attacker finds the worst perturbation inside the ε-box, the defender trains on it. Their attacker, projected gradient descent, is FGSM repeated: take a small sign step (size α), then project back into the box (clip every input to within ε of the original), and repeat 7 to 100 times, often from a random start inside the box.

🤔 Why this? PGD (projected gradient descent attack)
  1. The problem before FGSM takes one straight step and underestimates vulnerability where the model curves (in your playground, at ε = 0.3: 50.5% under FGSM, 39.5% under PGD). Defenses that beat FGSM often fell to anything stronger.
  2. What people tried Iterative FGSM (BIM), Carlini–Wagner optimization attacks, many ad-hoc defenses, several of which were later broken.
  3. Why this works Many small steps follow the loss surface as it bends; projection keeps the perturbation legal (‖η‖∞ ≤ ε); random restarts avoid bad local spots. Madry et al. argued it is a near “universal” first-order adversary: if a model resists PGD, it tends to resist other gradient-based attacks. It's now the standard evaluation and the standard training adversary.
  4. What it costs, when it fails k steps cost k forward-backward passes per example (7 to 100× FGSM). It's still a local, gradient-based search: models with obfuscated or broken gradients can look robust to PGD without being robust, so serious evaluations add adaptive and gradient-free attacks.
  5. What refined it Standardized attack suites (AutoAttack), targeted variants, and attacks under other threat models (patches, rotations, L2 balls).

Targeted vs untargeted. Untargeted: “make it wrong, any wrong answer”: step to increase the loss for the true label. Targeted: “make it say gibbon”: step to decrease the loss for the target label. Targeted is harder but more dangerous (the stop sign was made to read as a specific speed limit).

Out of the computer: physical attacks

  • Printed photos. Kurakin, Goodfellow and Bengio (2016) printed adversarial images, photographed them with a cell-phone camera, and found that a large fraction still fooled an ImageNet Inception classifier.
  • Stop-sign stickers. Eykholt et al. (2018) optimized sticker patterns that survive changes in distance and angle: 100% targeted misclassification in the lab, 84.8% of frames from a moving car.
  • A 3D-printed turtle. Athalye et al. (2018) made a turtle classified as a rifle from almost every viewpoint, by optimizing over many random rotations, distances and lighting (“expectation over transformation”).

Attacks also transfer: Szegedy et al. found the same perturbation can fool a different network trained on a different subset of the data, so an attacker doesn't always need your weights. For LLMs, the cousins of these attacks are adversarial suffixes and jailbreak prompts: see AI Engineer R.2 (injection and jailbreaks) and R.4 (data and model attacks).

Adversarial training: fight fire with fire

The most reliable defense is simple to state: at every training step, attack the batch with PGD and train on the attacked inputs. In words: “minimize the loss on the worst nearby version of each example”. The model learns to rely on features an ε-perturbation can't move.

What you should see: with training ε = 0.25, the robust model's boundary sits further from the points with a wider, softer transition. Under PGD at ε = 0.25 it keeps 78.5% where the standard one keeps 51%; at ε = 0.4 it's 53.5% vs 16%. That trade-off is real at scale: in Madry et al.'s CIFAR-10 runs (ε = 8 out of 255), the standard wide network scored 95.2% clean and 3.5% under PGD; the PGD-trained one 87.3% clean and 45.8% under PGD.

🤔 Why this? Adversarial training
  1. The problem before A standard CIFAR-10 network at 95.2% accuracy fell to 3.5% under PGD with ε = 8/255. Many proposed defenses (input denoising, randomization, detection) were broken by adaptive attacks within months.
  2. What people tried Training on FGSM examples (cheap, but models learned to mask gradients), distillation, preprocessing defenses.
  3. Why this works It directly optimizes the thing you care about: loss on the worst point in the ε-box. The model is pushed to ignore “non-robust” features (patterns that are predictive but movable by tiny changes) and to keep a margin around the data. In the playground: 51% → 78.5% under PGD at ε = 0.25; Madry et al.: 3.5% → 45.8% on CIFAR-10.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Training is k+1 times slower (k PGD steps per batch). Clean accuracy usually drops (95.2% → 87.3% on CIFAR-10); Tsipras et al. (2018) argued this tension can be fundamental (“Robustness May Be at Odds with Accuracy”). Robustness holds only for the threat model you trained for (an L∞ ε-box doesn't protect against rotations or stickers), and robust accuracy is still far below clean accuracy.
  5. What refined it Cheaper variants (“free” and “fast” adversarial training), extra unlabeled or generated data, and certified robustness: methods such as randomized smoothing (Cohen et al., 2019) prove that no perturbation within a radius can change the prediction, rather than just failing to find one.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Libraries: Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (IBM/LF AI, attacks and defenses for many frameworks), Foolbox, torchattacks, and the AutoAttack suite for honest evaluations. Both attacks and adversarial training fit in a page of PyTorch. This toy has one strong feature and 100 weak ones (each easy to flip), the situation Tsipras et al. describe:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
n, d = 4000, 100
s = torch.randint(0, 2, (n,)) * 2 - 1                  # labels as −1 / +1
robust = s[:, None] + 0.6 * torch.randn(n, 1)           # 1 strong feature (hard to flip)
weak = 0.3 * s[:, None] + torch.randn(n, d)             # 100 weak features (each easy to flip)
X, y = torch.cat([robust, weak], 1), (s > 0).long()
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:3000], y[:3000], X[3000:], y[3000:]

def fgsm(model, x, y, eps):
    x = x.clone().requires_grad_(True)
    F.cross_entropy(model(x), y).backward()
    return (x + eps * x.grad.sign()).detach()          # one step in the sign of the gradient

def pgd(model, x, y, eps, steps=10):
    alpha, x_adv = eps / 4, x.clone()
    for _ in range(steps):
        x_adv.requires_grad_(True)
        F.cross_entropy(model(x_adv), y).backward()
        x_adv = x_adv.detach() + alpha * x_adv.grad.sign()
        x_adv = x + (x_adv - x).clamp(-eps, eps)        # project back into the ε-box
    return x_adv

def fit(adv_eps=0.0):
    torch.manual_seed(1)
    model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d + 1, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
    for _ in range(600):
        xb = pgd(model, Xtr, ytr, adv_eps, steps=5) if adv_eps else Xtr   # train on attacked inputs
        opt.zero_grad(); F.cross_entropy(model(xb), ytr).backward(); opt.step()
    return model

acc = lambda m, x: (m(x).argmax(1) == yte).float().mean().item()
for name, model in [("standard", fit()), ("adversarially trained", fit(0.4))]:
    print(f"{name:22s} clean {acc(model, Xte):.3f} | FGSM ε=0.4 {acc(model, fgsm(model, Xte, yte, 0.4)):.3f}"
          f" | PGD ε=0.4 {acc(model, pgd(model, Xte, yte, 0.4)):.3f}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, about 10 s):
# standard               clean 0.999 | FGSM ε=0.4 0.201 | PGD ε=0.4 0.201
# adversarially trained  clean 0.971 | FGSM ε=0.4 0.821 | PGD ε=0.4 0.811

The standard model leans on the 100 weak features (nearly perfect clean accuracy) and collapses when ε = 0.4 cancels them all. The adversarially trained model learns to trust the strong feature: 81% under attack, for 2.8 points of clean accuracy. Gotcha: always call model.eval() and zero gradients properly when attacking real models with dropout or BatchNorm, and evaluate with attacks stronger than the one you trained with.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do adversarial examples exist, and how would you make a model robust?” A strong answer:

  • Linearity in high dimensions: an ε-step on each of n inputs, aligned with the weights' signs, changes the score by ε·m·n; random noise only by ~ε·m·√n.
  • FGSM (one sign step), PGD (iterated, projected; the standard evaluation); targeted vs untargeted; transfer and physical attacks (stickers, 3D-printed objects).
  • Defense: adversarial training (minimize the worst-case loss in the ε-ball), with its costs: slower training, lower clean accuracy, protection only for the trained threat model. Mention certified robustness (randomized smoothing).
  • Evaluation hygiene: adaptive attacks, no gradient masking; report robust accuracy at a stated ε and norm.
🏭 In the real world
The discovery 📜 History · 2013 Szegedy et al.: Intriguing properties of neural networks

How: optimized a “hardly perceptible” perturbation per image that maximized the network's error.

Why this method: it showed networks are not locally smooth around their training data, and that the same perturbation can fool a different network trained on a different subset of the data.

The panda, the gibbon and FGSM 🧪 Research · 2014 Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy: Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples

How: the linear explanation and the one-step sign attack; adversarial training with FGSM examples.

Why this method: cheap enough to generate attacks inside the training loop, turning adversarial examples into a regularizer.

Robustness as a min-max game 🧪 Research · 2017 Madry et al.: Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks

How: PGD as the inner attacker, training on its outputs; ε = 0.3 for MNIST and 8/255 for CIFAR-10.

Why this method: a principled objective; CIFAR-10 robust accuracy went from 3.5% to 45.8% under PGD, and the recipe is still the baseline today.

Stickers on a real stop sign 🧪 Research · 2018 Eykholt et al.: Robust Physical-World Attacks on Deep Learning Models

How: optimized black-and-white sticker patterns over varied distances and angles, then tested on a real sign in the lab and from a moving car.

Why this method: it proved attacks can survive cameras and the real world: 100% lab, 84.8% drive-by targeted misclassification.

🤯 Fun fact · a turtle that's a rifle

Athalye et al. 3D-printed a toy turtle with an adversarial texture. Photographed from random angles, an ImageNet classifier labelled it a rifle in most views. The trick, “expectation over transformation”, is to optimize the texture to fool the model on average over many random rotations, zooms and lightings, so no single viewpoint is needed.

Check yourself

1. A linear model has 3,072 inputs (a CIFAR image) with average |weight| 0.02. An FGSM attack uses ε = 4/255 ≈ 0.0157. About how much can it change the score?
ε × m × n = 0.0157 × 0.02 × 3,072 ≈ 0.96. Random ±ε noise would move it by only about ε × m × √n = 0.0157 × 0.02 × 55 ≈ 0.017. Aligned tiny changes add up linearly; random ones mostly cancel.
2. A team reports their new defense keeps 90% accuracy under FGSM with ε = 8/255. Under a 50-step PGD attack it keeps 4%. What's going on?
FGSM assumes the model is linear around each input. A defense can bend or hide the gradient so one step fails, while an iterative attack walks around the bend. Robustness claims must use strong, adaptive attacks such as PGD with restarts or AutoAttack.
3. You adversarially train a traffic-sign classifier with PGD at ε = 8/255 (L∞). In a field test, a sticker attack still fools it. Why is that not a contradiction?
An L∞ ε-box allows every pixel to move a little. A sticker moves a few pixels a lot, which is far outside that box. Defenses don't generalize across threat models, so you define the threat model first (what can the attacker change?) and train and evaluate for it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Adversarial examples: imperceptible, deliberate changes that flip predictions (panda 57.7% → gibbon 99.3% with ε = 0.007). They transfer across models and survive the physical world (stop-sign stickers: 100% lab, 84.8% drive-by).
  • Why: linearity in high dimensions. Aligned ε steps change a score by ε·m·n; random noise only by ~ε·m·√n.
  • FGSM = one sign step of the input gradient. PGD = many small steps + projection: stronger, the standard test.
  • Adversarial training (train on PGD examples) is the most reliable defense: CIFAR-10 robust accuracy 3.5% → 45.8%, clean 95.2% → 87.3%, and only for the threat model you trained on.
  • Certified methods (randomized smoothing) prove robustness within a radius. LLM jailbreaks are the same game on text.
