Phase 0 · Lesson 4

Activations I: step, sigmoid, tanh, ReLU (and why ReLU won)

The tiny bend after every neuron decides whether a deep network can learn at all. Swap one function and a network that was stuck starts learning six times faster.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A neuron computes a weighted sum z = w·x + b, then bends it with an activation function f(z) (Lesson 0.1).
  • Stacking layers only helps if there is a bend between them (Lesson 0.3).
  • A gradient says how much the loss changes if you nudge a weight. Training nudges every weight against its gradient (math 3.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2012 a network called AlexNet won the ImageNet image-recognition contest with a top-5 error of 15.3%. The runner-up had 26.2%. Deep learning took over computer vision almost overnight.

One of the changes its authors highlighted was tiny: they replaced the smooth S-shaped tanh bend with ReLU, which is just max(0, z). In their test, a small network with ReLUs reached 25% training error on the CIFAR-10 images six times faster than the same network with tanh.

How can a function this dumb beat a beautiful smooth curve? By the end of this lesson you'll have broken a deep network with sigmoids, fixed it with ReLU, then killed half its ReLUs on purpose, and you'll know exactly why each thing happened.

Numbers first: why there's a bend at all

Take two layers with no bend. Layer 1 computes 3x + 1, layer 2 takes that and computes 2·(…) − 1:

2 · (3x + 1) − 1 = 6x + 1

Two layers collapsed into one straight line. A hundred layers would too. Without a bend, depth buys nothing: the whole network is one linear model and can never learn a curved boundary like a circle (you proved this with XOR in Lesson 0.2).

Put a bend f between the layers and the algebra stops collapsing: 2·f(3x + 1) − 1 can be a curve. So job one of an activation is to be non-linear. Almost any bend does that job.

Job two is the one that decides everything: during training, the learning signal (the gradient) travels backwards through every layer, and at each layer it gets multiplied by the activation's slope f′(z) (that's the chain rule, math 3.2). Ten layers means ten multiplications:

Slope at each of 10 layersSignal left at layer 1What that means
0.25 (sigmoid's best case)0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.000001Early layers get a millionth of the signal. They barely move.
0.42 (tanh at z = 1)0.42¹⁰ ≈ 0.0002Better, still almost nothing.
1 (ReLU, any z > 0)1¹⁰ = 1The signal arrives intact.

Weights also multiply the signal, so real networks aren't exactly these numbers; good initialization (Lesson 1.4) keeps the weights' part near 1. The activation's slope is the part you choose.

💡 Big idea An activation function has two jobs: bend the signal on the way forward, and pass the gradient on the way back. The history of activations is a hunt for a function whose slope stays near 1, so that deep networks can learn, while staying cheap and still being a bend.
🧠 Analogy: a line of people whispering a correction

The output layer knows how wrong the answer was and whispers a correction back down a line of 10 people. Each person passes on only a fraction of what they heard. With sigmoid, each passes on at most a quarter: the first person in line hears a millionth and shrugs. With ReLU, each person either repeats the message exactly (if they were “on” for this example) or says nothing (if they were “off”). Messages arrive intact through the people who are on.

The step function: where it started

The 1958 perceptron (Lesson 0.2) fired 1 if z > 0, else 0. A perfect on/off switch, like a real neuron spiking. Its slope is 0 everywhere (and undefined at 0), so gradient descent gets no hint about which way to move. The perceptron used its own error-correction rule instead, which works for one layer and not for many.

🤔 Why this? Sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)
  1. The problem before The step function has slope 0, so a multi-layer network can't be trained by following gradients: nudging a weight changes nothing until the output suddenly flips.
  2. What people tried Hand-designed features with a single trainable layer; learning rules that only work for one layer.
  3. Why this works Sigmoid is a smoothed step: it slides from 0 to 1, so every nudge changes the output a little and backprop has a slope to follow. Its output reads as a probability (σ(2) = 0.88 means “88% yes”). It made the 1986 backprop revival possible.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Its slope is at most 0.25 and nearly 0 for |z| > 4 (σ′(4) = 0.018): it saturates. Stack it deep and gradients vanish (0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶). Its outputs are always positive (mean ≈ 0.5), which makes the next layer's gradient updates zig-zag. It needs an exponential: slower than a comparison.
  5. What refined it tanh for hidden layers (next), then ReLU. Sigmoid survives where you want a 0–1 dial: binary outputs (Lesson 0.6) and the gates of LSTMs (Lesson 5.3).
🤔 Why this? tanh(z)
  1. The problem before Sigmoid's slope tops out at 0.25 and its outputs sit around 0.5, never centered on 0.
  2. What people tried Shifting and rescaling sigmoid; normalizing inputs to mean 0.
  3. Why this works tanh is a rescaled sigmoid: tanh(z) = 2σ(2z) − 1. It runs from −1 to 1 and is zero-centered, and its slope at 0 is 1, four times sigmoid's. For a decade (1990s–2000s) it was the hidden-layer default (LeCun's 1998 “Efficient BackProp” recommends it).
  4. What it costs, when it fails It still saturates: tanh′(2) = 0.07, tanh′(4) = 0.001. Once pre-activations drift away from 0, the gradient dies just like sigmoid's. Deep tanh nets were notoriously hard to train before good initialization (Lesson 1.4).
  5. What refined it ReLU, which doesn't saturate for positive inputs. tanh lives on inside LSTM/GRU cells (Lesson 5.3) and in some output layers that need a −1…1 range.

ReLU: the dumb function that won

ReLU (rectified linear unit) is f(z) = max(0, z). Positive numbers pass through unchanged; negative numbers become 0. That's it. It's still a bend (a kink at 0), so stacked ReLU layers don't collapse: each unit is a hinge, and many hinges added together can trace any curved shape, like bending a wire at many points.

🤔 Why this? ReLU max(0, z)
  1. The problem before Sigmoid and tanh saturate. Sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so ten stacked layers shrink the gradient by up to 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶ and early layers stop learning. Deep networks often needed layer-by-layer unsupervised “pre-training” to get started at all.
  2. What people tried tanh (slope up to 1, still saturates), careful initialization, and greedy layer-wise pre-training.
  3. Why this works For every positive input the slope is exactly 1, so gradients pass through unshrunk, however deep you go (1¹⁰ = 1). It never saturates on the positive side. It's one comparison, no exponential. And it outputs true zeros for about half the inputs, so activations are sparse: each input lights up only some units. Glorot, Bordes and Bengio (2011) showed deep rectifier networks reach their best results without unsupervised pre-training; AlexNet (2012) made it the default.
  4. What it costs, when it fails For z < 0 the slope is exactly 0. A unit whose z is negative for every input gets zero gradient forever: it can never recover. This is the dying ReLU problem, usually caused by a learning rate that's too high or a big negative bias. Outputs aren't zero-centered, and the kink at 0 has no slope (frameworks just use 0 there).
  5. What refined it Leaky ReLU and PReLU (below) keep a small slope for z < 0. Then the smooth versions GELU and SiLU/Swish, used in transformers, and the gated SwiGLU used in Llama-style LLMs (all in Lesson 0.5). He initialization (Lesson 1.4) was designed for ReLU's “half the units are off” behaviour.

What you should see: with a learning rate of 1, the push moves the histogram left a little and the neuron stays alive. Crank it past about 6 and one step shoves every z below 0: 0 of 300 inputs on. Now press Push up as often as you like. The gradient is exactly 0, so nothing moves, ever. That unit is now a permanent zero in your network, and its weights are wasted memory. With Leaky ReLU, the dead side still has slope 0.01, so each push nudges the bias back up and the neuron returns after a couple of ×10 pushes.

🤔 Why this? Leaky ReLU and PReLU
  1. The problem before Dead ReLUs: a unit that's off for every input has zero gradient and never comes back. Andrej Karpathy's Stanford CS231n notes warn that with a too-high learning rate, as much as 40% of a network can end up dead.
  2. What people tried Lower learning rates, small positive initial biases, careful initialization.
  3. Why this works Leaky ReLU (Maas, Hannun & Ng, 2013) gives negative inputs a small slope instead of zero: f(z) = z for z > 0, 0.01·z otherwise. A unit that's off still passes 1% of the gradient, so it can always climb back. PReLU (He et al., 2015) learns that slope per channel, and helped their network become the first reported to beat human-level error on ImageNet.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One more number (the leak) to pick or learn; outputs still not zero-centered; the kink is still there. In practice plain ReLU with a sensible learning rate and He init usually works as well, so the gain is often small. And a leak doesn't save you from a learning rate that's simply too big: training still diverges (try it below).
  5. What refined it ELU (smooth negative side that saturates at −1), then GELU and SiLU/Swish, smooth curves with a little dip below 0, now standard in transformers; then gated units like SwiGLU (Lesson 0.5).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In PyTorch, activations are modules (nn.ReLU(), nn.LeakyReLU(0.01), nn.PReLU(), nn.Sigmoid(), nn.Tanh()) or functions (torch.relu, F.leaky_relu, torch.sigmoid). This snippet measures the vanishing gradient for real: a 10-layer stack, each activation paired with its usual initialization (Lesson 1.4).

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)

def deep(act, init, depth=10, width=64):
    layers = []
    for _ in range(depth):
        lin = nn.Linear(width, width)
        init(lin.weight)
        nn.init.zeros_(lin.bias)
        layers += [lin, act()]
    return nn.Sequential(*layers, nn.Linear(width, 1))

x = torch.randn(256, 64)
for name, act, init in [
    ("sigmoid", nn.Sigmoid, nn.init.xavier_normal_),
    ("tanh", nn.Tanh, nn.init.xavier_normal_),
    ("relu", nn.ReLU, lambda w: nn.init.kaiming_normal_(w, nonlinearity="relu")),
]:
    net = deep(act, init)
    net(x).pow(2).mean().backward()          # any loss works for this check
    first = net[0].weight.grad.norm().item()
    last = net[-1].weight.grad.norm().item()
    print(f"{name:8s} layer-1 / last-layer gradient = {first / last:.0e}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# sigmoid  layer-1 / last-layer gradient = 3e-08
# tanh     layer-1 / last-layer gradient = 2e-01
# relu     layer-1 / last-layer gradient = 7e-02

Gotcha: PyTorch's default nn.Linear init is not He init. Pair ReLU with kaiming_normal_(…, nonlinearity="relu") (or use normalization and residuals, Phase 2) or you'll see gradients shrink even with ReLU.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do we use ReLU instead of sigmoid?” A strong answer:

  • States the backward pass reason, not just “it's faster”: gradients are multiplied by f′ at every layer; sigmoid's f′ ≤ 0.25 and saturates, so deep stacks vanish; ReLU's f′ = 1 for z > 0.
  • Mentions cost (a comparison vs an exponential) and sparsity.
  • Names the failure mode (dying ReLU: zero gradient for z < 0, often from a high learning rate) and fixes (Leaky/PReLU, lower LR, He init).
  • Knows where sigmoid still belongs (binary outputs, gates) and what modern LLMs use instead (GELU/SiLU, SwiGLU).
🏭 In the real world
ReLU makes deep CNNs practical 📜 History · 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton: ImageNet Classification with Deep CNNs (AlexNet)

How: every convolutional and fully connected layer of the 60-million-parameter network used ReLU.

Why this method: the authors call them “non-saturating neurons”; in their CIFAR-10 test a 4-layer ReLU network hit 25% training error six times faster than tanh, which made training such a big network feasible at all.

Deep nets without pre-training 🧪 Research · 2011 Glorot, Bordes & Bengio: Deep Sparse Rectifier Neural Networks

How: replaced tanh with rectifiers in deep networks and compared on several benchmarks.

Why this method: rectifier networks matched or beat tanh, produced sparse representations with true zeros, and reached their best performance without the unsupervised pre-training deep nets had needed.

A learned leak beats human-level on ImageNet 🧪 Research · 2015 He et al.: Delving Deep into Rectifiers (PReLU, He init)

How: PReLU learns the negative slope per channel; the same paper derives the initialization scale for ReLU networks (Lesson 1.4).

Why this method: almost no extra parameters and a measurable gain; their model reached 4.94% top-5 error, which they report as the first to surpass human-level performance (5.1%) on that benchmark.

Leaky ReLU in a real-time detector 🧪 Research · 2015 Redmon et al.: You Only Look Once (YOLO v1)

How: every layer except the last uses a leaky rectifier with slope 0.1 for negative inputs (Lesson 4.2 covers YOLO).

Why this method: as cheap as ReLU, with no units permanently switched off in a network that must train reliably from scratch.

🤯 Fun fact · the dead zone is real

Karpathy's CS231n notes warn that with a learning rate set too high, you may find that “as much as 40% of your network can be ‘dead’”: units that never activate on any training example. You just reproduced that in the playground. Checking the fraction of dead units is a real debugging trick (Lesson 2.6).

Check yourself

1. A network has 6 sigmoid hidden layers, and every unit sits at its best point (z = 0, slope 0.25). Ignoring the weights, what fraction of the output gradient reaches layer 1?
The gradient is multiplied by the slope once per layer: 0.25⁶ = 0.000244. Even in sigmoid's best case the first layer gets about 1/4,000 of the signal. That's the vanishing gradient. With ReLU (slope 1 for z > 0) it would be 1⁶ = 1.
2. Halfway through training, a ReLU network's loss suddenly flattens. You check and 60% of the units in layer 5 output 0 for every training example. What's the most likely cause and fix?
A ReLU unit that's negative on every input has slope exactly 0, so no gradient ever reaches its weights: it's permanently dead. A big update (high learning rate) is the classic way to push units there. Lower the LR and restart (or reinitialize), or use Leaky ReLU so off units still get 1% of the gradient. Adding layers or switching to tanh doesn't address the cause.
3. You're building a spam filter whose last layer must output “probability this email is spam”. Which activation belongs on that output unit?
“Use ReLU” is advice for hidden layers, where gradients must pass through many layers. At the output you want the range that matches the answer: a probability between 0 and 1, which is exactly sigmoid. Pairing it with cross-entropy loss cancels its saturation in the gradient (Lessons 0.6 and 1.1).
📝 TL;DR
  • Without a bend, any stack of layers collapses into one linear layer. The activation adds the bend.
  • On the way back, the gradient is multiplied by the activation's slope at every layer. Sigmoid's slope is ≤ 0.25 and saturates: deep sigmoid nets stall (0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶).
  • tanh is zero-centered with slope up to 1, but still saturates. ReLU = max(0, z) has slope exactly 1 for z > 0, is one comparison and gives sparse activations: the hidden-layer default since AlexNet.
  • ReLU's price: zero slope below 0, so units can die (high learning rates). Leaky ReLU / PReLU keep a small slope; GELU, SiLU and SwiGLU come next (0.5).
  • Sigmoid and tanh still belong where you want their range: probability outputs and gates.
