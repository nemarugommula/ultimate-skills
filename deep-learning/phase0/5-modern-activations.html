Phase 0 · Lesson 5

Activations II: Leaky, ELU, GELU, SiLU, SwiGLU

ReLU won, then got smoothed, then got a gate. Today's open LLMs nearly all run the gated version, and the paper that introduced it admits nobody knows exactly why it works.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • ReLU = max(0, z): slope 1 for z > 0 (gradients pass intact), slope 0 for z < 0 (units can die). Leaky ReLU keeps slope 0.01 there (Lesson 0.4).
  • On the way back, the gradient is multiplied by the activation's slope at every layer (Lesson 0.4; math 3.2).
  • σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z) slides from 0 to 1: a dial that can mean “how open” (Lesson 0.4).
🎯 Hook

Open the technical reports of today's big open language models: Llama 3, Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3, OpenAI's gpt-oss. Their feed-forward layers all use the same activation, SwiGLU. It comes from a five-page 2020 paper by Noam Shazeer (a co-author of the original Transformer). The paper tests a handful of variants, finds the gated ones win, and ends by admitting it has no explanation: the authors “attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence.”

Before that, BERT and GPT-2 ran on GELU, a smooth ReLU with a strange little dip below zero. And SiLU, the curve inside SwiGLU, was discovered three times and ended up with three names.

What's wrong with ReLU that people kept replacing it, and why would multiplying two things together (a “gate”) beat a single bend? You'll compare the curves, open and close a gate by hand, and race all of them on real training.

Where we left ReLU

Lesson 0.4 ended with two flaws of ReLU: units can die (slope exactly 0 for every negative input), and it has a sharp kink at 0, where the slope jumps from 0 to 1. Leaky ReLU fixed dying with a 0.01 slope on the left, but kept the kink. Three questions drove everything after:

  • Can the negative side carry useful information instead of flat zeros (or a token 1% leak)?
  • Does a smooth curve, whose slope changes gradually, make optimization easier?
  • Can the network decide, per input, how much of each signal to let through?

For big positive z they all behave like ReLU (output ≈ z, slope ≈ 1), so they keep its big advantage. They differ on the left and near zero. ELU goes negative and levels off at −1. GELU and SiLU dip slightly below zero and then come back toward 0: they're not monotonic.

🤔 Why this? ELU (Clevert, Unterthiner & Hochreiter, 2015)
  1. The problem before ReLU's outputs are all ≥ 0, so a layer's average output is positive (a “bias shift” for the next layer) and dead units give no gradient. Leaky ReLU's 0.01 slope barely moves the average.
  2. What people tried Leaky ReLU and PReLU (Lesson 0.4); batch normalization to re-center activations (Lesson 2.3).
  3. Why this works ELU(z) = z for z > 0, ez − 1 otherwise. Negative inputs give real negative outputs (ELU(−1) = −0.63), so the mean activation moves toward 0 “like batch normalization but with lower computational complexity”, as the paper puts it. It still has slope 1 on the right, and on the left the slope ez is never exactly 0 (e−2 = 0.14), so units don't die. It also saturates at −1: very negative inputs all mean “absent”, which the authors argue makes it robust to noise. With α = 1 the slope is continuous at 0 (no kink in the slope's value).
  4. What it costs, when it fails An exponential for every negative input (slower than a comparison); the left side can saturate like sigmoid (slope e−5 = 0.007); one more choice (α). The paper reported faster learning and better generalization than ReLU for networks with more than 5 layers; today ELU is used much less than GELU and SiLU.
  5. What refined it SELU (a scaled ELU for self-normalizing nets), then GELU and SiLU, which became the transformer defaults. In PyTorch: nn.ELU().
🤔 Why this? GELU (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016)
  1. The problem before ReLU makes a hard yes/no decision by the sign of z: a unit at z = 0.01 passes everything, at z = −0.01 nothing. Meanwhile dropout (Lesson 2.4) randomly multiplies units by 0 or 1 during training. Two separate ideas, both about “multiply the input by 0 or 1”.
  2. What people tried ReLU plus dropout; ELU for a smoother left side.
  3. Why this works GELU(z) = z · Φ(z), where Φ(z) is the probability that a standard bell-curve value is below z (math 4.3). Read it as: keep the input with probability Φ(z), and output the average result. A big positive z is almost surely kept (Φ(2) = 0.98, GELU(2) = 1.95); a big negative one almost surely dropped (GELU(−3) = −0.004); near 0 it's a coin flip, so it's half-kept (slope 0.5 at z = 0). The result is smooth everywhere, with a small dip to −0.17 at z ≈ −0.75. BERT used it (“a gelu activation rather than the standard relu”), and so did OpenAI's GPT and GPT-2.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Φ needs the error function, so frameworks often use the tanh approximation 0.5z(1 + tanh(√(2/π)(z + 0.044715z³))) (max error 0.0005; GPT-2's code uses it). Slower than ReLU. On small networks it's often no better than ReLU (you'll see that below); its gains were measured on large models.
  5. What refined it SiLU, a near twin; and gated versions (GEGLU) in Gemma. In PyTorch: nn.GELU() or nn.GELU(approximate="tanh").
🤔 Why this? SiLU / Swish: z · σ(z)
  1. The problem before Every hand-designed ReLU replacement gave inconsistent gains, so ReLU stayed the default. Could a smooth ReLU be found that reliably helps deeper models?
  2. What people tried Hand-designing curves (Leaky, PReLU, ELU, GELU). Then Ramachandran, Zoph and Le (2017) let a computer search: an exhaustive plus reinforcement-learning-based search over formulas, judged by how well small networks trained.
  3. Why this works The search's best find was f(z) = z · σ(βz), which they called Swish; with β = 1 it's the SiLU (sigmoid-weighted linear unit) that Elfwing, Uchibe and Doya (2017) had proposed for reinforcement learning. It is the input times a soft on/off dial σ(z): a self-gate. Like GELU it's smooth, unbounded above, bounded below (minimum −0.28 at z ≈ −1.28), non-monotonic, and z · σ(1.702z) stays within about 0.02 of GELU everywhere. Replacing ReLU with Swish improved ImageNet top-1 accuracy by 0.9% for Mobile NASNet-A and 0.6% for Inception-ResNet-v2 in their tests.
  4. What it costs, when it fails A sigmoid per unit (an exponential); small gains that can vanish on small or shallow models; with β it's another knob (almost everyone uses β = 1).
  5. What refined it SwiGLU (below), which uses Swish as the gate of a two-branch unit. In PyTorch: nn.SiLU() / F.silu.

🧠 Analogy: a light switch, a dimmer, and a smart dimmer

ReLU is a light switch with a hard click at 0. GELU and SiLU are dimmers: as the input rises, the light comes on gradually, so a tiny change of input makes a tiny change of output, which is what gradient descent likes. And they're smart dimmers: the dimmer's position is decided by the input itself (z · σ(z): the signal sets its own volume). The next step is to let a different signal turn the dimmer. That's a gate.

Gates: let one signal control another

In 2016 Dauphin, Fan, Auli and Grangier built a language model from convolutions and needed a way for each unit to decide how much of its signal to pass on. Their gated linear unit (GLU) computes two linear projections of the same input, a gate and a value, and multiplies them:

GLU(x) = σ(xW + b) ⊗ (xV + c)     (⊗ = multiply element by element)

The σ part is a dial between 0 (closed) and 1 (open), learned from the input. The value part passes through linearly: the paper notes this gives gradients “a linear path” through the unit, which helps deep stacks. Swap the σ for another bend and you get the family Shazeer tested: ReGLU (ReLU gate), GEGLU (GELU gate), SwiGLU (Swish gate).

What you should see: with a sigmoid gate the dial lives between 0 and 1. At g = 0 it's half open (3 → 1.5). Push g below about −4.1 and almost nothing gets through (σ(−5) = 0.007); above about +2.95 it passes 95%. A sigmoid gate can only scale v, never flip it. A Swish gate can go slightly negative (down to −0.28 at g ≈ −1.28), so the output can flip sign, and for large g it isn't capped at 1: it amplifies. Tick “self-gated” and the gate is driven by the value itself: the output curve is exactly SiLU.

💡 Big idea A plain activation bends one number. A gate multiplies two numbers computed from the same input, so the network can learn rules like “pass feature A only when feature B is present”. Multiplication is an interaction a sum of bends can only approximate with many units. That one extra product is the whole difference between a classic MLP layer and the SwiGLU layer inside modern LLMs.

SwiGLU: the gated feed-forward block in today's LLMs

A transformer block has an MLP (Lesson 0.3 counted GPT-2's: 768 → 3,072 → 768). The classic version is two matrices with a bend between. Shazeer's 2020 paper replaced the first matrix and the bend with a gated unit:

classic: FFN(x) = GELU(xW₁) W₂     SwiGLU: FFN(x) = ( Swish(xW) ⊗ xV ) W₂

In code those are the gate_proj, up_proj and down_proj you'll see in Hugging Face's Llama, Mistral and Qwen models: down(silu(gate(x)) * up(x)).

✏️ Worked example: the 2/3 trick that keeps the budget fixed

Three matrices instead of two means 50% more parameters at the same hidden width. To compare fairly, Shazeer shrank the hidden width by 2/3. Check it with model width d = 4,096 and the usual 4d = 16,384 hidden units:

  • Classic, 2 matrices: 2 × 4,096 × 16,384 = 134,217,728 weights.
  • SwiGLU, 3 matrices with hidden h: 3 × 4,096 × h. Equal when h = (2/3) × 16,384 = 10,922.7.
  • LLaMA (2023) says it uses “2/3 4d instead of 4d”; its 7B model's config has h = 11,008 (rounded up to a multiple of 256): 3 × 4,096 × 11,008 = 135,266,304, within 1% of the classic budget.
  • Later models pick their own widths: Llama 3 8B has d = 4,096 and an FFN dimension of 14,336 (3.5d), spending more on the MLP on purpose.

In Shazeer's T5 experiments (d = 768) the hidden size went from 3,072 to 2,048: 2 × 768 × 3,072 = 3 × 768 × 2,048 = 4,718,592, exactly equal.

🤔 Why this? SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020)
  1. The problem before Transformer MLPs used ReLU (original Transformer, T5) or GELU (BERT, GPT-2): one bend per hidden unit. In T5-base pre-training the ReLU MLP reached a held-out log-perplexity of 1.677 (lower is better).
  2. What people tried Swapping the bend: GELU (1.679) and Swish (1.683) were no better than ReLU at the same parameter count in that table.
  3. Why this works A gate multiplies a learned dial by a learned value, giving each hidden unit an input-dependent volume knob, and the value branch is a linear path for gradients. With parameters and compute held equal (the 2/3 trick), the gated variants won: GEGLU 1.633, SwiGLU 1.636, ReGLU 1.645, plain GLU 1.663 after 524,288 steps, and they also gave better results on many fine-tuned language-understanding tasks. Why exactly? The paper offers no explanation.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Three weight matrices instead of two, so a smaller hidden width for the same budget; an extra elementwise multiply and a sigmoid; the gain is a few percent of perplexity, visible at scale and easy to lose in noise on small models. gpt-oss even reports an “unconventional” SwiGLU with clamping, a hint that large gated activations can need taming.
  5. What refined it It became the default: LLaMA (2023) adopted it from PaLM, and Llama 3, Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3 and gpt-oss all report SwiGLU; Gemma uses GeGLU. In mixture-of-experts models such as DeepSeek-V3 and gpt-oss each expert is a SwiGLU block (Lesson 6.10).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

All of these are one line in PyTorch (F.elu, F.gelu, F.silu, and F.glu for the sigmoid-gated version). SwiGLU is a tiny module; this is the shape used in Llama-family code, plus a check of the 2/3 budget trick:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

z = torch.tensor([-2.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0])
print("elu ", F.elu(z))
print("gelu", F.gelu(z))
print("tanh", F.gelu(z, approximate="tanh"))   # GPT-2's version
print("silu", F.silu(z))

class SwiGLU(nn.Module):
    """Llama-style feed-forward block: down( silu(gate(x)) * up(x) )."""
    def __init__(self, d, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.gate = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)
        self.up = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)
        self.down = nn.Linear(hidden, d, bias=False)
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.down(F.silu(self.gate(x)) * self.up(x))

d = 768
classic = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, 4 * d, bias=False), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(4 * d, d, bias=False))
swiglu = SwiGLU(d, hidden=int(2 / 3 * 4 * d))     # 2048: same budget with 3 matrices
count = lambda m: sum(p.numel() for p in m.parameters())
print("classic FFN:", count(classic), " SwiGLU FFN:", count(swiglu))
x = torch.randn(2, 5, d)                          # (batch, tokens, features)
print("output shape:", tuple(swiglu(x).shape))

Gotcha: when loading pretrained weights, match the exact variant. A model trained with exact GELU but run with the tanh approximation (or vice versa) gives slightly different outputs; Hugging Face configs name it (gelu, gelu_new, gelu_pytorch_tanh, silu).

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do modern LLMs use SwiGLU instead of ReLU?” A strong answer:

  • ReLU's issues: dead units, the kink, one hard yes/no per unit. GELU/SiLU are smooth ReLU-like curves (z·Φ(z), z·σ(z)) that never die.
  • SwiGLU is a gated unit: Swish(xW) ⊗ xV, so one projection scales another, input by input. Three matrices; the hidden size is cut to about 2/3·4d to keep parameters equal.
  • Evidence is empirical: lower perplexity at equal compute (Shazeer 2020), adopted by LLaMA, Llama 3, Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3. No accepted theory of why (the paper jokes about divine benevolence).
  • Knows the code shape (down(silu(gate(x)) * up(x))) and that on small models the difference may be invisible.
🏭 In the real world
GELU in BERT 🧪 Research · 2018 Devlin et al.: BERT

How: every feed-forward layer of the encoder uses GELU; the released config says "hidden_act": "gelu" with 768 → 3,072 MLPs in BERT-base.

Why this method: the authors followed OpenAI's GPT, which also used GELU; it became the default for a generation of transformers.

Swish found by search 🧪 Research · 2017 Ramachandran, Zoph & Le: Searching for Activation Functions

How: exhaustive and reinforcement-learning-based search over formula building blocks, scoring each candidate by training a small network.

Why this method: hand-designed replacements for ReLU gave inconsistent gains; the searched-for x·σ(βx) improved ImageNet top-1 by 0.9% (Mobile NASNet-A) and 0.6% (Inception-ResNet-v2) by swapping it in.

SwiGLU at 2/3 width in LLaMA 🏢 Meta · 2023 Touvron et al.: LLaMA

How: replaces ReLU with SwiGLU in every feed-forward block, with a hidden dimension of 2/3·4d (11,008 for the 7B model).

Why this method: taken from PaLM “to improve the performance”; the 2/3 factor keeps the parameter count close to a classic 4d MLP.

GeGLU in Gemma 🏢 Google DeepMind · 2024 Gemma Team: Gemma

How: “the standard ReLU non-linearity is replaced by the approximated version of the GeGLU activation function”: a GELU (tanh approximation) gate.

Why this method: GEGLU tied SwiGLU for the best perplexity in Shazeer's table; the choice between them is mostly taste and lineage.

A clamped SwiGLU in open-weight MoE 🏢 OpenAI · 2025 gpt-oss-120b & gpt-oss-20b Model Card

How: the mixture-of-experts blocks use gated SwiGLU; the card calls its implementation unconventional, with clamping and a residual connection (the config exposes a swiglu_limit of 7.0).

Why this method: SwiGLU is the de-facto LLM MLP; clamping limits how far the unbounded gate × value product can grow.

🤯 Fun fact · one curve, three names

x · σ(x) was written down in the 2016 GELU paper, where Hendrycks and Gimpel called it the SiLU. Elfwing, Uchibe and Doya proposed it for reinforcement learning in early 2017 (first calling it “SiL”). Late in 2017 Google Brain's search rediscovered it and named it Swish. The GELU paper's later revision tells the story from its authors' side and notes that TensorFlow and PyTorch eventually renamed the function “SiLU”. That's why PyTorch has nn.SiLU and no nn.Swish.

🌉 How it connects

GELU's Φ is the normal distribution's cumulative probability (math 4.3); the slopes come from the chain rule (math 3.2). Gates aren't new: LSTM and GRU cells (Lesson 5.3) are built from sigmoid gates, and attention (Phase 6) is another way of multiplying signals by input-dependent weights. You'll meet SwiGLU again inside the transformer block (6.4) and as each expert in mixture-of-experts layers (6.10). Next lesson: the bend at the very end, softmax and sigmoid outputs (0.6). Check yourself

1. A classic transformer MLP has width d = 1,024 and hidden size 4,096 (two matrices, no biases). You switch to SwiGLU (three matrices) and want the same number of weights. What hidden size?
Classic: 2 × 1,024 × 4,096 = 8,388,608. SwiGLU: 3 × 1,024 × h. Setting them equal gives h = 2/3 × 4,096 ≈ 2,731. Keeping 4,096 would cost 50% more parameters, which would make any comparison unfair. Real models round to a hardware-friendly multiple (LLaMA-7B: 11,008 for d = 4,096).
2. You swap ReLU for GELU in a 3-layer MLP for a small tabular dataset. Validation accuracy changes from 91.2% to 91.0%. What's the most reasonable conclusion?
0.2 points is within seed-to-seed noise for most small problems (you saw ReLU vs GELU swap places across seeds in the race). The published wins for GELU, SiLU and SwiGLU are measured on large models at equal compute. Copying an LLM's choice into a tiny model is cargo culting; measure over several seeds.
3. In a GLU unit (sigmoid gate), the gate pre-activation is g = −5 and the value is v = 10. Roughly what does the unit output?
The gate is σ(g), not g: σ(−5) = 0.0067, so the output is 0.0067 × 10 ≈ 0.067. A sigmoid gate never goes below 0 or above 1: it scales the value, never flips or amplifies it, and it's never exactly 0. A Swish gate would give Swish(−5) × 10 = −0.033 × 10 ≈ −0.33: slightly negative.
📝 TL;DR
  • ELU (2015): z or ez − 1. Negative outputs pull the mean toward 0, never dies, saturates at −1; costs an exponential.
  • GELU (2016): z·Φ(z), “keep the input with probability Φ(z)”. Smooth, slope 0.5 at 0, dips to −0.17. BERT, GPT-2.
  • SiLU / Swish (2016–17): z·σ(z), a self-gated ReLU (dips to −0.28), found again by automated search. One curve, three names.
  • Gates multiply a learned dial by a learned value. SwiGLU = (Swish(xW) ⊗ xV)W₂, with hidden size ≈ 2/3·4d to keep parameters equal; it beat ReLU and GELU MLPs at equal compute and runs in Llama 3, Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3, gpt-oss.
  • On small nets the differences are tiny and noisy (ReLU often trains just as fast); the gains are real but measured at scale.
