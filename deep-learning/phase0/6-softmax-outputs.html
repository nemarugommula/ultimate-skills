Output layers: sigmoid, softmax and what a network “says”

A network's last layer produces raw scores. One small function turns them into an answer: a probability, a choice among thousands, or a number. Pick the wrong one and the model can't say what you need, or says it with confidence it hasn't earned.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A neuron's score z = w·x + b; σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z) turns it into a 0–1 probability (Lessons 0.1 and 0.4).
  • Hidden layers use ReLU-like bends (0.4, 0.5). This lesson is about the last layer, where you want the range that matches the answer.
  • ex grows fast: e10 ≈ 22,000 (math 0.4).
🎯 Hook

Every time a large language model writes a word, its last layer scores every token in its vocabulary (128,000 of them for Llama 3) and one function, softmax, turns those scores into probabilities that add up to exactly 1. AlexNet's last layer was a “1000-way softmax” over ImageNet classes. Change one number in that function, the temperature, and the same model goes from boringly predictable to wildly creative.

And here's a surprise from 2017: Guo and colleagues found that a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100 was much more accurate than a 1998-style LeNet, but its confidence was “substantially higher than its accuracy”. In plain words: LeNet's confidence matched how often it was right; the ResNet's ran well ahead of it.

Why exponentiate the scores? What does temperature actually do? And why can a better network be a worse judge of its own certainty?

Logits: the raw scores

The last layer of a classifier is plain nn.Linear: one weighted sum per class, no bend. Its outputs are called logits. Say an image classifier for 3 classes outputs z = [2.0, 1.0, 0.1] for cat, dog, fox. “Cat” scored highest, but by how much? Is 2.0 a lot? A logit alone means nothing; you need a rule that turns the scores into something a person (or a loss function) can use.

Numbers first: softmax by hand

Softmax does two steps: exponentiate every logit, then divide by the total.

✏️ Worked example: softmax of [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]

ez ÷ total0.6590.2420.0991.000

Every probability is positive and they add to 1. Notice the ratios: cat vs dog is e2.0 / e1.0 = e1 = 2.72. A difference of 1 in logits always means a factor of 2.72 in probability, whatever the logits are. Now add 100 to every logit: e102 / e101 is still 2.72, and the probabilities are exactly the same. Only differences between logits matter.

As a formula (derived in math 8.2): softmax(z)i = ezi / Σj ezj. With 2 classes it is exactly the sigmoid of the difference: softmax([z, 0])₁ = σ(z).

🤔 Why this? Softmax for one-of-K outputs
  1. The problem before Raw logits can be negative and don't add up to anything. Just dividing by the sum fails: [2, −1, −1] sums to 0. Using “pick the max” (argmax) gives an answer but no confidence and no slope, so gradient descent can't learn from it.
  2. What people tried One sigmoid per class (probabilities that don't add to 1, so “90% cat and 80% dog”); clipping negatives to 0 and normalizing (flat, no gradient for clipped classes).
  3. Why this works ez is always positive, keeps the order, and turns logit differences into probability ratios (+1 logit = ×2.72). Dividing by the total makes them a proper distribution. It's a soft argmax: the top class gets the most, everyone gets something, and every logit gets a gradient. And because log(ez) = z, it pairs perfectly with the log in cross-entropy: the gradient on each logit is simply pi − yi (predicted minus true; Lesson 1.1).
  4. What it costs, when it fails ez overflows for big logits (e89 is already infinity in float32: fix below). It forces the classes to compete, so it can't say “none of these” or “two of these” (use sigmoids). Probabilities can be badly overconfident. And over a 128,000-token vocabulary it's a big computation at every generated token.
  5. What refined it The stable max-subtraction / log-sum-exp form (below), temperature, label smoothing (Lesson 2.4), calibration by temperature scaling (Lesson 9.6), and sampling tricks for LLMs.

Temperature: one knob for how sure

Divide every logit by a number T before the softmax. T = 1 is plain softmax. T < 1 stretches the differences (a gap of 1 becomes a gap of 2 at T = 0.5), so the top class grabs more: [2, 1, 0.1] becomes [0.86, 0.12, 0.02]. T > 1 shrinks the gaps, spreading the probability out: at T = 2 it's [0.50, 0.30, 0.19].

What you should see: the defaults [2, 1, 0.5, −1] give cat 61%, dog 22%. Temperature can't make dog win: dividing by T keeps the order of the logits, so it only sharpens or flattens the current answer. T = 0.1 makes cat 99.995% sure; T = 5 flattens everything to between 18% and 32%, and somewhere past T = 7 no class is above 30%. To get dog to 90% you must change the logits themselves (raise dog's to about 4.4, or lower the others). +5 to every logit changes nothing at all. ×3 every logit is like T = 1/3: same answer, much more confident. That's the trap of the next section.

🤔 Why this? Temperature T (softmax(z / T))
  1. The problem before A trained model's softmax has a fixed sharpness. Sometimes you need sharper (pick the best answer), sometimes softer (generate varied text, or teach a smaller model which wrong classes are “almost right”), or the probabilities are simply overconfident.
  2. What people tried Always taking the argmax (repetitive, no confidence), or retraining the model.
  3. Why this works Dividing logits by T rescales every gap without changing the order: T → 0 approaches argmax (all probability on the top class), T → ∞ approaches uniform. It's one number, needs no retraining, and has three big uses: sampling from language models (higher T = more surprising tokens), knowledge distillation (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015: “raise the temperature of the final softmax” so the teacher's soft targets show which wrong classes are close), and calibration (Guo et al., 2017: learn a single T on held-out data so confidences match accuracy).
  4. What it costs, when it fails It can't fix a wrong ranking (the top class stays the top class). For generation, high T gives nonsense and T near 0 gives repetitive text. It's one global knob: it can't fix overconfidence that differs by class or input.
  5. What refined it Top-k and nucleus (top-p) sampling for text; per-class calibration methods; distillation recipes (Lesson 10.4); calibration and uncertainty in depth in Lesson 9.6.

Confidence is not correctness

The ×3 button showed it: multiply the logits by 3 and [2, 1, 0.1] goes from 66% cat to 95% cat. Same answer, same accuracy, but the model now sounds far surer. Nothing in training forces a 90% to mean “right 9 times out of 10”: cross-entropy keeps rewarding bigger logits on training examples the model already gets right, so big modern networks drift toward overconfidence. That's what Guo et al. measured: on CIFAR-100, LeNet (1998) had 44.9% error and confidences that matched its accuracy; a 110-layer ResNet had only 30.6% error but was overconfident.

🧠 Analogy: the weather forecaster

A good forecaster isn't one who's always confident. It's one whose “70% chance of rain” days are rainy about 70% of the time. That's calibration. Softmax gives you a number that looks like a probability; whether it behaves like one has to be checked on data the model hasn't seen. In medicine or self-driving, “I'm only 55% sure, ask a human” is worth more than a confident wrong answer (Lesson 9.6).

The overflow trap and the max-subtraction trick

Softmax looks harmless until logits get big. e1000 is far beyond the largest number a computer stores, so it becomes infinity, and infinity ÷ infinity is NaN (“not a number”). One NaN in a loss and every weight it touches becomes NaN on the next step. The fix uses the shift invariance you just saw: subtract the biggest logit first. The largest exponent is then e0 = 1, nothing can overflow, and the answer is mathematically identical.

What you should see: the naive version breaks as soon as the bigger logit (s + 1) passes the format's limit. In float64 that's 709.8 (e709.78 is about the largest float64), so s ≈ 709 gives NaN. In float32 the limit is 88.7, so s = 88 already fails (PyTorch: torch.exp(torch.tensor(89.)) is inf). In float16 it's about 11.1, so s = 11 fails: e12 is bigger than float16's maximum, 65,504. The stable version gives [0.269, 0.731] every time, because after subtracting the max the exponents are just e−1 and e0. More on number formats in math 8.5 and Lesson 10.2.

🤔 Why this? Max subtraction (and log-sum-exp)
  1. The problem before ez overflows to infinity above z ≈ 88.7 in float32 (≈ 11.1 in float16), and very negative logits underflow to 0, so naive softmax returns NaN, and log(0) in the loss returns −∞.
  2. What people tried Clipping logits to a safe range (changes the answer, and kills their gradients); computing in float64 (slow, and only moves the limit to 709.8).
  3. Why this works Softmax ignores a constant shift, so subtract m = max(z): softmax(z) = ez − m / Σ ez − m. Every exponent is ≤ 0, so every e… is between 0 and 1 and the biggest is exactly 1: no overflow, and the denominator is at least 1. For the loss, the same trick gives log-softmax = z − m − log Σ ez − m, the log-sum-exp trick, which never takes log(0).
  4. What it costs, when it fails One extra pass to find the max (cheap). It doesn't stop tiny probabilities rounding to 0 in low precision, which is why you should compute losses from logits, with fused, stable functions, rather than from probabilities.
  5. What refined it Every framework's softmax, log_softmax, logsumexp and cross_entropy does this internally. FlashAttention (Lesson 6.9) uses an “online” running-max version of the same trick to compute attention's softmax in tiles.

Multi-label vs multi-class: who has to share?

Softmax makes classes share one unit of probability: raise one and the others must fall. Perfect when exactly one answer is true (a digit is one digit). Wrong when several can be true at once: a photo can contain a person and a woman. For that, give every label its own sigmoid: each one is an independent yes/no, and they don't have to add up to anything. The YOLOv3 detector made exactly this switch, noting that a softmax “imposes the assumption that each box has exactly one class”, which fails for overlapping labels like Woman and Person.

Regression: sometimes the best bend is none

When the answer is a number (a house price, tomorrow's temperature, a bounding box's width), the output layer is a plain weighted sum with no activation. A sigmoid would cap the answer at 1; a ReLU would make negative answers impossible and give zero gradient below 0. Small tweaks when the range is known: softplus or exp for “must be positive” (a standard deviation), tanh for “between −1 and 1” (a steering angle), and predicting log(price) instead of price when values span orders of magnitude.

Why sigmoid and softmax come with cross-entropy (a preview)

Suppose the truth is “spam” (y = 1) but the logit is z = −5, so σ(z) = 0.0067: very wrong. How hard does training push z up?

LossGradient on the logit zAt z = −5
Mean squared error (p − y)²2(p − y) · p(1 − p)−0.013
Cross-entropy −log pp − y−0.993

With squared error, the sigmoid's flat tail (its slope p(1 − p) = 0.0066) multiplies the gradient, so the most wrong answers learn the slowest: 75× slower here. Cross-entropy's log exactly cancels the exponential inside the sigmoid or softmax, leaving the clean “predicted minus true”. That's why PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss and CrossEntropyLoss take logits and do the sigmoid/softmax themselves, stably. The full story is Lesson 1.1 (and math 6.3).

💡 Big idea The output layer is a contract about what the network says. Sigmoid: “probability of yes”. Several sigmoids: “each tag, independently”. Softmax: “one of these K, and here's how the belief is shared”. Linear: “this number”. Choose the contract that matches the question, pair it with its matching loss, compute both from logits, and remember the numbers are beliefs, not guarantees.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Everything above in PyTorch. Note the two loss functions take raw logits:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

logits = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
print("softmax      ", torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1))
print("+100 to all  ", torch.softmax(logits + 100, dim=-1))   # shift invariance
for T in (0.5, 2.0):
    print(f"T = {T}      ", torch.softmax(logits / T, dim=-1))

big = torch.tensor([1000.0, 1001.0])                         # float32 by default
naive = torch.exp(big) / torch.exp(big).sum()
print("naive        ", naive)                                  # inf / inf
print("stable       ", torch.softmax(big, dim=-1))            # subtracts the max inside

# Losses take RAW logits: they apply (log-)softmax / sigmoid internally, stably
target = torch.tensor([0])
print("cross_entropy(logits)         ", F.cross_entropy(logits[None], target).item())
print("cross_entropy(softmax(logits))", F.cross_entropy(torch.softmax(logits, -1)[None], target).item(), "← double softmax bug")

# Multi-label: one sigmoid per label, binary cross-entropy per label
tags = torch.tensor([[3.0, 2.5, -4.0, -1.0]])                # person, woman, dog, car
print("sigmoid      ", torch.sigmoid(tags))
print("BCE loss     ", F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(tags, torch.tensor([[1., 1, 0, 0]])).item())

Gotcha: nn.CrossEntropyLoss applies log-softmax itself. Feeding it probabilities (softmax twice) gives a wrong, squashed loss and weak gradients, and nothing crashes. Put softmax only where you need probabilities for people (or for sampling), never before the loss.

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through softmax. Why the exponential, and how do you implement it safely?” A strong answer:

  • Exponentiate then normalize: positive, sums to 1, keeps order; logit differences become probability ratios (Δz = 1 → ×e); it's a differentiable soft argmax and with cross-entropy the logit gradient is p − y.
  • Shift invariant, so subtract the max to avoid overflow (e89 = inf in float32); compute losses with log-softmax / log-sum-exp, or just pass logits to cross_entropy.
  • Temperature z/T: sharper or flatter without changing the ranking; used for sampling, distillation and calibration.
  • Knows when not to use it: multi-label → independent sigmoids with BCE; regression → linear. And that softmax confidence isn't calibrated by default.
🏭 In the real world
A 1000-way softmax on ImageNet 📜 History · 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton: AlexNet

How: the last fully connected layer feeds a 1000-way softmax that produces a distribution over the 1,000 class labels.

Why this method: each image has exactly one label, so one-of-K with cross-entropy (maximizing the log-probability of the right class) is the natural contract.

Sigmoids instead of softmax for overlapping labels 🧪 Research · 2018 Redmon & Farhadi: YOLOv3

How: each box's classes are predicted with independent logistic classifiers trained with binary cross-entropy.

Why this method: in datasets like Open Images labels overlap (Woman and Person), and softmax assumes exactly one class per box.

High temperature to share “dark knowledge” 🧪 Research · 2015 Hinton, Vinyals & Dean: Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network

How: a big “teacher” model's softmax is run at a high temperature to produce soft targets; a small “student” is trained on them at the same temperature, then used at T = 1.

Why this method: at T = 1 the teacher's wrong-class probabilities are nearly 0; raising T reveals which wrong answers are close (a 2 that looks like a 3), which is extra information for the student (Lesson 10.4).

One number fixes overconfidence 🧪 Research · 2017 Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger: On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

How: after training, fit a single temperature T on a validation set and divide all logits by it.

Why this method: modern deep networks are overconfident; the paper found this single-parameter fix “surprisingly effective” on most datasets, and since it can't change the ranking, accuracy is untouched (Lesson 9.6).

A softmax over 128,000 tokens 🏢 Meta · 2024 Llama Team: The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: the final layer produces one logit per token of a 128K vocabulary; softmax (often with a temperature) turns them into next-token probabilities for sampling.

Why this method: next-token prediction is one-of-K by construction; the vocabulary size makes this last layer one of the bigger single matrices in the model.

🤯 Fun fact · e¹² breaks half precision

float16's largest number is 65,504, and e12 ≈ 162,755. So in 16-bit arithmetic a naive softmax overflows for a logit as small as 12, a value real networks produce all the time (PyTorch: torch.exp(torch.tensor(12., dtype=torch.float16)) is inf). That's one reason mixed-precision training keeps softmax and losses in float32, and why bfloat16, with float32's range (max ≈ 3.4 × 1038), became popular for training (Lesson 10.2).

Check yourself

1. A two-class model outputs logits [0, ln 3] (ln 3 ≈ 1.10). What does softmax give?
e0 = 1 and eln 3 = 3, total 4, so [1/4, 3/4] = [0.25, 0.75]. A logit gap of ln 3 means a probability ratio of exactly 3. (Same as σ(ln 3) = 0.75 for the second class.)
2. You're building a photo tagger: each image can have any of 20 tags (beach, dog, sunset…), including several or none. What output layer and loss?
Tags aren't mutually exclusive, so they mustn't share one unit of probability. Softmax would force “beach” and “sunset” to compete and could never say “no tags”. Independent sigmoids answer 20 separate yes/no questions; threshold each (e.g. at 0.5, tuned per tag). Predicting a tag index as a number makes no sense at all.
3. Your hand-written training loop computes probs = torch.exp(z) / torch.exp(z).sum(-1, keepdim=True) and then -torch.log(probs[target]). After a few hundred steps the loss becomes NaN. Logits were growing past 90. Best fix?
In float32, ez overflows above about 88.7: inf / inf = NaN, and log of an underflowed 0 is −∞. The stable formulation is mathematically identical and never overflows. A lower learning rate only delays it; clipping logits changes the model's answers and blocks their gradients.
📝 TL;DR
  • The last layer outputs logits. Yes/no → sigmoid; any-of-K tags → a sigmoid each; one-of-K → softmax; a number → no bend.
  • Softmax = exponentiate, then normalize. Logit gap of 1 = probability ratio of e ≈ 2.72; adding a constant changes nothing.
  • Temperature softmax(z/T): T < 1 sharper, T > 1 flatter, never changes the ranking. Used for sampling, distillation, calibration.
  • Subtract the max before exponentiating (e89 = inf in float32, e12 in float16); give losses logits, not probabilities.
  • Pair sigmoid/softmax with cross-entropy (gradient p − y). And confidence isn't correctness: big networks are often overconfident.
