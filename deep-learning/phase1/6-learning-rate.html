Phase 1 · Lesson 6

The learning rate: warmup, cosine and finding it

If you could tune only one number, tune this one. Too small and training crawls, too big and it blows up, and the best value sits right next to the cliff. Then the best runs change it over time: ramp it up, hold it, bring it down.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Each step moves the weights by lr × (the optimizer's direction) (Lesson 1.5).
  • In a valley with curvature c, gradient descent diverges when lr > 2/c (math 3.4b); the ravine of Lesson 1.5 had c = 4, so the limit was 0.5.
  • Adam's running average v starts at 0 and needs many steps to settle (Lesson 1.5's bias-correction example).
🎯 Hook

The RAdam paper (Liu et al., 2019) reports a striking experiment: training a standard Transformer translation model with Adam, removing the warmup stage increases the training loss from 3 to around 10. Warmup is just “use a smaller learning rate for the first few thousand steps”. That's all it takes to break, or save, the run.

Llama 3 405B was trained with a peak learning rate of 8 × 10⁻⁵, a linear warmup of 8,000 steps, then a cosine decay down to 8 × 10⁻⁷ over 1.2 million steps. Every one of those numbers is a learning-rate decision. Why ramp up at the start, why decay at the end, and how do you find the peak in the first place? You'll answer all three with live runs.

Three regimes: on the left it crawls (after 200 steps lr = 0.001 has barely moved from its starting loss); in the middle it works, over about one order of magnitude; on the right it breaks, and the break is sudden. The lowest losses sit just below the cliff.

💡 Big idea The learning rate sets how far each step goes. Steps must be small compared with the curvature of the loss (or they overshoot, lr > 2/c) but large enough to make progress in the flat directions. Early in training the network and Adam's statistics are unsettled, so you start small (warmup); late in training the noise of mini-batches keeps you bouncing around the minimum, so you end small (decay). The peak in between is the one number you search for.
🧠 Analogy: parking a car

Pull out of the driveway slowly while you check the mirrors (warmup: you don't know the road yet). Drive at highway speed for most of the trip (the peak). Slow down as you approach the house, and creep into the parking spot (decay). Driving at highway speed into the garage is the constant-learning-rate bug: you get close fast, then keep overshooting the spot.

The LR range test: find the edge in one run

Sweeping 80 learning rates costs 80 runs. Leslie Smith's LR range test (from his 2015 cyclical-learning-rate paper, popularized by fast.ai) does it in one short run: start tiny, raise the learning rate a little every step, and plot loss against lr. The loss first doesn't move, then falls, then explodes.

Schedules: change the learning rate over time

  • Step decay: divide by 10 at fixed points. Goyal et al.'s ResNet-50 recipe uses 0.1 · (batch/256) and ÷ 10 at epochs 30, 60 and 80 of 90.
  • Cosine decay (Loshchilov & Hutter's SGDR, 2016): glide from the peak to a minimum along half a cosine wave, lr = lrmin + ½(lrmax − lrmin)(1 + cos(π · t/T)). Their version restarts periodically (“warm restarts”); LLMs use a single cycle.
  • Linear warmup: ramp from near 0 to the peak over the first steps (2,000 for Llama 2, 8,000 for Llama 3 405B).
  • Warmup–stable–decay (WSD): warm up, hold the peak constant for most of training, then decay quickly at the end (named in the MiniCPM paper, 2024).
  • One-cycle (Smith): one ramp up to a large peak and one long ramp down far below the start; Smith reports “super-convergence”, training an order of magnitude faster in some settings.
🤔 Why this? Learning-rate decay (cosine)
  1. The problem before With mini-batches, every gradient is noisy. A constant learning rate that's big enough to make fast progress early keeps bouncing around the minimum late: in our replay below, a constant lr of 0.3 ends at a loss of 0.367.
  2. What people tried Step decay (÷ 10 at fixed epochs: works, but you must pick the epochs), decaying when validation stalls, 1/t decay (falls too fast).
  3. Why this works Big steps early cover distance; small steps late let the noise average out so the weights settle into the bottom. Cosine does this smoothly with no milestones to choose: it stays near the peak for a while, then falls fastest in the middle and flattens near the end. Same run, cosine to 0: final loss 0.018 instead of 0.367.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You must know the total number of steps in advance; stopping early leaves you at a high lr, and extending training means restarting the schedule. If the peak was already too small, decaying only makes it crawl sooner (try a peak of 0.03 in the replay).
  5. What refined it Warm restarts (SGDR), WSD (hold constant, decay at the end, so you can stop or extend any time), and decaying to ~10% of the peak rather than 0 (Llama 2).
🤔 Why this? Linear warmup
  1. The problem before At step 0 the weights are random, gradients can be large and erratic, and Adam's second-moment estimate v is built from just a handful of gradients. Liu et al. (RAdam) identify the adaptive learning rate's variance as “problematically large in the early stage”. Full-size steps then can push a large model into a bad region it never recovers from (loss ~10 instead of 3 in their Transformer example).
  2. What people tried A lower learning rate for the whole run (slow); a constant small lr for the first epochs and then a jump, which Goyal et al. found makes the error spike right after the jump.
  3. Why this works Ramping linearly from almost 0 to the peak gives the network and the optimizer's statistics time to settle before the big steps start. Goyal et al.'s gradual 5-epoch warmup let a batch of 8,192 images match the small-batch error (23.74% vs 23.60%); without warmup it was 24.84%, with the constant-then-jump warmup 25.88%.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One more number (warmup length: commonly 1–5% of training, or a few thousand steps). Too short doesn't help; too long wastes steps at a low lr. On a small network like ours it barely matters: warmup is mostly insurance for big models, big batches and Adam.
  5. What refined it RAdam builds a warmup-like correction into Adam itself; in practice nearly every LLM recipe still uses explicit linear warmup with AdamW.
✏️ Worked example: Llama 3 405B's schedule, by hand

Peak 8 × 10⁻⁵, linear warmup 8,000 steps, cosine to 8 × 10⁻⁷ over 1,200,000 steps.

  • Step 4,000 (halfway through warmup): 4,000/8,000 × 8 × 10⁻⁵ = 4 × 10⁻⁵.
  • Halfway through the cosine part, cos(π/2) = 0: lr = 8 × 10⁻⁷ + ½(8 × 10⁻⁵ − 8 × 10⁻⁷)(1 + 0) ≈ 4.04 × 10⁻⁵.
  • The end: 8 × 10⁻⁷, 1/100 of the peak. Warmup is 8,000 / 1,200,000 ≈ 0.7% of training.
🤔 Why this? Warmup–stable–decay (WSD)
  1. The problem before A cosine schedule must know the total length up front. Want to train longer, or compare checkpoints at 40%, 60% and 80% of the data? You'd need a separate full run for each length.
  2. What people tried Cosine with restarts; guessing the length; just stopping mid-cosine (worse loss).
  3. Why this works Hold the peak constant for most of training (the “stable” phase, which you can extend any time), then decay quickly at the end. MiniCPM found a decay over about 10% of the tokens was enough to reach their best results (2.5% fell short), and Hägele et al. (2024) found a constant lr plus a cooldown scales as reliably as cosine. One long stable run can spawn many cheap “decayed” checkpoints.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The loss during the stable phase looks worse than cosine's (it only drops during the decay), which confuses dashboards; the decay shape and length are new knobs.
  5. What refined it Variants are now common in LLM pre-training: DeepSeek-V3 held a constant 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ after warmup until 10T tokens, then decayed; Karpathy's nanochat uses warmup, constant, then a linear “warmdown” over the last 65% of steps (checked November 2026).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Schedulers live in torch.optim.lr_scheduler. Writing warmup + cosine as a LambdaLR keeps it explicit (it's what nanoGPT-style code does):

import math, torch
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LambdaLR, OneCycleLR

model = torch.nn.Linear(10, 1)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)   # lr here = the PEAK
total, warmup, final_frac = 1000, 100, 0.1

def warmup_cosine(step):                     # returns a multiplier of the peak lr
    if step < warmup:
        return (step + 1) / warmup            # linear warmup
    progress = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return final_frac + (1 - final_frac) * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

sched = LambdaLR(opt, warmup_cosine)
lrs = []
for step in range(total):
    # ... forward, loss, zero_grad, backward ...
    opt.step()                                # optimizer step first
    lrs.append(opt.param_groups[0]["lr"])
    sched.step()                              # then advance the schedule, once per step
for s in [0, 49, 99, 100, 550, 999]:
    print(f"step {s:4d}: lr = {lrs[s]:.2e}")

# One-cycle (Smith): warm up to max_lr, then anneal far below the start
opt2 = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)
oc = OneCycleLR(opt2, max_lr=0.1, total_steps=1000)
seen = []
for step in range(1000):
    opt2.step(); seen.append(opt2.param_groups[0]["lr"]); oc.step()
print(f"one-cycle: start {seen[0]:.4f}, max {max(seen):.4f} at step {seen.index(max(seen))}, end {seen[-1]:.1e}")

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# step    0: lr = 3.00e-06
# step   49: lr = 1.50e-04
# step   99: lr = 3.00e-04
# step  100: lr = 3.00e-04
# step  550: lr = 1.65e-04
# step  999: lr = 3.00e-05
# one-cycle: start 0.0040, max 0.1000 at step 299, end 4.0e-07

Gotchas: call sched.step() once per optimizer step (not per epoch, unless the schedule is defined in epochs) and after opt.step(), or PyTorch warns and you skip the first value. Log the lr every step: a schedule bug looks exactly like a bad learning rate. Built-ins also exist: LinearLR, CosineAnnealingLR, SequentialLR to chain them, ReduceLROnPlateau.

🎤 Interview angle

“How do you choose the learning rate? Why warmup and cosine?” A strong answer:

  • Calls it the most important hyperparameter; searches it on a log scale (3e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3…) or with a range test; knows the best value sits just below divergence (2/curvature in a quadratic).
  • Warmup: early gradients are large and erratic, Adam's v is poorly estimated, and large batches amplify this; linear warmup over ~1–5% of steps (Goyal: 5 epochs; Llama 2: 2,000 steps).
  • Decay: mini-batch noise keeps a constant-lr run bouncing; cosine (or WSD's cooldown) lets it settle; decay to ~10% of the peak.
  • Ties it to batch size (bigger batch → bigger lr, Lesson 1.7) and to debugging: a loss that spikes and never recovers often means lr too high or warmup too short.
🏭 In the real world
Warmup + cosine at 405B scale 🏢 Meta · 2024 The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: AdamW, peak 8 × 10⁻⁵, linear warmup over 8,000 steps, cosine decay to 8 × 10⁻⁷ over 1.2 million steps.

Why this method: the proven, stable recipe; the paper reports few loss spikes and no interventions needed to correct divergence.

Warmup, then hold, then decay 🏢 DeepSeek · 2024 DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report

How: linear warmup to 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ over 2K steps, constant until 10T tokens, cosine decay to 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ over the next 4.3T tokens, then constant low rates at the very end.

Why this method: a long stable phase keeps training flexible; the decay comes when the data plan is final, the WSD idea.

WSD named and analyzed 🧪 Research · 2024 Hu et al.: MiniCPM · Hägele et al.: Scaling Laws Beyond Fixed Training Durations

How: warmup, constant, then a short decay (about 10% of tokens in MiniCPM); Hägele et al. compare constant + cooldown with cosine across scales.

Why this method: no need to fix the training length in advance, so scaling studies reuse one run instead of training one model per length.

Cosine annealing with restarts 🧪 Research · 2016 Loshchilov & Hutter: SGDR

How: cosine from max to min within each cycle, then a “warm restart” back to the max.

Why this method: smooth decay without hand-picked milestones; the single-cycle version became the standard LLM schedule.

🤯 Fun fact · the Transformer's odd schedule

The original Transformer paper didn't use cosine. It warmed up linearly for 4,000 steps and then decayed proportionally to 1/√step. So warmup has been part of the transformer recipe since the very first paper, years before anyone had a good theory of why it helps.

Check yourself

1. A run uses linear warmup to a peak of 6 × 10⁻⁴ over 2,000 steps. What is the learning rate at step 500?
Linear warmup: lr = peak × step / warmup = 6 × 10⁻⁴ × 500/2,000 = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴. It reaches 3 × 10⁻⁴ at step 1,000 and the full peak at step 2,000 (these are nanoGPT's defaults).
2. A transformer's loss drops for 300 steps, then spikes to 3× its value and never recovers. You use AdamW at 1e-3 with no warmup. What do you try first?
A spike that doesn't recover early in training is the classic sign of steps that are too big for the network's (and Adam's) unsettled state. Warmup gives the second-moment estimate and the weights time to settle before full-size steps; a lower peak helps too. Raising the lr makes it worse; the decay shape matters late, not early.
3. You plan a 100k-step pre-training run but may extend it to 200k if results look good. Which schedule makes that easiest?
Cosine and step schedules are tied to the planned length: at 100k the lr is already near 0, so extending means re-warming. WSD's stable phase can be extended indefinitely; you start the short decay only when you decide to stop. MiniCPM found ~10% decay enough; Hägele et al. found constant + cooldown scales as reliably as cosine.
📝 TL;DR
  • The learning rate is the most important knob: too small crawls, too big breaks suddenly, the best sits just below the edge (2/curvature in a quadratic). Search on a log scale or with an LR range test.
  • Warmup (linear, ~1–5% of steps) protects the unsettled early phase, especially with Adam, big batches and big models (RAdam: loss 3 vs ~10 without it).
  • Decay lets noisy mini-batch training settle: cosine to ~10% of the peak is the classic LLM recipe (Llama 2/3); in our replay, cosine beat constant 20× at a high peak.
  • WSD (warmup, constant, short decay) frees you from fixing the length in advance; one-cycle and step decay are the CNN classics.
  • In PyTorch: LambdaLR (or LinearLR + CosineAnnealingLR), sched.step() after every opt.step(), and log the lr.
