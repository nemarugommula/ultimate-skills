Phase 1 · Lesson 1

Loss functions: MSE, cross-entropy, and why cross-entropy for classes

The loss is the one number a network tries to make small. Pick the wrong one and a network that is badly wrong can barely tell it is wrong. Pick the right one and the same network fixes itself ten times faster.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A network ends in an output layer: sigmoid turns a raw score (a logit z) into a probability, softmax turns a list of logits into probabilities that add to 1 (Lesson 0.6).
  • Training nudges every weight against the gradient of the loss (math 3.4).
  • Sigmoid's slope σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) is at most 0.25 and nearly 0 when |z| is large: it saturates (Lesson 0.4).
🎯 Hook

Here is a puzzle you'll run yourself in a minute. Two identical spam detectors start out confidently wrong: on real spam they say “5% spam”. Same data, same learning rate, same seed. The only difference is the loss: one uses mean squared error, the other cross-entropy.

The cross-entropy one is fixed after 12 steps. The MSE one needs 151, and with a slightly more confident start it is still stuck after 300. Same network, same data. How can the choice of “how to score a mistake” make a 12× difference?

Who cares: image classifiers and GPT-style language models (which classify “which token comes next?” billions of times) are trained by minimizing cross-entropy. By the end you'll know why, and when you'd pick something else.

Numbers first: a loss is a score for being wrong

A house-price model predicts $300k; the house sold for $320k. The error is 20 (in $k). Square it and you get the squared error: 20² = 400. Do that for every house and average: that's the mean squared error, MSE.

errors 20, −10, 5 → squares 400, 100, 25 → MSE = (400 + 100 + 25) ÷ 3 = 175

That number is all the network ever “sees” about how it's doing. Backprop (Lesson 1.2) asks one question of it: if I nudge this weight, does the number go up or down, and how fast? So a loss has two jobs: it must be small when the model is right, and its slope must point usefully toward better answers, strongly when the model is very wrong.

💡 Big idea The network never sees the loss's value during learning, only its slope. Two losses can agree on which answer is best and still train completely differently, because one gives a loud correction for a big mistake and the other whispers. Choosing a loss means choosing how loudly each kind of mistake is corrected.
🤔 Why this? Mean squared error (MSE)
  1. The problem before You need one number for “how wrong” over many predictions. Plain averaged errors cancel out: errors of +50 and −50 average to 0, a “perfect” score for a bad model.
  2. What people tried Absolute errors |ŷ − y| (no cancelling, but a kink at 0 where the slope jumps from −1 to +1).
  3. Why this works Squaring removes the sign, is smooth everywhere, and its slope 2(ŷ − y) is proportional to the error: off by 20 gives a push of 40, off by 2 a push of 4. Big mistakes get big corrections, and the push fades smoothly as you arrive. The best constant guess under MSE is the mean, and minimizing it is the same as maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise (math 5.3).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Squaring makes outliers dominate: one house off by 600 contributes 360,000, more than a thousand houses off by 15. And for classification behind a sigmoid, its slope gets multiplied by σ′, which vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong (the hook; you'll see it below).
  5. What refined it MAE and Huber for outliers (next), cross-entropy for classes (this lesson's main act).

The outlier problem, live

Suppose a model can only predict one number for every house. Which number is “best” depends on the loss: MSE picks the mean, absolute error (MAE, mean absolute error) picks the median, and Huber picks something in between.

🤔 Why this? MAE and Huber (smooth L1)
  1. The problem before MSE's push grows with the error, so a single mislabeled target (a typo, a sensor glitch) can produce gradients 100× larger than everyone else's and drag the model toward it.
  2. What people tried Cleaning the data by hand; MAE, whose push is always ±1 no matter how wrong (robust, but the slope jumps at 0 and never shrinks, so it jitters around the answer).
  3. Why this works Huber (1964) is “MSE near the answer, MAE far away”: for errors under δ it's ½e², beyond δ it grows linearly, so the push is capped at δ. You get MSE's gentle landing and MAE's robustness. With δ = 50, an error of 600 pushes with 50 instead of 1,200.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One more knob (δ), which depends on your units. If the “outliers” are real and important (rare but true big prices), Huber deliberately underweights them.
  5. What refined it Fast R-CNN's “smooth L1” (Huber with δ = 1) for bounding boxes (Lesson 4.2); DQN clips its error to ±1 for the same reason (RL 4.3).

Classification: score the probability of the right answer

Now the network outputs a probability: “this email is spam with probability p”. The truth is y = 1 (spam) or y = 0. How should we score p = 0.9? p = 0.02?

Cross-entropy says: look at the probability the model gave to the right answer, and score it by how surprised the model should be: −log(pright).

Model's probability for the right answerCross-entropy −ln pFeels like
0.990.01“Obviously.” Almost no penalty.
0.90.105Mild.
0.50.693A coin flip (you'll see 0.69 on many stuck loss curves).
0.12.30Surprised.
0.014.61Shocked. And it keeps growing: p → 0 means loss → ∞.

For two classes this is binary cross-entropy (BCE): −[y·ln p + (1 − y)·ln(1 − p)]. Only one of the two terms is ever “on”: if y = 1 it's −ln p, if y = 0 it's −ln(1 − p). Information theory calls −ln p the surprise of an event; cross-entropy is the average surprise at the truth (math 6.1, math 6.3 derives it).

🧠 Analogy: a weather forecaster's report card

A forecaster who said “1% chance of rain” on a day it poured should lose their job, not lose a point. MSE gives that forecaster (1 − 0.01)² = 0.98, barely worse than one who said 50% (0.25 → it's only 4× worse). Cross-entropy gives 4.6 versus 0.69: it treats confident nonsense as the disaster it is, and that's exactly the case where you want the loudest correction.

The slope is what matters: drag a prediction

The network doesn't output p directly; it outputs a logit z, and the sigmoid turns it into p = σ(z). So the push that reaches the network is the gradient with respect to z. By the chain rule (math 3.2):

MSE: slope with respect to p, times sigmoid's slope∂(p − y)²/∂z = 2(p − y) · σ′(z)    cross-entropy: σ′ cancels out∂BCE/∂z = p − y

That σ′ is the villain. When the model is confidently wrong (p near 0 while y = 1), z is very negative and σ′(z) ≈ 0. MSE multiplies the correction by almost nothing at exactly the moment it is most needed.

🤔 Why this? Cross-entropy for classes
  1. The problem before MSE behind a sigmoid gives a push of 2(p − y)σ′(z). When the model is confidently wrong, σ′ ≈ 0: at p = 0.001 the push is only 0.002. Networks starting in (or wandering into) confident mistakes crawl out very slowly.
  2. What people tried MSE on probabilities (the 1980s default), bigger learning rates (which blow up the parts that were fine), tanh outputs with targets of ±0.9.
  3. Why this works −ln p is the natural score for a probability: it's what maximum likelihood minimizes (math 5.3). Its slope −1/p exactly cancels sigmoid's σ′, leaving the beautifully simple push p − y: “how far off is the probability?”. It's large when you're badly wrong and fades to 0 when you're right. Softmax + cross-entropy cancels the same way, giving pk − yk for every class.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The loss is unbounded: one example labelled with near-zero probability can produce a huge loss (bad labels hurt). It rewards ever-more-confident outputs, so models become overconfident (99.9% sure and wrong). Computing log(sigmoid(z)) naively overflows; always use the fused versions (BCEWithLogitsLoss, CrossEntropyLoss) that take logits.
  5. What refined it Label smoothing (below) against overconfidence; focal loss for heavy class imbalance in detectors (Lesson 4.2); temperature scaling for calibration. Next-token cross-entropy trains every LLM (Lesson 6.7); calibration is Lesson 9.6.

Many classes: softmax + cross-entropy

With K classes the network outputs K logits, softmax turns them into probabilities, and the loss is again −ln(probability of the right class). A model that knows nothing and guesses uniformly scores ln K: ln 2 = 0.69 for two classes, ln 10 = 2.30 for digits. That's your sanity check on step 0 (Lesson 2.6).

Yes, raising the dog logit raises the loss: it steals probability from cat. Set all three equal and the loss is exactly ln 3. With a hard target you can drive the loss toward 0 only by pushing cat to infinity. Turn on label smoothing and the target becomes (0.933, 0.033, 0.033): now there is a best confidence, and pushing cat beyond it makes the loss go back up (the cat gradient turns positive).

🤔 Why this? Label smoothing
  1. The problem before With a hard target, cross-entropy is only 0 when p(right) = 1, which needs an infinitely large logit gap. Networks keep growing their logits, become overconfident, and (the Inception-v3 authors argued) less able to adapt.
  2. What people tried Weight decay, dropout, early stopping (Lesson 2.4): all indirect.
  3. Why this works Szegedy et al. (2015) mix the one-hot target with a uniform one: target = (1 − ε) on the right class plus ε/K spread over all K classes. With ε = 0.1 and 1,000 ImageNet classes, the right class's target is 0.9001 and every other class gets 0.0001. Now the loss has a finite best logit gap, so logits stop growing. They report a consistent gain of about 0.2% absolute in both top-1 and top-5 error. The original Transformer used ε = 0.1 too.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The model's probabilities stop meaning “true frequency” (it's taught to never say more than 0.93 in our 3-class example), and Müller, Kornblith & Hinton (2019) found it hurts knowledge distillation (Lesson 10.4). It's one more ε to tune.
  5. What refined it It's now a one-argument switch in PyTorch (label_smoothing=0.1). Calibration methods and mixup-style augmentation (Lesson 2.5) attack overconfidence in other ways.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

PyTorch losses are modules in torch.nn (or functions in torch.nn.functional). The two classification losses take raw logits, not probabilities: they apply sigmoid/softmax inside, in a numerically safe way.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

# Regression: one outlier among 8 house prices (in $k)
pred = torch.full((8,), 280.0)
y = torch.tensor([210., 240., 250., 260., 275., 290., 320., 900.])
for name, loss in [("MSE", nn.MSELoss()), ("MAE", nn.L1Loss()), ("Huber", nn.HuberLoss(delta=50.0))]:
    print(f"{name:5s} loss = {loss(pred, y).item():9.1f}")

# Binary: the model outputs a raw score (logit); the loss applies the sigmoid itself
logit = torch.tensor([-3.89], requires_grad=True)        # sigmoid(-3.89) ≈ 0.02: confidently wrong
target = torch.tensor([1.0])
nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()(logit, target).backward()
print("BCE:  dL/dlogit =", round(logit.grad.item(), 3))
logit.grad = None
nn.MSELoss()(torch.sigmoid(logit), target).backward()
print("MSE:  dL/dlogit =", round(logit.grad.item(), 3))

# Multi-class: CrossEntropyLoss wants raw logits and class indices
logits = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.1]])                   # cat, dog, fox
label = torch.tensor([0])                                  # the answer is “cat”
print("CE:", round(nn.CrossEntropyLoss()(logits, label).item(), 3))
print("CE with label smoothing 0.1:", round(nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)(logits, label).item(), 3))

Gotcha: never put nn.Softmax before nn.CrossEntropyLoss (it would apply softmax twice and gradients shrink; Lesson 1.3 shows the damage). And nn.HuberLoss(delta=1.0) equals nn.SmoothL1Loss(beta=1.0); for other values they differ by a factor of δ.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why not use MSE for classification?” A strong answer:

  • Goes to the gradient: with a sigmoid, MSE's gradient on the logit is 2(p − y)σ′(z), and σ′ vanishes when the model is confidently wrong; cross-entropy's is p − y.
  • Adds the probabilistic reason: cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of the labels (maximum likelihood), MSE is the likelihood under Gaussian noise, which doesn't fit 0/1 labels.
  • Mentions numerics: pass logits to BCEWithLogitsLoss / CrossEntropyLoss, never probabilities.
  • Knows the refinements: label smoothing against overconfidence, focal loss for imbalance, Huber when regression targets have outliers.
🏭 In the real world
Label smoothing on ImageNet 🧪 Research · 2015 Szegedy et al.: Rethinking the Inception Architecture (Inception-v3)

How: cross-entropy against a smoothed target, ε = 0.1 spread uniformly over 1,000 classes.

Why this method: it stops the largest logit from running away from the others; they report about 0.2% absolute improvement in top-1 and top-5 error.

The Transformer's training loss 🧪 Research · 2017 Vaswani et al.: Attention Is All You Need

How: cross-entropy over the output vocabulary with label smoothing ε = 0.1 (Phase 6 builds the model).

Why this method: the authors note it hurts perplexity, because the model learns to be more unsure, but improves accuracy and BLEU (a translation quality score).

Smooth L1 for bounding boxes 🧪 Research · 2015 Girshick: Fast R-CNN

How: class scores use cross-entropy; box coordinates use smooth L1 (Huber with δ = 1).

Why this method: the paper calls it less sensitive to outliers than L2, and notes that L2 on unbounded targets needs careful learning-rate tuning to avoid exploding gradients.

When smoothing helps and when it hurts 🧪 Research · 2019 Müller, Kornblith & Hinton: When Does Label Smoothing Help?

How: studied what label smoothing does to a network's internal representations and its probabilities.

Why this method: it improves calibration (probabilities closer to real frequencies) but makes networks worse teachers for distillation, a real cost to weigh.

🤯 Fun fact · the loss of knowing nothing

GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens. A model that guesses uniformly over them has a cross-entropy of ln 50,257 ≈ 10.8. That's why freshly initialized language models print a loss near 10.8 on step 0, and why you should worry if yours prints 60 or 0.01: something is wired wrong (Lesson 2.6).

Check yourself

1. A 4-class classifier gives the right class probability 0.25. What is its cross-entropy on this example (natural log)?
Cross-entropy is −ln(p of the right class) = −ln 0.25 = 1.386. That's also ln 4: a 25% guess on 4 classes is exactly the “know nothing” uniform guess. (0.75² = 0.56 would be the squared error on that class, not cross-entropy.)
2. A colleague's binary classifier (sigmoid output, MSE loss) sits at 50% accuracy for hundreds of steps; many examples get p ≈ 0.01 when the truth is 1. What's the best first fix?
MSE's push on the logit is 2(p − y)σ′(z); at p = 0.01, σ′ ≈ 0.0099 and the push is about 0.02. Cross-entropy's is p − y = −0.99, about 50× bigger. A huge learning rate would also blow up the examples that are already fine. Depth doesn't address the loss's flat gradient.
3. You predict delivery times. 2% of the logged times are wrong by hours (the courier forgot to tap “delivered”). Which loss is the sensible default?
MSE would let the 2% of broken logs dominate the gradient (pushes proportional to hours of error). Huber caps each example's push at δ, so the bad logs can't drag the model, while normal errors still get MSE's smooth behaviour. Cross-entropy is for class probabilities, not times.
📝 TL;DR
  • A loss is one number for “how wrong”; training only ever follows its slope, so the shape of the loss decides how loudly each mistake is corrected.
  • MSE (ŷ − y)² for regression: push proportional to the error, predicts the mean, outliers dominate. MAE predicts the median; Huber caps the push at δ.
  • Cross-entropy −ln pright = surprise at the truth. Behind sigmoid/softmax its push on the logits is simply p − y; MSE's has a σ′ that vanishes when confidently wrong (25× weaker at p = 0.02).
  • Know-nothing loss is ln K (0.69 for 2 classes, 10.8 for GPT-2's vocabulary): check it on step 0.
  • Label smoothing (ε = 0.1) gives the loss a finite best confidence; pass logits, not probabilities, to PyTorch's losses.
