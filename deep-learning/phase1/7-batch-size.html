Phase 1 · Lesson 7

Batch size: noise, speed and the scaling rules

How many examples should each step look at? One is noisy, all of them is slow, and the sweet spot moves during training. Get the batch size and learning rate to agree and you can train on 256 GPUs as accurately as on 8.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The DataLoader hands out mini-batches; one epoch = one pass over the data (Lesson 1.3).
  • The gradient of a mini-batch is an average of per-example gradients, a noisy estimate of the full-data gradient (math 7.2). Averages of B samples have noise ∝ 1/√B (math 4.7).
  • Warmup ramps the learning rate up at the start (Lesson 1.6).
🎯 Hook

In 2017 a team at Facebook took the standard ImageNet recipe, ResNet-50 with batches of 256 images, which took 29 hours on 8 GPUs, and trained it in 1 hour on 256 GPUs with batches of 8,192, with essentially the same accuracy (23.74% vs 23.60% top-1 error). Two rules did it: multiply the learning rate by the same factor as the batch, and warm up gradually.

And yet a 2018 study from Graphcore found that their best test results came from batches of just 2 to 32. Llama 3 405B started with a smaller batch for stability and doubled it twice during training. So is a bigger batch better or worse? Both, and by the end you'll know exactly when.

The catch is the square root: going from 16 to 256 makes each step 16× more expensive but its gradient only 4× less noisy. Whether that trade is worth it depends on whether noise is what's slowing you down, and on whether those 256 examples run in parallel (on a GPU they mostly do, until the memory is full).

💡 Big idea A mini-batch gradient = the true direction + noise that shrinks like 1/√B. When the noise is large compared with the true gradient, a bigger batch buys real progress per step, and with the learning rate scaled up to match, you get the same result in proportionally fewer steps. When the noise is already small, extra examples are wasted compute. That turning point is the critical batch size, and it grows as training goes on.
🤔 Why this? Mini-batches
  1. The problem before Full-batch gradient descent reads the entire dataset for one step: with 1.28 million ImageNet images, a single update is a full epoch. Pure SGD (B = 1) updates after every example, but its direction is very noisy and it can't use a GPU's thousands of parallel cores.
  2. What people tried Full-batch methods (slow per step), single-example online SGD (noisy, no parallelism).
  3. Why this works Average B examples: the direction gets √B less noisy, and the B forward/backward passes run in parallel on a GPU, so a batch of 64 often costs barely more wall-clock time than a batch of 1. Typical batches (32–512 for vision, millions of tokens for LLMs) sit in between. The leftover noise even helps a little: it keeps the optimizer from settling into the first narrow dip it finds.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Activation memory grows linearly with B (Lesson 1.3), so the GPU caps the batch. The best learning rate depends on B. Batch-dependent layers (BatchNorm, Lesson 2.3) behave badly with tiny batches.
  5. What refined it The linear scaling rule with warmup, gradient accumulation (both below), and batch-size schedules that grow B during training.

What you should see: the noise halves each time the batch grows 4× (B = 1, 4, 16, 64: about 0.28, 0.14, 0.070, 0.036 at the start). Early in training the true gradient is big, so even a single example has less noise than signal (ratio 0.82). Switch to “after 300 steps”: the model is close to the minimum and the true gradient has shrunk about 50×, but the per-example disagreement hasn't, so a single example is now 12× more noise than signal, and you need about B = 128 to get the ratio below 1. The noise scale Bnoise = (per-example variance) ÷ |true gradient|² climbs from under 1 to over 100. Batches much smaller than it are mostly noise; batches much larger waste compute.

✏️ Worked example: the gradient noise scale

McCandlish et al. (2018) measure Bnoise = tr(Σ) / |G|²: the total variance of one example's gradient divided by the squared length of the true gradient. At our “start”, tr(Σ) ≈ 0.078 and |G| ≈ 0.342, so Bnoise ≈ 0.078 / 0.117 ≈ 0.7: one example is already about as informative as the whole dataset. After 300 steps, tr(Σ) ≈ 0.0045 but |G| ≈ 0.0063, so Bnoise ≈ 0.0045 / 0.00004 ≈ 113.

Their finding across MNIST, ImageNet, language models and Dota 2 agents: this simple number roughly predicts the largest useful batch size, it differs by orders of magnitude between tasks (tens of thousands of images for ImageNet, millions for Dota), and it increases as the loss decreases. That's why Llama 3 started with 4M-token batches and doubled to 8M and then 16M.

Same number of examples, different batch sizes

What you should see (seeded): with a fixed lr, smaller is better: B = 2 ends at 0.008, B = 16 at 0.062, B = 256 at 0.628, simply because it took 128× fewer steps of the same size. With linear scaling, every batch from 2 to 128 ends between 0.060 and 0.070: B = 128 matches B = 16 with 8× fewer steps. That's Goyal et al.'s result in miniature. At B = 256 (lr 1.6) it breaks (0.416): the steps got too big for the loss surface, the same edge you found in Lesson 1.6. Past the critical batch size, more parallelism stops buying speed.

🤔 Why this? The linear scaling rule (+ warmup)
  1. The problem before To use more GPUs you need bigger batches, but keeping the old learning rate means fewer, equally sized steps per epoch: in our race, B = 256 at lr 0.1 ends at 0.628 vs 0.062 for B = 16. Large-batch training had a reputation for losing accuracy.
  2. What people tried Training longer at large batch (wastes the speed-up), square-root scaling of the lr, and early observations by Krizhevsky and others that Goyal et al. build on.
  3. Why this works Goyal et al.'s rule: “When the minibatch size is multiplied by k, multiply the learning rate by k.” Intuition: k small steps with nearly the same gradient ≈ one step k times as big with their average. So B × 8 with lr × 8 covers the same distance per epoch. With a 5-epoch gradual warmup, ResNet-50 at batch 8,192 (lr 0.1 × 32 = 3.2) reached 23.74% error vs 23.60% at batch 256, and trained in 1 hour on 256 GPUs with ~90% scaling efficiency.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The “nearly the same gradient” assumption fails early in training (hence warmup) and at very large batches: Goyal et al. saw accuracy degrade rapidly beyond about 8k on ImageNet; our toy broke at 256. Adam-style optimizers follow different rules (often closer to √k).
  5. What refined it The critical batch size / noise scale (McCandlish et al.), large-batch optimizers like LARS and LAMB, and batch-size warmup (Llama 3's 4M → 8M → 16M tokens).

Sharp vs flat minima (careful here)

Keskar et al. (2016) observed that large-batch training often generalized worse and presented evidence that large-batch methods tend to converge to sharp minima (the loss rises steeply around them) while small batches find flatter ones, which they linked to the noise in small-batch gradients. It's a popular intuition: noise shakes you out of narrow holes.

But be careful. Dinh et al. (2017) showed that for ReLU networks you can reparametrize a model to make its minimum arbitrarily sharp without changing what it computes, so “sharpness” alone can't explain generalization. Shallue et al. (2018), across 35 workloads, found no evidence that larger batches degrade out-of-sample performance once hyperparameters are re-tuned for each batch size; many earlier gaps came from tuning differences. The safe takeaway: large batches need their learning rate (and schedule) re-tuned; they aren't inherently worse.

When the batch doesn't fit: gradient accumulation

Your recipe says “batch of 512”, but only 64 examples fit in GPU memory. Run 8 micro-batches of 64, call backward() on each without calling zero_grad() in between (gradients add up, Lesson 1.2), divide each loss by 8, then take one optimizer step. The result is the same gradient as one batch of 512.

🤔 Why this? Gradient accumulation
  1. The problem before Activation memory grows linearly with batch size, so the batch a recipe needs (hundreds of thousands of tokens for an LLM) doesn't fit on one GPU, and changing the batch changes the learning rate you need.
  2. What people tried Smaller batches with re-tuned hyperparameters; more GPUs (expensive).
  3. Why this works PyTorch's .grad accumulates by design. k micro-batches of size b, each loss divided by k, sum to exactly the mean gradient over k·b examples (our check: identical to 2 × 10⁻⁸). Memory is set by b, the effective batch by k·b. nanoGPT's GPT-2 config does 40 micro-steps of 12 sequences × 1,024 tokens ≈ 0.5M tokens per optimizer step.
  4. What it costs, when it fails k times slower per optimizer step than a real k·b batch on k× the hardware (it's sequential). Forgetting to divide the loss by k multiplies the effective lr by k. BatchNorm still only sees micro-batches of b.
  5. What refined it Data parallelism across GPUs (each GPU a micro-batch, gradients averaged: DDP and FSDP, Lesson 10.3), and mixed precision plus activation checkpointing to fit bigger micro-batches.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Gradient accumulation, checked against a real big batch, and the 1/√B noise law measured on a small network:

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(20, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 3))
x, y = torch.randn(64, 20), torch.randint(0, 3, (64,))
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

# A: one batch of 64
model.zero_grad()
loss_fn(model(x), y).backward()
big = [p.grad.clone() for p in model.parameters()]

# B: 4 micro-batches of 16, gradients accumulated
model.zero_grad()
accum = 4
for xb, yb in zip(x.chunk(accum), y.chunk(accum)):
    (loss_fn(model(xb), yb) / accum).backward()     # divide, so the sum equals the mean over 64
small = [p.grad for p in model.parameters()]

diff = max((a - b).abs().max().item() for a, b in zip(big, small))
print(f"max difference between the two gradients: {diff:.1e}")

# Gradient noise: how far is a mini-batch gradient from the full-batch one?
X, Y = torch.randn(4096, 20), torch.randint(0, 3, (4096,))
def grad(idx):
    model.zero_grad(); loss_fn(model(X[idx]), Y[idx]).backward()
    return torch.cat([p.grad.flatten() for p in model.parameters()])
full = grad(torch.arange(4096))
for B in [8, 32, 128, 512]:
    devs = [(grad(torch.randint(0, 4096, (B,))) - full).norm().item() for _ in range(50)]
    print(f"batch {B:4d}: average distance from the full gradient {sum(devs) / 50:.3f}")

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# max difference between the two gradients: 2.2e-08
# batch    8: average distance from the full gradient 1.153
# batch   32: average distance from the full gradient 0.590
# batch  128: average distance from the full gradient 0.299
# batch  512: average distance from the full gradient 0.152

In a real loop: if (step + 1) % accum == 0: opt.step(); opt.zero_grad(). With DDP, wrap the non-final micro-steps in model.no_sync() so GPUs only average gradients once per optimizer step (nanoGPT sets require_backward_grad_sync for the same reason).

🎤 Interview angle

“You're moving training from 8 to 64 GPUs. What changes?” A strong answer:

  • Effective batch grows 8× (data parallelism), so steps per epoch drop 8×; apply the linear scaling rule (lr × 8 for SGD; re-tune for Adam) with a gradual warmup; keep everything else fixed and compare curves against examples seen.
  • Knows the limit: past the critical batch size (≈ the gradient noise scale), more batch buys nothing; it grows as loss falls.
  • Gives the memory answer: gradient accumulation for a large effective batch on few GPUs, dividing the loss by the number of micro-steps.
  • Is careful about generalization: sharp-vs-flat is a debated intuition; well-tuned large batches needn't generalize worse (Shallue et al.).
🏭 In the real world
ImageNet in one hour 🏢 Facebook AI Research · 2017 Goyal et al.: Accurate, Large Minibatch SGD

How: ResNet-50, batch 8,192 across 256 GPUs, lr = 0.1 × batch/256, 5-epoch gradual warmup, zero-γ init (Lesson 1.4).

Why this method: the rule kept the training curves matched to small-batch training, so a 29-hour job became a 1-hour job at the same accuracy.

The largest useful batch, predicted 🏢 OpenAI · 2018 McCandlish et al.: An Empirical Model of Large-Batch Training

How: measure the gradient noise scale during training.

Why this method: it roughly predicts the critical batch across supervised, RL and generative tasks, from tens of thousands (ImageNet) to millions (Dota 2), and shows it grows as the loss falls.

Growing the batch during pre-training 🏢 Meta · 2024 The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: 405B model starts at 4M tokens per batch (sequences of 4,096), doubles to 8M after 252M tokens, then to 16M after 2.87T tokens.

Why this method: the paper says the lower batch size early improves training stability and the increase later improves efficiency, which is the rising-noise-scale story.

The case for small batches 🧪 Research · 2018 Masters & Luschi (Graphcore): Revisiting Small Batch Training

How: compared batch sizes on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and ImageNet with learning rates scaled consistently.

Why this method: larger batches narrowed the range of stable learning rates; their best results came from batches of 2 to 32. Small batches are best per example; large batches are best per hour on lots of hardware.

🤯 Fun fact · 71 million loss measurements

To settle the batch-size debate, Shallue et al. (2018) trained 168,160 models across 35 workloads and published all 71,638,836 loss measurements as a public database. Their verdict: the steps-vs-batch-size curve has a perfect-scaling region, then diminishing returns, then a plateau, and where those bends happen varies enormously between workloads.

Check yourself

1. A recipe trains with batch 256 at lr 0.1 using SGD. You move to batch 2,048. By the linear scaling rule, what lr should you start from (after warmup)?
The batch grew by k = 2,048 / 256 = 8, so the lr grows by 8: 0.1 × 8 = 0.8, reached through a gradual warmup. (√8 scaling is sometimes used with Adam-type optimizers; the linear rule is Goyal et al.'s rule for SGD.)
2. A batch of 512 doesn't fit; 128 does. You accumulate 4 micro-batches but forget to divide each loss by 4. What happens?
Each backward adds its micro-batch's mean gradient, so four of them sum to 4× the mean over 512. With SGD that's a 4× larger step (possibly over the edge). Adam is mostly scale-invariant, so it hides the bug, until you switch optimizers. Divide the loss by the number of micro-steps.
3. You double the batch size four times (16× in total), scaling the lr each time. Steps-to-target fall 2×, 2×, 1.3×, then not at all. What's happening?
Bigger batches help only while noise dominates the gradient. Past the critical batch size (≈ the gradient noise scale), each step is already nearly the true gradient, and the lr can't grow further without hitting the stability edge. Shallue et al. found this perfect-scaling → diminishing-returns → plateau shape across workloads.
📝 TL;DR
  • Mini-batch gradient = truth + noise ∝ 1/√B. 16× the batch costs 16× the work for 4× less noise; GPUs make moderate batches nearly free.
  • Linear scaling rule (SGD): batch × k → lr × k, with a gradual warmup. ResNet-50 at batch 8,192: 1 hour on 256 GPUs, 23.74% vs 23.60% error.
  • The critical batch size ≈ the gradient noise scale tr(Σ)/|G|²: past it, more batch buys nothing. It grows as the loss falls, so big runs grow the batch (Llama 3: 4M → 8M → 16M tokens).
  • Sharp-vs-flat minima is an intuition, not a law; re-tuned large batches needn't generalize worse.
  • Gradient accumulation: k micro-batches, each loss ÷ k, one step = the same gradient as a batch k times bigger.
