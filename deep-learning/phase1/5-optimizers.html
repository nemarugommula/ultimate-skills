Phase 1 · Lesson 5

Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW

The optimizer is the rule that turns a gradient into a step. Plain gradient descent zig-zags down narrow valleys; add memory and it rolls; give every weight its own step size and it stops caring so much about your learning rate. Here's why the GPT-style models of 2026 almost all train with AdamW, and what's challenging it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − lr · g. Too big a learning rate overshoots; a valley with curvature c allows lr up to 2/c (math 3.4, 3.4b).
  • On mini-batches the gradient is a noisy estimate: that's SGD, stochastic gradient descent (math 7.2).
  • opt.step() applies the rule to every parameter (Lesson 1.3).
🎯 Hook

We trained the same small network on a spiral seven times, once per learning rate from 0.001 to 1. Plain SGD solved it at only 3 of the 7 learning rates. Adam solved it at 6 of 7, across a 300× range. Karpathy's training recipe puts it bluntly: “adam is safe”, and suggests starting with a learning rate of 3e-4.

Yet the large models don't use plain Adam. Llama 2 used AdamW with weight decay 0.1; Llama 3 405B used AdamW too. And in 2025 Moonshot AI trained Kimi K2, a 1-trillion-parameter model, on 15.5 trillion tokens with MuonClip, an optimizer that didn't exist in 2023. What do these optimizers know that plain gradient descent doesn't, and what does the “W” fix?

Numbers first: why plain gradient descent zig-zags

Picture a long, narrow valley: the loss is 0.05x² + 2y². Along x it's almost flat (curvature 0.1); across y it's steep (curvature 4). One learning rate has to serve both directions.

  • The steep direction caps the learning rate: above 2/4 = 0.5 the y-coordinate overshoots further each step and diverges.
  • At lr = 0.45 the flat direction only shrinks x by 0.45 × 0.1 = 4.5% per step. Starting at x = −5.5, reaching x ≈ 0.1 takes about 88 steps.
  • Meanwhile y bounces from wall to wall: each step flips its sign (1 − 0.45 × 4 = −0.8).

Most real loss surfaces are full of these ravines: some weights are steep, some flat. The steep ones set the speed limit and the flat ones crawl. Every optimizer in this lesson is a different answer to that one problem.

💡 Big idea Two ideas fix the ravine. Momentum: remember past gradients, so the consistent push along the valley floor builds up speed while the back-and-forth across the walls cancels out. Adaptive step sizes (RMSProp, Adam): divide each weight's step by the typical size of its own gradient, so steep and flat directions move at similar speed. Adam does both. AdamW then fixes how Adam handles weight decay.

Momentum: a heavy ball

Keep a running “velocity” m that adds up recent gradients, and step along it instead of along the raw gradient:

velocity = 0.9 × old velocity + new gradientm ← β·m + g   step along the velocityw ← w − lr·m

With β = 0.9, a gradient that points the same way every step builds up to 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10× its size. A gradient that flips sign every step mostly cancels. That's precisely the ravine: steady push along the floor, flip-flopping across the walls. Nesterov momentum is a small refinement: compute the gradient a little ahead, at where the velocity is about to take you, so it brakes earlier.

🤔 Why this? Momentum (Polyak's heavy ball, 1964)
  1. The problem before In a ravine SGD's lr is capped by the steepest direction (2/c), so flat directions crawl: 88 steps on our ravine even at the best lr, while bouncing between the walls.
  2. What people tried Smaller learning rates (slower), per-layer hand-tuned rates, second-order methods that need the curvature (far too expensive for millions of weights).
  3. Why this works Averaging gradients over time keeps what's consistent and cancels what oscillates. With β = 0.9 a steady direction gets up to 10× the speed. Tiny example: gradients +1, −1, +1, −1 give velocities 1, −0.1, 0.91, −0.18: the oscillation shrinks; gradients +1, +1, +1 give 1, 1.9, 2.71: it accelerates. Sutskever et al. (2013) showed that momentum with a good random init and a slowly increasing momentum schedule trains deep and recurrent networks to levels previously reached only with a much more expensive second-order method (Hessian-free).
  4. What it costs, when it fails One extra number per weight (the velocity). It can overshoot and orbit the minimum; the effective step is about lr/(1 − β), so changing β changes the right lr. Still one global learning rate for all weights.
  5. What refined it Nesterov momentum (look ahead); per-weight scaling (RMSProp, Adam, below); momentum is inside Adam, Lion and Muon too.

RMSProp and Adam: every weight gets its own step size

RMSProp (Hinton's Coursera lecture, 2012) keeps a running average of each weight's squared gradient, v, and divides by its square root. A weight whose gradients are typically 100 gets its steps divided by 100; one whose gradients are typically 0.01 gets them multiplied by 100. Every weight ends up moving at a similar rate. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) combines that with momentum:

momentum of gradientsm ← β₁m + (1 − β₁)g  typical squared sizev ← β₂v + (1 − β₂)g²  step: direction ÷ sizew ← w − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

The paper's defaults: lr 0.001, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸. Because m̂/√v̂ is roughly “±1 when the gradient is consistent”, the learning rate becomes roughly the step size in weight units, whatever the gradient's scale. The full derivation is math 7.3.

✏️ Worked example: why Adam needs bias correction

m and v start at 0. After the first gradient g: m₁ = 0.1·g and v₁ = 0.001·g². Divide them: 0.1g / √(0.001g²) = 0.1 / 0.0316 = 3.16. The very first step would be 3.16× bigger than intended, just because the averages started at zero.

Bias correction divides by (1 − βᵗ): m̂₁ = m₁ / (1 − 0.9) = g and v̂₁ = v₁ / (1 − 0.999) = g², so the first step is exactly lr × sign(g). By step 100, 1 − 0.9¹⁰⁰ ≈ 1 and the correction for m has faded; for v it takes longer (1 − 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.63 even at step 1,000). This slow start of v is one reason big models still use a learning-rate warmup (Lesson 1.6).

🤔 Why this? Adam (2014)
  1. The problem before Different weights have wildly different gradient scales (embeddings that are rarely used, biases, deep vs shallow layers). One global lr is too big for some and too small for others, so SGD needs careful tuning: in our grid it solved the spiral at only 3 of 7 learning rates.
  2. What people tried AdaGrad (divide by the sum of all past squared gradients: steps shrink to zero over time), RMSProp (a running average instead of a sum), momentum.
  3. Why this works Momentum for direction plus RMSProp's per-weight scaling, plus bias correction for the zero start. The update is scale-free: multiply the loss by 1,000 and Adam's steps barely change. Result: a wide range of learning rates work, and models with very uneven gradients (transformers, embeddings) train well.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Memory: two extra numbers per weight (m and v), so 3× the parameter memory before gradients. For ConvNets, Karpathy's recipe notes that a well-tuned SGD will almost always slightly outperform Adam, though in a much narrower lr range. And adding L2 weight decay to the loss interacts badly with the division by √v: the next box.
  5. What refined it AdamW (decoupled weight decay); β₂ = 0.95 for large-model stability (Llama 2); memory-light variants (Adafactor, 8-bit Adam); Lion and Muon (below).

AdamW: weight decay that actually decays

Weight decay shrinks every weight a little each step (w ← w − lr·λ·w), pulling unused weights toward 0; it's a regularizer (Lesson 2.4). For plain SGD it's identical to adding an L2 penalty ½λw² to the loss, so libraries implemented it that way: add λw to the gradient. In Adam, that added λw then gets divided by √v like every other gradient.

What you should see: at G = 1 the two agree (about 10⁻⁴ per step; the weight keeps about 37% after 10,000 steps). At G = 100, Adam + L2 shrinks it only 10⁻⁶ per step: the weight keeps 99%, so the regularization has silently vanished for exactly the weights with big gradients. At small G it does the opposite and decays far harder. AdamW gives every weight the same, predictable decay.

🤔 Why this? AdamW (decoupled weight decay, Loshchilov & Hutter 2017)
  1. The problem before Libraries implemented weight decay as an L2 term in the gradient. Loshchilov & Hutter point out that L2 and weight decay are equivalent for SGD but “not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam”: with L2, weights with large historic gradients are regularized less. They show fixing it lets Adam compete with SGD + momentum on image classification, where Adam was previously typically outperformed.
  2. What people tried Tuning λ harder; switching back to SGD for the final accuracy.
  3. Why this works Decouple: take the Adam step on the loss gradient alone, then shrink the weights separately, w ← w − lr·λ·w. Every weight decays at the same relative rate (10⁻⁴ per step in the playground, for any G), and the best λ becomes much less entangled with the learning rate.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Same memory as Adam. λ still needs tuning (0.01–0.1 is typical; Llama 2 used 0.1), and you usually exclude biases and normalization weights from decay. PyTorch's Adam(weight_decay=…) is still the L2 version: use AdamW.
  5. What refined it It's the default for transformers: BERT-style and GPT-style training, Llama 2 and 3 (AdamW with β₂ = 0.95). Lion and Muon (below) keep decoupled decay too.

What's newer (as of late 2026)

  • Lion (Chen et al., Google, 2023) was found by an automated program search. It keeps only momentum (half of Adam's extra memory) and steps by the sign of it, so every weight moves by exactly ±lr. The authors say it needs a smaller learning rate than AdamW and a larger weight decay, and that its gains shrink with small batches; on some large language and image-text datasets the difference wasn't statistically significant.
  • Muon (Keller Jordan, December 2024) is for the 2D weight matrices of hidden layers: take the momentum update and orthogonalize it (a few Newton–Schulz iterations) so the step changes every direction of the matrix by a similar amount. It set training-speed records on the NanoGPT and CIFAR-10 speedruns. Moonshot AI's “Muon is Scalable for LLM Training” (2025) reports about 2× the compute efficiency of AdamW, and their Kimi K2 (1 trillion parameters, 32 billion active) was pre-trained with MuonClip with, they report, zero loss spikes. PyTorch ships torch.optim.Muon since version 2.9; its docs say embeddings, biases and other non-matrix parameters should still use AdamW. Karpathy's nanochat trains its matrices with Muon and everything else with AdamW.

The honest summary for 2026: AdamW is the safe, universal default; Muon (with AdamW for the non-matrix parameters) is the most credible challenger for LLM pre-training; claims of new optimizers beating well-tuned AdamW deserve careful scrutiny.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

All live in torch.optim and share the same two calls, zero_grad() and step(). This compares four of them on the same small regression problem (300 mini-batch steps):

import torch, torch.nn as nn

def make_data(n=2000, seed=0):
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    x = torch.randn(n, 10, generator=g)
    y = torch.sin(x[:, :3].sum(1, keepdim=True)) + 0.1 * torch.randn(n, 1, generator=g)
    return x, y

x, y = make_data()
optims = {
    "SGD":          lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.05),
    "SGD+momentum": lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.05, momentum=0.9),
    "Adam":         lambda p: torch.optim.Adam(p, lr=1e-3),
    "AdamW":        lambda p: torch.optim.AdamW(p, lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.01),
}
for name, make in optims.items():
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(10, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 1))
    opt = make(model.parameters())
    for step in range(300):
        idx = torch.randint(0, 2000, (64,))
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x[idx]), y[idx])
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        full = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x), y).item()
    state = opt.state[model[0].weight]
    print(f"{name:13s} loss after 300 steps {full:.3f}   extra state per weight: {sorted(state.keys())}")

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# SGD           loss after 300 steps 0.181   extra state per weight: []

# Adam          loss after 300 steps 0.044   extra state per weight: ['exp_avg', 'exp_avg_sq', 'step']
# AdamW         loss after 300 steps 0.044   extra state per weight: ['exp_avg', 'exp_avg_sq', 'step']

Here momentum wins: on a short, well-scaled problem a tuned SGD+momentum is hard to beat; Adam's gift is robustness, not guaranteed speed. The state keys show the memory cost: exp_avg is m, exp_avg_sq is v. To skip decay on biases and norms, pass parameter groups: AdamW([{"params": matrices, "weight_decay": 0.1}, {"params": others, "weight_decay": 0.0}], lr=3e-4).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain Adam. Why AdamW for transformers?” A strong answer:

  • Momentum (first moment) smooths the direction; the second moment v gives each weight a step ÷ √v, so steep and flat directions move at similar speeds; bias correction fixes the zero start.
  • AdamW: L2 added to the gradient gets divided by √v, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized; decoupled decay w ← w − lr·λ·w treats every weight the same.
  • Knows the costs: 2 extra floats per parameter (16 bytes per parameter with fp32 weights + grads + m + v), β₂ = 0.95 for stability at scale, no decay on biases/norms.
  • Can mention what's new without overclaiming: Muon for matrix parameters (Kimi K2, PyTorch 2.9+), Lion.
🏭 In the real world
AdamW for an LLM family 🏢 Meta · 2023 Touvron et al.: Llama 2

How: AdamW with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, ε = 10⁻⁵, weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping 1.0, warmup then cosine (Lesson 1.6).

Why this method: the standard robust recipe for transformers; β₂ = 0.95 (instead of 0.999) lets v react faster to sudden gradient changes, which helps stability.

The fix for weight decay 🧪 Research · 2017 Loshchilov & Hutter: Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization

How: apply the weight decay directly to the weights, outside Adam's adaptive scaling.

Why this method: they show it makes the best weight decay largely independent of the learning rate and closes much of Adam's generalization gap with SGD on image classification.

A trillion-parameter model on a new optimizer 🏢 Moonshot AI · 2025 Kimi K2: Open Agentic Intelligence

How: MuonClip = Muon + weight decay + RMS matching + QK-Clip (a fix that caps exploding attention logits).

Why this method: Muon's token efficiency, with QK-Clip for stability; they report pre-training on 15.5 trillion tokens without a single loss spike.

Muon for the matrices, AdamW for the rest 🧰 Open source Karpathy: nanochat

How: a combined MuonAdamW optimizer: Muon for 2D matrix parameters, AdamW for embeddings and scalars (checked November 2026).

Why this method: Muon only makes sense for matrices (it orthogonalizes them); everything else keeps the proven AdamW.

🤯 Fun fact · RMSProp was never published

RMSProp, one of the most used optimizers of the 2010s and the “second half” of Adam, comes from a slide in Geoffrey Hinton's 2012 Coursera course (Lecture 6e). Papers, including Adam's, cite it as “Tieleman & Hinton, 2012”: a lecture. Adam's paper presents itself as combining the advantages of two methods, AdaGrad and that lecture slide.

Check yourself

1. SGD with momentum β = 0.9 receives the same gradient g every step for a long time. About how large does each step become?
The velocity converges to g + 0.9g + 0.81g + … = g / (1 − 0.9) = 10g, so each step is about 10 × lr × g. That's why momentum accelerates along consistent directions, and why turning momentum on usually means lowering the lr.
2. A transformer trained with torch.optim.Adam(params, lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1) overfits; a colleague suggests switching to AdamW with the same numbers. Why might that help?
In PyTorch, Adam(weight_decay=…) adds λw to the gradient, which Adam then rescales per weight. Loshchilov & Hutter showed this regularizes weights with large historic gradients less. AdamW applies the decay separately, so λ = 0.1 means what it says. Memory and default lr are the same.
3. A 1-billion-parameter model is trained with AdamW, all in float32. How much memory do the weights, gradients and optimizer state take (before activations)?
Per parameter: weight (4 bytes) + gradient (4) + m (4) + v (4) = 16 bytes. 10⁹ × 16 bytes = 16 GB. SGD without momentum would need 8 GB; Lion or Muon about 12 GB. Mixed precision and sharding (Phase 10) shrink this.
📝 TL;DR
  • Ravines (steep and flat directions together) make SGD zig-zag: the steep direction caps lr at 2/curvature, so flat ones crawl.
  • Momentum averages gradients: consistent directions speed up ~1/(1 − β) = 10×, oscillations cancel.
  • Adam = momentum + per-weight ÷ √(avg g²) + bias correction; works over a wide lr range (start at 3e-4 to 1e-3); costs 2 extra numbers per weight.
  • AdamW decouples weight decay (w ← w − lr·λ·w); Adam + L2 barely regularizes weights with large gradients. AdamW is the transformer default (β₂ = 0.95, λ ≈ 0.1, no decay on biases/norms).
  • Newer (2026): Muon for matrix weights (+ AdamW for the rest), in PyTorch since 2.9 and behind Kimi K2; Lion with half the memory.
