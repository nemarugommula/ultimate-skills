Phase 4 · Lesson 3

Segmentation: FCN, U-Net, Mask R-CNN, panoptic

Boxes say roughly where. Segmentation says exactly which pixels. The trouble is that CNNs throw away resolution to understand an image; this lesson is about how to get it back, how to score it, and how to tell two touching objects apart.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Convolution, stride and pooling shrink a feature map (Lessons 3.2 and 3.3): a 224 × 224 image becomes a 7 × 7 grid of rich features in a classifier.
  • IoU, masks vs boxes and mAP (Lesson 4.1); Faster R-CNN's region proposals (Lesson 4.2).
  • Cross-entropy is a per-example loss (Lesson 1.1); here every pixel is an example.
🎯 Hook: 30 training images

In 2015, Olaf Ronneberger and colleagues trained a network on the ISBI challenge for segmenting neuron membranes in electron-microscope images. The training set: 30 images of 512 × 512 pixels. ImageNet classifiers had trained on 1.28 million.

Their network beat the previous best method, then, applied to light-microscopy cell videos, won the ISBI cell tracking challenge 2015 “by a large margin”. It segments a 512 × 512 image in under a second. They called it U-Net, and a decade later it's still the backbone of medical image segmentation (the self-configuring nnU-Net) and the denoiser inside many diffusion image generators (Lesson 7.4).

How can 30 images be enough, and what does the “U” have to do with it?

Numbers first: segmentation is classification, 262,144 times

Semantic segmentation gives every pixel a class. A 512 × 512 image with 21 classes needs 512 × 512 × 21 = 5.5 million scores, one class distribution per pixel. The loss is just cross-entropy, averaged over all 262,144 pixels.

The catch: a classification CNN deliberately throws resolution away. Five stride-2 steps take 224 × 224 down to 7 × 7: each output cell summarizes a 32 × 32 patch. Great for “is there a cat?”, useless for “which exact pixels are cat?”. Every segmentation architecture is an answer to one question: how do you get the resolution back without losing the understanding?

🤔 Why this? Fully convolutional networks (FCN, 2014)
  1. The problem before Classifiers end in fully connected layers that need a fixed input size and output one vector. Early neural segmentation classified each pixel from a patch around it: one network run per pixel, very slow, with little context.
  2. What people tried Patch-wise classifiers, superpixels plus hand-made features, CRF post-processing.
  3. Why this works Long, Shelhamer and Darrell replaced the fully connected layers with convolutions, so the network takes any image size and outputs a coarse map of class scores in one pass; learned upsampling (transposed convolution) brings it back to full size. They also fused the coarse deep map with finer maps from earlier layers. Result: 62.2% mean IU on PASCAL VOC 2012 (20% relative better) at one third of a second per image, while reusing ImageNet classifiers.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Upsampling a 32×-downsampled map gives blobby edges; thin parts (legs, poles) vanish. The fusion with fine layers was shallow (just adding maps at 3 scales).
  5. What refined it Full encoder–decoders with skips at every scale (U-Net, next), and dilated convolution that avoids downsampling in the first place (DeepLab, below).

U-Net: a deep “what” plus a shallow “where”

U-Net has a contracting path (the encoder: convolutions and pooling, getting smaller and smarter) and a symmetric expanding path (the decoder: upsampling back to full size). The key move: at every scale, the decoder concatenates the encoder's feature map of the same size, a “skip connection” (the paper's diagram labels them “copy and crop”).

🧠 Analogy: the general and the scout

The deep layers are a general looking at a blurry satellite map: they know what is going on (“the cell is roughly here, that blob is debris”) but not the exact edges. The shallow layers are a scout on the ground: they see every edge sharply, but can't tell a cell from debris. The decoder is the meeting where the general points (“trace this one”) and the scout draws the exact border. Without the scout, the border is blurry. Without the general, you trace everything bright.

🤔 Why this? U-Net's skip connections (Ronneberger et al., 2015)
  1. The problem before FCN's decoder upsampled a map 32× smaller than the image: the 1/16 case in your playground, where a 2-pixel arm can't survive. Biomedical images need exact borders (touching cells, thin membranes), and there were only dozens of labelled images.
  2. What people tried Sliding-window patch classifiers (the previous ISBI winner), FCN's 3-scale fusion, CRF post-processing to sharpen edges.
  3. Why this works The decoder gets two inputs at every scale: upsampled deep features (what) and the encoder's same-size features (where). Concatenation keeps both and lets convolutions learn how to combine them, so borders come from the high-resolution path and decisions from the deep path. It's fully convolutional, so every pixel of every image is a training example, and heavy augmentation (the paper stresses elastic deformations) multiplies 30 images into many. Skips also give gradients a short path to the early layers.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Memory: you keep every encoder feature map until the decoder uses it, at full resolution. Its context is limited to its receptive field, so it can confuse look-alike structures far apart. Plain U-Net is semantic only: two touching cells of the same class merge unless you add tricks (the paper weighted the border pixels between cells).
  5. What refined it 3D U-Nets for CT and MRI volumes, nnU-Net (2018) that configures itself to each dataset, attention and transformer encoders; U-Nets also became the denoiser in diffusion models (Lesson 7.4). Residual connections (Lesson 2.2) are the same idea inside a block.

Dilated (atrous) convolution: see wider without shrinking

DeepLab took the opposite route: instead of shrinking and recovering, shrink less. A dilated (French: à trous, “with holes”) 3 × 3 convolution with rate 2 places its 9 weights 2 pixels apart, so it covers a 5 × 5 area; rate 4 covers 9 × 9; still 9 weights each.

🤔 Why this? Atrous convolution and ASPP (DeepLab, 2016)
  1. The problem before Pooling and striding give large receptive fields but cost resolution; the decoder then has to guess the details back.
  2. What people tried Bigger kernels (81 weights for 9 × 9 instead of 9), upsampling decoders (FCN), CRF post-processing.
  3. Why this works Spacing the kernel out enlarges the field of view “without increasing the number of parameters or the amount of computation” (DeepLab's words). Keep the last stages at 1/8 resolution with dilation and the network sees whole objects while keeping 4× more detail than a 1/32 map. ASPP (atrous spatial pyramid pooling) runs several rates in parallel to catch small and large objects at once. DeepLab reached 79.7% mIoU on PASCAL VOC 2012 test.
  4. What it costs, when it fails High-resolution feature maps in deep layers are expensive in memory and compute. Large rates on small maps sample mostly padding; repeated equal rates create a “gridding” pattern where some pixels are never looked at.
  5. What refined it DeepLabv3+ added a light decoder (dilation plus skips); transformer segmenters get global context from attention instead (Mask2Former, SAM in Lesson 4.4).

Scoring a mask: accuracy lies, IoU and Dice don't

Pixel accuracy = correct pixels ÷ all pixels. mIoU (mean IoU) computes IoU per class over the whole image (pixels both predicted and true ÷ pixels either predicted or true), then averages over classes. Dice = 2 × overlap ÷ (predicted pixels + true pixels), a close cousin: Dice = 2·IoU ÷ (1 + IoU).

Dice loss drops from 1 to 0: it only cares about the object.

🤔 Why this? Dice loss for small objects
  1. The problem before Per-pixel cross-entropy is an average over all pixels. When the object is 0.9% of the image, ignoring it entirely costs little (0.077 vs 0.051 in the playground), so networks learn to predict background, and pixel accuracy happily reports 99%.
  2. What people tried Class-weighted cross-entropy (multiply tumor pixels' loss by ~100), cropping around objects, oversampling images with objects.
  3. Why this works Dice loss = 1 − 2·overlap ÷ (predicted + true), computed with probabilities so it's differentiable (the “soft” Dice of V-Net, 2016). It's a ratio about the object only: background pixels that are correctly empty never enter it, so the object's size doesn't matter. Missing a 9-pixel tumor costs the full loss of 1.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Noisy gradients when the object is tiny or absent (an empty image makes Dice 0/0: add a small ε); it doesn't care about calibrated probabilities. Most systems therefore combine it with cross-entropy or focal loss: nnU-Net uses Dice + cross-entropy, SAM trains masks with focal + Dice.
  5. What refined it Tversky and focal-Tversky losses (weight false negatives more), boundary losses, and the Lovász loss that optimizes IoU directly.

Instance segmentation: Mask R-CNN

Semantic segmentation paints two touching people the same color. Instance segmentation separates them. Mask R-CNN (2017) does it with a small addition to Faster R-CNN (Lesson 4.2): for each detected box, a tiny FCN predicts a 28 × 28 mask of the object inside that box, then the mask is resized to the box. Detection decides which objects; the mask head only decides which pixels in this box.

✏️ Worked example: why rounding hurts masks (RoIAlign)

A box starts at x = 13.7 pixels. On a stride-16 feature map that's cell 13.7 ÷ 16 = 0.856. Fast R-CNN's RoI pooling rounds it to cell 1, which is pixel 16: a shift of 2.3 pixels. For a 40-pixel-wide object that's 6% of its width, and the mask is now drawn 2.3 pixels off. For classification it doesn't matter; for masks it does.

Mask R-CNN's RoIAlign keeps 0.856 and samples the feature map there with bilinear interpolation: “no quantization is performed on any coordinates” (the paper's words). It's in torchvision as torchvision.ops.roi_align.

🤔 Why this? Mask R-CNN's mask head
  1. The problem before Semantic segmentation can't count or separate touching objects; detection gives boxes with background inside. Earlier instance methods segmented first and then classified, or chained many stages, and were slow and complex.
  2. What people tried Proposing segments then classifying them (DeepMask-style), per-pixel softmax over “instance channels”.
  3. Why this works Reuse a strong detector and add a parallel branch: per region, a small FCN outputs one 28 × 28 mask per class with a per-pixel sigmoid, and the classification branch picks which mask to use. The paper calls this decoupling mask and class prediction: masks don't compete across classes, so the mask head just answers “object or not, pixel by pixel”. Together with RoIAlign's exact sampling, it showed top results in all three COCO tracks (masks, boxes, keypoints), beating every single-model entry including the COCO 2016 winners, at 5 fps and only a small overhead over Faster R-CNN.
  4. What it costs, when it fails 28 × 28 masks are coarse for big objects with fine detail (hair, fences); masks are limited to inside the predicted box, so a bad box means a bad mask; two-stage speed.
  5. What refined it Real-time instance segmentation in YOLO models (YOLOv8 to YOLO26 “-seg”), query-based Mask2Former (2021) that handles semantic, instance and panoptic with one architecture, and promptable SAM (Lesson 4.4).

Panoptic segmentation: everything, once

Kirillov et al. (2018) proposed panoptic segmentation: every pixel gets a class, and pixels of countable things (people, cars) also get an instance ID, while amorphous stuff (sky, road, grass) gets only a class. No overlaps allowed: one answer per pixel. They also proposed one metric for it, panoptic quality (PQ): match predicted and true segments (IoU > 0.5 makes the match unique), then

PQ = Σ IoU of matched pairs ÷ (TP + ½ FP + ½ FN) = (average IoU of matches, “SQ”) × (TP ÷ (TP + ½ FP + ½ FN), “RQ”)
✏️ Worked example: PQ by hand

A street photo has 5 cars. Your model matches 3 of them with IoUs 0.9, 0.8 and 0.7, predicts 1 car that isn't there (FP) and misses 2 (FN).

PQ = (0.9 + 0.8 + 0.7) ÷ (3 + 0.5 + 1) = 2.4 ÷ 4.5 = 0.533 · SQ = 2.4 ÷ 3 = 0.80, RQ = 3 ÷ 4.5 = 0.667, and 0.80 × 0.667 = 0.533 ✓

SQ says your masks are good when they exist; RQ says you're finding and not hallucinating enough of them. Like COCO's mAP, one number, two diagnoses.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

torchvision has DeepLabv3, FCN and Mask R-CNN; Hugging Face has Mask2Former and SegFormer; Ultralytics has YOLO segmentation models; MONAI and nnU-Net are the medical standards. A tiny U-Net with two skip connections and a Dice loss, run on CPU:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from torchvision.models.segmentation import deeplabv3_resnet50

torch.manual_seed(0)

class TinyUNet(nn.Module):
    """Encoder halves the size twice; decoder doubles it back and concatenates the skip features."""
    def __init__(self, c=16, n_classes=2):
        super().__init__()
        block = lambda i, o: nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(i, o, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(o, o, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU())
        self.enc1, self.enc2, self.mid = block(3, c), block(c, 2 * c), block(2 * c, 4 * c)
        self.up2, self.dec2 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(4 * c, 2 * c, 2, stride=2), block(4 * c, 2 * c)
        self.up1, self.dec1 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(2 * c, c, 2, stride=2), block(2 * c, c)
        self.head = nn.Conv2d(c, n_classes, 1)                  # one score per class per pixel
    def forward(self, x):
        e1 = self.enc1(x)                                       # full resolution: edges, fine detail
        e2 = self.enc2(F.max_pool2d(e1, 2))                     # 1/2
        m = self.mid(F.max_pool2d(e2, 2))                       # 1/4: context, "what"
        d2 = self.dec2(torch.cat([self.up2(m), e2], dim=1))     # skip connection 1
        d1 = self.dec1(torch.cat([self.up1(d2), e1], dim=1))    # skip connection 2
        return self.head(d1)

def dice_loss(logits, target, eps=1.0):
    p = logits.softmax(1)[:, 1]                                 # probability of "object" per pixel
    inter = (p * target).sum()
    return 1 - (2 * inter + eps) / (p.sum() + target.sum() + eps)

x = torch.rand(2, 3, 64, 64)
net = TinyUNet()
out = net(x)
print(out.shape, f"{sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters()):,} parameters")
target = torch.zeros(2, 64, 64); target[:, 30:33, 30:33] = 1   # a tiny 3x3 object
print("CE", F.cross_entropy(out, target.long()).item(), "Dice loss", dice_loss(out, target).item())

seg = deeplabv3_resnet50(weights=None, weights_backbone=None, num_classes=3).eval()   # no download
with torch.no_grad():
    print(seg(torch.rand(1, 3, 256, 256))["out"].shape)

The untrained net's cross-entropy (0.80) looks like an ordinary start; its Dice loss (0.995) says plainly that it has found none of the tiny object. Lab 4 trains a version of this net. Gotcha: F.cross_entropy wants logits of shape (N, C, H, W) and integer targets (N, H, W), not one-hot.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design a model to segment tumors in CT scans. Labels are scarce.” A strong answer:

  • U-Net (2D slices or 3D volumes), ideally via nnU-Net; skips for exact borders; strong augmentation (flips, elastic deformation, intensity changes); pretrained encoders where possible.
  • Loss: Dice + cross-entropy (or focal), because tumors are tiny; metric: Dice per case and per tumor, not pixel accuracy.
  • Knows semantic vs instance vs panoptic, and when to use Mask R-CNN or a promptable model like SAM to speed up labelling (Lesson 4.4).
  • Mentions validation by patient (not by slice) to avoid leakage, and checking errors with clinicians.
🏭 In the real world
Medical segmentation that configures itself 🧰 Open source · 2018 nnU-Net (Isensee et al.)

How: plain 2D and 3D U-Nets whose preprocessing, architecture and training are set automatically from the dataset's properties.

Why this method: the authors argue the U-Net already works and the details matter more than new blocks; at submission it had the highest mean Dice in phase 1 of the Medical Segmentation Decathlon, with no manual tuning between its ten tasks.

The original U-Net 📜 History · 2015 Ronneberger, Fischer & Brox: U-Net

How: contracting and expanding paths with copy-and-crop skips; elastic-deformation augmentation; trained end to end from very few images.

Why this method: biomedical labels are expensive, so the network had to use every pixel and every augmentation; it won the ISBI 2015 cell tracking challenge in its categories.

Street scenes for self-driving research 🧪 Research · 2016 Cordts et al.: The Cityscapes Dataset

How: stereo video from 50 cities; 5,000 images with fine pixel-level labels and 20,000 with coarse ones, for semantic and instance segmentation.

Why this method: driving needs every pixel of road, sidewalk and person; Cityscapes became a standard mIoU and PQ benchmark (more in the Autonomous Systems course).

Instance segmentation in real time 🧰 Open source Ultralytics: instance segmentation task

How: YOLO models with a mask branch output a box, class and mask per object in one pass (YOLO26 also adds semantic-segmentation models).

Why this method: masks at detector speed for robotics, measuring areas, and cutting out objects in video.

🤯 Fun fact · the output is smaller than the input

In the original U-Net diagram, a 572 × 572 input tile produces a 388 × 388 segmentation map: the paper used unpadded convolutions, so every layer nibbles a pixel off each border, and the skips have to “copy and crop”. To segment a big image they tiled it with overlapping tiles and mirrored the borders. Today almost everyone uses padding and gets the same size out as in, but the “U” in the diagram is still literally the shape of the network.

Check yourself

1. A segmentation model gets IoU 0.90 on “road”, 0.70 on “car” and 0.20 on “bicycle”. What's its mIoU over these three classes?
mIoU is the plain average of per-class IoUs: (0.90 + 0.70 + 0.20) ÷ 3 = 0.60. That's deliberate: every class counts equally however few pixels it has, so a model can't hide a terrible bicycle class behind a huge road class (which pixel accuracy would do).
2. Your lane-marking segmenter outputs smooth blobs: thin lane lines are missing or smeared, though it clearly knows where the road is. The model is an encoder that downsamples 32× followed by a single upsampling layer. What's the best fix?
Thin structures can't survive a 32× bottleneck and a single upsampling step: exactly the 1/16 case in the playground. The deep layers know “road”, the shallow ones know the exact edges; skips (or dilation, which avoids shrinking) give the decoder both.
3. A polyp segmentation model trained with plain cross-entropy reports 98.5% pixel accuracy, but doctors say it misses most small polyps. What should you change first?
Polyps are a small share of each image, so “background everywhere” already scores high accuracy and low cross-entropy. Dice ignores how much background exists and charges the full loss for a missed object; combining it with CE keeps training stable.
📝 TL;DR
  • Semantic segmentation = cross-entropy at every pixel. The hard part is recovering the resolution a CNN throws away.
  • FCN: convolutions only, coarse map, learned upsampling. U-Net: decoder + skip connections at every scale (deep “what” + shallow “where”); trains from very few images.
  • Dilated (atrous) convolution widens the field of view with the same 9 weights and no downsampling (DeepLab, ASPP).
  • Pixel accuracy lies on small objects (99.1% while finding nothing). Use mIoU, Dice; train with Dice + CE or focal.
  • Mask R-CNN = Faster R-CNN + RoIAlign + a 28 × 28 per-class mask head. Panoptic = class per pixel + ID per thing, scored by PQ = SQ × RQ.
