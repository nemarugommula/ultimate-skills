Phase 4 · Lesson 2

Object detectors: R-CNN → YOLO → DETR

Twelve years of detectors in one story: each generation found one expensive thing the last one did and stopped doing it. Running a CNN 2,000 times per image, then outside proposal tools, then the second stage, then anchors, then NMS.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Boxes, IoU, anchors, NMS and mAP (Lesson 4.1). A detector over-predicts candidate boxes; NMS removes the duplicates.
  • A CNN backbone turns an image into feature maps at strides 8, 16, 32 (Lessons 3.3 and 3.4); pretrained backbones transfer (Lesson 3.5).
  • Cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes (Lesson 1.1). Attention and transformers get their own phase (6.1); here you only need “every token can look at every other token”.
🎯 Hook: from 13 seconds to under 2 milliseconds

In 2014, R-CNN computed its CNN features in about 13 seconds per image on a GPU (53 on a CPU), because it ran the network separately on about 2,000 cropped regions. In 2015, YOLO processed 45 images per second with one network pass. In January 2026, Ultralytics' smallest YOLO26 runs in 1.7 ms on an NVIDIA T4 with TensorRT (different hardware, so this is a story, not a benchmark).

Today detectors count cars at intersections, find defects on production lines, spot weeds for farm robots, and blur faces in street imagery. Most of them are descendants of three ideas you'll build here.

What did each generation stop doing to get thousands of times faster, and why did accuracy go up instead of down?

Two-stage: first guess where, then ask what

R-CNN (2014) borrowed a classic trick: a fast, hand-made algorithm (selective search) proposes about 2,000 regions that “look like objects”. Each region is cropped, warped to a fixed size, pushed through a CNN pretrained on ImageNet, and classified. It was slow, but it beat the previous best on PASCAL VOC 2012 by more than 30% relative (53.3% mAP), the moment CNNs took over detection.

Fast R-CNN (2015) ran the CNN once on the whole image and cut each region out of the feature map instead of the pixels (“RoI pooling”, region-of-interest pooling, turns any region into a fixed 7 × 7 grid of features). 213× faster at test time. Now the slowest part was the proposal algorithm itself.

Faster R-CNN (2015) made proposals a learned layer: the region proposal network (RPN) slides over the shared feature map and, at each position, scores k = 9 anchors (3 sizes × 3 aspect ratios) for “object or not” and nudges their coordinates. The best 300 go to the second stage for classification and refinement.

✏️ Worked example: how many anchors?

The Faster R-CNN paper says a typical feature map has about W × H ≈ 2,400 positions (for example 60 × 40, stride 16 on a ~1000 × 600 image).

2,400 positions × 9 anchors = 21,600 anchors scored per image → keep ~300 proposals → classify each one

Of those 21,600, an image with 3 objects has maybe a few dozen anchors with IoU ≥ 0.7 to some object (the RPN's “positive” rule). Everything else is background. Hold that ratio in mind: it becomes the central problem of one-stage detectors.

🤔 Why this? Region proposals (R-CNN → Faster R-CNN's RPN)
  1. The problem before Sliding a classifier over every position, scale and aspect ratio means hundreds of thousands of windows per image. With a CNN as the classifier, that's far too slow; with cheap hand-made features (pre-2012), accuracy had plateaued.
  2. What people tried Sliding windows with a CNN (OverFeat, 2013), and generic “objectness” algorithms that propose likely regions without knowing the class (selective search, used by R-CNN).
  3. Why this works Split the job: a cheap step narrows 100,000+ possible windows to ~2,000 (later 300) likely ones, and the expensive classifier only looks at those. Faster R-CNN's RPN learns the narrowing step on the same features, so proposals cost almost nothing and get better with training. Two stages also mean the second stage sees a balanced, cleaned-up set of candidates.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two networks in sequence: more latency (5 fps for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16), more hyperparameters (anchor sizes, proposal counts, two sets of IoU rules), and anchors that must be hand-tuned to the dataset's object shapes. Objects the RPN misses can never be recovered.
  5. What refined it Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN, 2017) for objects at many scales; Mask R-CNN (2017) added a mask head (Lesson 4.3). One-stage detectors (next) dropped the second stage entirely; two-stage designs live on where accuracy matters more than latency.

One-stage: YOLO's grid

YOLO (“You Only Look Once”, 2015) asked: why not predict boxes directly? Resize the image to 448 × 448, run one CNN, and read the answer off an S × S grid (S = 7). The rule that makes it trainable: the grid cell that contains an object's center is responsible for that object. Each cell predicts B = 2 boxes (x, y, w, h, confidence) and one set of C class probabilities.

output = S × S × (B · 5 + C) = 7 × 7 × (2 · 5 + 20) = 7 × 7 × 30 = 1,470 numbers for PASCAL VOC's 20 classes

🤔 Why this? One-stage detection (YOLO, SSD)
  1. The problem before Two-stage detectors ran a proposal network and then a per-region classifier: 5 fps for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16. Too slow for video, robots or phones.
  2. What people tried Faster backbones and fewer proposals in two-stage designs, and dense sliding-window CNNs (OverFeat).
  3. Why this works Detection becomes plain regression: one forward pass outputs a fixed-size tensor, every cell in parallel, so speed is just the backbone's speed (YOLO v1: 45 fps). The network sees the whole image when it decides, so its authors found YOLO v1 “far less likely to predict false detections where nothing exists” than the best detectors of the day. SSD (2016) added predictions from several feature maps, so small objects use the fine maps and large ones the coarse maps.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Early one-stage detectors were less accurate, especially on small, crowded objects (one class per cell, coarse grid) and on precise localization. Worse, they score every one of their thousands of cells, almost all background, which swamps the training signal (next section).
  5. What refined it Anchors and multi-scale grids (YOLO v2/v3, SSD), focal loss (RetinaNet, next), anchor-free heads (YOLOv8), and NMS-free training (YOLOv10, YOLO26).

The background problem and focal loss

Numbers first. RetinaNet's authors note a one-stage detector evaluates about 100,000 candidate locations per image. Suppose 100,000 are easy background the model already gets right with probability 0.99, and 100 are hard (real objects or tricky background) it gets right with only 0.3. Cross-entropy per example: −ln 0.99 = 0.010, −ln 0.3 = 1.20. Totals: 100,000 × 0.010 = 1,005 from easy background vs 100 × 1.20 = 120 from the hard ones.

So ~80% of the training signal says “keep calling background background”, which the model already does. Focal loss multiplies each example's cross-entropy by (1 − pt)γ, where pt is the probability the model gives the correct answer. Confident, correct examples get a factor near 0; mistakes keep almost their full loss.

FL(pt) = −(1 − pt)γ · ln(pt) · γ = 0 is plain cross-entropy · with γ = 2: pt = 0.99 → × 0.0001, pt = 0.3 → × 0.49

What you should see: at γ = 0 (cross-entropy) easy background takes 82% of the loss. At γ = 0.5 the hard examples reach 43%; at γ = 1 they take 80%; at γ = 2, the value the RetinaNet paper found best, they take 98%, an easy example's loss is 10,000× smaller, and a hard example keeps 49% of its loss. Push γ to 5 and even medium examples vanish; the paper reports RetinaNet is fairly robust for γ from 0.5 to 5.

🤔 Why this? Focal loss (RetinaNet, 2017)
  1. The problem before One-stage detectors trailed two-stage ones in accuracy. The RetinaNet paper found the “central cause”: extreme foreground–background imbalance. With ~100,000 candidates and a handful of objects, easy negatives dominate the gradient (82% of the loss in your playground at γ = 0).
  2. What people tried Two-stage designs (proposals remove most background before classification), and in one-stage ones, sampling a fixed mini-batch of anchors (e.g., 256) or hard-example mining (keep only the worst-scored negatives).
  3. Why this works Instead of throwing examples away, re-weight all of them smoothly: (1 − pt)γ is near 0 for well-classified examples and near 1 for mistakes. With γ = 2, pt = 0.9 has 100× less loss and pt ≈ 0.968 has 1,000× less (the paper's own examples), while a misclassified example's loss shrinks by at most 4×. RetinaNet applies it to all ~100k anchors and matched the speed of one-stage detectors while beating the accuracy of all two-stage detectors of its time.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two more knobs (γ, plus a class weight α; the paper uses α = 0.25 with γ = 2). It focuses on hard examples, and label noise looks hard, so wrong labels get more attention. It's a training fix only: it does nothing for latency or NMS.
  5. What refined it Focal loss spread far beyond detection: SAM trains its masks with a focal + Dice combination (Lesson 4.4). Generalized Focal Loss (2020) merged box quality into the score and introduced Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) for boxes, used by YOLOv8 and YOLO11; YOLO26 removes DFL to simplify the head.

🤔 Why this? Anchor-free heads (YOLOv8 and most modern detectors)
  1. The problem before Anchors are hand-chosen template shapes (9 per position in Faster R-CNN). They must match the dataset (tall pedestrians vs wide cars), multiply the number of predictions, and add matching rules and hyperparameters.
  2. What people tried Clustering the training boxes to pick anchor shapes automatically (YOLO v2 used k-means on box sizes).
  3. Why this works Predict the box directly from each feature-map cell: “the object's edges are this far left, right, up and down from me”. Strong backbones and good label-assignment rules made templates unnecessary. One prediction per cell instead of 9 means fewer outputs and less tuning: YOLO26 at 640 × 640 has 8,400 candidates (80² + 40² + 20²).
  4. What it costs, when it fails The assignment rule (which cells are “positive” for an object) now carries the weight the anchors used to carry; poor rules hurt small or crowded objects, which is why YOLO26 adds a small-target-aware assignment (STAL).
  5. What refined it NMS-free one-to-one heads (YOLOv10, YOLO26) and DETR-style set prediction, next.

DETR: detection as a set

DETR (“DEtection TRansformer”, 2020) threw out anchors and NMS together. A CNN makes a feature map; a transformer encoder lets every location look at every other one; then a decoder takes N = 100 learned “object queries”, slots that each output one box and one class, or the special class “∅ no object”. The output is a set: no duplicates allowed, no NMS needed.

The training trick is bipartite (Hungarian) matching: before computing the loss, find the one-to-one assignment of predictions to true objects with the lowest total cost (class cost + box L1 + GIoU). Matched queries learn to fit their object; every unmatched query learns “∅”. A query that duplicates another's object gets matched to nothing, so it's trained to stay quiet: duplicates are punished inside the loss.

🔮 Predict: the simplest matching is greedy: repeatedly take the cheapest remaining pair. Will greedy always find the lowest total cost?

What you should see: greedy grabs Q3 → C (0.75) and then Q2 → A (1.25), the next-cheapest cell. That leaves B with only bad options (Q4 at 3.0): total 5.00. The optimal matching gives A to Q1 (1.80) so Q2 can take B (1.35): total 3.90. Q4 and Q5 are matched to nothing and learn “∅ no object”. The Hungarian algorithm finds the optimum in polynomial time (about n³ steps), which is cheap for 100 queries.

🤔 Why this? DETR's set prediction with Hungarian matching
  1. The problem before Every detector so far needed hand-made parts outside the network: anchor shapes, IoU rules to decide which predictions are positives, and NMS at the end (a hard threshold that fails in crowds, Lesson 4.1). The network was never trained on what was actually output.
  2. What people tried Better NMS (Soft-NMS), learned NMS, tuning assignment rules per dataset.
  3. Why this works Treat the answer as a set and train with a loss that's the same whatever order the predictions come in: a one-to-one matching, then per-pair losses. Exactly one query is rewarded per object, so the model itself learns not to duplicate (attention lets queries “see” each other and divide the work). No anchors, no NMS; DETR matched a well-tuned Faster R-CNN on COCO and extends naturally to panoptic segmentation.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Slow training: the paper's long schedule is 500 epochs (and 300 epochs takes 3 days on 16 V100 GPUs), because matching is unstable early on and global attention learns slowly. It was weaker on small objects and heavy at high resolution (attention cost grows with the square of the number of feature-map positions).
  5. What refined it Deformable DETR (2020) attends to a few sampling points and needs 10× fewer epochs. RT-DETR (2023) was the first real-time end-to-end DETR (53.1 AP at 108 fps on a T4 for its ResNet-50 version); RT-DETRv2 (2024), D-FINE (2024, 54.0 AP at 124 fps for the L model), DEIM (2024, faster convergence) and RF-DETR (2025, the first real-time detector its authors report above 60 AP on COCO). One-to-one matching also inspired YOLOv10's and YOLO26's NMS-free heads.

The code below builds a randomly initialized Faster R-CNN (nothing downloaded), runs focal loss and Hungarian matching on this lesson's numbers:

import torch
from torchvision.models.detection import fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn
from torchvision.ops import sigmoid_focal_loss
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

torch.manual_seed(0)
# A two-stage detector, randomly initialized (weights=None: nothing is downloaded)
model = fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn(weights=None, weights_backbone=None, num_classes=3).eval()
print(f"{sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()):,} parameters")
with torch.no_grad():
    out = model([torch.rand(3, 480, 640)])              # a list of images in, a list of dicts out
print({k: tuple(v.shape) for k, v in out[0].items()})

# Focal loss on 3 easy negatives and 1 hard positive (logits)
logits = torch.tensor([-4.6, -4.6, -4.6, -0.85])
target = torch.tensor([0., 0., 0., 1.])
print(sigmoid_focal_loss(logits, target, alpha=-1, gamma=0.0))   # gamma 0 = plain cross-entropy
print(sigmoid_focal_loss(logits, target, alpha=-1, gamma=2.0))   # easy ones shrink ~10,000x

# DETR-style matching: rows = predictions, columns = ground-truth objects
cost = torch.tensor([[1.8, 4.2, 10.2], [1.25, 1.35, 8.6], [9.1, 6.7, 0.75], [5.4, 3.0, 4.7], [10.55, 12.95, 12.55]])
rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(cost.numpy())
print(rows, cols, cost[rows, cols].sum().item())

🎤 Interview angle

“Compare two-stage, one-stage and DETR detectors.” A strong answer:

  • Two-stage: proposals (RPN with anchors) then per-region classification with RoI pooling; accurate, slower.
  • One-stage: dense predictions from grid cells in one pass; the class-imbalance problem and focal loss; anchor-free heads; NMS.
  • DETR: object queries, Hungarian matching, a set loss that makes NMS unnecessary; slow convergence and small-object weakness of the original, fixed by deformable attention and real-time variants.
  • Picks by constraint: latency budget, hardware, object sizes, crowding, and whether the classes are fixed. Mentions NMS-free YOLOs (v10, 26) as the convergence of both lines.
🏭 In the real world
The current YOLO 🧰 Open source · 2026 Ultralytics YOLO26 documentation

How: five sizes (n to x) for detection, instance and semantic segmentation, classification, pose and oriented boxes; a dual head with an NMS-free one-to-one path.

Why this method: one network pass, simple export to edge hardware, and predictable latency without a post-processing step; the docs report up to 43% faster CPU ONNX inference for YOLO26n than YOLO11n.

Competition-winning two-stage detection 📜 History · 2015 Ren et al.: Faster R-CNN

How: the RPN shares convolutional features with the detection network, making proposals nearly free.

Why this method: learned proposals were both faster and better than hand-made ones; Faster R-CNN and RPN were the foundations of several 1st-place entries in the ILSVRC and COCO 2015 competitions.

Real-time transformers beat YOLOs 🧪 Research · 2023 Zhao et al.: DETRs Beat YOLOs on Real-time Object Detection (RT-DETR)

How: an efficient hybrid encoder for multi-scale features and “uncertainty-minimal query selection” for the decoder; speed can be tuned by dropping decoder layers without retraining.

Why this method: the authors observed NMS hurts YOLO's speed and accuracy; an end-to-end DETR fast enough for real time avoids it (53.1% AP at 108 fps on a T4).

A specialist DETR for any dataset 🏢 Roboflow · 2025 Robinson et al.: RF-DETR

How: fine-tunes a pretrained base network on the target dataset, then uses weight-sharing neural architecture search to pick accuracy–latency trade-offs without retraining.

Why this method: big open-vocabulary models often fail on real-world classes outside their pre-training; a light specialist transfers better. RF-DETR (nano) reaches 48.0 AP on COCO, 5.3 above D-FINE (nano) at similar latency.

🤯 Fun fact · YOLO goes to the museum

The original YOLO paper tested detectors trained on natural photos on artwork: the Picasso and People-Art datasets. YOLO beat all other methods, including R-CNN, “by a wide margin” when generalizing to paintings. Seeing the whole image at once taught it shapes and layouts, not just textures, so a cubist person still looked like a person.

Check yourself

1. A YOLO v1-style detector uses a 13 × 13 grid, 3 boxes per cell, and 80 classes. How many numbers does it output?
Each cell outputs B boxes × 5 numbers (x, y, w, h, confidence) plus one set of C class probabilities: 3 · 5 + 80 = 95 per cell, × 169 cells = 16,055. (Later YOLOs predict classes per box instead, so the formula changes.)
2. You train a one-stage detector for tiny cracks on a conveyor belt. Each image has 0–2 cracks and ~50,000 candidate locations. The loss falls quickly, but the model predicts “no crack” everywhere. What's the first fix to try?
A loss that falls while recall stays at zero is the class-imbalance signature: “everything is background” is nearly right for 49,998 of 50,000 locations. Focal loss down-weights those easy negatives so the rare hard positives drive learning. NMS and thresholds act after the model has already failed.
3. A team trains the original DETR on their dataset for 50 epochs and gets far worse mAP than a Faster R-CNN trained for the same 50 epochs. Most likely explanation?
DETR has no NMS by design, and matching 100 queries is cheap. Its known weakness is convergence: unstable early matching and slow-learning global attention. Deformable DETR needed 10× fewer epochs, and the real-time DETRs train in normal schedules.
📝 TL;DR
  • R-CNN: CNN on ~2,000 proposals (slow). Fast R-CNN: one CNN pass, crop features (RoI pooling). Faster R-CNN: learned proposals with 9 anchors per position (RPN).
  • YOLO: one pass, the cell containing an object's center is responsible; 7 × 7 × 30 outputs in v1. SSD adds multiple scales.
  • Dense detectors face ~100k candidates, almost all easy background; focal loss (1 − pt)γ with γ = 2 shrinks easy losses 10,000× and lets one-stage beat two-stage.
  • YOLO line: anchors (v2) → anchor-free (v8, 2023) → NMS-free (v10, 2024) → YOLO26 (Jan 2026: end-to-end, no DFL).
  • DETR: object queries + Hungarian matching = a set with no duplicates, no anchors, no NMS; slow to train at first. RT-DETR, D-FINE, DEIM and RF-DETR made it real-time.
