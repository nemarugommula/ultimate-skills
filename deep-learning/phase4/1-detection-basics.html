Phase 4 · Lesson 1

Vision tasks and detection basics: boxes, IoU, NMS, mAP

A classifier says “dog”. A detector must say “dog, here, and another dog there”. Four small ideas make that possible and measurable: the box, the overlap score IoU, the duplicate killer NMS, and the one number every detector paper reports, mAP.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A CNN slides small filters over an image and produces feature maps: grids where each cell describes one region of the picture (Lessons 3.2 and 3.3).
  • A network's last layer can output probabilities with sigmoid or softmax (Lesson 0.6), trained with cross-entropy (Lesson 1.1).
  • Precision and recall from classic ML: of what you flagged, how much was right; of what was there, how much you found (math 5.6b).
🎯 Hook: two detectors, one parking lot

A parking lot photo has 10 cars. Detector A finds all 10, but its boxes are a bit sloppy: each one overlaps its car by an IoU of 0.62 (you'll meet IoU in a minute; 1.0 is a perfect fit). Detector B also finds all 10, with tight boxes at IoU 0.92. Neither makes a single false alarm.

Under the old PASCAL VOC rule (a hit means IoU ≥ 0.5), both score a perfect 100%. Under the COCO benchmark's main metric, which every detector paper reports (Ultralytics, for example, lists its January 2026 YOLO26 family at 40.9 to 57.5 “mAP” on COCO), detector A scores 30% and B scores 90%.

Same objects found, same zero mistakes. Why does one score three times the other? By the end of this lesson you'll be able to compute both numbers by hand, and you'll have dragged boxes, killed duplicates and built a precision–recall curve yourself.

The box: four numbers, three dialects

A detection box is just 4 numbers. The trap is that libraries disagree on which 4. Image coordinates start at the top-left corner, with y growing downward.

FormatThe 4 numbersWho uses it
xyxyx1, y1 (top-left), x2, y2 (bottom-right)torchvision, most math (IoU is easiest here)
xywhx1, y1 (top-left), width, heightCOCO annotation files
cxcywhcenter x, center y, width, height (often divided by image size, so 0–1)YOLO label files, DETR
✏️ Worked example: one dog, three ways

In a 640 × 480 photo, a dog spans x from 100 to 300 and y from 120 to 420.

  • xyxy = [100, 120, 300, 420].
  • xywh = [100, 120, 200, 300] (width 300 − 100, height 420 − 120).
  • cxcywh = [200, 270, 200, 300] (center (100 + 300) / 2, (120 + 420) / 2). Normalized YOLO-style: [200/640, 270/480, 200/640, 300/480] = [0.3125, 0.5625, 0.3125, 0.625].

Feed a xywh box to code expecting xyxy and it silently becomes [100, 120, 200, 300]: a box a third the size, in the wrong place. Every metric then looks terrible, and nothing crashes. Check formats first when a detector's numbers make no sense.

IoU: how good is a box?

Numbers first. Two 10 × 10 boxes. Slide one right by 5, half its width. They share a 5 × 10 strip = 50. Together they cover 100 + 100 − 50 = 150. Overlap ÷ total = 50 ÷ 150 = 0.33.

That's IoU, intersection over union: the shared area divided by the combined area. 1 means identical boxes, 0 means no overlap. Notice the surprise: a box that's “half right” scores only 0.33, not 0.5. IoU is strict.

IoU = area(A ∩ B) ÷ area(A ∪ B) = area(A ∩ B) ÷ (area(A) + area(B) − area(A ∩ B))

🔮 Predict: COCO's strict threshold is IoU ≥ 0.75. If your box has exactly the right size, how far (as a share of its width) can it slide sideways and still pass? 10%? 25%? Guess, then try below.

What you should see: a correctly sized box slid by 10% of its width still scores 0.82; at 15% it drops to 0.74 and fails COCO's strict test (the formula: shifting by a fraction s gives (1 − s) ÷ (1 + s), so 0.75 allows s ≤ 14%). IoU punishes boxes that are too big as hard as boxes that are off-center.

🤔 Why this? IoU (intersection over union)
  1. The problem before To grade a box you need one number for “how well do these two rectangles agree”. Distance between centers ignores size: a box 100× too big with the right center would score perfectly.
  2. What people tried Center distance, corner distance in pixels, “does the box contain the object”. Each can be gamed and none works across object sizes: a 10-pixel error is huge for a 20-pixel bird and nothing for a 600-pixel bus.
  3. Why this works IoU uses areas, so it punishes being off-center, too big and too small at once, and it's scale-free: shift a 20-pixel box by 2 pixels or a 600-pixel box by 60, both give 0.82. One threshold (0.5) works for birds and buses. It's also cheap: a few min/max operations.
  4. What it costs, when it fails For boxes that don't overlap, IoU is 0 no matter how far apart, so as a training loss it gives no gradient telling a box which way to move. It's harsh on tiny objects (a 1-pixel slip on a 4-pixel object is a big IoU drop), and boxes themselves are crude for thin, diagonal things like a pole or a snake.
  5. What refined it GIoU (2019) adds a penalty based on the smallest box enclosing both, so non-overlapping boxes still get a gradient; DIoU and CIoU (2019) add center distance and aspect ratio. Modern detectors train with these (Lesson 4.2). Masks instead of boxes (Lesson 4.3) fix the shape problem; mask IoU uses the same formula on pixels.

Where do candidate boxes come from? Anchors and confidence

A detector doesn't know in advance how many objects there are, so it over-predicts: every cell of its feature map proposes boxes. A typical modern detector looks at a 640 × 640 image with three feature maps of stride 8, 16 and 32 (each cell covers 8, 16 or 32 pixels):

(640 ÷ 8)² + (640 ÷ 16)² + (640 ÷ 32)² = 6,400 + 1,600 + 400 = 8,400 candidate boxes for a photo with maybe 3 objects

Each candidate comes with a confidence score: how sure the model is that an object of a class is there (in YOLO v1, “objectness” × class probability). Older detectors also attach anchors to each cell: a few template shapes (Faster R-CNN uses 3 sizes × 3 aspect ratios = 9 per position), and the network only predicts small corrections to them. Lesson 4.2 shows why anchors came and why most new detectors dropped them.

Two clean-up steps turn 8,400 guesses into a handful of answers: (1) drop candidates below a score threshold (Ultralytics' default for prediction is 0.25), then (2) remove duplicates, because one dog lights up many neighbouring cells. That second step is NMS.

🧠 Analogy: a room full of witnesses

Ask 8,400 witnesses “is there a dog near you, and where exactly?”. Most say “no” (score threshold removes them). The few near the dog all say “yes!” and point at slightly different rectangles. You trust the most confident witness, then politely ignore everyone who is pointing at the same dog. The hard part is knowing whether two witnesses mean the same dog or two dogs standing close together.

NMS: keep the best, delete its echoes

Non-maximum suppression (NMS) is a 5-line greedy loop: sort boxes by score; keep the best; delete every remaining box whose IoU with it is above a threshold; repeat with the next box still standing. It has exactly one knob, the IoU threshold.

What you should see: at 0.10 to 0.15, the best box of person A (score 0.96) overlaps person B's best box by 0.31, so NMS deletes B: a real person missed. From 0.20 to 0.30 B survives only through a sloppier box (IoU 0.62). From 0.35 to 0.50 it's perfect. From 0.55 up, echoes start leaking through as duplicates (false positives), and by 0.70, Ultralytics' default, you get 4 duplicates for 3 objects. No threshold is right for both crowds and lonely objects; that tension is why NMS-free detectors exist (Lesson 4.2).

🤔 Why this? Non-maximum suppression (NMS)
  1. The problem before Dense detectors score thousands of overlapping candidates, and one object lights up many neighbouring cells, each with a high-scoring box of its own. Without clean-up, one dog becomes a dozen dogs and precision collapses.
  2. What people tried Averaging nearby boxes (OverFeat, 2013, merged and accumulated boxes), clustering, simply keeping the top-k. Averaging blurs two close objects into one; top-k needs to know how many objects there are.
  3. Why this works Greedy NMS trusts the single most confident box and treats anything that overlaps it heavily as an echo. It needs no training, is a few lines (R-CNN used it in 2014), and on typical scenes the threshold 0.5 to 0.7 separates echoes (IoU 0.6 to 0.9 with the winner) from neighbours (usually IoU under 0.3). Run it per class (batched_nms) so a person never suppresses the bike they're riding.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One hard threshold for every scene: in crowds, real neighbours overlap above it and get deleted (you just did it). It's sequential, data-dependent work that's awkward to export and adds latency that grows with the number of boxes. And it's a hand-made rule outside the network, so training can't learn to fix its mistakes.
  5. What refined it Soft-NMS (2017) lowers the scores of overlapping boxes instead of deleting them. DETR (2020) predicts a set with one box per object and needs no NMS at all; YOLOv10 (2024) brought NMS-free training to YOLO, and YOLO26 (2026) supports end-to-end inference with nms=False (Lesson 4.2).

Precision, recall and AP: grading a whole ranked list

A detector outputs boxes with scores. To grade it, sort every prediction by score and walk down the list. Each prediction is a true positive (TP) if it overlaps a not-yet-matched true object with IoU ≥ the threshold, otherwise a false positive (FP): a wrong box, a sloppy box or a duplicate. After each step:

  • precision = TP so far ÷ predictions so far (“of what I've claimed, how much is right?”)
  • recall = TP so far ÷ number of true objects (“of what's there, how much have I found?”)

Walking down the list, recall can only go up; precision wobbles. Plot precision against recall and you get the precision–recall curve. Its area is the average precision (AP): one number for the whole trade-off, high only if the detector is right and its most confident boxes come first.

The standard trick first: at each recall level, use the best precision reachable from there on (that turns the wobbly curve into a staircase). Then add up width × height of each step: recall 0 → 0.6 at precision 1.00, 0.6 → 0.8 at 0.71 (rank 7's precision beats rank 6's), 0.8 → 1.0 at 0.71.

AP@0.5 = 0.6 × 1.00 + 0.2 × 0.714 + 0.2 × 0.714 = 0.886

Now the twist. The same 5 hits have IoUs 0.91, 0.78, 0.62, 0.84 and 0.56. At a threshold of 0.75 only three of them count, and AP@0.75 falls to 0.50. This is exactly the playground below.

COCO's AP@[.5:.95], the “mAP” in papers, repeats this at 10 IoU thresholds (0.50, 0.55, …, 0.95), averages them, then averages over all 80 object classes. COCO's own documentation calls AP at IoU = .50:.05:.95 the “primary challenge metric”, and notes it makes no distinction between AP and mAP. Averaging over IoUs rewards tight boxes: a box at IoU 0.62 counts at 3 of the 10 thresholds; one at 0.92 counts at 9. That's the whole hook: 30% vs 90%.

🤔 Why this? AP and COCO's mAP@[.5:.95]
  1. The problem before Accuracy doesn't exist for detection: there's no fixed list of answers, the number of predictions varies, and “nothing there” has no count. Precision alone rewards making only one very safe guess; recall alone rewards spraying boxes everywhere.
  2. What people tried Precision and recall at one score threshold (but which threshold?), and PASCAL VOC's AP at a single IoU of 0.5, the standard until COCO (2014).
  3. Why this works AP sweeps every score threshold at once, so it measures the ranking itself and no one has to pick a cut-off. Averaging over classes (mAP) stops one common class from dominating. COCO's average over 10 IoU thresholds adds localization: in the playground, tightening two boxes raised it from 0.47 to 0.68 while AP@0.5 didn't move.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One number hides a lot: it says nothing about speed, about which classes fail, or whether errors are misses or false alarms. It rewards ranking, not calibrated scores, so you still need to pick an operating threshold for deployment. And a +1 mAP on COCO may not transfer to your warehouse photos; measure on your own data.
  5. What refined it COCO also reports AP50, AP75, AP for small (area under 32²), medium and large objects, and recall at 1/10/100 boxes per image. Panoptic quality (PQ) does the same job for segmentation (Lesson 4.3); tracking adds MOTA and HOTA (Lesson 4.7).

Two details that bite in practice. COCO evaluates the curve at 101 recall points (0, 0.01, …, 1) instead of the exact area; the numbers differ only slightly. And validation runs keep almost every box (Ultralytics validates at a confidence of 0.001, versus 0.25 for prediction) because AP needs the whole ranked list, including the low-score tail.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

torchvision.ops has the box toolkit: IoU, format conversion, NMS and per-class NMS. For full COCO evaluation use pycocotools (the official COCO API) or a metric library; Ultralytics prints mAP50 and mAP50-95 after every validation run.

import torch
from torchvision.ops import box_iou, nms, box_convert, batched_nms

gt = torch.tensor([[10., 10., 50., 90.]])                  # xyxy: x1, y1, x2, y2 (pixels)
pred = torch.tensor([[12., 14., 52., 92.],                  # a good box
                     [30., 10., 70., 90.]])                 # shifted by half its width
print(box_iou(pred, gt).squeeze(1))                         # IoU of each prediction with the target

print(box_convert(gt, in_fmt="xyxy", out_fmt="xywh"))       # COCO format: top-left + width, height
print(box_convert(gt, in_fmt="xyxy", out_fmt="cxcywh"))     # YOLO-style: center + width, height

boxes = torch.tensor([[10., 10., 50., 90.], [12., 12., 52., 88.], [40., 10., 80., 90.]])
scores = torch.tensor([0.95, 0.80, 0.90])
print(nms(boxes, scores, iou_threshold=0.5))               # indices kept, best score first
labels = torch.tensor([0, 1, 0])                            # per-class NMS: different classes never suppress
print(batched_nms(boxes, scores, labels, iou_threshold=0.5))

# What we got (torch 2.8, torchvision 0.23, CPU):
# tensor([0.8415, 0.3333])
# tensor([[10., 10., 40., 80.]])
# tensor([[30., 50., 40., 80.]])
# tensor([0, 2])
# tensor([0, 2, 1])

Notice the half-shifted box scores exactly 0.3333, the “numbers first” example. With per-class NMS, box 1 survives because it's labelled a different class. Gotcha: box_iou and nms expect xyxy; convert COCO's xywh first.

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain mAP@[.5:.95] and how you'd debug a detector whose mAP is low.” A strong answer:

  • Defines IoU, TP/FP matching at a threshold (one prediction per object, duplicates are FPs), precision and recall down the ranked list, AP as the area under the PR curve, the average over classes and over 10 IoU thresholds.
  • Splits the problem: compare AP50 with AP75 (big gap → localization problem); check AP small/medium/large (small objects are the usual weakness); look at per-class AP.
  • Checks the boring things first: box format and normalization, the NMS and confidence thresholds used during evaluation, label quality.
  • Knows NMS's failure in crowds and that NMS-free detectors exist (DETR, YOLOv10, YOLO26).
🏭 In the real world
The benchmark everyone reports 📜 History · 2014 Lin et al.: Microsoft COCO: Common Objects in Context

How: 328k images with 2.5 million labelled instances, each outlined with a per-instance segmentation; evaluation with AP averaged over IoU .50:.05:.95.

Why this method: complex everyday scenes with many small and overlapping objects, where a single loose IoU threshold no longer separated good detectors from great ones.

mAP50 and mAP50-95 after every training run 🧰 Open source Ultralytics: YOLO performance metrics guide

How: validation reports precision, recall, mAP50 and mAP50-95 per class; prediction applies a confidence threshold (default 0.25) and NMS (default IoU 0.7).

Why this method: mAP50 tells you whether objects are found, mAP50-95 how precisely, so the pair points at detection vs localization problems.

Greedy NMS in the first deep detector 🧪 Research · 2014 Girshick et al.: Rich feature hierarchies (R-CNN)

How: about 2,000 region proposals per image are scored by a CNN, then non-maximum suppression removes overlapping detections per class.

Why this method: many proposals overlap each object; greedy NMS is cheap and needs no training. R-CNN improved VOC 2012 mAP by more than 30% relative to the previous best.

Detection without NMS 🧪 Research · 2024 Wang et al.: YOLOv10: Real-Time End-to-End Object Detection

How: “consistent dual assignments” train one head to output one box per object, so NMS can be dropped at inference.

Why this method: the authors note NMS “hampers the end-to-end deployment” and adds latency; removing it makes speed predictable.

🤯 Fun fact · a 4-year-old's vocabulary

The COCO paper describes its categories as object types “easily recognizable by a 4 year old”: 91 of them, from person and bicycle to toothbrush. Detection papers usually evaluate the 80 categories that have enough labels. Some of the most-used numbers in deep learning are about recognizing things a preschooler names effortlessly.

Check yourself

1. Box A = [0, 0, 4, 4] and box B = [2, 0, 6, 4] (xyxy). What is their IoU?
Each box is 4 × 4 = 16. They share x from 2 to 4 over the full height: 2 × 4 = 8. Union = 16 + 16 − 8 = 24. IoU = 8 ÷ 24 = 0.33. Half-overlapping boxes always give 1/3, which is why IoU feels strict.
2. Your pedestrian detector misses people walking in tight groups, but works fine on lone pedestrians. Raw predictions (before post-processing) do contain a box for every person. What's the most likely culprit?
If the raw boxes exist and disappear after post-processing, NMS ate them: people in a group overlap each other more than the threshold, so the best one suppresses its neighbours. Raising the threshold keeps them at the cost of more duplicates; Soft-NMS and set-prediction detectors avoid the hard cut-off.
3. Model X: AP50 = 0.80, AP75 = 0.35. Model Y: AP50 = 0.72, AP75 = 0.60. A robot must grasp the objects. Which model, and why?
The big drop from AP50 to AP75 means X's boxes are often near the object but not on it. Y holds up at the strict threshold, so its COCO AP@[.5:.95] is likely higher too. For counting cars, X could be fine; for grasping, localization is the job.
📝 TL;DR
  • Classification → one label; detection → boxes; semantic seg → a class per pixel; instance → a mask per object; panoptic → both; keypoints → joints.
  • Boxes come in xyxy, xywh (COCO) and cxcywh (YOLO, often normalized). Mixing them up fails silently.
  • IoU = overlap ÷ union: scale-free and strict (a half-shifted box scores 0.33). It's 0 for non-overlapping boxes, so training uses GIoU/DIoU/CIoU.
  • Detectors over-predict (8,400 candidates at 640²); a score threshold and NMS keep the best box per object. NMS's one threshold fails in crowds.
  • AP = area under the precision–recall curve of the ranked list; mAP averages over classes; COCO's AP@[.5:.95] also averages 10 IoU thresholds, so tight boxes matter.
