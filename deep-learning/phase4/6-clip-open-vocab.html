CLIP and open-vocabulary vision

Train on 400 million images with their captions, and you get a model that can classify into categories nobody ever labelled: just type them. The trick is one square matrix and its diagonal. Then the same idea turns detectors and segmenters into tools you can talk to.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An embedding is a vector that places similar things close together; cosine similarity measures closeness (math 1.5).
  • Softmax turns scores into probabilities; a temperature sharpens or flattens them (Lesson 0.6, math 8.2). Cross-entropy punishes giving the right answer low probability (Lesson 1.1).
  • A ViT encodes an image into a vector (Lesson 4.5); a transformer can encode text the same way (Lesson 6.5).
🎯 Hook: ImageNet accuracy without ImageNet labels

In 2021 OpenAI's CLIP was trained on 400 million (image, text) pairs from the internet, with one simple task: “predict which caption goes with which image”. It was never shown an ImageNet label. Then, given just the 1,000 class names, it matched the accuracy of the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet (76.2%), “without needing to use any of the 1.28 million training examples it was trained on”.

CLIP-style models now sit everywhere: Stable Diffusion v1 uses CLIP's text encoder to understand prompts, SigLIP encoders are the eyes of open vision-language models like PaliGemma, and photo search and content moderation use image–text embeddings. Yet the same CLIP struggled to count objects and could be fooled by handwritten text stuck on an object.

How can a model classify into classes it never trained on, and why does writing the prompt “a photo of a boxer, a type of pet” instead of “boxer” fix a real bug?

Numbers first: one batch, one matrix

CLIP has two encoders: an image encoder (a ViT or ResNet) and a text encoder (a transformer). Both output a vector of the same length, normalized to length 1. Take a batch of N image–caption pairs. Compute every image's cosine similarity with every caption: an N × N matrix. The N correct pairs are on the diagonal; the other N² − N cells are mismatches.

CLIP's batch: N = 32,768 → 32,768² = 1,073,741,824 similarities per step, of which 32,768 are “right”

The task: make every diagonal cell the biggest in its row (image → its caption) and in its column (caption → its image). Every other pair in the batch is a free “negative” example.

✏️ Worked example: the contrastive loss for a batch of 2

Similarities: image 1 vs captions (0.9, 0.1); image 2 vs captions (0.2, 0.8). Divide by a temperature τ, apply softmax along each row and take −log of the diagonal; do the same down each column; average the two directions.

That's why CLIP uses a small, learned temperature, and why near-duplicate captions in a batch (two “a photo of a dog” pairs) hurt training.

🤔 Why this? Contrastive image–text pretraining (CLIP, 2021)
  1. The problem before Vision models learned a fixed list of classes from human labels (ImageNet: 1,000 classes, 1.28 M images). Any new concept needed new labelled data, and labels are expensive.
  2. What people tried Predicting the exact caption words from the image (image captioning as pretraining), and earlier zero-shot attempts like Visual N-Grams, which reached 11.5% on ImageNet.
  3. Why this works Captions on the web are free, noisy supervision about everything. Predicting the exact words is hard and wasteful; just choosing which caption in the batch belongs to which image is a much easier signal, and the CLIP paper found it far more efficient. Each batch gives N positives and N² − N negatives for free. The shared embedding space means text and images can be compared directly: the basis of zero-shot classification and retrieval.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Huge data and compute; big batches matter (more negatives) and the softmax needs the whole N × N matrix gathered across all GPUs. It learns what captions talk about: weak on counting, on spatial relations and on fine-grained or specialist images (satellite, medical, traffic signs). Web data brings web biases.
  5. What refined it ALIGN (bigger, noisier data), open reproductions (OpenCLIP), SigLIP's sigmoid loss (below), SigLIP 2's extra objectives, and CLIP-style text encoders inside detectors, segmenters and image generators.

Zero-shot classification: the classifier is a sentence

To classify an image into K classes, write one sentence per class, encode each with the text encoder, and pick the class whose text is most similar to the image. The “weights” of the classifier are just text embeddings, so a new class costs one line of text.

The wording matters. The CLIP paper found the template “A photo of a {label}.” improves ImageNet accuracy by 1.3% over the bare label, and gives a real bug: in the Oxford-IIIT Pets dataset, the class boxer means the dog breed, but to a text encoder without context, it “could just as likely refer to a type of athlete”. Their fix for pets: “A photo of a {label}, a type of pet.”

🤔 Why this? Zero-shot classification with text prompts
  1. The problem before A classifier's last layer has one weight vector per class, learned from labelled examples. No examples, no class. Changing the class list meant retraining.
  2. What people tried Attribute-based zero-shot learning (describe classes by hand-picked attributes), word-vector class embeddings; both far below supervised accuracy.
  3. Why this works CLIP's text encoder is a generator of classifier weights: encode “a photo of a {label}” and you get the weight vector for that class. Because pretraining saw captions, prompts that look like captions work best; context words resolve ambiguity (polysemy); averaging many templates (an “ensemble” of prompts) helps further. One model covers ImageNet, flowers, food, cars, actions…
  4. What it costs, when it fails Accuracy depends on wording (prompt engineering for vision); classes that are rare or never described on the web fail; it ranks the classes you give it, so a missing “none of these” class means confident nonsense. Specialist domains usually need fine-tuning or a few labelled examples (a linear probe on the frozen features).
  5. What refined it Prompt ensembles and learned prompts, LLM-written class descriptions, and fine-tuning recipes that keep zero-shot robustness. For localization, the same text embeddings drive open-vocabulary detection (below).

SigLIP: a yes/no question per pair

CLIP's loss is a softmax across the whole batch: to compute any row you need every caption in the batch, gathered from every GPU. SigLIP (Google, 2023) asks a simpler question for each pair independently: “do this image and this caption match, yes or no?”, a sigmoid (Lesson 0.4) with a learnable scale t and bias b.

CLIP: −log softmax over the row and column · SigLIP: −log σ(z · (t · cos + b)), with z = +1 for the matching pair and −1 for every other pair; the scale t starts at 10 (stored as log 10) and the bias b at −10

The bias starts very negative because almost every pair in a batch is a non-match: with a batch of 32k, there are about 32,000 “no” answers for each “yes”.

🤔 Why this? SigLIP's sigmoid loss
  1. The problem before CLIP's softmax needs a global view of all pairwise similarities for normalization: the full N × N matrix across devices, memory-hungry, and it needs huge batches to work well.
  2. What people tried Gathering embeddings across GPUs with clever communication; smaller batches (worse results).
  3. Why this works A sigmoid per pair needs no normalization over the batch, so each device can compute its chunk of pairs locally. The paper reports it performs better at small batch sizes, allows scaling the batch further, and is memory-efficient: with only four TPUv4 chips a SigLiT model reached 84.5% ImageNet zero-shot accuracy in two days. They pushed batches to one million and found the benefit flattens: around 32k is enough.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two hyperparameters (t, b) that must be initialized sensibly, or the flood of negatives dominates early training (hence b = −10). It's a training improvement: the same blind spots about counting and spatial relations remain.
  5. What refined it SigLIP 2 (February 2025) adds captioning-based pretraining, self-distillation and masked prediction (Lesson 9.1 ideas), online data curation, multilingual data and native-aspect-ratio variants, released at 86 M, 303 M, 400 M and 1 B parameters, with better localization and dense features. SigLIP-So400m is the vision encoder of PaliGemma (Lesson 8.5).

Open-vocabulary detection: boxes for any words

Classification says “there's a forklift in the image”. You usually need where. Open-vocabulary detectors replace the detector's fixed class layer with text embeddings: each box's feature is compared with the embedding of each query (“forklift”, “person without a helmet”), exactly like zero-shot classification but per box.

🤔 Why this? Open-vocabulary detection
  1. The problem before Detectors know a fixed list: COCO's 80 classes. Detection labels (boxes) are far scarcer than captions, so long-tail categories (“fire extinguisher”, “pallet jack”) have few or no boxes to learn from.
  2. What people tried Training on bigger box datasets (LVIS with ~1,200 classes, Objects365), few-shot detection.
  3. Why this works Borrow the concept knowledge from image–text pretraining and learn only localization from boxes. The class layer becomes “similarity with a text embedding”, so the vocabulary is whatever you type, and pseudo-labelling web images (OWLv2) scales the box data itself. One model, any class list, chosen at inference time.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Slower and bigger than a closed-set YOLO of the same accuracy on known classes; confidence scores are poorly calibrated across different prompts; rare, specific or out-of-distribution concepts still fail (the RF-DETR authors note open-vocabulary detectors “often fail to generalize” to real-world classes outside their pre-training). Long phrases with relations (“the cup to the left of the laptop”) are hard.
  5. What refined it Use them to auto-label your data, then fine-tune a small closed-set detector for production (Lessons 4.2 and 4.4); YOLOE and SAM 3 push open vocabulary toward real time.

Known limits (as of 2026)

  • Counting and space: the CLIP paper lists counting objects (CLEVRCounts) and distance to the nearest car (KITTI) among its weak tasks; relations like “left of” are a known weakness of contrastive models.
  • Polysemy and wording: boxer, crane, mouse; answers change with the prompt.
  • Reading too well: OpenAI found CLIP neurons that respond to both pictures and words, and “typographic attacks”: even photographs of hand-written text can often fool the model.
  • Bias: in the CLIP paper's own audit with FairFace images, adding crime-related and non-human labels to the class list led to some groups' faces being misclassified into those categories at noticeably different rates. Test on your users' data before deploying.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Both losses in a few lines, checked on random embeddings (no models downloaded):

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

def clip_loss(img, txt, temperature=0.07):
    """Symmetric InfoNCE: in a batch of N pairs, image i must pick text i out of N, and text i must pick image i."""
    img, txt = F.normalize(img, dim=-1), F.normalize(txt, dim=-1)
    logits = img @ txt.T / temperature                      # N x N cosine similarities, sharpened
    labels = torch.arange(len(img))                         # the right answers are on the diagonal
    return (F.cross_entropy(logits, labels) + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)) / 2

def siglip_loss(img, txt, t=10.0, b=-10.0):
    """Sigmoid loss: every (image, text) pair is its own yes/no question. No softmax over the batch."""
    img, txt = F.normalize(img, dim=-1), F.normalize(txt, dim=-1)
    logits = img @ txt.T * t + b
    labels = 2 * torch.eye(len(img)) - 1                    # +1 on the diagonal, -1 elsewhere
    return -F.logsigmoid(labels * logits).sum() / len(img)

torch.manual_seed(0)
txt = torch.randn(8, 512)
aligned = txt + 0.1 * torch.randn(8, 512)                   # images whose embeddings match their captions
shuffled = aligned[torch.randperm(8)]                       # same images, paired with the wrong captions
print(f"CLIP loss   aligned {clip_loss(aligned, txt):.4f}   shuffled {clip_loss(shuffled, txt):.4f}   chance ln(8) = {torch.log(torch.tensor(8.)):.4f}")
print(f"SigLIP loss aligned {siglip_loss(aligned, txt):.4f}   shuffled {siglip_loss(shuffled, txt):.4f}")

# CLIP loss   aligned 0.0000   shuffled 12.4588   chance ln(8) = 2.0794

Note how the shuffled CLIP loss (12.5) is far above chance (ln 8 = 2.08): with τ = 0.07 the model is confidently wrong. Gotcha: always normalize embeddings before the dot product, and compare scores only within one prompt set: CLIP similarities aren't calibrated probabilities. 🎤 Interview angle

“How does CLIP do zero-shot classification, and what are its failure modes?” A strong answer:

  • Two encoders into one normalized space; symmetric InfoNCE over the N × N batch matrix with a learned temperature; diagonal = positives, everything else = negatives.
  • Zero-shot = encode “a photo of a {label}” per class, cosine with the image embedding, softmax; prompt templates and ensembles matter (boxer: dog vs athlete).
  • SigLIP's sigmoid loss: per-pair, no global normalization, better at small batches.
  • Failure modes: counting, spatial relations, polysemy, out-of-distribution domains, typographic attacks, social biases; mitigations: a “none” class, fine-tuning or linear probes, evaluation on your own data.
🏭 In the real world
The text encoder of an image generator 🧰 Open source · 2022 CompVis: Stable Diffusion v1-4 model card

How: a latent diffusion model that “uses a fixed, pretrained text encoder (CLIP ViT-L/14)” to turn the prompt into vectors the denoiser attends to (Lessons 7.5 and 7.7).

Why this method: CLIP's text embeddings already live in a space aligned with images, so the generator doesn't have to learn language from scratch.

The eyes of an open VLM 🏢 Google · 2024 Beyer et al.: PaliGemma

How: a SigLIP-So400m vision encoder feeds image tokens into the Gemma-2B language model.

Why this method: contrastively pretrained encoders give general-purpose visual features that transfer to captioning, question answering, remote sensing and segmentation tasks (Lesson 8.5).

Detect anything you can describe 🧪 Research · 2023 Liu et al.: Grounding DINO

How: a feature enhancer, language-guided query selection and a cross-modality decoder fuse text into a DETR-style detector.

Why this method: category names and referring expressions (“the man in red”) as input; 52.5 AP on COCO zero-shot and a record 26.1 mean AP on the ODinW zero-shot benchmark.

Open vocabulary at YOLO speed 🧪 Research · 2024 Cheng et al.: YOLO-World

How: a vision-language path-aggregation network and region–text contrastive loss; prompts are encoded once and folded into the network.

Why this method: real-time open-vocabulary detection (35.4 AP on LVIS at 52 fps on a V100), available in Ultralytics.

🤯 Fun fact · fooled by a dollar sign

Studying CLIP's neurons, OpenAI found a “finance neuron” that fires for pictures of piggy banks and for the string “$$$”. By forcing it to fire they could make the model classify a dog as a piggy bank. Because CLIP learned to read, the team called the trick a typographic attack: “even photographs of hand-written text can often fool the model”. Its strength (reading text in images, great for OCR-like tasks) is also an attack surface.

Check yourself

1. A batch of 4 pairs has perfect embeddings: each image's similarity with its own caption is 1, and with every other caption is 0. With τ = 0.25, what's the image → text loss per row?
Logits are 1/0.25 = 4 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. Softmax gives e⁴ ÷ (e⁴ + 3) = 54.6 ÷ 57.6 = 0.948, and −ln 0.948 ≈ 0.054. Even perfect embeddings don't give zero loss at a finite temperature; a smaller τ pushes it closer. ln 4 would be a uniform, clueless model.
2. Your zero-shot classifier for a garden app labels “crane” photos (the bird) as “excavator” half the time. What's the cheapest fix to try first?
“Crane” is polysemous; the text encoder can't know which sense you mean, and the CLIP paper names exactly this ImageNet example. Context words in the prompt fix the class embedding for free. Temperature only rescales scores; it can't fix a wrong ranking.
3. A team asks an open-vocabulary detector for “a person not wearing a helmet” on a construction site and gets boxes on almost every person, helmeted or not. What's the most likely issue and a sensible fix?
Contrastive embeddings capture what a phrase is about (“person”, “helmet”) far better than logic like “not”. Decompose the query into concepts the model handles well and do the logic yourself, or label a few hundred images and train a specialist; measure on your own site footage.
📝 TL;DR
  • CLIP: image and text encoders into one normalized space; for a batch of N pairs, make the N × N similarity matrix's diagonal win along rows and columns (symmetric InfoNCE) with a learned temperature (init 0.07).
  • 400 M web pairs → zero-shot ImageNet 76.2%, matching ResNet-50 with none of its labels.
  • Zero-shot classification = cosine with “a photo of a {label}”; wording matters (polysemy: boxer, crane); prompt ensembles help.
  • SigLIP: a sigmoid yes/no per pair, no batch-wide softmax; better at small batches, memory-efficient. SigLIP 2 (2025) adds dense features and multilinguality.
  • Open-vocabulary detection (OWL-ViT/OWLv2, Grounding DINO, YOLO-World, YOLOE, SAM 3) compares box features with text embeddings. Limits: counting, relations, negation, rare concepts, typographic attacks, bias.
