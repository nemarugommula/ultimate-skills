Phase 10 · Lesson 2

Mixed precision: fp32, fp16, bf16, fp8

Store numbers in 16 or 8 bits instead of 32 and training gets about twice as fast per halving, with half the memory. The catch: some numbers fall off the edge. Learn exactly which ones, and the three tricks that catch them.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Big matmuls are compute-bound, most other ops are memory-bound; fewer bytes per number helps both (Lesson 10.1).
  • Gradients are often tiny numbers, and Adam keeps two extra numbers per weight (Lesson 1.5).
  • Computers store real numbers as floating point: a sign, an exponent and a few significant digits (math 8.5).
🎯 Hook

In 2017, researchers at Baidu and NVIDIA tried to train the SSD object detector with 16-bit numbers. It diverged. Same model, same data, fine in 32-bit.

The fix was almost comically small: multiply the loss by 8 before backprop, and divide the gradients by 8 afterwards. With that one change, 16-bit training matched 32-bit accuracy (Micikevicius et al., “Mixed Precision Training”).

Today every large model is trained this way or with its descendant, bf16, and the frontier is pushing to 8 bits: DeepSeek-V3 (2024) reported training at scale with an fp8 framework. Why would multiplying by 8 save a network, and why did the industry then pick a format where it isn't needed at all?

Numbers first: what a float actually stores

A floating-point number is scientific notation in binary: sign × 1.mantissa × 2exponent. The exponent bits decide the range (how big and how small). The mantissa bits decide the precision (how many significant digits). Every format is a different split of the same budget:

Read the two 16-bit rows side by side. fp16 spends its bits on precision and has a tiny range: anything under about 6 × 10⁻⁸ becomes exactly 0, anything over 65,504 becomes infinity. bf16 (“brain float”, from Google Brain) keeps fp32's 8 exponent bits, so it has fp32's whole range, and pays with precision: only about 2 to 3 significant digits.

🧠 Analogy: a ruler with a fixed number of marks

You get the same number of tick marks either way. fp16 packs them close together on a short ruler: precise, but a tiny gradient falls off the left end and a huge activation off the right. bf16 spreads the same marks over a ruler as long as fp32's: nothing falls off, but each measurement is rounded more coarsely. Deep learning turns out to forgive coarse marks far more than it forgives falling off the ruler.

The first leak: tiny gradients become zero

Gradients are much smaller than weights or activations. The 2017 paper plotted the activation gradients of the SSD detector and found that many sat below 2⁻²⁴ ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁸, fp16's floor: they would all become exactly 0. Their key observation: values below 2⁻²⁷ didn't matter for training, but values between 2⁻²⁷ and 2⁻²⁴ did, and fp16 was throwing them away.

The fix uses the chain rule: if you multiply the loss by a constant S, every gradient in the network is multiplied by S too. So scale the loss up before backward(), which lifts the gradients onto fp16's ruler, then divide by S in fp32 before the optimizer step. This is loss scaling.

What you should see: at S = 1, fp16 flushes about 9% of these gradients to zero. S = 8 (the paper's factor) cuts that to 1.2%; from S = 2⁸ none are lost and the survivors' rounding error drops from 6% to 0.1%. Push past S = 2²² and the largest gradients overflow: one infinity poisons the update, so the step is skipped. The dynamic scaler climbs from PyTorch's default 2¹⁶ until it hits an overflow, halves, and then saw-tooths along the edge. Switch to bf16: nothing is lost at S = 1. That's the whole reason bf16 won.

🤔 Why this? Loss scaling
  1. The problem before fp16's smallest positive number is 2⁻²⁴ ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁸. A meaningful chunk of real gradients is smaller, so in pure fp16 they become 0: in the paper's SSD run, training diverged.
  2. What people tried Staying in fp32 (2× the memory, much slower tensor-core-free math), or keeping only some layers in fp16.
  3. Why this works Backprop is linear in the loss: scale the loss by S and every gradient is scaled by S. A gradient of 10⁻⁸ times S = 1,024 is 10⁻⁵, comfortably on fp16's ruler. Divide by S in fp32 before updating, and the math is unchanged. The paper found S = 8 enough for SSD.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Pick S too big and large gradients overflow to infinity (65,504 is the ceiling), so the step must be skipped. The right S drifts during training. And it's one more thing to get wrong in custom training loops (gradient clipping must happen after unscaling).
  5. What refined it Dynamic loss scaling: start high (PyTorch: 65,536), halve and skip on overflow, double after 2,000 clean steps. That's torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda"). Then bf16, which doesn't need any of it.

The second leak: tiny updates vanish

Even when the gradient survives, the update can vanish. fp16 has about 3 significant digits near 1.0: its next number after 1.0 is 1.000977. Add an update of 0.0001 to a weight of 1.0 and the true answer, 1.0001, rounds straight back to 1.0. The paper notes this happens whenever the weight is about 2,048× bigger than its update. Thousands of steps, all lost.

✏️ Worked example: one update, three formats
w = 1.0, add 0.0001Why
fp321.0001steps of 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ near 1.0: plenty
fp161.0steps of 9.8 × 10⁻⁴; 0.0001 is less than half a step, so it rounds away
bf161.0steps of 7.8 × 10⁻³: even worse

We ran this in PyTorch: torch.tensor(1.0).half() + 1e-4 gives 1.0; in fp32 it gives 1.0001. Hence the second ingredient of mixed precision: keep an fp32 master copy of the weights for the update, and use 16-bit copies only for the forward and backward math.

🤔 Why this? fp32 master weights (the “mixed” in mixed precision)
  1. The problem before In pure 16-bit training, updates smaller than half a step of the weight round to nothing (1.0 + 0.0001 = 1.0 in fp16). The paper also found about 5% of weight gradients in one model had exponents below −24.
  2. What people tried Bigger learning rates (unstable), or giving up and training in fp32.
  3. Why this works The expensive part, the matmuls of forward and backward, runs in 16 bits on tensor cores. The cheap part, w ← w − η·g, runs in fp32, where 1.0 + 0.0001 is 1.0001. Each step, the 16-bit copy is refreshed from the master.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Memory: per parameter you now hold 2 bytes (16-bit weight) + 2 (16-bit gradient) + 4 (fp32 master) + 8 (Adam's two fp32 moments) = 16 bytes. A 7B model needs 112 GB just for that, which is where Lesson 10.3 starts.
  5. What refined it Sharding the master weights and optimizer states across GPUs (ZeRO / FSDP, Lesson 10.3); 8-bit optimizer states; stochastic rounding, which lets some systems update low-precision weights directly.
🤔 Why this? bf16 (brain floating point)
  1. The problem before fp16's 5 exponent bits give it a range of only about 6 × 10⁻⁸ to 65,504. Gradients fall off the bottom and activations sometimes off the top, so fp16 needs loss scaling, skipped steps and care.
  2. What people tried Loss scaling (above) and per-layer exceptions that stay in fp32.
  3. Why this works bf16 is simply the top 16 bits of fp32: same sign, same 8 exponent bits, mantissa cut from 23 bits to 7. Same range as fp32, so 1e-8 and 70,000 both survive and no loss scaling is needed. Google's TPU team wrote that bf16 “works as well as the FP32 format” for the models they trained, and is close to a drop-in replacement. SGD is noisy anyway; 2–3 significant digits in the matmul inputs are enough, as long as accumulations happen in fp32 inside the tensor cores.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Less precision than fp16 (step 0.0078 near 1.0). Long sums in bf16 lose information, so reductions, softmax, norms and the loss are kept in fp32 (autocast does this for you). Hardware support arrived later: on NVIDIA from the A100 (2020); older GPUs like the V100 only have fp16 tensor cores.
  5. What refined it bf16 became the default training format for large models. Below it: fp8 for the matmuls (next), with bf16 or fp32 for everything else.

Going to 8 bits: fp8 and scaling factors

With only 8 bits, you can't have both range and precision, so there are two fp8 formats (Micikevicius et al., 2022, a joint NVIDIA, Arm and Intel proposal): E4M3 (more precision, max 448) and E5M2 (more range, max 57,344). Their recommendation: E4M3 for weights and activations, E5M2 for gradients. On an H100, fp8 tensor-core math is 2× bf16 (1,979 vs 989 dense TFLOP/s).

Even E5M2's range is narrow, so fp8 always comes with scaling factors: before casting, divide a tensor by a scale chosen from its largest absolute value, so its values land in the format's sweet spot; multiply back after. The finer the groups that share one scale, the less an outlier hurts its neighbours:

Scaling granularityWho uses itIdea
One scale per tensorNVIDIA Transformer Engine on H100 (the scale is often inferred from a history of recent maximum values)Simple; one huge outlier squashes everything else toward 0
Per tile or blockDeepSeek-V3: 1 × 128 tiles for activations, 128 × 128 blocks for weights, E4M3 everywhereOutliers only affect their own block
Microscaling (MX) formatsOCP standard: MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4 with a shared power-of-two (E8M0) scale per block of 32Block scaling built into hardware
NVFP4NVIDIA Blackwell: 4-bit E2M1 values, an E4M3 scale per 16 values, plus one fp32 scale per tensorEven smaller blocks for 4-bit
🤔 Why this? fp8 training with scaling
  1. The problem before Even in bf16, the biggest models cost tens of millions of GPU-hours, and the matmuls dominate. Halving the bits again doubles tensor-core throughput and halves memory traffic.
  2. What people tried Integer (int8) training, which is hard because gradients span many orders of magnitude; fp8 with no scaling, which flushes or saturates too many values.
  3. Why this works Two formats split the job: E4M3's precision for forward values, E5M2's range for gradients. A per-tensor or per-block scale slides each tensor into the format's window, just like loss scaling did for fp16, but automatically and everywhere. DeepSeek-V3 went further and used E4M3 for all tensors, relying on fine-grained (1 × 128 and 128 × 128) scaling to tame outliers.
  4. What it costs, when it fails More engineering: computing scales, keeping sensitive layers (embeddings, output head, norms, optimizer) in higher precision, and guarding against rare outliers that cause loss spikes. It only helps on hardware with fp8 tensor cores (H100 and later).
  5. What refined it MX block formats and NVFP4 put the block scales into the hardware, pushing toward 4-bit matmuls. The same scale-and-round idea is the heart of quantization for inference (Lesson 10.4).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The standard recipe is torch.autocast: it runs matmuls and convolutions in the low-precision type and keeps sensitive ops (softmax, norms, losses) in fp32, while your weights and optimizer stay fp32. This ran on a laptop CPU with bf16:

import torch, torch.nn as nn

# What each format does to a tiny gradient, a normal one and a big activation
g = torch.tensor([1e-8, 1e-3, 70000.0])
for dt in [torch.float16, torch.bfloat16, torch.float8_e4m3fn, torch.float8_e5m2]:
    print(f"{str(dt):22s}", g.to(dt).float().tolist())

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(16, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 1))
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
x, y = torch.randn(64, 16), torch.randn(64, 1)
with torch.autocast(device_type="cpu", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
    out = model(x)
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(out.float(), y)
loss.backward()
opt.step()
print("output:", out.dtype, "| loss:", loss.dtype, "| weight:", model[0].weight.dtype, "| grad:", model[0].weight.grad.dtype)

# Output (torch 2.8, CPU):
# torch.float16          [0.0, 0.0010004043579101562, inf]
# torch.bfloat16         [1.0011717677116394e-08, 0.00099945068359375, 70144.0]
# torch.float8_e4m3fn    [0.0, 0.001953125, nan]

fp8 training on H100-class GPUs goes through libraries such as NVIDIA Transformer Engine or torchao's float8 training, which handle the scaling factors.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do we train in bf16 instead of fp16?” A strong answer:

  • Explains the bit split: bf16 has 8 exponent bits (fp32's range) and 7 mantissa bits; fp16 has 5 and 10.
  • Names fp16's failure: gradients under 6 × 10⁻⁸ underflow to 0 and values over 65,504 overflow, hence loss scaling and skipped steps.
  • Explains why reduced precision is fine: SGD is noisy; accumulations, softmax, norms and the loss stay in fp32; the fp32 master weights catch tiny updates.
  • Bonus: memory math (16 bytes/param with Adam), and fp8's E4M3/E5M2 split with per-block scaling.
🏭 In the real world
The recipe everyone still uses 🧪 Research · 2017 Micikevicius et al.: Mixed Precision Training

How: fp16 math, fp32 master weights, loss scaling. Tested on image classification, detection, speech, translation and language models.

Why this method: matched fp32 accuracy with about half the memory; SSD only trained with loss scaling (a factor of 8 was enough).

bf16 on TPUs 🏢 Google · 2019 BFloat16: the secret to high performance on Cloud TPUs

How: TPU matrix units multiply in bf16; the format was conceived at Google Brain.

Why this method: the same dynamic range as fp32 makes it close to a drop-in replacement, unlike fp16, which typically needs loss scaling.

Two 8-bit formats, one standard 🧪 Research · 2022 Micikevicius et al.: FP8 Formats for Deep Learning

How: defines E4M3 (max 448, no infinities) and E5M2 (IEEE-like, max 57,344).

Why this method: E4M3 for weights and activations, E5M2 for gradients; with scaling, fp8 training matched 16-bit results across the models they tested.

fp8 at frontier scale 🏢 DeepSeek · 2024 DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report

How: an fp8 mixed-precision framework with 1 × 128 activation tiles and 128 × 128 weight blocks, each with its own scale, using E4M3 for all tensors.

Why this method: fine-grained scaling contains outliers, so the higher-precision E4M3 can be used even for gradients; the report says this is the first validation of fp8 training on an extremely large-scale model.

🤯 Fun fact · bf16 is fp32 with the bottom half cut off

Write an fp32 number as 32 bits. The first 16 (sign, all 8 exponent bits, the top 7 mantissa bits) are exactly a bf16 number. That's why converting between them is so cheap, and why bf16 keeps fp32's range for free. Try it in the explorer: flip between the fp32 and bf16 bit strips for the same number and compare the first 16 bits.

Check yourself

1. A gradient is 1 × 10⁻⁹. fp16 rounds anything below about 3 × 10⁻⁸ (half its smallest step) to zero. What's the smallest power-of-two loss scale that keeps it alive?
You need 10⁻⁹ × S > 3 × 10⁻⁸, so S > 30: the smallest power of two is 32. S = 8 gives 8 × 10⁻⁹, still under the line. 65,536 also works, but the question asks for the smallest; dynamic scalers start high and back off only when something overflows.
2. You move an fp16 training job (with GradScaler) from V100s to A100s and switch autocast to bf16. What can you delete?
Loss scaling exists because fp16's range ends at 6 × 10⁻⁸. bf16's 8 exponent bits reach 10⁻⁴⁰ like fp32, so the scaler has nothing to rescue. The optimizer states and master weights still stay in fp32: bf16 has even less precision than fp16, so tiny updates would vanish.
3. To save memory, a teammate stores the weights only in bf16 (no fp32 copy). With a learning rate of 1e-5 the loss stops improving, though gradients look healthy. Why?
bf16 has about 2–3 significant digits: near a weight of 0.05 its steps are about 0.0002. An update of 1e-5 × gradient is far smaller than half a step, so it's rounded away every time. That's why mixed precision keeps fp32 master weights (or uses stochastic rounding). Underflow isn't the issue: bf16's range matches fp32.
📝 TL;DR
  • Exponent bits = range, mantissa bits = precision. fp16: 5/10 (range 6 × 10⁻⁸ to 65,504). bf16: 8/7 (fp32's range, ~2–3 digits). fp8: E4M3 (max 448) and E5M2 (max 57,344).
  • fp16 drops tiny gradients to 0 → loss scaling: multiply the loss by S, divide the gradients by S; dynamic scalers halve on overflow (torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")).
  • Tiny updates vanish in 16 bits (1.0 + 0.0001 = 1.0) → keep fp32 master weights and optimizer states: 16 bytes per parameter with Adam.
  • bf16 needs no loss scaling and is the default for training; use torch.autocast(device_type, dtype=torch.bfloat16).
  • fp8 doubles tensor-core throughput again, with per-tensor or per-block scales (DeepSeek-V3, MX formats, NVFP4).
