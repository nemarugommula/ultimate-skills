GPU basics: compute, memory and why matmul is king

A GPU can do about a thousand trillion multiplications a second, yet most of the operations in your network leave it idle. Learn the one ratio that tells you which operations are fast, which are slow, and what to do about it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A dense layer is a matrix multiplication (matmul): a batch of inputs times a weight matrix (Lesson 0.3, math 2.2).
  • Training runs a forward pass, a backward pass and an optimizer step, over and over (Lesson 1.3).
  • Attention builds an L × L table of scores for a sequence of L tokens (Lesson 6.1).
🎯 Hook

Take an NVIDIA H100, the workhorse training chip of 2023–2025. Give it two jobs on 16-bit numbers:

  • Job A: multiply two 8192 × 8192 matrices. That's 1.1 trillion floating-point operations (FLOPs).
  • Job B: add two vectors of 1 billion numbers. That's 1 billion FLOPs, a thousand times less work.

Using NVIDIA's own spec sheet, the best possible time for Job A is about 1.1 ms. For Job B it's about 1.8 ms. The job with a thousand times less math takes longer.

This is why engineers at every lab obsess over “memory-bound” vs “compute-bound”, why FlashAttention made GPT-2 training up to 3× faster without changing the math, and why your batch size and even your vocabulary size are rounded to multiples of 64. By the end you'll be able to predict which of any two operations is faster, with a calculator you build in your head.

Numbers first: a GPU is a stadium of simple workers

A laptop CPU has a handful of big, clever cores (8 to 24 is common). Each one is fast at a single task and very good at messy code with branches. A data-center GPU is built the other way round: an NVIDIA A100 has 108 streaming multiprocessors (SMs), each running many simple arithmetic lanes in lockstep, so thousands of multiplications happen at once.

The lanes are grouped in bundles of 32 called warps. All 32 lanes of a warp execute the same instruction at the same time, each on its own data. NVIDIA calls this SIMT (single instruction, multiple threads). It's perfect when every number gets the same treatment, like every cell of a matrix product. It hurts when lanes disagree: if half a warp takes an if branch and half the else, the warp runs both, one after the other.

🧠 Analogy: four professors vs a stadium of students

A CPU is four professors: each solves any problem fast, including tricky ones. A GPU is a stadium of 5,000 students who can each only do one multiplication at a time and must all follow the same instruction from the loudspeaker. Give them 5 million independent multiplications and the stadium wins by miles. Give them one long chain where each step needs the previous answer and the professors win, because 4,999 students sit idle.

🤔 Why this? GPUs for deep learning
  1. The problem before Neural nets are mostly matmuls: a 4096 × 4096 layer applied to 512 tokens is 17 billion FLOPs, for one layer, one step. A CPU core doing a few billion FLOPs a second needs seconds for that.
  2. What people tried Clusters of CPUs and hand-tuned math libraries. Then researchers started borrowing graphics cards built for video games: AlexNet (2012) trained for five to six days on two NVIDIA GTX 580 gaming GPUs with 3 GB each.
  3. Why this works Every output cell of a matmul is an independent dot product: exactly the “millions of identical small jobs” a GPU is built for. Modern GPUs add tensor cores: units that multiply a whole small tile of a matrix in one step. On an H100, tensor cores give about 989 trillion bf16 FLOPs per second (dense), versus 67 trillion for its ordinary fp32 units.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Branchy, sequential or tiny work leaves lanes idle. Each kernel launch costs a few microseconds. And the lanes can only compute as fast as memory feeds them numbers, which is the rest of this lesson.
  5. What refined it Tensor cores (first in NVIDIA's V100), lower-precision formats that double throughput (Lesson 10.2), kernel fusion (below) and compilers that do it automatically (Lesson 10.5). TPUs and other accelerators follow the same idea: a huge matmul engine fed by fast memory.

The catch: the workers are faster than the trucks

Numbers live in two kinds of memory. HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is big: 80 GB on an H100. On-chip SRAM (the SMs' shared memory, caches and registers) is tiny but about 10× faster. The FlashAttention paper gives the A100's numbers: 40–80 GB of HBM at 1.5–2.0 TB/s, and 192 KB of SRAM per SM (108 SMs) at around 19 TB/s.

Every operation must haul its inputs from HBM to the SMs and its outputs back. So each operation has two speed limits:

NVIDIA's headline numbers assume 2:4 structured sparsity (Lesson 10.4), which doubles throughput; dense math is half. Real kernels reach a fraction of either peak.

That last column is the most important number in this lesson. An H100 can do 295 FLOPs in the time it takes to fetch one byte. If your operation does fewer than 295 FLOPs per byte it moves, the math units wait for memory: the operation is memory-bound. If it does more, memory waits for the math: it's compute-bound.

💡 Big idea Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes moved. Compare it with the GPU's ratio (295 for an H100 in bf16). Below the ratio you're paying for trucks, above it you're paying for workers. Matmuls reuse every number many times, so they can climb above the line; elementwise operations touch each number once and never can.

Now the hook makes sense. Job A: 1.1 × 10¹² FLOPs ÷ 989 × 10¹² FLOP/s = 1.11 ms (moving its 0.4 GB takes only 0.12 ms). Job B: moving 6 GB at 3.35 TB/s = 1.79 ms, while its math alone would take 1 µs. A matmul of size n reuses each number it loads n times; an add uses it once.

The roofline: one picture for every operation

Plot speed against arithmetic intensity on log axes and you get a roofline (Williams, Waterman and Patterson, 2009): a slanted line on the left (memory bandwidth × intensity) that hits a flat roof (peak FLOP/s) at the ridge point. Every operation sits under the roof at its own intensity. To get faster, either move right (more reuse per byte) or buy a taller roof.

What you should see: the matmul crosses the ridge at n = 896 (intensity 299; n = 832 gives 277). On the H200, with faster memory, the ridge drops to 206 and n = 640 is enough. A 4096 × 4096 layer with one token runs at 0.3% of peak; you need a batch of 345 tokens before it's compute-bound. That is exactly why chat decoding (one new token per step) is memory-bound and why servers batch many users together (Lesson 10.5). Plain attention is stuck near intensity 64 at any length, because it writes and re-reads the L × L score table; tick fused and intensity grows as L/2, compute-bound from L = 1024. Decoding one token against a long cache stays at intensity ≈ 1 no matter what.

Why shapes come in multiples of 8 and 64

Tensor cores chew matrices in fixed-size tiles. NVIDIA's performance guide says fp16 matmuls are “most efficient with multiples of 8; on A100, multiples of 64”. A dimension of 50,257 leaves a ragged edge of partial tiles that wastes work or falls back to slower code. Karpathy's nanoGPT pads GPT-2's vocabulary of 50,257 up to 50,304, “padded up to nearest multiple of 64 for efficiency”. It costs 47 unused rows and makes the output layer faster.

Batch size matters for the same reason, and for intensity: a bigger batch reuses each loaded weight across more tokens (the layer row of the table). That's the hardware half of the batch-size story from Lesson 1.7.

Kernel fusion: stop shipping half-finished work

Each GPU operation is a kernel: a small program launched on the GPU. In plain (“eager”) PyTorch, y = gelu(x * 2 + 1) launches three kernels. Each one reads its input from HBM and writes its output back, so the intermediate results make a pointless round trip. A fused kernel does all three steps while the numbers sit in on-chip SRAM and writes only the final answer.

🤔 Why this? Kernel fusion
  1. The problem before Elementwise ops, normalizations, activations, dropout and softmax have intensity well under 10 (NVIDIA lists ReLU at 0.25 FLOPs per byte, layer norm under 10). Run as separate kernels, a chain of k of them moves the data 2k times. In transformers these ops can take a large share of the time despite doing a tiny share of the FLOPs.
  2. What people tried Hand-written fused CUDA kernels for the hottest patterns (fused Adam, fused LayerNorm, fused bias + GELU) inside libraries like Apex and Megatron.
  3. Why this works Time for a memory-bound op ≈ bytes ÷ bandwidth. Fusing x*2, +1 and gelu drops traffic from 12 to 4 bytes per number: 12 GB → 4 GB for a billion numbers, 3.6 ms → 1.2 ms. FlashAttention is fusion plus tiling for attention: by never writing the L × L scores to HBM it reported a 3× speed-up training GPT-2 (sequence length 1K) with exactly the same result.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Hand-fused kernels are hard to write, debug and keep up to date; each new architecture needs new ones. Fusion only helps memory-bound chains: a big matmul is already compute-bound.
  5. What refined it Compilers that fuse automatically: torch.compile with TorchInductor generates fused Triton kernels on GPUs and C++ on CPUs (Lesson 10.5); XLA does the same for JAX and TPUs. FlashAttention lives on as F.scaled_dot_product_attention (Lesson 6.9).

MFU: how much of the GPU are you really using?

Training throughput is reported as MFU (model FLOPs utilization): the FLOPs your model mathematically needs per second, divided by the hardware's peak. The PaLM paper (2022) popularized it. A dense transformer needs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token for a training step (2 forward, 4 backward; Lesson 6.8).

✏️ Worked example: MFU of a 7B-parameter model

One H100 processes 9,000 tokens per second for a 7-billion-parameter model.

6 × 7 × 10⁹ × 9,000 = 3.78 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s → 378 ÷ 989 = 38% MFU

That's a good number. Real runs: PaLM 540B reached 46.2% MFU on 6,144 TPU v4 chips; Llama 3 405B reported 38–43% bf16 MFU on 8,192–16,384 H100s. The missing 55–60% goes to memory-bound ops, communication between GPUs (Lesson 10.3), pipeline bubbles and recomputation.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You can feel the roofline even on a laptop CPU: a matmul does thousands of times more math than an add of the same matrices, but takes only tens of times longer.

import torch, time

def bench(fn, reps=10):
    fn()                                   # warm-up
    t = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(reps):
        fn()
    return (time.perf_counter() - t) / reps

n = 2048
a, b = torch.randn(n, n), torch.randn(n, n)
t_mm = bench(lambda: a @ b)               # 2·n³ FLOPs, touches 3·n² numbers
t_add = bench(lambda: a + b)              # n² FLOPs, touches 3·n² numbers
print(f"matmul: {2 * n**3 / t_mm / 1e9:7.1f} GFLOP/s")
print(f"add:    {n**2 / t_add / 1e9:7.1f} GFLOP/s")
print(f"matmul does {2 * n:,}x more math, but takes only {t_mm / t_add:.0f}x longer")

# One run on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8); your numbers will differ:
# matmul:   918.7 GFLOP/s
# add:        7.8 GFLOP/s
# matmul does 4,096x more math, but takes only 35x longer

On a GPU, the habits that follow from this lesson (not run here: no GPU on this laptop):

torch.set_float32_matmul_precision("high")   # let fp32 matmuls use TF32 tensor cores
props = torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0)    # name, total memory, SM count
out = torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)  # fused attention

Gotcha: GPU calls are asynchronous. Time them with torch.cuda.synchronize() before reading the clock (or torch.cuda.Event), or you'll measure only the launch. For a real breakdown use torch.profiler.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why is LLM token generation memory-bound, and what would you do about it?” A strong answer:

  • Defines arithmetic intensity and the GPU's FLOPs-per-byte ratio (≈ 300 for an H100 in bf16).
  • Explains that decoding one token per sequence reads every weight once for ~2 FLOPs per weight: intensity ≈ batch size, far below the ridge.
  • Lists fixes that raise intensity or shrink bytes: batch many requests, quantize weights to 8 or 4 bits, shrink the KV cache (GQA), speculative decoding.
  • Knows the contrast: training and prompt processing (prefill) run big matmuls and are compute-bound, so there precision and MFU matter instead.
🏭 In the real world
FlashAttention: attention made memory-aware 🧪 Research · 2022 Dao et al.: FlashAttention

How: computes attention in tiles that fit in SRAM and never writes the L × L score matrix to HBM.

Why this method: attention was memory-bound, not compute-bound; cutting HBM traffic gave 3× faster GPT-2 training (sequence length 1K) and a 15% end-to-end speed-up on BERT-large, with exact results.

MFU as the scoreboard of a training run 🏢 Google · 2022 Chowdhery et al.: PaLM

How: defined model FLOPs utilization and reported 46.2% for PaLM 540B on 6,144 TPU v4 chips (vs 21.3% they list for GPT-3).

Why this method: hardware utilization can be inflated by recomputation; MFU counts only the FLOPs the model truly needs, so runs on different hardware compare fairly.

Llama 3 405B at 38–43% MFU 🏢 Meta · 2024 The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: pre-trained on up to 16,384 H100s at 380–430 TFLOPs per GPU in bf16.

Why this method: every point of MFU on a cluster that size is worth thousands of GPU-hours, so the paper reports it per training stage.

Tensor-core-friendly shapes 🏢 NVIDIA Matrix Multiplication Background User's Guide

How: tables of which dimensions use tensor cores most efficiently, and worked intensity examples (an 8192³ GEMM: 2,730 FLOPs per byte, compute-bound).

Why this method: the cheapest speed-up in deep learning is often changing a 50,257 into a 50,304.

🤯 Fun fact · the 2,730 that NVIDIA computed too

Our n/3 rule gives an 8192 × 8192 matmul an intensity of 8192 ÷ 3 ≈ 2,731 FLOPs per byte. NVIDIA's performance guide works the same example and gets 2,730. Shrink one side to 128 (an 8192 × 128 × 8192 product) and their number drops to 124: memory-bound on the older V100. Same formula, same answer, no GPU needed.

Check yourself

1. On an H100 in bf16 (989 TFLOP/s, 3.35 TB/s), you multiply a 512 × 512 matrix by a 512 × 512 matrix. Using intensity ≈ n / 3, is it compute- or memory-bound?
The ridge is 989 ÷ 3.35 ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte. A square matmul has intensity n/3, so you need n ≥ 886 (896 in multiples of 64) before the math units become the bottleneck. Small matmuls are memory-bound too, which is one reason tiny models leave big GPUs idle.
2. Your profiler shows a transformer step where LayerNorm, GELU, dropout and residual adds take 40% of the time but under 1% of the FLOPs. What's the best first move?
These ops have intensity well under 10, so their time is bytes ÷ bandwidth. More FLOP/s or lower-precision matmuls don't touch them. Fusion keeps intermediates in on-chip SRAM, so a chain of k ops moves the data once instead of 2k times.
3. A chatbot serves one user at a time. Each new token multiplies a 4096-vector by 4096 × 4096 weight matrices. Which change raises GPU utilization the most?
With one token, intensity is ≈ 1 FLOP per byte: the GPU spends almost all its time loading weights. Batching B requests reuses each weight B times, so intensity ≈ B (compute-bound from about 345 for this layer on an H100). More FLOP/s doesn't help a memory-bound op; more bandwidth or fewer bytes (quantization) would.
📝 TL;DR
  • A GPU is thousands of simple lanes in warps of 32 running the same instruction (SIMT), plus tensor cores that multiply small matrix tiles. Great for matmuls; bad at branchy or sequential work.
  • Two speed limits: peak FLOP/s and memory bandwidth. Their ratio (≈ 295 FLOPs per byte for an H100 in bf16) is the ridge point of the roofline.
  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes. Matmuls (n/3, or ≈ batch size for a layer) can be compute-bound; elementwise ops (≈ 0.17) and decoding (≈ 1) are memory-bound.
  • Speed up memory-bound work by moving fewer bytes: fusion, FlashAttention, batching, quantization. Speed up compute-bound work with lower precision and tensor-core shapes (multiples of 8/64).
  • MFU = useful model FLOPs ÷ peak. 40% on a big cluster is excellent (PaLM 46%, Llama 3 405B 38–43%).
