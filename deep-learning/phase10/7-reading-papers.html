Reading deep learning papers

Thousands of deep-learning papers appear every month, and you can read maybe five. Learn the three-pass method, what to pull out of each section, the red flags that should make you sceptical, and how to keep up without drowning.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Residual connections: y = F(x) + x, the gradient highway that made 100+ layers trainable (Lesson 2.2).
  • Seed noise, confidence intervals and ablations (Lesson 10.6).
  • FLOPs and compute budgets: 6 · N · D for training (Lessons 6.8, 10.1).
🎯 Hook

In April 2025, Nature analysed citations across five databases and named the most-cited paper of the twenty-first century, across all of science. It wasn't about genes, cancer or climate. It was “Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition” (He, Zhang, Ren and Sun, 2015): the ResNet paper you met in Lesson 2.2.

Its central idea fits on one line, y = F(x) + x. Yet the paper itself is a dense mix of claims, tables, training details and ablations, and most readers only ever see its abstract.

How do you read a paper like that efficiently, know what to extract from each section, and spot when a paper's claims are weaker than its abstract? By the end you'll have taken ResNet apart section by section and played a red-flag detection game.

Numbers first: a reading budget

S. Keshav's short note “How to Read a Paper” proposes three passes, each deeper and rarer than the last:

PassTime (Keshav)ReadYou come away with
1. Bird's-eyeabout 5–10 minutestitle, abstract, introduction, section and sub-section headings, conclusions; glance at the referencesthe five Cs: Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity. Often: stop here.
2. Contentup to about an houreverything, with care, but skip proofs; study figures, tables and axes; mark unread referencesyou can summarize the main thrust, with evidence, to someone else
3. Re-implementabout 4–5 hours for beginners, about 1 hour with experienceeverything, making the same assumptions as the authors and “virtually re-implementing” the workyou can rebuild the paper from memory and name its hidden assumptions and weak points
🧠 Analogy: house hunting

Pass 1 is scrolling listings: photos, price, neighbourhood, 30 seconds each, most rejected. Pass 2 is the viewing: you walk every room and open the cupboards. Pass 3 is the structural survey before you buy: you check the foundations the seller didn't mention. Nobody surveys every house in the city.

Pick a section

Start with the abstract, like a first pass would.

What you should see: the abstract and introduction give you Category, Contributions and most of Context in five minutes. The claim that depth itself (not overfitting) was the problem rests on the training-error curves: the deeper plain network has higher training error, which overfitting can't explain. ResNet's ablation of shortcut types (options A/B/C) and its 1202-layer CIFAR run, which it says overfits, are the honest parts most summaries skip.

🤔 Why this? Ablations
  1. The problem before A paper adds four changes and reports +3 points. Which change mattered? Maybe one did all the work and three are noise, or the gain came from a longer training schedule nobody mentioned.
  2. What people tried Trusting the abstract; comparing against numbers copied from older papers with different setups (Melis et al. showed how misleading that can be, Lesson 10.6).
  3. Why this works Remove or swap one component at a time, keeping everything else fixed, and measure each row with the same budget and several seeds. ResNet does this twice: plain vs residual networks of identical depth, width and parameters (so the shortcut is the only difference), and three shortcut variants (A: zero-padding, B: projections only when shapes change, C: all projections) showing that projections aren't essential.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each row is a training run, so ablations of big models are done small-scale and may not transfer. Components can interact (removing two at once may hurt more than the sum). An ablation without variance estimates can be as misleading as the headline number.
  5. What refined it Ablation tables are now expected by reviewers; the NeurIPS paper checklist asks for error bars and compute resources for every experiment. Scaling-law studies (Lesson 6.8) are ablations across model sizes.

🤔 Why this? The three-pass method
  1. The problem before Reading every paper front to back takes hours each and buries you; skimming only abstracts leaves you trusting claims you never checked.
  2. What people tried Reading lists, summaries by others, social-media threads (fast, but you inherit someone else's mistakes).
  3. Why this works Each pass has a stopping point. Pass 1 (5–10 minutes) is enough to decide whether a paper deserves an hour; pass 2 lets you explain it; pass 3 is only for the few papers you'll build on. Keshav notes that most reviewers and readers make only one pass, which is also advice for writing your own papers: make pass 1 count.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Discipline: it's tempting to dive into the method before knowing whether the results hold up. For a brand-new subfield, pass 1 may fail because you lack context; read a survey first.
  5. What refined it Keshav's literature-survey recipe (find recent papers, then the shared citations, then the key authors and venues); reproducing results yourself (RL 9.2).

Reproducing a result

The fastest way to really understand a paper is to reproduce one of its numbers. Start with the smallest experiment the paper reports (ResNet's CIFAR-10 results, not ImageNet). Match the setup exactly first (their learning rate, schedule, batch size, augmentation: ResNet trains with SGD, momentum 0.9, weight decay 0.0001, learning rate 0.1 divided by 10 on plateaus), then change one thing at a time. When your number differs, the gap is information: an unstated detail, a bug, or a fragile result. ResNet reported its CIFAR ResNet-110 as “best (mean ± std)” over 5 runs, so you know how close counts as success.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A paper's numbers are claims you can check. Two quick checks from this course, run on a laptop: count a model's parameters, and estimate training compute from the paper's own numbers with 6 · N · D.

import torch, torchvision     # torchvision ships ResNet definitions (no weights downloaded here)

model = torchvision.models.resnet50(weights=None)
n = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"ResNet-50 parameters: {n / 1e6:.1f} M")

# Back-of-envelope training compute for a paper's LLM: 6 × params × tokens
params, tokens = 7e9, 2e12
print(f"≈ {6 * params * tokens:.1e} FLOPs")

# Output (torch 2.8, torchvision 0.23, CPU):
# ResNet-50 parameters: 25.6 M
# ≈ 8.4e+22 FLOPs

If a paper's reported parameter count or compute doesn't match numbers like these, ask why before trusting its comparisons.

🎤 Interview angle

“Tell me about a paper you read recently. Would you use its method?” A strong answer:

  • States the problem, the key idea in one sentence and the main evidence (with numbers and baselines).
  • Names a weakness: missing ablation, single seeds, compute not matched, data limitations.
  • Connects to practice: what it would cost to try in your setting and how you'd test it (a small reproduction, an ablation on your data).
  • Bonus: what the OpenReview discussion or follow-up work said about it.

🤯 Fun fact · the deepest network lost

The ResNet paper trained a 1,202-layer network on CIFAR-10. It optimized fine (similar training error) but tested worse than the 110-layer one, and the authors say so plainly: “We argue that this is because of overfitting.” A paper that reports its own failure modes is a paper you can trust more.

Check yourself

1. Using Keshav's times (about 10 minutes for pass 1, about an hour for pass 2), how long do pass 1 on 24 papers plus pass 2 on 3 of them take?
24 × 10 min = 240 min = 4 hours, plus 3 × 1 hour = 3 hours: about 7 hours. That's why pass 1 must be ruthless: most papers should stop there.
2. A paper's new optimizer beats AdamW by 0.4 points. The baseline used default hyperparameters; the new method was tuned with 100 trials. One seed each. What's the strongest criticism?
A fair comparison gives every method the same tuning budget and reports several seeds with error bars. Melis et al. and Lucic et al. (Lesson 10.6) showed that many “improvements” vanish under equal tuning. 0.4 points is well within typical seed spread.
3. You want evidence that a 56-layer plain network fails because of optimization, not overfitting. Which result settles it?
Overfitting means low training error and high test error. If the deeper network can't even fit the training set as well as the shallower one (which it could, in principle, by copying it and adding identity layers), the problem is optimization. That's ResNet's Figure 1 argument.
📝 TL;DR
  • Three passes (Keshav): 5–10 min bird's-eye (five Cs), ~1 h content, hours to virtually re-implement. Most papers stop at pass 1.
  • Extract: the scoped claim, the baselines (re-run? equally tuned?), variance (seeds, error bars), ablations, compute and data.
  • Red flags: single seeds, unequal tuning, stale baselines, unmatched compute, test-set selection, no ablation, no code or key details.
  • Reproduce the smallest experiment first, matching the setup exactly; gaps are information.
  • Stay current with arXiv, Hugging Face Papers (Papers with Code redirects there since July 2025), OpenReview reviews and proceedings; one deep read a week beats fifty skims.
