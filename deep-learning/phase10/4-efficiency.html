Phase 10 · Lesson 4

Efficiency: quantization, pruning, distillation

Three ways to make a trained model smaller and faster: store its numbers in fewer bits, delete the weights it doesn't need, or teach a small model to imitate it. Each one has a sweet spot and a cliff.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • LLM decoding is memory-bound: speed ≈ bytes of weights read per token ÷ bandwidth (Lesson 10.1).
  • Floats trade range for precision; scaling factors move a tensor into a format's sweet spot (Lesson 10.2).
  • Softmax with a temperature T: divide the logits by T before softmax; higher T means flatter probabilities (Lesson 0.6, math 8.2).
🎯 Hook

Fine-tuning a 65-billion-parameter model normally needs more than 780 GB of GPU memory. In 2023 the QLoRA paper did it on a single 48 GB GPU, “while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance”, by storing the frozen model in a new 4-bit format.

A year earlier, GPTQ compressed 175B-parameter GPT models to 3 or 4 bits per weight in about four GPU-hours with negligible accuracy loss. And in 2019, DistilBERT made BERT 40% smaller and 60% faster while keeping 97% of its language-understanding scores.

Every phone keyboard, on-device photo search and cheap LLM API you use relies on tricks like these. How can you throw away 75% of the bits, or half the weights, and lose almost nothing? And when do you lose everything?

Because generating one token reads every weight once (Lesson 10.1), halving the bytes roughly halves the time per token, as long as the kernels can unpack the small numbers fast. The challenge is doing it without destroying the model.

1. Quantization: round to a small grid

To store a weight in 8 bits, pick a scale s and store the integer q = round(w / s), clamped to −128…127. To use it, multiply back: w ≈ q · s. With symmetric quantization, s = max |w| ÷ 127, so the biggest weight maps to 127. (Asymmetric quantization adds a zero-point offset so a range like 0…6 uses all 256 levels; it's common for activations after ReLU.)

✏️ Worked example: four weights to int8 and int4

Weights [0.50, −0.12, 0.03, −0.81]. Largest magnitude 0.81.

int8 (−127…127)0.81 / 127 = 0.00638[78, −19, 5, −127][0.498, −0.121, 0.032, −0.810]0.002

Now imagine one weight in the tensor is 4.0 instead. The int4 scale becomes 0.57, and every weight smaller than 0.29 rounds to zero. One outlier ruins its whole group. That's the central problem of quantization.

🧠 Analogy: one ruler for the whole class photo

Measure everyone in a class with a ruler marked only every 10 cm and the kids blur together. Now add the basketball coach to the photo and re-mark the ruler to reach his height: the kids all read “0 to 1 mark”. The fix is to give each row of kids its own ruler (per-channel), or every small group its own ruler (per-group).

🤔 Why this? Quantization (int8 / int4, per-channel and per-group)
  1. The problem before A 70B model in bf16 is 140 GB, and decoding speed is limited by reading those bytes. Phones and laptops have a few GB to spare.
  2. What people tried Smaller models (worse quality); per-tensor int8 for CNNs, which worked for many vision models. Jacob et al. (Google, 2017) showed integer-only inference on phones and training with simulated quantization to recover accuracy.
  3. Why this works Trained weights are mostly bell-shaped and the network tolerates small noise, so 256 levels per channel lose very little (our playground: under 1%). Finer groups give each slice its own ruler. For LLMs, LLM.int8() keeps the rare outlier feature dimensions (which appear in all layers from about 6.7B parameters) in 16-bit; GPTQ rounds weights one at a time and nudges the not-yet-rounded ones to cancel the error, using second-order (curvature) information; AWQ finds the ~1% of salient weight channels from activation statistics and scales them up before rounding; SmoothQuant moves activation outliers into the weights so both can be int8.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Below 4 bits quality drops fast; outliers, small models and activations are harder than big models' weights. Speed-ups need kernels for the format (otherwise you just dequantize and save only memory). Post-training quantization (PTQ) needs a small calibration set; quantization-aware training (QAT) recovers more accuracy but costs a training run.
  5. What refined it 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4, QLoRA), whose levels are spaced for normally distributed weights, plus “double quantization” of the scales (about 0.37 bits per parameter saved); hardware block formats (MXFP4, NVFP4, Lesson 10.2); and quantized LLM serving in practice (AI Engineer 7.4).

2. Pruning: delete the weights that don't matter

Many weights are close to zero. Magnitude pruning zeroes the smallest ones and (usually) fine-tunes the rest to recover. The question is which pattern of zeros, because hardware only runs faster for some patterns:

  • Unstructured: any weights can be zero. Most accurate for a given sparsity, but a dense GPU kernel still multiplies the zeros. Saves storage (with sparse formats), rarely time.
  • Structured: remove whole output channels, attention heads or layers. The matmul literally gets smaller, so it's faster everywhere, but it hurts accuracy more.
  • 2:4 semi-structured: in every group of 4 consecutive weights, exactly 2 are zero. NVIDIA's Sparse Tensor Cores (A100 onward) skip them: twice the math throughput of dense, with accuracy kept by their retraining workflow.

🤔 Why this? Structured and 2:4 pruning
  1. The problem before Unstructured pruning can remove 90% of weights (Han et al.'s Deep Compression pipeline pruned AlexNet's connections 9× and cut its storage 35×, from 240 MB to 6.9 MB, with no accuracy loss), but GPUs run dense matmuls: scattered zeros save storage and little time.
  2. What people tried Sparse matrix kernels, which only win at very high sparsity and lose arithmetic intensity (Lesson 10.1).
  3. Why this works Removing whole channels or heads leaves a smaller dense model, which every library speeds up. The 2:4 pattern is a hardware deal: a fixed, regular pattern the Sparse Tensor Cores skip, giving 2× math throughput while NVIDIA's workflow (prune, then retrain) kept accuracy across many tasks.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Structured pruning hurts accuracy more per removed weight; 2:4 is capped at exactly 50% and needs retraining and supported kernels; savings in matmuls don't shrink memory-bound parts. For LLMs, quantization usually gives more speed per point of quality lost.
  5. What refined it The lottery ticket hypothesis (below) explains why sparse subnetworks can train well; one-shot LLM pruning methods; and distillation to recover accuracy after structured pruning.
🧪 The lottery ticket hypothesis

Frankle and Carbin (2019) pruned trained networks, then reset the surviving weights to their original initial values and retrained only that sparse subnetwork. It trained as well as the full network, often faster. They consistently found such “winning tickets” at 10–20% (or less) of the original size for the MNIST and CIFAR-10 networks they studied. Give the same sparse network fresh random initial values and it learns slower and ends up far worse: the lucky initialization matters.

3. Distillation: a small student learns from a big teacher

The third trick changes the model's size directly: train a small student to imitate a big teacher. The key insight (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015) is to imitate the teacher's full probability list, not just its top answer. Their example: an image of a BMW has only a tiny chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that's still many times more likely than mistaking it for a carrot. Those tiny ratios encode what the teacher knows about which classes are similar, knowledge nicknamed “dark knowledge”.

At temperature 1 those small probabilities are nearly invisible: they are so close to zero that they barely affect the loss. Raising the temperature T softens the distribution and lets them speak.

What you should see: at T = 1 the non-cat classes share only 6%, almost nothing for the student to learn from beyond the hard label. At T = 4 they carry 53%: dog 22%, fox 17%, and the vehicles and banana about 3–6% each. The teacher is now saying “cats look like dogs and foxes, not like trucks”. At T = 20 everything flattens toward 1/6 and the signal fades again. The raw KL also shrinks as T grows (gradients scale as 1/T²), which is why Hinton et al. multiply the soft loss by T².

🤔 Why this? Knowledge distillation with soft targets
  1. The problem before The best models are huge ensembles or giant networks, too slow to serve. Training a small model on the same hard labels gives a worse model: a one-hot label carries about one number of information per example.
  2. What people tried Caruana and colleagues compressed ensembles into one model by matching its logits; or just training smaller models longer.
  3. Why this works The teacher's soft probabilities give the student a rich target for every example: which wrong answers are “nearly right”. Raising T reveals those small probabilities; multiplying the soft loss by T² keeps its gradient on the same scale as the hard loss. DistilBERT applied it during pre-training: 40% fewer parameters, 60% faster, 97% of BERT's GLUE score.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You need the teacher (and its forward passes) during training. A student far smaller than its teacher can't absorb everything. T and α are extra hyperparameters to tune.
  5. What refined it Feature and attention-map distillation (match hidden states), self-distillation, and for LLMs, training small models on a big model's generated text or full token distributions (AI Engineer 7.2).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Quantization, pruning and a distillation loss by hand (ran on a laptop CPU in under a second):

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
import torch.nn.utils.prune as prune

torch.manual_seed(0)
W = torch.randn(256, 512) * torch.linspace(0.01, 0.3, 256)[:, None]   # rows with very different scales
W[3, 7] = 4.0                                                            # one outlier weight

def fake_quant(w, bits, dim=None):
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    amax = w.abs().amax() if dim is None else w.abs().amax(dim=dim, keepdim=True)
    scale = amax / qmax
    return (w / scale).round().clamp(-qmax - 1, qmax) * scale

def rel_err(w, q):   # average over output channels of ||w - q|| / ||w||
    return ((w - q).norm(dim=1) / w.norm(dim=1)).mean().item()

for bits in (8, 4):
    print(f"int{bits}  per-tensor: {rel_err(W, fake_quant(W, bits)):.3f}   "
          f"per-channel: {rel_err(W, fake_quant(W, bits, dim=1)):.3f}")

lin = nn.Linear(512, 256)
prune.l1_unstructured(lin, name="weight", amount=0.5)       # zero the 50% smallest |w|
print("sparsity:", (lin.weight == 0).float().mean().item())

def kd_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=4.0, alpha=0.5):
    soft = F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1),
                    F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1), reduction="batchmean") * T * T
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard

# Output (torch 2.8, CPU):
# int8  per-tensor: 0.105   per-channel: 0.008
# int4  per-tensor: 0.844   per-channel: 0.135
# sparsity: 0.5

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you cut the serving cost of a 13B model by 4×?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from the bottleneck: decoding is memory-bound, so bytes per weight matter most: 4-bit weight-only quantization (GPTQ/AWQ, group size ~128) gives ~4× less memory traffic.
  • Explains scales, per-channel/per-group, and why outliers break per-tensor schemes; mentions checking quality on the real eval set, not just perplexity.
  • Adds batching and KV-cache savings (Lessons 10.5, 6.9); considers distilling into a smaller model if quality allows.
  • Knows pruning's catch: unstructured zeros don't speed up dense GPU kernels; 2:4 or structured do.
🏭 In the real world
Fine-tune 65B on one GPU 🧪 Research · 2023 Dettmers et al.: QLoRA

How: a frozen base in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) with double-quantized scales; gradients flow into small 16-bit LoRA adapters; paged optimizers absorb memory spikes.

Why this method: memory falls from over 780 GB to under 48 GB with no loss versus 16-bit fine-tuning in their experiments.

175B weights at 3–4 bits 🧪 Research · 2022 Frantar et al.: GPTQ

How: one-shot post-training quantization using approximate second-order information, layer by layer.

Why this method: about four GPU-hours for a 175B model with negligible accuracy loss; no retraining.

Protect the 1% that matters 🧪 Research · 2023 Lin et al.: AWQ

How: uses activation statistics to find salient weight channels and scales them before 4-bit rounding.

Why this method: protecting only about 1% of weights greatly reduces quantization error, with a hardware-friendly uniform format.

A smaller, faster BERT 🏢 Hugging Face · 2019 Sanh et al.: DistilBERT

How: distillation during pre-training with a triple loss (language modelling, distillation, cosine distance between hidden states).

Why this method: 40% fewer parameters and 60% faster while retaining 97% of BERT's language understanding (GLUE).

2:4 sparsity in hardware 🏢 NVIDIA · 2021 Mishra et al.: Accelerating Sparse Deep Neural Networks

How: Sparse Tensor Cores in the Ampere architecture skip the zeros of a 2-out-of-4 pattern.

Why this method: twice the math throughput of dense matrix units, with a prune-and-retrain workflow that maintains accuracy.

🤯 Fun fact · outliers appear at a size threshold

Dettmers et al. found that transformers grow a few huge-magnitude “outlier features” in their hidden states, and that at around 6.7B parameters these suddenly appear in all layers. Plain 8-bit quantization works below that size and falls apart above it. That observation is why LLM.int8() handles those few dimensions in 16-bit, and why later methods (SmoothQuant, AWQ) are all, at heart, about outliers.

Check yourself

1. You quantize a weight group symmetrically to int4 (levels −7…7). Its largest magnitude is 1.4. What is the scale, and what does a weight of 0.08 become?
Scale = max |w| / 7 = 1.4 / 7 = 0.2. Then 0.08 / 0.2 = 0.4, which rounds to 0. Small weights in a group with a large one get wiped out, which is why groups are kept small (64–128 weights) and outliers get special handling.
2. You prune 60% of a model's weights (unstructured, smallest magnitude), fine-tune, and accuracy is unchanged. On your GPU the model runs at exactly the same speed. Why?
Scattered zeros are stored in the same dense matrix, so the matmul does the same work. To get speed you need a pattern the hardware or library exploits: removing whole channels/heads (a smaller dense matrix) or the 2:4 pattern that sparse tensor cores skip.
3. When distilling, your student learns no better than with hard labels. You used T = 1. What's the most likely fix?
At T = 1 a confident teacher puts ~94% on the top class, so its soft targets are almost the hard label. Higher T reveals the “dark knowledge” in the other classes. Because the soft-loss gradients shrink as 1/T², multiply by T² to keep the balance with the hard loss.
📝 TL;DR
  • Quantization: q = round(w / s), w ≈ q·s. int8 is nearly free; int4 needs per-group scales. Outliers stretch the scale and wipe out small weights: per-channel/per-group, LLM.int8, GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, NF4 all fight that.
  • PTQ is quick (calibrate scales); QAT simulates rounding during training for the last points of accuracy.
  • Pruning: unstructured is accurate but rarely faster; structured (channels/heads) and 2:4 (2× on sparse tensor cores) give real speed. Lottery tickets: sparse subnetworks at 10–20% size can train from their original init.
  • Distillation: a student matches the teacher's softmax at temperature T (soft loss × T²) plus the hard label. DistilBERT: 40% smaller, 60% faster, 97% of BERT.
  • For LLM serving, quantize first (bytes are speed); for big speed-ups with a training budget, distill.
