Phase 5 · Lesson 1

Word embeddings: word2vec and GloVe

How do you turn the word “cat” into numbers that know a cat is like a dog and nothing like a car? Not by asking anyone. You read billions of sentences and let each word be described by the company it keeps.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A vector is a list of numbers; the dot product and cosine similarity measure how much two vectors point the same way (math 1.3, math 1.5).
  • Sigmoid σ(z) squashes any score into a 0–1 “probability of yes” (Lesson 0.4).
  • Training = follow the gradient of a loss downhill (math 3.4).
🎯 Hook

In 2013 a team at Google trained a tiny network to do one boring thing: guess which words appear near which other words. Nobody told it what any word meant. Afterwards they took the learned vectors and computed vector(“King”) − vector(“Man”) + vector(“Woman”). The closest word to the result was Queen.

Their paper adds that it “takes less than a day to learn high quality word vectors from a 1.6 billion words data set”. The released Google News vectors hold 300 numbers for each of 3 million words and phrases, and their descendants sit at the bottom of every language model you use today: the first layer of a chatbot is still a table of word (token) vectors.

How can “guess the neighbour” produce vectors that do analogies? And why did that famous result turn out to be partly a trick? You'll train a mini word2vec live, do vector arithmetic yourself, and catch the trick in the act.

Numbers first: why “one-hot” fails

A network only eats numbers. The obvious way to feed it a word: give every word in the vocabulary a slot, and send a vector of zeros with a single 1 in that word's slot. This is a one-hot vector.

vocab = [cat, dog, car] → cat = [1, 0, 0] · dog = [0, 1, 0] · car = [0, 0, 1]

Two things go wrong, and both get worse with size:

ProblemNumbersWhy it hurts
Every word is equally far from every othercos(cat, dog) = cos(cat, car) = 0What the net learns about “dog” teaches it nothing about “puppy”. Every word starts from zero knowledge.
Huge, almost-empty vectors3,000,000 slots, one of them 1A first layer with 256 units would need 3M × 256 = 768 million weights just to read one word.

The fix: give every word a short dense vector (say 300 numbers, all of them used) and learn those numbers so that similar words get similar vectors. That vector is the word's embedding, and it's a set of trainable weights.

🤔 Why this? Dense, learned word embeddings
  1. The problem before One-hot (or bag-of-words) vectors make every pair of words orthogonal: cos = 0 for “cat”/“kitten” and for “cat”/“carburettor”. With a 3-million-word vocabulary each vector is 3 million long, and the model must learn about every word separately from its own rare examples.
  2. What people tried Hand-built resources like WordNet (synonym lists written by linguists), and counting co-occurrences then compressing the giant count matrix (latent semantic analysis, 1990s).
  3. Why this works A dense vector of ~100–300 numbers lets similarity be a matter of degree: if “kitten” lands near “cat”, everything learned for “cat” partly transfers. In code it's just a table lookup: row 17 of a V × d matrix. Multiplying a one-hot vector by that matrix gives exactly the same row, so the embedding layer is a linear layer on one-hot input, minus 99.99% of the wasted work.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The table is V × d weights (3M × 300 = 900M numbers ≈ 3.6 GB in float32 for Google News). Words never seen in training have no vector, and rare words get poor ones. Each word gets one vector, so “bank” (river) and “bank” (money) are forced to share.
  5. What refined it Subword pieces so unseen words can be built from parts (fastText; tokenization in Lesson 6.6), and contextual embeddings that change with the sentence (end of this lesson, then Phase 6).

The idea that makes it learnable

We want “cat” near “dog”. But where do the numbers come from? From a 1950s idea in linguistics, the distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. The linguist J. R. Firth put it as “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (1957).

Look at the contexts: “the ___ drinks milk”, “the ___ sleeps on the sofa”. Cat, dog and kitten all fit. Car doesn't. You never need a definition: the blanks a word can fill are its meaning, for a computer's purposes.

💡 Big idea Invent a fake prediction task that can only be solved by knowing which words share contexts, train on it, and then throw the task away and keep the weights. The embedding is the side effect. (Modern language models are the same trick at a vastly bigger scale: predict the next token, keep what was learned.)
🧠 Analogy: guessing people from their friends

You don't know two strangers at a party. But both hang out with the same five people, at the same bar, on the same nights. You'd bet they have a lot in common. word2vec makes that bet for every word, billions of times, and nudges the vectors of words with the same “friends” towards each other.

word2vec: skip-gram and CBOW

Mikolov and colleagues at Google (2013) proposed two fake tasks, both using a window of a few words around each position:

ModelFake taskExample, window = 2, sentence “the cat drinks warm milk”
Skip-gramGiven the centre word, predict each word around itdrinks → the, cat, warm, milk (4 training pairs)
CBOW (continuous bag of words)Given the surrounding words (averaged), predict the centrethe, cat, warm, milk → drinks (1 example)

Each word has two vectors: an input vector used when it's the centre, and a context vector used when it's a neighbour. The model's score for “does c appear near w?” is simply their dot product w·c. The word2vec docs sum it up: skip-gram is slower and better for infrequent words; CBOW is fast. Both are one layer deep: there is no hidden nonlinearity at all.

The expensive part, and the trick

“Predict the neighbour” naively means a softmax over the whole vocabulary (Lesson 0.6, math 8.2): to score one pair you compute a dot product with all 3 million context vectors, every step. Negative sampling replaces it with a yes/no question:

  • Real pair (cat, drinks): push σ(wcat·cdrinks) towards 1 (“yes, these go together”).
  • Draw k random words (say “fuel”, “road”) as fake neighbours: push σ(wcat·cfuel) towards 0.

In words: the loss is “−log of how sure you are about the real pair, minus log of how sure you are each fake pair is fake”:

L = −log σ(w·creal) − Σk fakes log σ(−w·cfake)

That's binary cross-entropy (math 6.3) on 1 + k tiny classification problems. With k = 5, a step costs 6 dot products instead of 3,000,000.

✏️ Worked example: one skip-gram step by hand (2D vectors)

Centre wcat = (0.5, 0.2), real neighbour cdrinks = (0.4, 0.1), learning rate 0.5.

  1. Score: 0.5 × 0.4 + 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.22. Probability “they go together”: σ(0.22) = 0.555. Loss: −ln 0.555 = 0.589.
  2. Gradient on wcat: (σ − 1) × cdrinks = −0.445 × (0.4, 0.1) = (−0.178, −0.045).
  3. Step downhill: (0.5, 0.2) − 0.5 × (−0.178, −0.045) = (0.589, 0.222). The cat vector moved towards “drinks”.
  4. New score 0.589 × 0.4 + 0.222 × 0.1 = 0.258, σ = 0.564: a little more confident. A fake pair does the mirror image and pushes cat away from the fake word.

Now the key step: “dog” also gets pulled towards “drinks”, “sofa” and “chases”. Two words pulled towards the same neighbours end up near each other, without ever appearing in the same window.

🤔 Why this? Skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS)
  1. The problem before Earlier neural language models (Bengio et al., 2003) learned embeddings too, but through hidden layers and a full softmax over the vocabulary: every training step cost a dot product with every word, so training on billions of words was out of reach.
  2. What people tried Hierarchical softmax (a binary tree of yes/no decisions, log₂V ≈ 22 steps for 3M words, also in word2vec) and noise-contrastive estimation.
  3. Why this works Drop the hidden layer (a dot product is enough) and turn “which word of 3M?” into “real neighbour or random word?”. The paper reports k = 5–20 negatives for small datasets and as few as 2–5 for large ones, drawn from word frequency raised to the 3/4 power (so common words like “the” aren't drawn quite as often as their raw counts say). An optimized single machine trains on “more than 100 billion words in one day”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Without negatives the task is trivially solved by making every dot product huge: all vectors drift to the same direction (you'll cause this below). Results depend on window size (small windows → “similar role”, like cat/dog; large → “same topic”, like cat/vet), subsampling of frequent words, and corpus. One vector per word, whatever the sense.
  5. What refined it GloVe (next), fastText's subword vectors, and then contextual embeddings from language models (ELMo, BERT; Phase 6). The same “real vs fake pairs” idea returns as contrastive learning (CLIP, Lesson 4.6; SimCLR, Lesson 9.1).

GloVe: count first, then fit

word2vec streams through text one window at a time. Pennington, Socher and Manning at Stanford (2014) asked: why not count all co-occurrences once, in a big table X (Xij = how often word j appears near word i), and fit vectors to the table? That's GloVe (Global Vectors).

✏️ Worked example: the ice/steam table from the GloVe paper

From a 6-billion-token corpus, the probability that a context word k appears near “ice” vs near “steam”:

k =solidgaswaterfashion
P(k | ice)1.9 × 10⁻⁴6.6 × 10⁻⁵3.0 × 10⁻³1.7 × 10⁻⁵
P(k | steam)2.2 × 10⁻⁵7.8 × 10⁻⁴2.2 × 10⁻³1.8 × 10⁻⁵
ratio8.90.0851.360.96

The raw probabilities are all tiny and hard to read. The ratio is crisp: ≫ 1 means “about ice, not steam” (solid), ≪ 1 means “about steam” (gas), ≈ 1 means “irrelevant to the difference” (water relates to both, fashion to neither). GloVe is designed so that differences between word vectors encode these ratios. That's why vector differences like king − man carry meaning.

In words, GloVe's objective is: make wi·cj + biases ≈ log Xij, for every pair that ever co-occurred, with a weight that grows with the count but is capped (at 100) so “the”–“of” doesn't dominate. It is weighted least squares on the log of the count table.

🤔 Why this? GloVe (global co-occurrence counts)
  1. The problem before Count-based methods (compress the co-occurrence matrix, like latent semantic analysis) use all the statistics at once but did poorly on analogies; window methods like word2vec do well on analogies but see each window separately and never use the global counts directly.
  2. What people tried Different counting and reweighting schemes (log counts, pointwise mutual information) followed by matrix factorization.
  3. Why this works Count once, then fit vectors so dot products match log counts. The log turns ratios into differences, so analogies become vector arithmetic. Training touches only the non-zero cells of X, not every word in every sentence again. The paper reports 75% accuracy on the word analogy test, and released vectors trained on up to 840 billion tokens of web text.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You must build and store the co-occurrence table (vocabulary², though sparse). Same limits as word2vec: one vector per word, out-of-vocabulary words have nothing, bias in the text is baked in. Once tuned, the two often perform similarly; Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed SGNS is itself implicitly factorizing a (shifted) co-occurrence statistic, so the two families are closer than they look.
  5. What refined it fastText (subwords), then contextual models (ELMo 2018, BERT 2018) that replaced static vectors for most tasks (Phase 6).

    Bias: embeddings learn what the text says, including the ugly parts

    Bolukbasi and colleagues (2016) studied the Google News word2vec vectors (3 million words, 300 dimensions) and found it would answer “man is to computer programmer as woman is to x” with x = homemaker, and that father is to doctor as mother is to nurse. Journalists wrote the text; the vectors absorbed the statistics of who is described doing what. Their paper's title says it all: Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?

    Two cautions, both from the research. First, the “forced to differ” trick you just saw makes analogies look more biased than the space is (that's Nissim et al.'s point: man:doctor :: woman:doctor is the unbiased answer, and the demo isn't allowed to return it). Second, the bias is real anyway: Bolukbasi et al. proposed removing a “gender direction” from neutral words, and Gonen and Goldberg (2019) showed the bias mostly stays hidden in the geometry (their title: Lipstick on a Pig). If you ship embeddings in a search or hiring system, measure bias on your own task.

    From static to contextual

    word2vec and GloVe give one vector per word, forever. But “I sat by the bank of the river” and “I paid at the bank” need different meanings. In 2018 ELMo (“Embeddings from Language Models”) computed each word's vector from the whole sentence, using a deep bidirectional recurrent language model (the networks of Lessons 5.2–5.3). BERT did the same with a transformer and took over NLP (Phase 6).

    🧰 Tools you'll actually use

    In PyTorch an embedding table is nn.Embedding(V, d): a V × d weight matrix indexed by integer ids. Here it is, plus one skip-gram-with-negatives loss step written out:

    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
vocab = {"cat": 0, "dog": 1, "car": 2}
emb = nn.Embedding(num_embeddings=10_000, embedding_dim=300)   # one row per word
print(sum(p.numel() for p in emb.parameters()))                  # 10,000 × 300

ids = torch.tensor([vocab["cat"], vocab["dog"]])
vecs = emb(ids)                                                  # a lookup, not a matmul
print(vecs.shape)

one_hot = F.one_hot(ids, num_classes=10_000).float()
print(torch.allclose(one_hot @ emb.weight, vecs))               # same thing, 10,000× more work

# one-hot: every pair of different words has cosine 0
print(F.cosine_similarity(one_hot[0], one_hot[1], dim=0).item())

# skip-gram with negative sampling: one step on (center, context, negatives)
W = nn.Embedding(10_000, 300); C = nn.Embedding(10_000, 300)
nn.init.uniform_(W.weight, -0.5 / 300, 0.5 / 300); nn.init.zeros_(C.weight)   # word2vec's init
center, context = torch.tensor([0]), torch.tensor([1])
negatives = torch.randint(0, 10_000, (1, 5))                      # k = 5 random "noise" words
pos = (W(center) * C(context)).sum(-1)                           # score of the real pair
neg = (W(center).unsqueeze(1) * C(negatives)).sum(-1)            # scores of the fake pairs
loss = -(F.logsigmoid(pos) + F.logsigmoid(-neg).sum(-1)).mean()
print(round(loss.item(), 3))                                    # 6 × ln 2 at the start: every guess is 50/50

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# 3000000
# torch.Size([2, 300])
# True
# 0.0
# 4.159

    In practice: gensim's Word2Vec and FastText train static vectors on your own corpus in minutes; for search and RAG you'd use a sentence-embedding model instead (AI Engineer 2.1). To start a model from pretrained vectors: nn.Embedding.from_pretrained(tensor, freeze=False). Use padding_idx for the padding token so its row stays zero.

    🎤 Interview angle

    “How does word2vec learn meaning, and why not just use one-hot vectors?” A strong answer:

    • One-hot makes all words orthogonal and vocabulary-sized; dense learned vectors let similarity transfer, and an embedding layer is just a lookup (= one-hot × matrix).
    • The distributional hypothesis plus a fake task: skip-gram predicts neighbours from the centre, CBOW the reverse; words with the same neighbours get pulled to the same place.
    • Negative sampling: replace a V-way softmax by 1 real + k fake binary decisions (k ≈ 5–20), so a step costs k + 1 dot products. Without negatives, everything collapses together.
    • Caveats: one vector per word (polysemy), out-of-vocabulary words, bias from the corpus, and analogy demos that exclude input words. What came next: fastText subwords, contextual embeddings (ELMo/BERT).
    🏭 In the real world
    word2vec and the Google News vectors 🧰 Open source · 2013 word2vec project archive

    How: C code for skip-gram and CBOW plus pre-trained vectors “trained on part of Google News dataset (about 100 billion words)”: 300 dimensions for 3 million words and phrases.

    Why this method: no hidden layer and negative sampling made training on 100-billion-word corpora practical on one machine, and the released vectors became the default input layer for NLP models for years.

    GloVe vectors 🧪 Research · 2014 Pennington, Socher & Manning: GloVe (EMNLP 2014)

    How: fit vectors to the log of a global co-occurrence table built from Wikipedia + Gigaword (6 billion tokens, 400,000-word vocabulary) and from Common Crawl web text.

    Why this method: one pass to count, then cheap training on the non-zero cells; ratios of co-occurrence probabilities become vector differences, which is exactly what analogies need.

    Measuring and reducing gender bias 🧪 Research · 2016 Bolukbasi et al.: Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?

    How: found a “gender direction” in the Google News vectors, showed stereotyped analogies, and removed that direction from gender-neutral words.

    Why this method: embeddings were already inside search and recommendation systems; a measurable direction gave a measurable fix (later shown to be incomplete by Gonen & Goldberg, 2019).

    Contextual word vectors 📜 History · 2018 Peters et al.: Deep contextualized word representations (ELMo)

    How: each word's vector is a learned mix of the internal states of a deep bidirectional language model, so it changes with the sentence.

    Why this method: static vectors can't tell “bank” from “bank”; ELMo models polysemy directly and improved the state of the art across the six NLP tasks in the paper. BERT (Phase 6) pushed the idea further.

    Check yourself

    1. A vocabulary of 50,000 words with 300-dimensional embeddings stored in float32 (4 bytes). How big is the embedding table?
    One row of 300 per word: 50,000 × 300 = 15,000,000 weights × 4 bytes = 60 MB. The one-hot alternative feeding 256 units would need 50,000 × 256 = 12.8 million weights for the first layer and still learn no similarity.
    2. You train skip-gram on your company's documents and every word's nearest neighbour has cosine ≈ 0.99, even unrelated ones. The loss went to almost 0 quickly. Most likely cause?
    The positive-only objective is satisfied by pointing every vector the same way and growing it. Negatives (random words pushed to σ ≈ 0) are what force different words apart. You caused exactly this with k = 0 in the playground.
    3. A search engine uses GloVe vectors. Queries about river banks keep returning banking products. What's the underlying issue and the modern fix?
    word2vec and GloVe store one vector per word type, a blend of all its senses, dominated by the most frequent one. Contextual models (ELMo, BERT, modern embedding models) compute a vector from the whole sentence, so the two banks separate.
    📝 TL;DR
    • One-hot vectors are vocabulary-sized and make all words equally unrelated. Dense embeddings (~300 numbers) are learned weights; looking one up equals one-hot × matrix.
    • Distributional hypothesis: a word is known by its company. word2vec's skip-gram predicts neighbours from the centre word (CBOW the reverse); shared neighbours pull words together.
    • Negative sampling: 1 real pair pushed to σ → 1 plus k random pairs pushed to σ → 0, so a step costs k + 1 dot products, not V. Without negatives, everything collapses.
    • GloVe fits dot products to log co-occurrence counts; ratios become differences, so analogies become arithmetic.
    • Caveats: analogy demos exclude input words (0.74 → 0.21 without that), embeddings absorb bias from text, and one vector per word can't handle multiple senses: hence contextual embeddings (Phase 6).
