Recap: Sequences & classic NLP

From one-hot vectors to the first attention: word embeddings, recurrent networks and backprop through time, LSTMs and GRUs, encoder–decoders with attention, and 1D convolutions. Each idea fixed the weakness of the one before; this page lines them up, then you design a character-level name generator yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🔤

5.1 Word embeddings

Lookup = one-hot × table. SGNS loss = −log σ(w·c) − Σk log σ(−w·cneg), k ≈ 5–20.

Words with the same neighbours get the same vectors (Firth: the company a word keeps). GloVe fits log co-occurrence counts. Analogy demos exclude the input words; embeddings absorb bias; one vector per word can't separate senses.

🧰 nn.Embedding, gensim Word2Vec / FastText

🔁

5.2 RNNs and BPTT

ht = tanh(Wht−1 + Uxt + b). Gradient from k back = Π (W·tanh′): 0.5²⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶.

Same weights every step, any length. Below 1 the gradient vanishes, above 1 it explodes (clip it). Truncated BPTT: carry the state, cut the gradient every K steps.

🧰 nn.RNN, h.detach(), clip_grad_norm_

🧵

5.3 LSTM and GRU

c = f⊙c + i⊙g, h = o⊙tanh(c). Along the belt the gradient is just Π f.

Sigmoid gates are 0–1 valves, tanh carries signed content. Forget-gate bias ≈ 1+ so it starts by remembering. GRU: keep z, write 1 − z; 3 blocks vs 4. Still sequential.

🧰 nn.LSTM, nn.GRU (gates packed [i, f, g, o])

🔀

5.4 Seq2seq and attention

ej = vᵀtanh(Ws + Uhj) → α = softmax(e) → context = Σ αjhj.

One fixed vector is a bottleneck (quality falls with length). Attention looks back at every encoder state; weights = alignment. Teacher forcing → exposure bias. Beam search keeps the top B.

🧰 F.softmax, F.scaled_dot_product_attention, generate(num_beams=…)

〰️

5.5 1D convolutions

Receptive field = 1 + (k − 1)·Σd. k = 2, d = 1…512 → 1,024 (WaveNet).

A width-k filter is a learned k-gram detector; max over time = “did it appear?”. Causal = pad left. Dilation growth ≤ kernel width avoids holes. Parallel, stable gradients; fixed reach.

🧰 nn.Conv1d (B, C, T), dilation=, F.pad

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “It's 2014 and you're building English-to-French translation. Walk me from raw words to a translated sentence: how words become numbers, how the network reads a sentence, why the obvious design fails on long sentences, and what you'd change. Then tell me why, three years later, everyone switched to something else.”

A strong answer covers: one-hot vs learned embeddings (word2vec/GloVe, shared neighbours); an encoder RNN with shared weights over time; why vanilla RNNs forget (gradient = product of W·tanh′ factors) and how LSTM/GRU gates with an additive cell state fix it (forget bias); the encoder–decoder and its fixed-vector bottleneck (BLEU falls with length; the reversed-source hack); attention as score → softmax → weighted sum, with alignments; teacher forcing, exposure bias and beam search; and finally that recurrence is sequential, so transformers (self-attention only, parallel over positions) took over, with 1D CNNs as the other parallel alternative.

🏗️ Build it yourself: a character-level name generator

✏️ The problem

You have a list of 32,000 first names (“emma”, “olivia”, “ava”, …), lowercase letters only. Build a model that invents new, plausible names one character at a time, like Karpathy's char-RNN from Lesson 5.2 but small enough to train on a laptop CPU in a few minutes.

Decide: the tokens and vocabulary, the architecture and every tensor shape, the parameter count, the loss and how targets line up with inputs, the training recipe, how you sample a new name, and what could go wrong. Then open the reveal.

Reveal: one good answer

Tokens

  • 26 letters + one boundary token “.” that means both start and end = 27 tokens. “emma” becomes the training sequence . e m m a .
  • Character level means no unknown words and a tiny vocabulary, at the price of longer sequences (names are short, so that's fine).

Architecture and shapes (batch B, length T)

  1. Input ids (B, T) → nn.Embedding(27, 32) → (B, T, 32). 864 weights.
  2. nn.LSTM(32, 128, batch_first=True) → (B, T, 128). 4 × (128 × 32 + 128 × 128 + 2 × 128) = 82,944 weights. (A GRU would be 62,208.)
  3. nn.Linear(128, 27) → logits (B, T, 27): a score for every possible next character at every position. 3,483 weights.
  4. Total ≈ 87,291 parameters: tiny. One LSTM layer is enough; names rarely need more than ~10 characters of memory, well within an LSTM's reach.

Loss and targets

  • Teacher forcing: inputs = . e m m a, targets = e m m a . (shifted by one). Cross-entropy over the 27 classes at every position (Lesson 1.1), averaged.
  • Pad names in a batch to the same length and pass ignore_index for padded targets, so padding isn't learned as a character.
  • Baseline to beat: uniform guessing costs ln 27 ≈ 3.30 nats per character. A good small model gets far below that.

Training recipe

  • Adam, learning rate ~3 × 10⁻³, batch 64, a few thousand steps; clip_grad_norm_(…, 1.0) (Lesson 5.2); forget-gate bias 1 (Lesson 5.3).
  • Hold out 10% of names and watch validation loss; stop when it stops falling (Lesson 2.4).

Sampling a new name

  1. Start from “.” with a zero state; run one step; softmax the logits (divided by a temperature, e.g. 0.8); sample a character; feed it back in; repeat until “.” appears.
  2. Greedy argmax would produce the same single name every time (and can loop); sampling gives variety, temperature trades creativity for plausibility. Beam search (Lesson 5.4) finds the most probable name, which is boring here.

Classic mistakes

  • Forgetting the shift: targets equal to inputs, so the model learns to copy and the loss drops to ~0.
  • Learning the padding: no ignore_index, so generated names end in strings of pad tokens.
  • A bidirectional LSTM: it peeks at the future characters it's supposed to predict.
  • Calling it creative when it's memorizing: check how many sampled names already exist in the training list.
  • Exposure bias: after one odd character, samples can drift; a lower temperature helps.

The same recipe with a transformer instead of the LSTM is a tiny GPT; Phase 6 builds exactly that.

🏆 Boss quiz

1. How many parameters does PyTorch's nn.LSTM(input_size=100, hidden_size=50) have (one layer, two bias vectors)?
Each of the 4 gate blocks has 50 × 100 input weights, 50 × 50 recurrent weights and 2 × 50 biases = 7,600; × 4 = 30,400. 7,600 is a vanilla RNN, 22,800 a GRU (3 blocks) (Lesson 5.3).
2. A causal 1D CNN uses kernel width 3 and dilations 1, 2, 4, 8. How many timesteps can its last output see?
1 + (k − 1)(1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 1 + 2 × 15 = 31, with no holes because the dilation growth (×2) is below the kernel width (3) (Lesson 5.5).
3. Your vanilla-RNN sentiment model is perfect on short reviews but ignores a “but…” clause 40 words before the end. Gradient norms for early steps are ~10⁻⁹. What's the most direct fix?
This is the vanishing gradient through time: 40 factors of W·tanh′ below 1. Clipping only stops explosions; K = 10 would cut the gradient even shorter. A gated cell's additive cell state (or attention's direct look-back) gives the gradient a short path (Lessons 5.2–5.4).
4. Skip-gram with negative sampling on a 100,000-word vocabulary with k = 5. How many dot products does one (centre, context) training pair cost, compared with a full softmax?
One for the real pair plus one for each of the k = 5 random fakes: 6. The full softmax would score every word in the vocabulary. That speed-up is what let word2vec train on 100 billion words (Lesson 5.1).
5. A 2015-style LSTM translator with attention trains for a week on 8 GPUs. A colleague says “we'd train much faster with a transformer on the same GPUs”. What's the core reason?
Recurrence is inherently sequential within a training example, so a GPU can't parallelize over positions; the 2017 transformer paper makes exactly this argument. Attention already solved the bottleneck; dropping recurrence solved the speed (Lessons 5.3, 5.4 → 6.1).

📝 TL;DR
  • One-hot → dense embeddings (shared neighbours → similar vectors) → contextual embeddings.
  • RNN = learned for-loop; its gradient through time is a product, so it vanishes or explodes; clip explosions, truncate BPTT for memory.
  • LSTM/GRU: gated, additive memory; the forget gate (and its bias) decides how long things last.
  • Encoder–decoder + attention: no bottleneck, learned alignments; teacher forcing and beam search for training and decoding.
  • 1D CNNs: parallel n-gram detectors; dilation grows the receptive field exponentially.
