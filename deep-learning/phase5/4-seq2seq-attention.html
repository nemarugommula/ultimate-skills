Phase 5 · Lesson 4

Seq2seq and the first attention

Translate a sentence by reading it into one vector and writing the translation out of that vector. It works shockingly well, until sentences get long. The fix, “let the writer look back at the reader's notes”, is the idea that became the transformer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An LSTM or GRU reads a sequence and keeps a fixed-size state; it remembers far longer than a vanilla RNN (Lesson 5.3).
  • Softmax turns any list of scores into positive weights that sum to 1 (Lesson 0.6, math 8.2).
  • A weighted average of vectors Σ αj vj with α summing to 1 is a “soft pick” between them.
🎯 Hook

In 2014 Sutskever, Vinyals and Le at Google trained a pair of 4-layer LSTMs to translate English to French. Their best ensemble scored 34.8 BLEU (a 0–100 score of overlap with human translations), beating a mature phrase-based system's 33.3, with no hand-built linguistics at all.

The strangest trick in the paper: they fed the English sentence in backwards. “I love cats” became “cats love I”. That alone raised BLEU from 25.9 to 30.6.

Why would reading backwards help? The answer exposes a bottleneck, and the fix for it, published the same year by Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, is called attention. You'll paint attention weights by hand to translate a phrase whose word order flips, and watch a single vector forget the start of a sentence.

Encoder–decoder: read, then write

Translation is many-to-many with different lengths: 5 English words can become 7 French ones, in a different order. The sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) recipe uses two recurrent networks:

  1. Encoder: an LSTM reads the source sentence. Its final state is a summary vector z (the “thought vector”).
  2. Decoder: another LSTM starts from z and generates the target one word at a time, feeding each word it just produced back in as its next input, until it emits an end-of-sentence token.
🤔 Why this? The encoder–decoder (seq2seq)
  1. The problem before An RNN emits one output per input step, so input and output must have the same length and line up. Translation breaks both: lengths differ and word order changes. Statistical phrase-based systems handled it with many hand-engineered parts (alignment models, phrase tables, reordering rules, a separate language model).
  2. What people tried Neural networks as one component inside phrase-based systems (for example rescoring their candidate lists).
  3. Why this works Decouple reading and writing: any length in, any length out, one network trained end to end on sentence pairs to maximize the probability of the reference translation. Sutskever et al.'s 4-layer LSTMs (1,000 cells per layer, 384M parameters) reached 34.8 BLEU; Cho et al. (2014) proposed the same encoder–decoder shape with the GRU.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The whole source must squeeze through one fixed-size vector (8,000 numbers in Sutskever's deep LSTM). Cho et al. found the model's quality “degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence” grows. Decoding is sequential, word by word.
  5. What refined it Reversing the source (a hack), then attention (below), then the transformer encoder–decoder (Lesson 6.5), which is still how models like T5 and Whisper (Lesson 8.2) are built.

Numbers first: why reversing helped

With “I love cats” → “j'aime les chats”, the first word to translate (“I”) was read first, so by the time the decoder starts it's 3 steps old. Reverse the input to “cats love I” and “I” is the most recent thing the encoder saw. The authors' explanation: reversing “introduces many short term dependencies”. The average distance between matching words stays the same, but the first few target words become very close to their sources, which gets the decoder off to a good start. Their test perplexity dropped from 5.8 to 4.7.

A hack that works by moving words closer in time is a sign that distance through the state is the real problem. Bahdanau et al. said it outright: “the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck”.

Attention: let the decoder look back

Keep every encoder state h1…hT (Bahdanau et al. called them “annotations”; their encoder read the sentence in both directions so each one describes a word in its full context). When the decoder is about to write target word i, it:

  1. Scores every source position: how relevant is hj to what I'm about to write, given my state si−1? Bahdanau's additive score is a tiny one-hidden-layer network: eij = vᵀ tanh(W si−1 + U hj).
  2. Softmaxes the scores into attention weights αij that sum to 1.
  3. Builds a context vector ci = Σj αij hj, a soft pick of the relevant source words, and feeds it to the decoder with its previous word.

Every target word gets its own context. Nothing has to survive the whole sentence in one vector. And the weights α are learned without anyone ever labelling which word aligns with which: they fall out of training to translate. The paper's title says it: Jointly Learning to Align and Translate.

💡 Big idea Don't compress, look it up. Attention is a soft, differentiable lookup: score every stored item against what you need now, softmax the scores, take the weighted average. In Phase 6 this lookup is all that remains: the transformer is attention without the RNN.
🧠 Analogy: a translator with the original on the desk

The plain encoder–decoder is a translator who reads the sentence once, closes the book, and writes the translation from memory. Attention lets them keep the book open and glance back at the relevant words before writing each one: mostly at one word, sometimes at two (“the man” together decides between “le” and “l'”).

✏️ Worked example: additive attention by hand

Three encoder states h₁ = (1, 0), h₂ = (0, 1), h₃ = (1, 1); decoder state s = (0.5, −0.5). To keep it small, W = U = identity and v = (1, 1).

  1. Scores ej = sum of tanh(s + hj): tanh(1.5, −0.5) → 0.905 − 0.462 = 0.443; tanh(0.5, 0.5) → 0.924; tanh(1.5, 0.5) → 1.367.
  2. Softmax: α = (0.195, 0.315, 0.490). They sum to 1.
  3. Context: 0.195·(1, 0) + 0.315·(0, 1) + 0.490·(1, 1) = (0.685, 0.805), mostly h₃ with some of the others.

Cost: one score per (target word, source word) pair. A 30-word sentence translated into 30 words is 900 scores: trivial here, but this T² grows fast and is the reason Lesson 6.9 exists.

🤔 Why this? Attention (instead of one bottleneck vector)
  1. The problem before The encoder–decoder pushes every sentence through one fixed-length vector. Quality degrades rapidly with length; reversing the input to shorten some dependencies was the best available patch.
  2. What people tried Bigger states, deeper LSTMs, reversed or doubled inputs, and limiting training to short sentences.
  3. Why this works Keep all T encoder states and let each decoding step compute its own weighted average of them. Information about word 1 is exactly as reachable as word 30: one hop, not 30 recurrent steps. The weights are a free, readable alignment (the heatmaps below). On sentences of 50+ words Bahdanau's model showed no deterioration.
  4. What it costs, when it fails T × T′ scores per sentence (quadratic in length), all encoder states kept in memory, and a softmax per step. Weights are soft, so attention can spread itself thin or attend to the wrong word (translations that skip or repeat words). An attention map is a hint about what the model used, not a guaranteed explanation.
  5. What refined it Luong et al. (2015): simpler scores (dot, general, concat) and local attention; then self-attention, where a sequence attends to itself, and the transformer (Lesson 6.1). Today's fused kernel is F.scaled_dot_product_attention.

Luong attention: simpler scores

Luong, Pham and Manning (2015) compared ways to score a decoder state against encoder states:

They also used the current decoder state (Bahdanau used the previous one) and tried local attention over a window. Their ensemble set a new state of the art on WMT'15 English→German at 25.9 BLEU. The dot score is the ancestor of the transformer's scaled dot-product attention (Lesson 6.1, math 8.4).

Training and decoding: teacher forcing, exposure bias, beam search

During training the decoder is fed the correct previous word, not its own guess. That's teacher forcing: all target positions can be scored in one pass, and a mistake at word 2 doesn't derail words 3–20.

Training (teacher forcing)Inference (free running)
Input at step ithe reference word yi−1the model's own previous output ŷi−1
If step 2 is wrongstep 3 still sees the right historystep 3 sees a history it never saw in training

Ranzato et al. (2015) named that mismatch exposure bias: the model “is only exposed to the training data distribution, instead of its own predictions”. Fixes include scheduled sampling (Bengio et al., 2015: gradually feed the model its own predictions during training) and sequence-level training, which led towards the RL fine-tuning of language models (RL course 7.1).

🤔 Why this? Teacher forcing
  1. The problem before Feeding the decoder its own early guesses during training means an untrained model conditions on garbage: one wrong word and the rest of the sentence gets a useless learning signal. Training is slow and unstable.
  2. What people tried Training only on short sequences; sampling the model's own outputs (very noisy gradients).
  3. Why this works Condition each step on the true prefix, so every position gets a clean cross-entropy target, and (in transformers) all positions can be trained in parallel. It turns sequence generation into many independent next-token classification problems.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Exposure bias: at test time the model must recover from its own mistakes, which it never practised. Errors can compound over long outputs.
  5. What refined it Scheduled sampling; sequence-level losses and RL fine-tuning; and in practice, very large models trained with teacher forcing (every LLM's pretraining, Lesson 6.7) plus good decoding.

At inference, picking the single most likely word each step (greedy) can paint you into a corner. Beam search keeps the B most probable partial translations at every step. Sutskever et al. found their system “performs well even with a beam size of 1, and a beam of size 2 provides most of the benefits of beam search”.

🤔 Why this? Beam search
  1. The problem before The best sequence maximizes a product over all steps, but there are VL sequences (80,000 words, 20 steps: far too many). Greedy decoding picks the best word now and can miss a much better sentence that starts with a slightly worse word.
  2. What people tried Greedy decoding; random sampling (good for creativity, bad for accuracy).
  3. Why this works Keep the top B partial sequences at every step and extend each one: cost B × V scores per step instead of VL in total, and a cheap insurance policy against an early wrong turn. Sutskever et al. saw most of the gain with B = 2.
  4. What it costs, when it fails B× the compute of greedy; it still isn't exact. Raw log-probabilities favour short outputs, so systems add length normalization (GNMT also added a coverage penalty). Large beams can produce bland, repetitive text, which is why chatbots usually sample instead.
  5. What refined it Length and coverage penalties; for open-ended generation, temperature and nucleus sampling; best-of-N with a reward model (RL course 7.7).
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Bahdanau's additive attention as a PyTorch module, on top of a bidirectional GRU encoder, plus the one-line fused dot-product version you'll use in Phase 6:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
class AdditiveAttention(nn.Module):              # Bahdanau et al. 2014
    def __init__(self, dec_dim, enc_dim, attn_dim=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.W = nn.Linear(dec_dim, attn_dim, bias=False)
        self.U = nn.Linear(enc_dim, attn_dim, bias=False)
        self.v = nn.Linear(attn_dim, 1, bias=False)
    def forward(self, s, H):                      # s: (B, dec_dim), H: (B, T, enc_dim)
        e = self.v(torch.tanh(self.W(s).unsqueeze(1) + self.U(H))).squeeze(-1)   # (B, T) scores
        alpha = F.softmax(e, dim=-1)                                            # weights sum to 1
        context = (alpha.unsqueeze(-1) * H).sum(1)                              # (B, enc_dim)
        return context, alpha

enc = nn.GRU(32, 128, batch_first=True, bidirectional=True)   # encoder reads the source both ways
src = torch.randn(2, 7, 32)                                  # 2 sentences, 7 source tokens
H, _ = enc(src)                                              # one annotation per source token
print(H.shape)
attn = AdditiveAttention(dec_dim=256, enc_dim=256)
s = torch.randn(2, 256)                                      # current decoder state
context, alpha = attn(s, H)
print(context.shape, alpha.shape, alpha.sum(-1))

# Luong's "dot" score is a single matmul; today's fused kernel does the same thing (Lesson 6.1)
ctx2 = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(s.unsqueeze(1), H, H)   # (B, 1, 256)
print(ctx2.shape)

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# torch.Size([2, 7, 256])
# torch.Size([2, 256]) torch.Size([2, 7]) tensor([1.0000, 1.0000], grad_fn=<SumBackward1>)
# torch.Size([2, 1, 256])

Teacher forcing in a loop: feed tgt[:, :-1] as decoder inputs and compute cross-entropy against tgt[:, 1:]. Mask padded source positions by setting their scores to −∞ before the softmax. For real translation today you'd fine-tune a pretrained transformer (Hugging Face transformers, e.g. a Marian or T5 model) and call model.generate(num_beams=4).

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain attention in seq2seq and why it was needed.” A strong answer:

  • Encoder–decoder compresses the source into one vector; quality degrades with length (Cho et al. 2014); reversing the source helped because it shortened early dependencies.
  • Attention keeps all encoder states; per decoding step: score (additive or dot), softmax, weighted sum → context vector. Weights learned end to end; they double as alignments.
  • Costs: quadratic number of scores; soft weights can be diffuse; maps aren't explanations.
  • Training/decoding: teacher forcing and exposure bias (scheduled sampling), beam search vs greedy with length normalization. Connects to self-attention and the transformer.
🏭 In the real world
Sequence to sequence learning 🧪 Research · 2014 Sutskever, Vinyals & Le: Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks

How: 4-layer LSTM encoder and decoder, reversed source sentences, a left-to-right beam search; an ensemble of 5 reached 34.8 BLEU on WMT'14 English→French.

Why this method: one general architecture trained end to end beat a phrase-based baseline (33.3) on direct translation.

Learning to align and translate 🧪 Research · 2014 Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio: Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate

How: a bidirectional RNN encoder produces one annotation per word; the decoder computes additive attention over them at every step.

Why this method: removes the fixed-length bottleneck; their model showed no deterioration on sentences of 50+ words, and the attention maps showed sensible, even non-monotonic, alignments.

Effective approaches to attention 🧪 Research · 2015 Luong, Pham & Manning

How: global and local attention with dot, general and concat scores.

Why this method: simpler and faster scoring; local attention gave 5.0 BLEU over non-attentional systems and the ensemble set a WMT'15 English→German record (25.9).

Attention in production translation 🏢 Google · 2016 Wu et al.: Google's Neural Machine Translation System

How: 8-layer LSTM encoder and decoder with attention, beam search with length normalization and a coverage penalty.

Why this method: attention made the deep encoder–decoder work on real traffic; human raters saw 60% fewer errors than the phrase-based system.

🤯 Fun fact · the hack that hinted at the answer

Sutskever and colleagues first thought reversing the input would only help the start of the translation. Instead, models trained on reversed sentences “did much better on long sentences” too. Moving related words closer together in time helped so much that it pointed at the real problem: distance through the state. Attention made every distance one hop.

Check yourself

1. Attention scores for one decoder step over 3 source words are (0, 0, ln 2) ≈ (0, 0, 0.693). What are the attention weights?
Softmax: e⁰ = 1, e⁰ = 1, e^(ln 2) = 2; total 4 → (1/4, 1/4, 2/4). The third word gets twice the weight of each other word. Weights only hit exactly 0 or 1 in the limit of infinitely different scores.
2. Your seq2seq translator (no attention) scores well on 10-word sentences but its BLEU collapses on 40-word ones. Training loss is fine. Most likely cause and fix?
This is exactly the curve Cho et al. and Bahdanau et al. reported: plain encoder–decoders degrade rapidly with length because one vector must hold everything. Attention lets every target word look back at the relevant source states. A huge beam can't recover information the vector never kept.
3. A model trained with teacher forcing writes great first sentences, but once it makes one odd word choice, the rest of the paragraph drifts into nonsense. What is this called?
Teacher forcing feeds the true previous word during training; at inference the model conditions on its own outputs. The first unusual word puts it in a situation it never practised. Scheduled sampling, sequence-level training and RL fine-tuning target this.
📝 TL;DR
  • Seq2seq: an encoder RNN reads the source into a vector, a decoder RNN writes the target from it; any lengths, trained end to end (Sutskever 2014: 34.8 BLEU).
  • One fixed vector is a bottleneck: quality falls with sentence length; reversing the source (25.9 → 30.6 BLEU) helped by shortening early dependencies.
  • Attention: keep all encoder states; per target word, score → softmax → weighted sum = context. No bottleneck; weights are learned alignments (even word-order swaps).
  • Bahdanau = additive score; Luong = dot/general/concat. The dot version grows into the transformer.
  • Teacher forcing trains on true prefixes (exposure bias at test time); beam search keeps the top B partial outputs (B = 2 gave most of the gain).
