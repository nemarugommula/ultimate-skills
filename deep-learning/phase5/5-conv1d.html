Phase 5 · Lesson 5

1D convolutions for text and sequences

The image filters of Phase 3, turned sideways: a small window that slides along a sentence or a sound wave and lights up on a pattern. No recurrence, every position computed at once, and with dilation it can see a thousand steps back in ten layers.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A convolution slides a small filter of shared weights over the input and takes a dot product at each position; max pooling keeps the strongest response (Lessons 3.2–3.3, math 8.3b).
  • A sentence is a sequence of word vectors (Lesson 5.1); RNNs read it one step at a time (5.2–5.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2014 Yoon Kim put a single convolution layer on top of pre-trained word2vec vectors: filters 3, 4 and 5 words wide, 100 of each, a max, and a softmax. With “little hyperparameter tuning” it improved the state of the art on 4 of 7 sentence-classification benchmarks, including sentiment analysis.

Two years later DeepMind's WaveNet generated raw audio, 16,000 samples per second, with stacks of convolutions, and human listeners rated its text-to-speech “significantly more natural sounding” than the best existing parametric and concatenative systems, for English and Mandarin. Its trick: each layer looks twice as far back as the one below, so one 10-layer block sees 1,024 samples.

How does a filter that's only 2 or 3 steps wide see 1,024 steps of history? You'll build an n-gram detector by hand, then grow a receptive field to 64 steps in as few layers as you can.

Numbers first: a filter is an n-gram detector

Give each word a tiny 4-number vector: [positive, negative, “not”, filler]. “good” = [1, 0, 0, 0], “not” = [0, 0, 1, 0], “the” = [0, 0, 0, 1]. A width-2 filter is a 2 × 4 grid of weights: one row for the first word of the window, one for the second.

filter row 1 (first word): “not” weight +1 · row 2 (second word): “positive” weight +1 · bias −1
window “not good”: 1 + 1 − 1 = 1 → ReLU → 1 🔥 · window “was great”: 0 + 1 − 1 = 0 → 0 · window “was not”: 0 + 0 − 1 → 0

That filter is a “not + positive word” detector, a learned bigram. Slide it along the sentence and you get a feature map, one number per position. Then max over time keeps its strongest response: “did ‘not good’ appear anywhere?”, regardless of where. A model with 300 such filters has 300 learned phrase detectors.

What you should see: the width-1 “positive word” filter lights up on “good” and on “great”, so it can't tell “not good” from “good”. The width-2 “not + positive” preset fires only on the window “not good” (score 1) and is silent everywhere else, including on “great”. Switch to sentence 2, where “not good” sits at a different position: the feature map's peak moves, but the max-pooled score is the same 1. For “not very good” you need width 3 with “not” in row 1 and “positive” in row 3: a gap in the middle the filter simply ignores.

🤔 Why this? 1D convolution for text
  1. The problem before Bag-of-words models lose order (“not good” = “good not”); n-gram features explode in number (a 50,000-word vocabulary has 2.5 billion possible bigrams) and don't share anything between similar phrases; RNNs keep order but run step by step.
  2. What people tried Hand-picked n-gram features with linear classifiers; recursive networks over parse trees; RNNs/LSTMs.
  3. Why this works A width-k filter over embeddings is a soft, learned k-gram detector that works on similar phrases too (“not great” lights it up nearly as much, because “great” is near “good”). The same weights slide everywhere, so the detector is position-independent, and all positions are computed in parallel. Max-over-time pooling turns any-length text into a fixed-size vector. Kim's model: 3 widths × 100 filters on 300-dim vectors, about 360,000 convolution weights.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each filter sees only k words: dependencies longer than the window (or than the receptive field of a stack) are invisible. Max pooling throws away where and how often a pattern occurred. Works best for classification, less well for tasks that need the whole sentence's structure.
  5. What refined it Deeper and dilated stacks (below), gated convolutions for translation (ConvS2S, 2017), and eventually transformers, whose attention can connect any two words directly (Phase 6).
✏️ Worked example: shapes and parameters of Kim's CNN

Input: 40 tokens, each a 300-number word2vec vector → a 300 × 40 matrix (channels × time).

  1. Width-3 filters: each has 3 × 300 = 900 weights + 1 bias. 100 of them: 90,100 weights. Output: 100 feature maps of length 40 − 3 + 1 = 38.
  2. Widths 4 and 5: 100 × (1,200 + 1) = 120,100 and 100 × (1,500 + 1) = 150,100. Total convolution weights: 360,300.
  3. Max over time: 3 × 100 = 300 numbers, one per filter, whatever the sentence length.
  4. Dropout 0.5, then a linear layer to 2 classes: 300 × 2 + 2 = 602 weights. Done.

All 38 positions of every filter are independent dot products: one batched matrix multiply on a GPU, versus 40 sequential steps for an RNN.

Looking further back: causal and dilated convolutions

For generating a sequence (the next audio sample, the next word) the model must not see the future. A causal convolution only looks left: output at time t uses inputs t, t−1, … (in code: pad on the left only). Stack L causal layers of width 2 and the last output sees L + 1 steps. To see 1,024 samples (64 ms of 16 kHz audio) you'd need 1,023 layers.

A dilated convolution skips: with dilation d, a width-2 filter reads t and t − d. WaveNet doubles d every layer (1, 2, 4, … 512), so the reach doubles too:

receptive field = 1 + (k − 1) × (d₁ + d₂ + … + dL) → k = 2, d = 1, 2, 4, …, 512: 1 + (1 + 2 + … + 512) = 1 + 1,023 = 1,024 with 10 layers

Exponential reach with linear cost: each layer still has just k weights per channel pair.

What you should see: without dilation, 3 layers of width 2 see just 4 steps, and covering 64 would take 63 layers. Doubling dilations reach 64 in exactly 6 layers (2⁶). Width 3 with doubling gives 1 + 2 × 31 = 63 after 5 layers, one short, so it also needs 6; but width 3 with ×3 dilations (1, 3, 9, 27) covers 81 in 4 layers with no holes. Width 2 with ×3 dilations jumps further but leaves holes: inputs no path ever reaches (sometimes called “gridding”). The rule: dilation growth ≤ kernel width keeps coverage gap-free.

🤔 Why this? Dilated causal convolution
  1. The problem before Audio has 16,000+ samples per second; useful structure spans thousands of samples. Plain causal convolutions grow the receptive field by k − 1 per layer (1,023 layers for 1,024 samples), and RNNs over 16,000 steps per second are slow and forget.
  2. What people tried Bigger filters (cost grows with width), pooling/striding (loses the time resolution you need to output every sample), RNNs.
  3. Why this works Dilations that double per layer double the reach per layer: receptive field 2L for width 2. WaveNet stacks blocks of dilations 1, 2, …, 512 (receptive field 1,024 each) and repeats them. Causal padding means output t never sees input t + 1, so the same network can be trained in parallel on whole clips and then generate sample by sample. Every layer keeps full time resolution.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The receptive field is fixed by the architecture: anything older is invisible, however important. Generating is still one step at a time (WaveNet's original sampling was slow). Growth faster than the kernel width leaves holes. Needs the raw history in memory at inference.
  5. What refined it TCNs (below) for general sequences; faster parallel audio generators and neural codecs for speech (Lessons 8.3–8.4); and for text, attention, whose “receptive field” is the whole context window.

TCNs and the “do we even need RNNs?” paper

In 2018 Bai, Kolter and Koltun built a generic temporal convolutional network (TCN): dilated causal 1D convolutions, residual blocks (Lesson 2.2) and weight normalization, and compared it with LSTMs and GRUs on tasks “commonly used to benchmark recurrent networks” (sequential MNIST, the copy memory task, polyphonic music, character and word language modelling). Their summary: the simple convolutional architecture “outperforms canonical recurrent networks such as LSTMs across a diverse range of tasks and datasets, while demonstrating longer effective memory”, and convolutional networks “should be regarded as a natural starting point for sequence modeling tasks”.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

nn.Conv1d expects (batch, channels, time), so embeddings (batch, time, dim) must be transposed first: the most common 1D-conv bug. Kim's model, then a causal dilated stack whose receptive field we measure with a gradient:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
class KimCNN(nn.Module):                                   # Kim (2014): filters of width 3, 4, 5, 100 each
    def __init__(self, vocab=20_000, dim=300, n_classes=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(vocab, dim)
        self.convs = nn.ModuleList(nn.Conv1d(dim, 100, kernel_size=k) for k in (3, 4, 5))
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(0.5)
        self.out = nn.Linear(300, n_classes)
    def forward(self, ids):                                # ids: (B, T)
        x = self.emb(ids).transpose(1, 2)                  # Conv1d wants (B, channels, T)
        feats = [F.relu(c(x)).amax(dim=2) for c in self.convs]   # max over time: one number per filter
        return self.out(self.drop(torch.cat(feats, dim=1)))

model = KimCNN()
print(model(torch.randint(0, 20_000, (8, 40))).shape)
print(sum(p.numel() for p in model.convs.parameters()))

# a dilated causal stack: kernel 2, dilations 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32
class CausalConv(nn.Conv1d):
    def forward(self, x):
        pad = (self.kernel_size[0] - 1) * self.dilation[0]
        return super().forward(F.pad(x, (pad, 0)))         # pad on the left only: no peeking at the future
stack = nn.Sequential(*[CausalConv(1, 1, kernel_size=2, dilation=2 ** i) for i in range(6)])
x = torch.randn(1, 1, 100, requires_grad=True)
stack(x)[0, 0, -1].backward()                              # which inputs can the LAST output see?
seen = (x.grad[0, 0] != 0).nonzero().flatten()
print(len(seen), seen.min().item(), seen.max().item())    # receptive field

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# torch.Size([8, 2])
# 360300
# 64 36 99

The gradient trick (backprop from one output, see which inputs get a non-zero gradient) is the honest way to check any architecture's receptive field. nn.Conv1d(..., padding="same") is fine for classification but is not causal: it looks at the future.

🎤 Interview angle

“When would you use a 1D CNN instead of an RNN for sequences?” A strong answer:

  • Filters are learned n-gram/motif detectors with shared weights; all positions run in parallel; max-over-time pooling gives a fixed-size summary (Kim 2014).
  • Receptive field math: 1 + (k − 1)·Σd; dilations doubling per layer give 2L for k = 2 (WaveNet: 1,024 per block); causal padding for generation.
  • Pros vs RNNs: parallel training, gradients through depth not time. Cons: fixed reach, history must be stored at inference (TCN paper's own caveats).
  • Today: attention for most text; 1D convs still inside audio models and as cheap local mixers inside modern architectures (the Mamba block includes a short convolution, Lesson 9.3).
🏭 In the real world
CNNs for sentence classification 🧪 Research · 2014 Kim: Convolutional Neural Networks for Sentence Classification

How: one convolution layer (widths 3, 4, 5; 100 filters each), max over time, dropout 0.5, on top of word2vec vectors, static or fine-tuned.

Why this method: cheap, parallel, and pre-trained embeddings did most of the work: state of the art on 4 of 7 tasks with little tuning.

WaveNet 🏢 DeepMind · 2016 van den Oord et al.: WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio

How: dilated causal convolutions with dilations 1, 2, …, 512, repeated, predicting each audio sample from the previous ones.

Why this method: a receptive field of thousands of samples at full 16 kHz resolution; in listening tests its naturalness scores (MOS above 4.0 on a 5-point scale) were the highest reported for those datasets.

Temporal convolutional networks 🧪 Research · 2018 Bai, Kolter & Koltun: An Empirical Evaluation of Generic Convolutional and Recurrent Networks

How: dilated causal convs + residual blocks, compared head to head with LSTMs/GRUs on standard recurrent benchmarks.

Why this method: parallel, stable gradients, controllable memory; the authors found it outperformed canonical recurrent networks across a diverse range of tasks. Code: locuslab/TCN.

Convolutional translation 🏢 Facebook AI · 2017 Gehring et al.: Convolutional Sequence to Sequence Learning

How: an encoder–decoder built entirely of convolutions with gated linear units and attention in every decoder layer.

Why this method: fully parallel training; the authors report beating the deep LSTM of Google's GNMT on WMT'14 English–German and English–French “at an order of magnitude faster speed”. The transformer appeared weeks later (Phase 6).

🤯 Fun fact · babbling without words

Trained on speech without any text to condition on, WaveNet produced, in its authors' words, “non-existent but human language-like words” with realistic-sounding intonation, and it even mimicked the speakers' breathing and mouth movements. A stack of 1D convolutions, predicting one sample at a time, learned what talking sounds like.

Check yourself

1. A causal stack of width-2 filters uses dilations 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. How many past timesteps (including the current one) can the last output see?
1 + (k − 1)(1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16) = 1 + 31 = 32 = 2⁵. Without dilation, 5 layers would see only 6 steps. Each doubling layer doubles the reach.
2. A sentiment CNN with width-3 filters and max pooling gets “not good” right but fails on “I wouldn't say this film, despite its stars, is good”. Why?
Each filter only sees k consecutive words. Long-range interactions need a bigger receptive field (deeper or dilated stacks) or a model that can connect any two words (an LSTM, or attention).
3. You build a next-sample audio model with nn.Conv1d(..., padding="same"). Training loss drops to nearly zero, but generation produces noise. What went wrong?
A centred filter at time t reads t + 1, the very value it's asked to predict, so training is trivially easy and the model never learns to predict. At generation time the future isn't there. Causal convolutions pad on the left only so output t uses inputs up to t.
📝 TL;DR
  • A 1D filter of width k over word vectors is a learned k-gram detector; max over time asks “did it appear anywhere?”. Kim (2014): widths 3/4/5 × 100, one layer, strong results.
  • All positions are computed in parallel; gradients flow through depth, not time.
  • Causal = look left only (pad left). Dilations doubling per layer: receptive field 1 + (k − 1)Σd = 2L for k = 2; WaveNet's 1…512 block sees 1,024.
  • Keep dilation growth ≤ kernel width or the receptive field gets holes.
  • TCNs rival LSTMs on many sequence tasks; their costs are a fixed reach and storing history at inference. Attention (Phase 6) has neither limit for text.
