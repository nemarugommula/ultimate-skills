Phase 8 · Lesson 3

Text-to-speech: from text to waveform

Turning text into a voice is speech recognition run backwards, with one extra problem: the text doesn't say how long each sound lasts, or what the voice should sound like. Every big idea in TTS answers one of those two questions.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A log-mel spectrogram is an 80-row picture of sound that throws away phase (Lesson 8.1).
  • Autoregressive models generate one step at a time, each step conditioned on the previous ones (Lessons 5.4 and 6.5); attention lets a decoder look back at the input (5.4).
  • A GAN trains a generator against a discriminator that tries to tell real from fake (Lesson 7.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2016 DeepMind's WaveNet produced the most natural synthetic speech anyone had heard, by predicting audio one sample at a time: at 16,000 samples a second, that's 16,000 trips through a deep network for every second of speech. It was far too slow to serve. Google later distilled it into Parallel WaveNet, which generates “more than 20 times faster than real-time” and was deployed in Google Assistant.

Four years later, HiFi-GAN generated 22 kHz speech 167.9× faster than real time on one GPU, with quality close to human recordings. Today an open 82-million-parameter model (Kokoro) runs on a laptop, and models like VALL-E clone a voice from a 3-second recording.

TTS (text-to-speech) powers screen readers, navigation, audiobooks, voice assistants and every voice agent. What changed between “one sample at a time” and “thousands of times faster”, and why is a 3-second voice clone both a breakthrough and a problem?

Numbers first: text is short, sound is long

Take “hello world”. Written down it's 11 characters. As speech:

Stage“hello world”How many numbers
Text11 characters11
Phonemes (sound units)HH AH L OW · W ER L D8
Mel frames (80 rows, 11.6 ms each)about 65 frames for 0.75 s65 × 80 = 5,200
Waveform at 22,050 Hz65 frames × 256 samples16,640

Each stage is longer than the one before, and nothing in the text says that “OW” lasts 14 frames and “L” only 6. That missing information (duration, pitch, loudness, the speaker's voice) is why TTS is a one-to-many problem: one sentence can be spoken in endless correct ways.

💡 Big idea TTS is split into easy steps: text → phonemes (how to pronounce it) → an acoustic model that draws the mel spectrogram (when and how loud each sound is) → a vocoder that turns the picture into a waveform (filling in the phase). Each generation of models either made a step parallel (fast) or merged steps end to end (fewer hand-made parts).

Stage 1: text → phonemes

English spelling is a poor guide to sound (“though”, “through”, “tough”), so classic systems first convert text to phonemes with a pronunciation dictionary plus a learned model for unknown words (a grapheme-to-phoneme or G2P model). They also normalize text: “$5” becomes “five dollars”, “Dr.” becomes “doctor” or “drive”. Modern end-to-end models often read characters or text tokens directly and learn pronunciation from data, but many production systems still keep a phoneme step because it makes rare names fixable by hand.

Stage 2: drawing the mel spectrogram

Tacotron 2: attention decides the timing (2017)

Tacotron 2 (Shen et al., Google) is a sequence-to-sequence model (Lesson 5.4): an encoder reads the characters, and an autoregressive decoder produces one mel frame (12.5 ms) at a time, using attention over the characters to decide which one it's saying. A modified WaveNet then turns the frames into audio. It reached a mean opinion score (MOS: listeners rate naturalness 1–5) of 4.53, against 4.58 for professional recordings.

The weak spot is the attention. If it slips, the model skips words, repeats them or babbles forever, and because it's autoregressive it's slow: a 10-second sentence is 800 decoder steps in a row.

FastSpeech 2: predict the durations instead

FastSpeech (Ren et al., 2019) and FastSpeech 2 (2020) replace “let attention figure out the timing” with an explicit duration predictor: a small network that reads each phoneme's encoding and predicts how many frames it lasts. A length regulator then simply repeats each phoneme's vector that many times, and a decoder turns the whole stretched sequence into mel frames in parallel. FastSpeech 2 adds pitch and energy predictors and learns durations from a forced aligner (the Montreal Forced Aligner) instead of from a teacher model.

🤔 Why this? The duration predictor (non-autoregressive TTS)
  1. The problem before Autoregressive acoustic models produce frames one after another (800 steps for 10 s) and rely on attention for timing; when attention fails they skip or repeat words, and nothing lets you control the speed.
  2. What people tried Location-sensitive and monotonic attention (Tacotron 2 uses one), guided attention losses: fewer failures, still sequential.
  3. Why this works Make timing an explicit number per phoneme. With durations known, the output length is fixed in advance, so every frame can be generated at once. FastSpeech reports 270× faster mel generation and 38× faster end-to-end synthesis than an autoregressive transformer TTS, almost no skipped or repeated words, and smooth speed control from 0.5× to 1.5× just by scaling the durations.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You need duration labels (from a teacher model or a forced aligner), and errors in them become robotic rhythm. Predicting one average duration, pitch and energy per phoneme makes speech flat: real speakers vary, and averaging the many valid ways to say a sentence gives a bland one.
  5. What refined it VITS learns the alignment itself and samples durations from a learned distribution (a stochastic duration predictor) for more natural rhythm; flow-matching and diffusion TTS (F5-TTS) model the full variety instead of the average.

Stage 3: the vocoder

🤔 Why this? A neural vocoder
  1. The problem before A mel spectrogram has no phase and only 80 blurry frequency rows. Turning it back into a waveform with the classic Griffin-Lim algorithm (guess the phase, iterate) gives metallic, buzzy audio; the Tacotron 2 paper calls its artifacts “characteristic”.
  2. What people tried Griffin-Lim; signal-processing vocoders from the 1990s that model pitch and spectral envelope (robotic); WaveNet as a vocoder (beautiful, but one sample at a time).
  3. Why this works A neural network learns the missing detail from data: which phase and fine harmonics make a frame sound like a real voice. HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., 2020) upsamples the mel frames with transposed convolutions straight to samples, all in parallel, and is trained against discriminators that look at the waveform at several periods and scales, so it learns what real audio sounds like rather than averaging. It generates 22.05 kHz speech 167.9× faster than real time on a V100 GPU, a tiny 0.92M-parameter version still beat the best public models of its day, and its fastest version runs 13.4× faster than real time on a CPU.
  4. What it costs, when it fails A separate model to train and ship; trained on one kind of audio it can buzz on another (singing, noise, unseen voices). And it only fixes what's in the mel picture: a bad acoustic model still sounds bad.
  5. What refined it End-to-end models (VITS) that train the vocoder jointly; neural codec decoders (Lesson 8.4) that turn discrete tokens into audio, which replaced the mel spectrogram as the intermediate in many 2024–2026 systems.

WaveNet is still worth knowing because its idea returns in Lesson 8.4: model audio as a sequence and predict the next piece. It used dilated causal convolutions (each layer looks twice as far back as the previous one, so a stack of 10 layers sees 1,024 past samples) and turned each sample into one of 256 values with μ-law companding, so predicting the next sample became a 256-way softmax. The same “make it discrete, then predict the next token” move is how codec language models work.

End to end: VITS

VITS (Kim et al., 2021) trains text → waveform as one model: a variational autoencoder (Lesson 7.2) whose latent is aligned to the text with a learned monotonic alignment, a normalizing flow to make the prior expressive, a stochastic duration predictor so rhythm varies naturally, and a HiFi-GAN-style decoder trained adversarially. On the single-speaker LJ Speech dataset its listeners' scores were comparable to the ground-truth recordings. Many open, lightweight TTS voices in 2026 are still VITS descendants or StyleTTS-style models (Kokoro-82M is built on StyleTTS 2).

Speech as tokens: codec language models

In 2023 Microsoft's VALL-E treated TTS as language modelling. A neural audio codec (EnCodec, Lesson 8.4) turns speech into discrete tokens: 75 frames a second, 8 tokens per frame from 8 stacked codebooks. Given phonemes of the new text and the tokens of a 3-second recording of any speaker, a transformer continues the token sequence in that voice, and the codec's decoder turns the tokens back into audio.

🤔 Why this? Codec language-model TTS
  1. The problem before Mel-based TTS was trained on tens to hundreds of hours of clean studio speech per voice; new voices needed fine-tuning, and speaker similarity collapsed for unseen speakers.
  2. What people tried Speaker embeddings and adaptation: fine-tune on a few minutes of the target voice.
  3. Why this works Once speech is tokens, the LLM recipe applies: train on huge, messy data (VALL-E used 60,000 hours of English, hundreds of times more than earlier TTS) and prompt it. Like a text LLM continuing a paragraph in the same style, it continues a voice prompt in the same voice, emotion and even room acoustics. A 10-second clip is a 750 × 8 token matrix; VALL-E predicts the first codebook autoregressively and the other 7 in parallel passes.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Autoregressive sampling brings back LLM failure modes: skipped or repeated words, occasional babble, slower generation. And zero-shot voice cloning from 3 seconds is a misuse risk: the VALL-E paper itself warns of “spoofing voice identification or impersonating a specific speaker”.
  5. What refined it Lower-rate codecs (Mimi at 12.5 Hz, Lesson 8.4), LLM-based streaming TTS (CosyVoice 2), flow-matching models (F5-TTS), and continuous rather than discrete speech tokens (Microsoft's VibeVoice uses 7.5 Hz continuous tokenizers to generate up to 90 minutes with up to 4 speakers).

Fast or slow: counting sequential steps

A GPU is fast at doing many things at once and slow at doing things one after another. So the number that predicts TTS speed is how many sequential network passes one second of audio needs. The real-time factor RTF = generation time ÷ audio length: below 1 means faster than real time (leaderboards often report its inverse, RTFx).

What you should see: at 1 ms per step, sample-by-sample needs 24,000 steps per second of audio: 240 s for a 10-second clip, an RTF of 24. To be real time each step must take under 1 ÷ 24,000 s ≈ 0.04 ms, which a deep network can't do; that is why WaveNet needed distillation. Frame-level AR needs 80 steps a second (0.85 s for 10 s of audio, RTF 0.085), a codec LM 75 steps plus 7 parallel passes. A fully parallel model needs just 2 passes regardless of length. At 10 ms per step (a realistic speed for a large transformer), the codec LM sits at RTF 0.79: real time, but with little headroom, which is why low-frame-rate codecs matter so much.

A model that clones a voice from seconds of audio can impersonate a CEO on a phone call or fake a relative in distress. Responsible practice in 2026: get consent from the person whose voice you clone, watermark generated audio (Chatterbox ships Resemble AI's Perth watermarker; VibeVoice adds an audible disclaimer and an imperceptible watermark), disclose AI voices to listeners, and never use voice alone as authentication. Building detection models, as the VALL-E authors suggested, is now an active field of its own.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The duration predictor plus length regulator is a few lines of PyTorch. This runs on CPU in milliseconds and shows the shapes (8 phonemes in, 65 frames out):

import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
phonemes = torch.randn(1, 8, 256)                  # encoder output: 8 phonemes × 256 features
duration_predictor = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 1))
log_d = duration_predictor(phonemes).squeeze(-1)       # predicts log-duration (trained with MSE)
durations = torch.tensor([[6, 7, 6, 14, 6, 12, 6, 8]])     # at inference: round(exp(log_d) × speed)
frames = torch.repeat_interleave(phonemes[0], durations[0], dim=0)  # the length regulator
print(log_d.shape, frames.shape, int(durations.sum()) * 256 / 22050)
# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU): torch.Size([1, 8]) torch.Size([65, 256]) 0.7546485260770975

To actually speak, use a pretrained model. Hugging Face exposes pipeline("text-to-speech", model=…) (an alias of "text-to-audio") for models that support it; Kokoro ships its own package. Both download weights, so we didn't run them here:

# pip install kokoro soundfile     (from the Kokoro-82M model card)
from kokoro import KPipeline
import soundfile as sf
tts = KPipeline(lang_code="a")                      # "a" = American English
for i, (graphemes, phonemes, audio) in enumerate(tts("Hello world!", voice="af_heart")):
    sf.write(f"{i}.wav", audio, 24000)                  # 24 kHz output

Gotcha: a vocoder only works with mel features computed exactly like its training data (sample rate, FFT size, hop, mel range, log base). Mixing an acoustic model and a vocoder from different recipes gives static or a chipmunk voice.

🎤 Interview angle

“Design the TTS for a voice assistant that must start speaking within 200 ms.” A strong answer:

  • Names the stages (text normalization, G2P, acoustic model, vocoder) and which ones can stream.
  • Argues for a parallel or streaming model: sequential steps per second of audio drive latency; RTF must be well below 1 and the first chunk must be fast (synthesize sentence by sentence).
  • Mentions control (durations for speed and emphasis, pronunciation fixes via phonemes) and the mel-recipe mismatch trap.
  • Raises voice-cloning consent, watermarking and disclosure without being asked.
🏭 In the real world
WaveNet 📜 History · 2016 van den Oord et al.: WaveNet, a generative model for raw audio

How: dilated causal convolutions predict each 16 kHz sample from the previous ones as a 256-way softmax (μ-law).

Why this method: modelling the raw waveform directly captured detail no earlier system could; DeepMind reported it closed the gap with human speech by over 50%. Its cost (one network pass per sample) drove the next five years of research.

Duration predictors 🧪 Research · 2020 Ren et al.: FastSpeech 2

How: a variance adaptor predicts duration, pitch and energy per phoneme; durations come from forced alignment; the decoder runs in parallel.

Why this method: fast, controllable and robust synthesis, with 3× faster training than the first FastSpeech.

The GAN vocoder 🧪 Research · 2020 Kong, Kim & Bae: HiFi-GAN

How: a convolutional generator upsamples 80-band mels (FFT 1024, hop 256) to waveform; multi-period and multi-scale discriminators judge it.

Why this method: near-human quality at 167.9× real time on a V100; it became the default vocoder in open TTS.

Speech as a language 🏢 Microsoft · 2023 Wang et al.: VALL-E, neural codec language models are zero-shot TTS

How: EnCodec tokens (8 codebooks, 75 Hz); an autoregressive transformer for codebook 1 and a non-autoregressive one for codebooks 2–8; trained on 60,000 hours.

Why this method: scale plus prompting gives voice cloning from 3 seconds, keeping the speaker's emotion and acoustic environment.

A small open voice 🧰 Open source Kokoro-82M on Hugging Face

How: a StyleTTS 2-based model with 82 million parameters, Apache-licensed weights, 24 kHz output.

Why this method: its card describes quality comparable to larger models while being significantly faster and cheaper, which suits on-device and high-volume use.

🤯 Fun fact · the voice keeps the room

The VALL-E paper reports that the model “could preserve the speaker's emotion and acoustic environment of the acoustic prompt”. Prompt it with a 3-second clip recorded over a phone line in an echoey room, and the new sentence comes out sounding like the same phone, in the same room. A codec LM doesn't know what a “voice” is; it just continues the token sequence, and the room is part of the sequence.

Check yourself

1. A FastSpeech-style model predicts durations of 5, 9, 4 and 12 frames for four phonemes, with a hop of 256 samples at 22,050 Hz. How long is the audio?
5 + 9 + 4 + 12 = 30 frames. Each frame is 256 ÷ 22,050 ≈ 11.6 ms, so 30 × 11.6 ms ≈ 0.35 s (7,680 samples). Multiply all durations by 1.5 and it becomes about 0.52 s: that's the speed control.
2. Your new TTS model sounds great on short sentences, but on long paragraphs it sometimes repeats a phrase or skips half a sentence. Which design is most likely in use?
Skipping and repeating are the signature failures of autoregressive TTS: if the attention jumps ahead or falls back, words are lost or repeated, and longer inputs give it more chances to slip. A duration predictor fixes the length and order in advance. A vocoder mismatch makes noise or wrong pitch, not missing words.
3. Why is generating audio sample by sample (WaveNet-style) so much slower than frame by frame?
Speed is dominated by sequential steps, because each step waits for the previous one. 24,000 ÷ 80 = 300× more steps. To be real time a sample-level model must finish each pass in about 0.04 ms. Parallel models (HiFi-GAN) remove the sequence entirely and generate all samples in one pass.
📝 TL;DR
  • TTS pipeline: text normalization → phonemes → acoustic model (mel spectrogram) → vocoder (waveform). Output grows at every stage; the text doesn't say timing or voice (one-to-many).
  • Tacotron 2 uses attention for timing (natural but slow, can skip or repeat); FastSpeech 2 predicts durations and generates in parallel (270× faster mel generation for FastSpeech, controllable speed).
  • Neural vocoders recover phase and detail; HiFi-GAN is parallel and 167.9× faster than real time on a GPU. WaveNet was one pass per sample.
  • VITS trains end to end; codec LMs (VALL-E) treat speech as tokens and clone a voice from 3 seconds, bringing LLM scale and LLM failure modes.
  • Speed ≈ sequential steps per second of audio; RTF below 1 is real time. Cloning needs consent, watermarking and disclosure.
