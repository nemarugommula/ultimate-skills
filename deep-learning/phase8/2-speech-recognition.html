Speech recognition: CTC and Whisper

One second of speech is 100 spectrogram frames and maybe a dozen letters, and nobody tells the model which frame says which letter. Two ideas solved that: a blank token that lets the model “say nothing”, and a transformer that writes the transcript like a translator.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A log-mel spectrogram: 80 rows, 100 frames per second (Lesson 8.1).
  • Softmax turns a layer's scores into probabilities over a vocabulary (Lesson 0.6); cross-entropy is the loss on them (Lesson 1.1).
  • An encoder–decoder transformer reads one sequence and writes another, one token at a time (Lessons 5.4 and 6.5).
🎯 Hook

In 2022 OpenAI trained Whisper on 680,000 hours of audio scraped from the web, with whatever transcripts happened to come with it. Nobody cleaned them by hand. In the paper's own table, English error rate fell from 30.5% with 3,405 hours of that data to 9.9% with all 681,070 hours, same recipe, more data.

Two years later, researchers auditing Whisper found that roughly 1% of transcriptions contained whole phrases or sentences that were never said, often during silences. A model that learned to write fluent transcripts from the web had also learned to make things up when there was nothing to hear.

Speech recognition (ASR, automatic speech recognition) runs captions, meeting notes, call-center analytics, medical dictation and every voice assistant. How do you train a model when you don't know which sound frame goes with which letter, and why do the two big answers (CTC and Whisper-style decoders) fail in such different ways?

Numbers first: the alignment problem

Someone says “hello” in half a second. The spectrogram has 50 frames. The transcript has 5 letters. To train with plain cross-entropy you'd need a label for every frame: frames 1–7 are silence, 8–14 are “h”, 15–19 are “e”… Nobody has those labels for 680,000 hours of audio, and the boundaries are fuzzy anyway (where exactly does “e” turn into “l”?).

So we need a loss that takes the frames and the transcript and works out the alignment by itself. That's what CTC does.

CTC: let the model say “nothing”

CTC (connectionist temporal classification; Graves, Fernández, Gomez and Schmidhuber, 2006) lets the network output one symbol per frame, from the alphabet plus one extra symbol, the blank (written _). Then a fixed rule turns the frame-by-frame string into text:

  1. Merge repeats: hhh → h. A sound that lasts several frames becomes one letter.
  2. Drop blanks.
_ h h e _ l l _ l o o _  → merge →  _ h e _ l _ l o _  → drop _ →  hello

Notice the blank between the two l's. Without it, “ll” would be merged into one “l”. The blank does two jobs: it fills frames where nothing new is being said (silence, the middle of a long vowel), and it separates genuinely doubled letters.

🤔 Why this? CTC loss
  1. The problem before Frame-level cross-entropy needs a label for every frame (50 per “hello”). Getting them meant hand segmentation or a separate alignment model (the old hidden-Markov-model pipelines), and errors in those labels became errors in the network.
  2. What people tried Hybrid systems: a neural network predicts sound classes per frame, and an HMM (hidden Markov model) plus a pronunciation dictionary does the alignment and decoding. Powerful, but many separately trained parts.
  3. Why this works CTC says: any frame string that collapses to the transcript is a correct answer. The loss is −log of the sum of the probabilities of all of them. There are a lot (8,008 ways to spell “hello” in 12 frames, as you'll see), but a dynamic program, the same table trick as edit distance, adds them up in time proportional to frames × letters. Gradients then push up whichever alignments the network already finds plausible, so the alignment is learned, not given.
  4. What it costs, when it fails CTC assumes each frame's output is independent of the others given the audio. It can't learn spelling or grammar by itself (“their/there” must come from the sound), so it's usually paired with an external language model at decoding time. Output can't be longer than the number of frames.
  5. What refined it RNN-T (Graves, 2012) adds a network that sees previous outputs, removing the independence assumption; attention-based encoder–decoders like Whisper drop alignment from the loss entirely. CTC is still everywhere because it's fast and streams well (below).
🤔 Why this? The blank token
  1. The problem before If every frame must output a letter, a 50-frame “hello” must stretch 5 letters over 50 frames, and there is no way to tell “helo” said slowly from “hello”.
  2. What people tried Predicting letter durations or a special “repeat” flag; both need frame-level labels again.
  3. Why this works A blank means “nothing new here”. Silence, pauses and the steady middle of a sound become blanks, so the model only has to be confident at a few frames: trained CTC models famously output sharp “spikes” of letters separated by long runs of blank. And “l _ l” vs “l l” is exactly the difference between “ll” and “l”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Most frames are blank, so the model's useful outputs are sparse spikes and timing is only approximate (a spike can land a little before or after the sound). Forgetting the blank between doubled letters is a classic bug in hand-written decoders.
  5. What refined it Transducers (RNN-T, TDT) use a blank-like “move to the next frame” symbol too; TDT (token-and-duration transducer) also predicts how many frames to skip, which makes decoding faster.

What you should see: “hello” has 8,008 valid 12-frame spellings out of 6¹² ≈ 2.2 billion possible strings (the counter uses this playground's six symbols: blank, space, h, e, l, o). At 6 frames there's exactly one: h e l _ l o, because the doubled l forces a blank. At 5 frames it's impossible. Paint h e l l o with no blank and you get “helo”. With 50 frames (half a second) the count passes 23 billion; that's why CTC needs a dynamic program and can't list them.

✏️ Worked example: all the ways to say “cat” in 4 frames

Four frames, three letters, so exactly one frame is “spare”. It can be a blank in any of 4 places or a repeat of any of the 3 letters:

_cat · c_at · ca_t · cat_ · ccat · caat · catt  =  7 paths

If the network gives each path some probability, CTC's loss is −log(p₁ + p₂ + … + p₇). Suppose after some training the paths have probabilities 0.20, 0.05, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.05, 0.10: the sum is 0.70, so the loss is −ln 0.70 ≈ 0.36. The gradient raises all seven, weighted by how likely each already is; the network gradually commits to the alignment that matches the audio.

Decoding: greedy and beam search

At test time, greedy CTC decoding takes the most likely symbol at every frame, then collapses. It's one argmax per frame: fast enough to run on a phone while you talk. Beam search keeps the best few partial transcripts instead and can add a language model's score, which fixes errors like “wreck a nice beach” vs “recognize speech” that sound alike. Greedy decoding picks the single best path, which isn't always the most likely text (several mediocre paths for one text can outweigh one good path for another), but in practice it's close.

Pretraining without transcripts: wav2vec 2.0

Labelled speech is rare in most languages. wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski et al., Meta, 2020) learns from audio alone, the way BERT learns from unlabelled text (Lesson 6.7): a CNN turns the raw waveform into latent frames (about 20 ms each), some spans are masked, and a transformer must pick the true (quantized) latent for each masked frame out of distractors, a contrastive task. Then a small CTC head is fine-tuned on transcripts.

Labelled data used for fine-tuningUnlabelled pretrainingWord error rate, LibriSpeech clean / other
10 minutes (48 recordings)53,000 hours4.8% / 8.2%

From the paper's abstract. Ten minutes of transcripts reaching single-digit error rates is what made self-supervised speech models the default starting point for low-resource languages.

Transducers and Conformers, in one paragraph

An RNN-T (recurrent neural network transducer; Graves, 2012) keeps CTC's frame-by-frame view but adds a small prediction network that sees the letters already emitted, so outputs can depend on each other (no independence assumption), and it still streams: it emits text while audio arrives, which is why on-device dictation uses transducers. The encoder is often a Conformer (Gulati et al., Google, 2020): a transformer block with a convolution module inside, because attention is good at global context and convolution at local patterns like a single phoneme. Its 118M-parameter model reached 2.1% / 4.3% on LibriSpeech without a language model. The 2025 paper behind Hugging Face's Open ASR Leaderboard (which compares 86 open and proprietary systems) found that Conformer encoders with transformer decoders give the best average error, while CTC and TDT decoders are the fastest. In 2026 the open models near the top include NVIDIA's Parakeet and Canary families, Qwen3-ASR, IBM's Granite Speech and Cohere Transcribe; rankings change every few months, so check the leaderboard.

Whisper: speech recognition as translation

Whisper skips alignment tricks altogether. It's a standard encoder–decoder transformer (Lesson 6.5), the same shape as a machine-translation model:

  1. The encoder reads the 80 × 3,000 log-mel picture of a 30-second window. Two 1D convolutions (the second with stride 2) turn it into 1,500 positions; transformer layers add context.
  2. The decoder writes text tokens one at a time, attending to the encoder output with cross-attention (Lesson 5.4), exactly like GPT writing the next word, but “looking at” the audio.
  3. The first few decoder tokens are instructions: which language, transcribe or translate, timestamps or not. One model does many tasks; the playground below builds that prefix.

Cross-attention learns the alignment implicitly: when the decoder writes “kitchen”, its attention points at the frames where “kitchen” was said. The cost is the opposite of CTC's: outputs depend on each other (good spelling, punctuation, casing for free), but nothing forces each word to come from the audio. That's the seed of hallucination.

🤔 Why this? Whisper's weak supervision at scale
  1. The problem before Models trained on one clean dataset (LibriSpeech: audiobooks, about 1,000 hours) scored superhuman-looking error rates on that dataset and fell apart on phone calls, accents or noise. Self-supervised models like wav2vec 2.0 needed fine-tuning per dataset.
  2. What people tried Bigger clean datasets (expensive), heavy augmentation, fine-tuning a pretrained encoder for each new domain.
  3. Why this works Trade quality for quantity: 680,000 hours of web audio with its existing, imperfect transcripts (“weak” labels), filtered with heuristics (for example removing transcripts that look machine-generated), including 117,000 hours in 96 other languages and 125,000 hours of translation into English. The model sees so many voices, microphones and rooms that it works zero-shot on new datasets. In the paper's data-scaling table, English error drops from 30.5% (3,405 hours) to 9.9% (681,070 hours).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Training cost (the large model has 1.55 billion parameters), 30-second windows that need stitching for long audio, slow autoregressive decoding, and a decoder that writes fluent text even when the audio is silent or unclear: it can repeat itself or hallucinate whole sentences.
  5. What refined it large-v3 (128 mel bins, trained on 1 million hours of weakly labelled plus 4 million hours of pseudo-labelled audio), turbo (the same model with the decoder cut from 32 layers to 4, about 8× faster), and the encoder-plus-LLM models of Lesson 8.4.

Measuring it: word error rate

To score a transcript, line it up against the reference with the smallest number of edits (the same edit distance used for spell-checkers), counting S substituted words, D deleted words and I inserted words. Then:

WER = (S + D + I) ÷ N    (N = number of words in the reference)

It's a rate of errors, not of correct words, so lower is better, and insertions can push it above 100%. Two transcripts with the same WER can be very different: one wrong word in “turn off the oven” is 20% WER and a dangerous meaning flip.

When Whisper writes what nobody said

The Whisper paper itself lists failure modes: getting stuck in repeat loops, skipping the first or last words of a window, and “complete hallucination” of a transcript unrelated to the audio. In 2024 Koenecke et al. (“Careless Whisper”) ran 13,140 audio segments through OpenAI's Whisper API (spring 2023) and found roughly 1% contained entire hallucinated phrases or sentences, and 38% of those included explicit harms such as invented violence or false authority. Hallucinations were more common for speakers with long pauses, such as people with aphasia.

Why silence? The decoder is a language model trained to produce plausible transcripts of web audio. With no speech to attend to, nothing in the audio constrains it, so its text habits take over; the Careless Whisper authors suggest that this reliance on language modelling is what produces the invented sentences. Practical fixes: cut silence first with a VAD (voice activity detection), use the model's no-speech probability, check timestamps for text inside silent regions, and for high-stakes use, compare with a CTC or transducer model, which can't easily emit long text without matching audio frames.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

PyTorch has nn.CTCLoss built in. This toy runs in a second on CPU: it learns frame-by-frame logits for “hello” from scratch, then greedy-decodes.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
vocab = ["_", "h", "e", "l", "o"]                # index 0 = blank
T, N, C = 12, 1, len(vocab)                      # 12 frames, batch of 1
logits = torch.randn(T, N, C, requires_grad=True)  # stands in for a network's output
target = torch.tensor([[1, 2, 3, 3, 4]])          # h e l l o
ctc = nn.CTCLoss(blank=0)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([logits], lr=0.1)
for step in range(200):
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = ctc(logits.log_softmax(-1), target, torch.tensor([T]), torch.tensor([5]))
    loss.backward(); opt.step()
best = logits.argmax(-1)[:, 0].tolist()             # greedy: best symbol per frame
out, prev = [], None
for i in best:                                   # merge repeats, drop blanks
    if i != prev and i != 0: out.append(vocab[i])
    prev = i
print(f"loss {loss.item():.3f}", "".join(vocab[i] for i in best), "->", "".join(out))
# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):  loss 0.006 _he_ll_loo__ -> hello

from transformers import pipeline

Gotchas: nn.CTCLoss wants log-probabilities shaped (time, batch, classes), not raw logits. Input lengths must be at least the target length plus the number of doubled letters, or the loss is infinite (set zero_infinity=True to survive bad samples). For a CTC model with pretrained weights, see Wav2Vec2ForCTC.

🎤 Interview angle

“How does CTC work, and when would you pick it over a Whisper-style model?” A strong answer:

  • States the alignment problem (frames ≫ letters, no frame labels) and the collapse rule (merge repeats, drop blanks), including why the blank is needed for doubled letters.
  • Says the loss sums over all alignments with a dynamic program, and names the cost: conditional independence between frames.
  • Contrasts with encoder–decoder attention: better text, multitask, but slower and able to hallucinate; mentions transducers for streaming.
  • Talks about WER with normalization, and mitigations for hallucination (VAD, no-speech probability, timestamp checks).
🏭 In the real world
The original CTC 📜 History · 2006 Graves et al.: Connectionist Temporal Classification (ICML 2006)

How: an RNN outputs label probabilities per frame plus an extra unit for “blank”; a forward–backward algorithm sums over all alignments.

Why this method: it trained phoneme recognizers directly on unsegmented speech, without the pre-segmented data or HMM alignment earlier neural systems needed.

Ten minutes of transcripts 🏢 Meta · 2020 Baevski et al.: wav2vec 2.0

How: masked contrastive pretraining on 53,000 hours of unlabelled audio, then CTC fine-tuning.

Why this method: most of the world's languages have little transcribed speech but plenty of audio; 10 minutes of labels reached 4.8% / 8.2% WER on LibriSpeech.

Whisper 🧰 Open source openai/whisper on GitHub

How: encoder–decoder transformer on log-mel windows, multitask special tokens, sizes from 39M to 1.55B parameters, plus turbo (809M).

Why this method: one open model that handles many languages, noise and domains zero-shot became the default starting point for transcription products and research.

Measuring the field 🧪 Research · 2025 Open ASR Leaderboard paper · live leaderboard

How: standardized text normalization, WER and inverse real-time factor (RTFx: seconds of audio processed per second of compute) across many datasets and toolkits.

Why this method: WER numbers are only comparable with identical normalization; the paper also shows the accuracy (Conformer + transformer decoder) vs speed (CTC, TDT) split.

Auditing hallucinations 🧪 Research · 2024 Koenecke et al.: Careless Whisper

How: compared Whisper API transcripts with ground truth for AphasiaBank speakers and a control group.

Why this method: WER alone hides invented sentences; they found about 1% of transcripts contained them, more often for speakers with longer pauses.

🤯 Fun fact · spikes

The 2006 CTC paper noticed that its network “predicts only the sequence of phonemes (typically as a series of spikes, separated by ‘blanks’)”. A trained CTC model is almost always saying “blank”, and fires a letter for one or two frames when it's sure. You can see it in the toy above: the learned best path _he_ll_loo__ is mostly blanks and short bursts.

Check yourself

1. A CTC model outputs the frame string _ b b _ o o _ o k k _. What text does it decode to?
Merge repeats: _ b _ o _ o k _ (the two “oo” runs stay separate because a blank sits between them). Drop blanks: book. If the model had written o o o with no blank, you'd get “bok”. Blanks are never part of the output text.
2. Reference: “call mom at five” (4 words). Hypothesis: “call my mom at nine o'clock”. What's the WER?
Best alignment: call = call, insert “my”, mom = mom, at = at, five → nine is a substitution, insert “o'clock”. S = 1, D = 0, I = 2, so WER = 3 ÷ 4 = 75%. Insertions count even though every reference word except one is right.
3. A clinic's Whisper-based note taker sometimes adds sentences like “Thank you for watching” or invented medication names during long pauses. What's the best first fix?
The pattern (text during pauses, web-caption phrases) is decoder hallucination when there's no speech to attend to, not missing vocabulary. Removing silent stretches and using Whisper's own no-speech signal attacks the cause; a CTC or transducer model as a cross-check helps for high-stakes notes. Timestamps actually help you detect it.
📝 TL;DR
  • ASR's core problem is alignment: 100 frames a second, a few letters, no frame labels.
  • CTC outputs a symbol per frame plus a blank; decode by merging repeats then dropping blanks (a blank is required between doubled letters). The loss sums over every valid alignment with a dynamic program. Fast and streamable; outputs are independent per frame.
  • wav2vec 2.0 pretrains on unlabelled audio (masked, contrastive), then fine-tunes with CTC: 10 minutes of labels → 4.8% / 8.2% WER. Transducers add dependence on previous outputs and stream; Conformers mix convolution and attention.
  • Whisper is an encoder–decoder transformer trained on 680,000 hours of weakly labelled web audio; special tokens choose language, task and timestamps. Robust zero-shot, but it can hallucinate during silence.
  • WER = (S + D + I) ÷ N, after normalization; it can exceed 100% and hides meaning flips.
