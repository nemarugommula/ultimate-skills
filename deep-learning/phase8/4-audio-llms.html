Phase 8 · Lesson 4

Audio tokens and audio language models

To let a language model listen and talk, you first need a vocabulary for sound. Neural codecs turn audio into a few hundred tokens a second, and then everything you know about LLMs applies, including their context-window bill.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Vector quantization (VQ): replace a vector by the nearest entry of a learned codebook and keep only its index (Lesson 7.8).
  • An LLM predicts the next token; its context window is how many tokens it can attend to at once (Lessons 6.5 and 6.11).
  • Whisper turns 30 s of audio into 1,500 encoder vectors; a codec LM (VALL-E) generates speech as tokens (Lessons 8.2 and 8.3).
🎯 Hook

Before May 2024, talking to ChatGPT meant a pipeline of three models: one transcribed your speech, GPT answered in text, a third read the answer aloud. OpenAI reported average latencies of 2.8 seconds (GPT-3.5) and 5.4 seconds (GPT-4). Then GPT-4o, “a single new model end-to-end across text, vision, and audio”, responded to audio in as little as 232 ms, 320 ms on average.

Months later, the French lab Kyutai published Moshi, an open model that listens and speaks at the same time, with a theoretical latency of 160 ms (200 ms in practice), below the 230 ms average gap between turns in human conversation that the authors cite.

Both rest on one trick: turn sound into tokens, so a transformer can read and write audio the way it reads and writes words. How do you get from 24,000 samples a second to about 100 tokens, and what does a model lose, or gain, by skipping text?

Audio is expensive context: even a good codec spends 10–100× more tokens than the words being said, because the tokens also carry how they're said (voice, emotion, room, background). The whole lesson is about shrinking that number without losing what matters.

Neural audio codecs

A neural audio codec is an autoencoder for sound (Lesson 7.1). An encoder of strided 1D convolutions squeezes the waveform into a short sequence of vectors (SoundStream and EnCodec: one every 320 samples, so 75 a second at 24 kHz), a quantizer turns each vector into a few integers, and a decoder rebuilds the waveform. It's trained on reconstruction plus GAN discriminators (as in HiFi-GAN, Lesson 8.3) so the result sounds real, not just close on average. Google's SoundStream (2021) at 3 kbps beat the Opus codec at 12 kbps in listening tests; Meta's EnCodec (2022) supports 1.5 to 24 kbps from one model.

The hard part is the quantizer. At 6 kbps and 75 frames a second, each frame gets 6,000 ÷ 75 = 80 bits. A plain codebook with one entry per 80-bit code would need 2⁸⁰ ≈ 10²⁴ vectors, which the SoundStream paper calls “obviously unfeasible”.

🤔 Why this? Residual vector quantization (RVQ)
  1. The problem before One VQ codebook is fine at low bitrates, but its size grows exponentially with the bits per frame: 80 bits means 2⁸⁰ entries. Fewer bits means bad audio.
  2. What people tried Product quantization (split the vector into pieces, quantize each); scalar quantization of each dimension; multiple separate models for different bitrates.
  3. Why this works Quantize in stages. Codebook 1 picks the nearest coarse entry; compute the residual (what's left: x − c₁); codebook 2 quantizes the residual; and so on. Each stage fixes part of the previous stage's error. With 8 codebooks of 1,024 entries (10 bits each), you get 80 bits per frame from only 8 × 1,024 = 8,192 stored vectors. And you can drop the last codebooks to lower the bitrate on the fly: EnCodec trains with a random number of codebooks so one model serves 1.5, 3, 6, 12 and 24 kbps.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each frame becomes a stack of tokens, so a language model must predict 8 tokens per frame (600 per second), not one. Later codebooks carry fine detail and get little use if training is careless (“dead” codewords).
  5. What refined it Lower frame rates (Mimi: 12.5 Hz), smarter ways to predict the stack (VALL-E's AR + NAR split, Moshi's small Depth Transformer and per-level delays), and finite scalar quantization in some newer codecs.

✏️ Worked example: bits, frames and tokens
  • EnCodec, 24 kHz: 75 frames/s, codebooks of 1,024 entries = 10 bits each. One codebook = 75 × 10 = 750 bps. So 1.5 kbps = 2 codebooks, 6 kbps = 8, 24 kbps = 32. At 6 kbps: 75 × 8 = 600 tokens a second.
  • VALL-E's 10-second clip: 24,000 × 10 ÷ 320 = 750 frames × 8 codebooks = a 750 × 8 token matrix (6,000 tokens).
  • Mimi: 12.5 frames/s × 8 codebooks × log₂(2,048) = 11 bits = 1,100 bps (the paper's 1.1 kbps), 100 tokens a second.
  • AudioLM's semantic tokens: 25 per second from k-means with 1,024 clusters: 25 × 10 = 250 bps.

We checked the EnCodec and Mimi shapes with randomly initialized models from Hugging Face transformers (no download): 1 s of audio gives codes of shape (1, 1, 8, 75) for EnCodec at 6 kbps and (1, 8, 13) for Mimi (12.5 frames rounds up to 13).

Two kinds of audio tokens: what is said vs how it sounds

Codec tokens are acoustic: they're optimized to rebuild the waveform, so they spend most of their bits on voice quality, noise and room. A language model trained only on them babbles: it produces convincing speech sounds that don't form words. Google's AudioLM (2022) added a second kind of token.

🤔 Why this? Semantic + acoustic tokens
  1. The problem before Acoustic tokens (SoundStream) reconstruct audio beautifully, but long-range structure (words, syntax, melody) is buried under fine detail, so a model generating them loses the thread after a second or two.
  2. What people tried Generating from text (needs transcripts), or modelling only the coarse codebooks.
  3. Why this works Get coarse semantic tokens from a self-supervised speech model (AudioLM: k-means on w2v-BERT features, 25 per second): they capture what is said, with little about who says it. Generate those first, for long-term coherence; then generate acoustic tokens conditioned on them, for quality and speaker identity. The paper shows the two kinds “complement each other”: semantic tokens for phonetics, acoustic tokens for reconstruction quality.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two or three stages of models, two tokenizers to train and keep in sync, and semantic tokens alone can't be played back.
  5. What refined it Codecs that put semantics inside the first codebook: Moshi's Mimi distills a self-supervised model (WavLM) into its first RVQ level, so one tokenizer gives both. Moshi's “Inner Monologue” adds time-aligned text tokens as an even more semantic layer.

The context bill: tokens per second

Once audio is tokens, a 5-minute phone call has to fit in the model's context window next to the instructions and the conversation history. And the model has to generate those tokens fast enough to speak in real time.

Full duplex: listening while speaking

Human conversation overlaps. We say “mm-hm” while the other person talks, we interrupt, we start answering before they've finished. A cascade waits for silence (voice activity detection decides you're done), then thinks, then speaks, and while it speaks it usually isn't listening.

🤔 Why this? Full-duplex speech models (Moshi)
  1. The problem before Turn-based systems need an end-of-turn detector: wait too long and the agent feels slow; too short and it cuts you off. They can't back-channel (“mm-hm”), and interruptions are handled by special-case logic. Every hop (ASR, LLM, TTS) adds latency, and tone of voice is lost in the text step.
  2. What people tried Streaming every stage of the cascade, faster turn detectors, sentence-by-sentence TTS (see the AI Engineer course's latency budget).
  3. Why this works Model two audio streams at once, the user's and the model's own, as parallel token streams at 12.5 Hz. At every 80 ms step the model reads the newest user tokens and writes its own, which may be silence. There are no turns: overlap, interruptions and interjections are just patterns in the two streams. Moshi also predicts time-aligned text tokens for itself (“Inner Monologue”), which the paper says significantly improves the linguistic quality of what it says. Theoretical latency: 160 ms.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You need lots of two-speaker conversational audio (or synthetic dialogues), the model's reasoning is that of a smaller LLM running at speech pace, and it's much harder to control, log and moderate than a text pipeline (no transcript to check before it speaks).
  5. What refined it Many 2025–2026 voice systems keep a strong text LLM in the loop and stream everything around it; native speech-to-speech models are improving quickly. Check current model cards before choosing.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Hugging Face transformers includes EnCodec and Mimi. Building them from their config gives random weights, no download, which is enough to check token shapes (real use: EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz") or MimiModel.from_pretrained("kyutai/mimi"), which download weights).

import torch
from transformers import EncodecConfig, EncodecModel, MimiConfig, MimiModel
torch.manual_seed(0)
wav = torch.randn(1, 1, 24000)                         # 1 s at 24 kHz (noise is fine for shapes)
cfg = EncodecConfig()
print(cfg.target_bandwidths, cfg.codebook_size)
enc = EncodecModel(cfg).eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    for bw in [1.5, 6.0, 24.0]:
        print(bw, "kbps ->", tuple(enc.encode(wav, bandwidth=bw).audio_codes.shape))
    mimi = MimiModel(MimiConfig()).eval()
    print("mimi", tuple(mimi.encode(wav, num_quantizers=8).audio_codes.shape))
# What we got (transformers 4.57, CPU):
# [1.5, 3.0, 6.0, 12.0, 24.0] 1024
# 1.5 kbps -> (1, 1, 2, 75)
# 6.0 kbps -> (1, 1, 8, 75)
# 24.0 kbps -> (1, 1, 32, 75)
# mimi (1, 8, 13)

Audio-in LLMs are available as Qwen2AudioForConditionalGeneration (with AutoProcessor) in transformers; Moshi has its own open-source package and weights (kyutai-labs/moshi). Hosted realtime voice APIs are covered in the AI Engineer course.

Gotcha: codec tokens are only meaningful to the decoder they came from. Tokens from EnCodec can't be decoded by Mimi, and changing the number of codebooks changes what a downstream LM must predict.

🎤 Interview angle

“How would you turn a text LLM into something that can hear and talk?” A strong answer:

  • Lays out the options: cascade, audio encoder + projector (audio in, text out), and native audio tokens in and out, with latency and control trade-offs.
  • Explains codec tokens: encoder → RVQ (coarse to fine codebooks, droppable for bitrate) → decoder, and computes tokens per second (frames × codebooks).
  • Distinguishes semantic from acoustic tokens and why both are needed for coherent, natural speech.
  • Budgets context (a 5-minute call at 100 tokens/s = 30,000 tokens) and sequential steps (parallel streams / depth transformer).
🏭 In the real world
The first RVQ neural codec 🏢 Google · 2021 Zeghidour et al.: SoundStream

How: convolutional encoder (stride 320 → 75 frames/s at 24 kHz), residual vector quantizer, decoder, adversarial training; runs in real time on a smartphone CPU.

Why this method: RVQ made high bitrates affordable; at 3 kbps it outperformed Opus at 12 kbps in listening tests.

One model, five bitrates 🏢 Meta · 2022 Défossez et al.: EnCodec, High Fidelity Neural Audio Compression

How: up to 32 codebooks of 1,024 entries at 24 kHz; trained with a random number of codebooks per batch.

Why this method: 1.5–24 kbps from one network; its 6 kbps tokens became the vocabulary of VALL-E and many audio generators.

Semantic then acoustic 🏢 Google · 2022 Borsos et al.: AudioLM

How: w2v-BERT semantic tokens (25/s) generated first, then SoundStream acoustic tokens (50 frames/s at 16 kHz, 600 tokens/s).

Why this method: long-term coherence from semantic tokens plus quality from acoustic ones: coherent speech and piano continuations with no text at all.

Full-duplex dialogue 🧰 Kyutai · 2024 Défossez et al.: Moshi

How: Mimi codec (12.5 Hz, 8 codebooks of 2,048, first level distilled from WavLM), Helium (a 7B text LLM) as the main transformer plus a small Depth Transformer, two audio streams plus inner-monologue text.

Why this method: no turns, no cascade: 160 ms theoretical latency, and overlap and interruptions handled naturally.

An audio encoder feeding an LLM 🏢 Alibaba Qwen · 2024 Chu et al.: Qwen2-Audio Technical Report

How: Whisper-large-v3-initialized encoder, pooling to ~40 ms per vector, into Qwen-7B; trained for voice chat and audio analysis.

Why this method: reuses the best open speech encoder and a strong LLM; one model answers questions about speech, sounds and music in text.

🤯 Fun fact · a model that can't stop listening

Moshi's text and audio tokens are offset by a small delay. The paper shows that this single hyperparameter turns the same recipe into different products: put text a little ahead of audio and you get streaming text-to-speech (they delay audio by 2 seconds), put audio ahead of text and you get streaming speech recognition, “with no changes in the loss, architecture, or training data”.

Check yourself

1. A codec runs at 50 frames per second with 4 codebooks of 1,024 entries. What are its bitrate and token rate?
1,024 entries = 10 bits per code. 50 frames × 4 codes = 200 tokens a second; × 10 bits = 2,000 bps (2 kbps). A 5-minute call would be 60,000 tokens if flattened.
2. Your speech LM trained only on EnCodec tokens produces audio that sounds like a real voice, with the right accent, but the words are gibberish after a second or two. What's the most likely fix?
Acoustic tokens spend their bits on how it sounds, so the model learns voice texture but not long-range linguistic structure. AudioLM's fix is a semantic layer generated first; Moshi adds text tokens (Inner Monologue). More codebooks make the babble sound even more realistic.
3. Why does residual vector quantization use several small codebooks instead of one big one?
Each stage quantizes what the previous ones missed, so bits add up while codebook sizes add up only linearly. 8 codebooks of 10 bits give 80 bits per frame from 8,192 vectors; one flat 80-bit codebook would need about 10²⁴. Because stages go coarse to fine, dropping the last ones still gives a usable, lower-bitrate sound.
📝 TL;DR
  • Neural codecs (SoundStream, EnCodec, Mimi) are autoencoders: conv encoder → residual vector quantizer → decoder, trained with GAN losses. Tokens per second = frame rate × codebooks.
  • RVQ quantizes the leftover error stage by stage: bits add up, codebook sizes don't explode, and dropping stages lowers the bitrate.
  • Acoustic tokens rebuild sound; semantic tokens (self-supervised features, or the first Mimi level) carry what's said. AudioLM generates semantic first, then acoustic.
  • Audio into LLMs: cascade, audio encoder + projector (Qwen2-Audio: Whisper encoder, ~25 vectors/s), or native speech-to-speech (Moshi, GPT-4o).
  • Full duplex models both speakers' streams at once (Moshi: 12.5 Hz, 160 ms theoretical latency), so interruptions and back-channels need no turn logic. Budget the context: 5 minutes at 100 tokens/s = 30,000 tokens.
