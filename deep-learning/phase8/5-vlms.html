Vision-language models: how an image becomes tokens

A language model only reads token vectors. To make it see, you turn an image into a few hundred “visual words” that land in the same space as its text embeddings. One small connector does that, and how many tokens it produces decides what the model can read and what it costs.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A ViT cuts an image into 14×14 or 16×16-pixel patches and turns each into a vector (Lesson 4.5).
  • CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder so matching pairs have similar vectors (Lesson 4.6).
  • An LLM turns each text token into an embedding vector, and self-attention cost grows with the square of the sequence length (Lessons 6.1 and 6.9).
🎯 Hook

In 2023 the LLaVA team built a model that chats about images by connecting a frozen CLIP image encoder to an open LLM with one linear layer. In LLaVA-1.5 they swapped it for a two-layer MLP and reported the connector is “surprisingly powerful and data-efficient”: about 21 million parameters, trained cheaply, bridging two models with billions each.

Meanwhile Salesforce's BLIP-2 squeezed every image into just 32 query vectors and beat DeepMind's 80-billion-parameter Flamingo by 8.7% on zero-shot visual question answering with 54× fewer trainable parameters.

Vision-language models (VLMs) now read screenshots, receipts, charts and video for chat assistants, document pipelines, agents that use computers, and robots. How does a photo become tokens, why does one model spend 32 tokens on it and another 2,700, and why do VLMs sometimes describe objects that aren't there?

Numbers first: from pixels to tokens

✏️ Worked example: LLaVA-1.5, by hand
  • Image resized to 336 × 336. Patch size 14: 336 ÷ 14 = 24 patches per side.
  • 24 × 24 = 576 patches; the CLIP ViT-L encoder turns each into a 1,024-number vector.
  • An MLP projector maps each 1,024-vector to the LLM's embedding size (4,096 for a 7B model): 576 image tokens, sitting in the prompt next to the text tokens.
  • Projector size: 1,024 × 4,096 + 4,096 + 4,096 × 4,096 + 4,096 = 20,979,712 parameters (we counted it in PyTorch, code below).

576 tokens is roughly the length of a page of text, for one small image. A 1,920 × 1,080 screenshot at native resolution with patch 14 would be 10,000+ patches. So every VLM makes a choice: shrink the image, cut it into tiles, merge neighbouring patches, or compress everything into a fixed handful of tokens.

The recipe: encoder → connector → LLM

  1. Image encoder: a pretrained ViT, usually from CLIP or SigLIP (Lesson 4.6), because contrastive training already put images near the words that describe them.
  2. Connector: turns encoder outputs into vectors the LLM can read. This is where designs differ.
  3. LLM: a pretrained text model reads image tokens and text tokens together and writes the answer.

Training is usually two stages: first train only the connector on image–caption pairs so image tokens “mean” the right words (both big models frozen), then fine-tune connector + LLM on visual instruction data (“What's unusual about this image?”).

🤔 Why this? The projector connector (LLaVA)
  1. The problem before Image encoders and LLMs live in different vector spaces. A CLIP patch vector (1,024 numbers) means nothing to a Vicuna LLM expecting 4,096-number word embeddings; feeding it in raw is like pasting random words into the prompt.
  2. What people tried Big purpose-built architectures (Flamingo's cross-attention layers, BLIP-2's Q-Former) with extensive multimodal pretraining.
  3. Why this works CLIP features already encode “what's in the patch” in a language-aligned way, so the gap between the spaces is mostly a change of coordinates. A learned matrix (or a small MLP) can rotate, scale and stretch one space onto the other. LLaVA-1.5's ablations found the MLP connector, plus better instruction data, gave state-of-the-art results across 11 benchmarks with a very simple design. You'll do it by hand in the second playground.
  4. What it costs, when it fails One token per patch: 576 at 336 px, thousands at high resolution, all paid for in context length and attention cost. Fixed-resolution encoders blur small text when big images are shrunk.
  5. What refined it Tiling (LLaVA-NeXT's AnyRes), patch merging (Qwen2-VL, InternVL), and natively variable-resolution encoders (Qwen2-VL's “naive dynamic resolution”).
🤔 Why this? The Q-Former (BLIP-2)
  1. The problem before Training a vision-language model end to end is expensive, and passing hundreds of patch tokens per image into an LLM costs context and compute. Flamingo trained new cross-attention layers inside an 80B model.
  2. What people tried Average-pooling the patch features (loses detail), or cross-attention adapters inside the LLM (many new parameters).
  3. Why this works A small transformer (188M parameters, initialized from BERT) holds 32 learned query vectors. They cross-attend to the frozen image encoder's patches and pull out what's useful, like 32 reporters each asking the picture a question. It's pretrained in stages to extract text-relevant features, then its 32 outputs go to a frozen LLM. The image always costs 32 tokens, whatever its resolution.
  4. What it costs, when it fails 32 vectors can't hold a full page of small text or a dense chart; fine-grained reading suffers. LLaVA-1.5, with a plain MLP and 576 tokens, later scored well above InstructBLIP (a Q-Former model) on benchmarks such as GQA.
  5. What refined it InstructBLIP (instruction-aware queries); Flamingo's Perceiver Resampler is the same “learned queries” idea (64 tokens); query-based compression returns in video models where frames are numerous.

High resolution: tiles and native resolution

A 336 × 336 encoder can't read a receipt photographed at 3,000 × 4,000 pixels. Two families of fixes:

  • Tiling: cut the image into encoder-sized tiles, encode each, and add one downscaled “thumbnail” of the whole image for global context. LLaVA-NeXT's AnyRes uses grids of 2×2, 1×{2,3,4} or {2,3,4}×1 tiles of 336 px (up to 672 × 672 or 336 × 1,344); InternVL 1.5 uses 1 to 40 tiles of 448 px plus a thumbnail, with a pixel shuffle that cuts tokens to a quarter (1,024 patches → 256 tokens per tile).
  • Native resolution: let the ViT take any size. Qwen2-VL resizes each side to a multiple of 28, uses 14 px patches with 2D rotary positions (M-RoPE) instead of fixed position embeddings, and an MLP merges each 2×2 block of patch vectors into one token. A 224 × 224 image becomes 64 tokens plus 2 special markers = 66, the paper's own example.
🤔 Why this? Dynamic resolution and tiling
  1. The problem before Fixed-resolution encoders squash every image to 224 or 336 px. A full-page document shrunk that far is unreadable, and a wide panorama gets distorted or padded with black bars. The LLaVA-NeXT authors say low resolution also makes models “conjecture” (hallucinate) visual content.
  2. What people tried Bigger fixed resolutions (cost grows with the square of the side), interpolating the ViT's position embeddings (works only so far).
  3. Why this works Spend tokens where the pixels are. Tiling reuses the same pretrained encoder at its native size, so nothing has to be relearned; native resolution keeps the aspect ratio and lets the token count follow the image. Merging 2×2 neighbours keeps most information (neighbouring patches are similar) at a quarter of the tokens.
  4. What it costs, when it fails A big image can now cost thousands of tokens: four Full-HD screenshots exceed an 8k budget (try it). Tiles cut objects at tile borders, which is why a thumbnail is added. Systems need min/max pixel limits so a giant image can't blow the context (Qwen2-VL trains with up to 16,384 tokens per image).
  5. What refined it Per-request resolution controls in hosted APIs, token pruning and merging inside the LLM, and video-specific compression (below).

Natively multimodal: early fusion

Everything so far is late fusion: a separately pretrained vision model is bolted onto a separately pretrained LLM. The alternative is to train one model on mixed sequences of text and image tokens from the start.

🤔 Why this? Early fusion (Chameleon)
  1. The problem before Bolted-on VLMs can read images but usually can't write them, and the LLM's knowledge was learned without ever seeing a picture; vision is a guest in its context.
  2. What people tried Separate image generators called as tools; adapters added late in training.
  3. Why this works Meta's Chameleon (2024) tokenizes each 512 × 512 image into 1,024 discrete tokens from a codebook of 8,192 (the VQ idea of Lesson 7.8), puts them in the same 65,536-entry vocabulary as text, and trains one transformer from scratch on about 10 trillion tokens of interleaved text, images and code (including 1.4 billion text–image pairs). Text and image tokens are equal citizens, so it can answer about an image and generate images in any order (“here's the recipe, with a picture of each step”). Meta's Llama 4 also describes “native multimodality, incorporating early fusion” of text and vision tokens in one backbone, with a MetaCLIP-based vision encoder.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Very expensive to train from scratch, and the Chameleon team reports optimization-stability problems that needed architectural changes (such as normalizing queries and keys, “QK-norm”). A discrete image tokenizer loses detail: the paper names reconstructing images with lots of text as “a core weakness”, which limits OCR-heavy tasks.
  5. What refined it Hybrid designs: continuous patch embeddings for understanding with discrete or diffusion heads for generation; Llama 4's early fusion with a learned vision encoder rather than discrete tokens.

Video, evaluation and hallucination

Video is images × time, so the token bill explodes: one minute at 2 frames a second is 120 images. Models subsample frames, lower the per-frame resolution, merge adjacent frames (Qwen2-VL's depth-2 3D convolution halves the count) and cap the total (16,384 tokens per video in Qwen2-VL's training). Fine timing (“when exactly did the ball cross the line?”) is still hard.

Evaluation uses visual question answering sets (VQAv2, GQA, the ones BLIP-2 and LLaVA-1.5 report), OCR and chart tests, and multi-discipline reasoning benchmarks. The failure that worries practitioners most is object hallucination: describing things that aren't in the image. POPE (Li et al., 2023) measures it by polling the model with simple yes/no questions (“Is there a knife in the image?”) about objects that are present and absent. Absent objects are sampled three ways: random, popular (frequent in the dataset) and adversarial (often co-occur with what's there: a fork next to a plate). The paper found objects that frequently appear in training data or co-occur with the image's objects are the most likely to be hallucinated: the LLM's prior (“kitchens have knives”) beats the pixels.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The token arithmetic and the projector are plain PyTorch; this runs on CPU in a second:

import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
img = torch.randn(1, 3, 336, 336)                          # one 336×336 RGB image
patchify = nn.Conv2d(3, 1024, kernel_size=14, stride=14)     # ViT-L/14 patch embedding
feats = patchify(img).flatten(2).transpose(1, 2)            # (batch, patches, 1024)
projector = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1024, 4096), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(4096, 4096))
tokens = projector(feats)                                     # LLaVA-1.5-style MLP connector
print(tuple(feats.shape), tuple(tokens.shape), f"{sum(p.numel() for p in projector.parameters()):,}")
# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU): (1, 576, 1024) (1, 576, 4096) 20,979,712

For a real model, Hugging Face transformers has AutoProcessor plus AutoModelForImageTextToText (or LlavaForConditionalGeneration) and a matching "image-text-to-text" pipeline; Qwen2-VL-family processors take min_pixels / max_pixels to cap tokens per image. These download several GB of weights, so they aren't run here. Using hosted vision APIs and their per-image token prices is covered in AI Engineer 8.4.

Gotcha: the image placeholder in the prompt must match what the processor expects (it expands one <image> marker into hundreds of token slots). Build prompts with the processor's chat template rather than by hand, or the model sees misaligned image tokens.

🎤 Interview angle

“How does a VLM turn an image into something an LLM can use, and how would you control its cost?” A strong answer:

  • Encoder (CLIP/SigLIP ViT) → connector → LLM; computes patches = (side ÷ patch)², e.g. 576 at 336 px with patch 14.
  • Compares connectors: MLP projector (per-patch tokens), Q-Former/resampler (fixed few), cross-attention (Flamingo), early fusion.
  • Handles high resolution with tiling plus a thumbnail or native resolution with 2×2 merging; caps tokens with min/max pixels; crops to the region that matters.
  • Names object hallucination, how POPE measures it (yes/no polling with random, popular and adversarial negatives), and that low resolution makes it worse.
🏭 In the real world
The simple projector 🧪 Research · 2023 Liu et al.: Improved Baselines with Visual Instruction Tuning (LLaVA-1.5)

How: CLIP-ViT-L-336px encoder, a two-layer MLP connector (the original LLaVA used a single linear layer), Vicuna LLM, plus academic VQA data with formatting prompts.

Why this method: a tiny trainable bridge between two frozen-at-first giants; state of the art across 11 benchmarks at the time with a very simple recipe.

32 learned queries 🏢 Salesforce · 2023 Li et al.: BLIP-2

How: a 188M-parameter Q-Former with 32 queries between a frozen image encoder and a frozen LLM.

Why this method: compute-efficient: outperformed Flamingo-80B by 8.7% on zero-shot VQAv2 with 54× fewer trainable parameters.

Gated cross-attention 🏢 DeepMind · 2022 Alayrac et al.: Flamingo

How: a Perceiver Resampler gives 64 visual tokens per image or video; new cross-attention layers interleaved in a frozen LM, each multiplied by tanh(α) with α starting at 0.

Why this method: the tanh gate makes the new layers start as “off”, so the model initially behaves exactly like the original LM and learns to look at images gradually; strong few-shot learning from interleaved web data.

Native dynamic resolution 🏢 Alibaba Qwen · 2024 Wang et al.: Qwen2-VL

How: a 675M-parameter ViT that accepts any resolution, M-RoPE positions, 2×2 token merging, video at 2 fps with 3D convolutions.

Why this method: the token count follows the image, so documents and screenshots keep their detail and aspect ratio; the same ViT serves 2B, 7B and 72B LLMs.

Measuring hallucination 🧪 Research · 2023 Li et al.: Evaluating Object Hallucination in Large VLMs (POPE)

How: yes/no object questions with random, popular and adversarial absent objects; scored as binary classification.

Why this method: caption-based metrics were unstable; polling is simple, stable and exposes the language prior directly.

🤯 Fun fact · same encoder, three brains

Qwen2-VL uses the same 675M-parameter vision encoder for its 2B, 7B and 72B language models. In the 2B model the eyes are almost half the size of the brain (675M vs 1.5B); in the 72B model they're under 1%. The paper's stated reason: the encoder's compute stays constant whatever the size of the LLM.

Check yourself

1. A ViT with 16-pixel patches reads a 448 × 448 image, and a 2×2 merge follows. How many image tokens reach the LLM?
448 ÷ 16 = 28 patches per side, 28 × 28 = 784 patches. Merging each 2×2 block divides by 4: 196 tokens. Without the merge it would be 784; 28 is just the patches along one side.
2. Your invoice-reading VLM (LLaVA-1.5-style, 336 px input) gets totals wrong on full-page scans but fine on cropped photos of the total. What's the most likely cause and fix?
A full A4 page at 336 px has digits a few pixels tall: no connector can recover detail the encoder never saw. Cropped photos work because the digits are large. Tiling (AnyRes, InternVL) or native resolution (Qwen2-VL) spends more tokens to keep the pixels; cropping is the cheapest fix of all.
3. On POPE, a VLM answers “yes” to “Is there a fork in the image?” for most kitchen photos, even when no fork is visible. Which POPE setting exposes this best, and why does it happen?
POPE polls yes/no about objects; its adversarial negatives are chosen from objects that frequently co-occur with the image's real objects. The POPE paper found exactly those co-occurring and frequent objects are the most hallucinated. Higher resolution, better grounding data and penalizing “yes” bias help.
📝 TL;DR
  • VLM = image encoder (CLIP/SigLIP ViT) → connector → LLM. Patch tokens = (side ÷ patch)²: 336 px at patch 14 = 576 (LLaVA-1.5).
  • Connectors: MLP projector (one token per patch, simple, strong), Q-Former (32 fixed tokens), Perceiver Resampler + gated cross-attention (Flamingo, 64), patch merging (÷4), or early fusion (Chameleon: 1,024 discrete tokens per 512² image).
  • High resolution: tiles + thumbnail (AnyRes, InternVL) or native resolution with 2×2 merging (Qwen2-VL: 224² → 66 tokens). Cap tokens with min/max pixels; crop when you can.
  • Video multiplies tokens by frames: subsample, merge frames, cap the total. Attention cost grows with tokens².
  • Object hallucination comes from the LLM's prior; POPE polls yes/no with random, popular and adversarial negatives.
