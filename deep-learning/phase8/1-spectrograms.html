Phase 8 · Lesson 1

Sound as pictures: waveforms and spectrograms

Almost no speech model listens to sound directly. They look at a picture of it. Once you can draw that picture yourself, every audio model in this phase is a vision or sequence model you already know.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A convolution slides a small filter over a grid of numbers and is good at local patterns like edges (Lesson 3.2).
  • A vision transformer (ViT) cuts an image into patches and treats each patch as a token (Lesson 4.5).
  • A 1D convolution slides a filter along a sequence (Lesson 5.5).
🎯 Hook

OpenAI's Whisper was trained on 680,000 hours of audio in 97 languages. Here's the surprise: its neural network never sees a sound wave. Every 30-second clip is first turned into a grid of 80 rows × 3,000 columns, a small grayscale picture, and the network reads that.

The same trick powers voice assistants, meeting transcribers, music taggers and the text-to-speech voices in Lesson 8.3: turn sound into a picture, then use the image and sequence tools you already have.

So how does a wiggly line become a picture, and why does nearly every speech model use the same odd settings: 16,000 samples a second, 25 ms windows, 10 ms steps, 80 “mel” rows, and a logarithm? By the end you'll have drawn your own spectrograms, broken one on purpose, and know why each number is there.

Numbers first: a sound is a long list of numbers

A microphone measures air pressure many times a second. Each measurement is one number, called a sample. The sample rate is how many samples you take per second.

“Hz” (hertz) means “per second”. A 30-second voice note at 16 kHz is a vector of 480,000 numbers. Feeding that straight into a transformer would mean 480,000 tokens and attention over 480,000² ≈ 2.3 × 10¹¹ pairs. That's the first reason we need a better representation.

The last column is the Nyquist limit: with a sample rate of S you can only capture frequencies up to S ÷ 2. To see a wave go up and down you need at least two samples per cycle. Most of what makes speech understandable sits below 8 kHz, so 16 kHz is the sweet spot for speech recognition. Try to record something higher and it doesn't vanish: it folds back and shows up as a fake lower pitch (aliasing). You'll cause that in the first playground.

Frequency: what the ear actually cares about

A pure tone is a sine wave. Its frequency is how many times per second it repeats: 440 Hz is the A that orchestras tune to. Real sounds are many sine waves added together. A spoken vowel is a low buzz from your vocal cords (say 120 Hz) plus copies at 240, 360, 480 Hz… called harmonics, with some of them made louder by the shape of your mouth. Change your mouth shape and you change which harmonics are loud: that's the difference between “ee” and “oo”.

The Fourier transform takes a chunk of samples and answers: how much of each frequency is in here? Its fast algorithm, the FFT, does it in about N log N steps. You don't need its formula. You need one fact: give it N samples and it returns N ÷ 2 + 1 frequency “bins”, evenly spaced from 0 Hz up to the Nyquist limit.

🧠 Analogy: a prism for sound

A prism splits white light into a rainbow, showing how much red, green and blue it contains. The FFT is a prism for a short chunk of sound: it splits the chunk into its frequencies and tells you how bright each one is. A spectrogram is what you get when you hold the prism up to the sound every 10 ms and stack the rainbows side by side, like frames of a film.

✏️ Worked example: Whisper's numbers, by hand
  • Window: 25 ms at 16,000 Hz = 0.025 × 16,000 = 400 samples.
  • Frequency bins: 400 ÷ 2 + 1 = 201, spaced 16,000 ÷ 400 = 40 Hz apart, from 0 to 8,000 Hz.
  • A 440 Hz tone lands in bin 440 ÷ 40 = 11.
  • Hop: 10 ms = 160 samples, so 100 frames a second; 30 s → 3,000 frames.
  • Squash the 201 bins into 80 mel rows (below): the picture is 80 × 3,000 = 240,000 numbers, half the 480,000 raw samples, and far easier to read.

We checked these with torch.stft and Hugging Face's Whisper feature extractor (code in the tools box below): the 440 Hz tone peaks in bin 11, and the features come out as (1, 80, 3000).

The spectrogram: the FFT, again and again

One FFT of a whole recording tells you which frequencies occur but not when. Speech changes every few tens of milliseconds, so we use the short-time Fourier transform (STFT):

  1. Cut a short window of samples (25 ms).
  2. Taper its edges with a smooth bump (a Hann window), so the hard cut at each edge doesn't add fake frequencies of its own.
  3. FFT it, keep the size (magnitude) of each frequency: one column of the picture.
  4. Slide forward by a hop (10 ms) and repeat. Windows overlap, so nothing falls between the cracks.

Put time on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis (low at the bottom) and brightness for loudness. That's the spectrogram. A steady tone is a horizontal line. A siren is a line that slides up. A clap is a vertical line, because it's every frequency at one instant.

💡 Big idea A spectrogram turns a sound into an image where position means something: x is time, y is pitch. Patterns that matter (a vowel's harmonics, a rising question, an “s” hiss) become local shapes. Local shapes are exactly what convolutions and patch-based transformers are built to find.
🤔 Why this? The spectrogram
  1. The problem before Raw audio is enormous (16,000 numbers a second) and the same word looks completely different as a waveform when it's said a bit louder, or with a shifted phase. A network reading samples must first learn on its own what a frequency is.
  2. What people tried Hand-made features from the 1980s on: MFCCs (below), fed to statistical models of speech. Later, neural networks on raw waveforms, which work but need a lot of data and compute.
  3. Why this works The FFT is a fixed, free, perfect “first layer”: it turns 400 jumpy samples into 201 numbers that say how much of each pitch is present, and it throws away phase, which the ear mostly ignores. Ten milliseconds becomes one column, so 30 s is 3,000 time steps instead of 480,000.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Throwing phase away means you can't simply turn a spectrogram back into sound: text-to-speech needs a learned vocoder for that (Lesson 8.3). And you must pick a window size, which trades time detail against pitch detail (Playground 2).
  5. What refined it The mel scale and the log (below) make it closer to how we hear. wav2vec 2.0 (Lesson 8.2) learns its own features from raw waveforms with a CNN, and neural codecs (Lesson 8.4) learn a compressed representation from the waveform.

What you should see: the 500 → 2,500 Hz glide draws a straight diagonal line climbing to the right. Two steady tones draw two horizontal lines. Three tones at 300, 600 and 900 Hz make the “ladder” that every voiced sound has. And the alias trap: a 5,000 Hz tone shows up at 3,000 Hz (it folds back from 4,000 by the same 1,000 Hz it overshot). Glide a tone from 3,000 to 6,000 Hz and the line climbs, hits the ceiling and bounces back down. This is why recorders filter out everything above the Nyquist limit before sampling: once folded, a fake pitch is indistinguishable from a real one.

The one setting you can't win: window size

A longer window contains more cycles of each wave, so the FFT can tell close pitches apart. But a longer window also blurs everything that happens inside it into one column. Pitch detail and time detail pull in opposite directions.

What you should see: at 32 ms (256 samples) the clicks are two crisp vertical lines but the tones are one fat band (bins are 31 Hz apart and each tone smears over several bins). At 64 ms the tones split into two lines, and the clicks start to merge into one blob. At 128 ms the tones are razor-sharp and the clicks are one smudge. No window gets both. Speech models settle on about 25 ms: short enough to catch the quickest sounds (a “t” or “k” burst), long enough to resolve the harmonics of a voice. That's a design choice, not a law of nature, and it's why Whisper's numbers look the way they do.

Two fixes that make it sound like hearing: mel and log

A linear spectrogram has two problems. Its bins are spread evenly, so 75% of them describe the 2,000–8,000 Hz range where speech carries less information per hertz. And its brightness is linear, so a quiet sound next to a loud one is basically invisible. Human hearing works differently on both counts.

🤔 Why this? The mel scale
  1. The problem before With 201 even bins (40 Hz each), the step from 100 to 200 Hz and the step from 7,000 to 7,100 Hz get the same resolution. To a listener, the first is a whole octave (a huge jump in pitch) and the second is barely noticeable.
  2. What people tried Using all the linear bins (more numbers, mostly spent on high frequencies), or hand-picked frequency bands.
  3. Why this works The mel scale comes from 1937 listening experiments (Stevens, Volkmann and Newman) asking people to judge equal steps of pitch. A common formula is mel = 2595 · log₁₀(1 + f ÷ 700): roughly linear below 1,000 Hz and logarithmic above. 100 → 200 Hz is 133 mels; 7,000 → 7,100 Hz is only about 15. A mel filterbank is a set of overlapping triangle filters, narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones, spaced evenly in mels. Each triangle sums the FFT bins under it into one row. Whisper uses 80 of them (128 in large-v3).
  4. What it costs, when it fails It throws away high-frequency detail on purpose, which hurts tasks where that detail matters (bird song, some music, machine noise). With too many mel rows for a small FFT, the lowest triangles become narrower than one bin and come out empty (you'll see this at 128 rows below).
  5. What refined it Learnable filterbanks and models that read raw waveforms (wav2vec 2.0, Lesson 8.2; neural codecs, Lesson 8.4). For speech recognition, plain log-mel is still the default input in 2026.
🤔 Why this? Log compression (decibels)
  1. The problem before Sound energy spans a huge range: a whisper and a shout differ by a factor of thousands in amplitude. On a linear scale the loudest bin sets the brightness and everything 30× quieter is near black, yet you can hear it perfectly well.
  2. What people tried Normalizing each clip by its maximum (still linear, still invisible), or clipping the loud parts.
  3. Why this works Loudness perception is roughly logarithmic: each 10× in power sounds like a similar step. So we take log(magnitude), usually in decibels: 20 · log₁₀(amplitude ratio). A tone 1/30 as strong as the loudest one is at 3% brightness on a linear scale but at −30 dB, halfway up a 60 dB display. The log also turns a volume change (multiplying the whole sound by 2) into a simple shift (+6 dB everywhere), which is easy for a network to ignore after normalization.
  4. What it costs, when it fails log(0) is −∞, so you add a tiny floor (Tacotron 2 clips magnitudes to a minimum before the log; Whisper clamps to 8 orders of magnitude below the maximum). The floor amplifies faint background noise into visible texture.
  5. What refined it Per-dataset normalization (Whisper scales its features to roughly −1…1) and learned compression like PCEN in some keyword-spotting systems.

MFCCs: the feature that ruled for 30 years

Before deep learning, speech systems took the log-mel spectrogram one step further: a cosine transform over each column, keeping only the first dozen or so numbers. These are MFCCs (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients). They describe the overall shape of the spectrum (the mouth shape) and discard fine detail, and their numbers are nearly uncorrelated, which suited the simple statistical models of the time. Neural networks don't need uncorrelated inputs and do better with the fuller log-mel picture, so MFCCs are mostly history now. You'll still see them in tiny on-device models and older papers.

Why vision models can read sound

A log-mel spectrogram is a one-channel image: 80 rows, 100 columns per second. Everything from Phase 3 and Phase 4 applies, with one twist: the two axes mean different things. Shifting a pattern along time doesn't change what it is (a word said a little later is the same word), which is exactly the translation invariance convolutions give you. Shifting along frequency changes the pitch, which may or may not matter.

  • Whisper runs two small 1D convolutions along time over the 80 mel rows (the second with stride 2, so 3,000 frames become 1,500 positions), then a transformer encoder (Lesson 8.2).
  • AST (Audio Spectrogram Transformer, 2021) cuts the spectrogram into 16×16 patches exactly like a ViT (Lesson 4.5) and was the first purely attention-based audio classifier, starting from ImageNet-pretrained weights.
  • Classic keyword spotters (“Hey …”) run small 2D CNNs over a second of log-mel on a phone.
🤔 Why this? SpecAugment
  1. The problem before Speech models overfit: labelled speech is expensive, and a model can memorize the exact voices and rooms in its training set. Older augmentations worked on the waveform (speed changes, added noise, simulated rooms) and were slow to compute and to tune.
  2. What people tried Speed perturbation, noise mixing, room simulation: helpful but expensive, and done before training.
  3. Why this works Park et al. (Google, 2019) augment the picture instead: warp it slightly in time, blank out a few random bands of mel rows (frequency masking) and a few random stretches of frames (time masking). It's Cutout from vision applied to spectrograms. The model must recognize a word even with part of its pitch range or a few milliseconds missing, so it learns redundant cues. It's nearly free and runs on the fly. With it, their model reached 6.8% word error on LibriSpeech test-other without a language model, beating the previous best hybrid system (7.5%).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Masks that are too wide erase whole words and the model can't learn from those examples. Mask widths and counts are hyperparameters, and very long masks hurt small datasets.
  5. What refined it Adaptive masking that scales with the clip length; it is still a default ingredient when training or fine-tuning speech models from scratch, alongside noise augmentation.
🧰 Tools you'll actually use

torch.stft computes the STFT. Hugging Face transformers ships Whisper's exact feature pipeline as WhisperFeatureExtractor; constructing it with settings downloads nothing. torchaudio.transforms.MelSpectrogram, FrequencyMasking and TimeMasking do mel features and SpecAugment (torchaudio wasn't installed on our test laptop, so that part isn't run here). librosa is the classic NumPy toolbox.

import torch, math
sr = 16000
t = torch.arange(sr) / sr                        # 1 second
x = torch.sin(2 * math.pi * 440 * t)             # an A4 tone
S = torch.stft(x, n_fft=400, hop_length=160,         # 25 ms window, 10 ms hop
               window=torch.hann_window(400), return_complex=True)
print(S.shape)                                   # (frequency bins, frames)
peak = S.abs().mean(1).argmax().item()
print("peak bin:", peak, "->", peak * sr / 400, "Hz")

from transformers import WhisperFeatureExtractor
fe = WhisperFeatureExtractor(feature_size=80)       # Whisper's log-mel recipe (128 for large-v3)
feats = fe(x.numpy(), sampling_rate=sr, return_tensors="pt").input_features
print(feats.shape, fe.n_fft, fe.hop_length)      # padded to 30 s

# What we got (torch 2.8, transformers 4.57, CPU):
# torch.Size([201, 101])

Gotcha: resample to the rate the model expects (16 kHz for Whisper) before computing features. A 44.1 kHz file read as if it were 16 kHz plays almost 3× too slowly to the model, and accuracy collapses without any error message.

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through what happens to audio before it reaches a speech model.” A strong answer:

  • Resample (16 kHz for speech; Nyquist: max frequency = half the rate), split into ~25 ms windows every 10 ms, taper with a Hann window, FFT, keep magnitudes.
  • Apply a mel filterbank (80 or 128 triangles) and a log; normalize. Result: about 100 frames a second × 80 features.
  • Explains why: mel matches pitch perception, log matches loudness perception and turns gain into an offset, the window trades time against frequency resolution.
  • Mentions SpecAugment for training, and that the picture is not invertible without a vocoder.
🏭 In the real world
Whisper reads 80-row log-mel pictures 🧪 Research · 2022 Radford et al.: Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision

How: audio resampled to 16,000 Hz; an 80-channel log-magnitude mel spectrogram on 25 ms windows with a 10 ms stride, scaled to about −1…1, in 30-second segments.

Why this method: a compact, standard input lets one encoder–decoder transformer train on 680,000 hours from the web; large-v3 later moved to 128 mel bins.

SpecAugment 🏢 Google · 2019 Park et al.: SpecAugment

How: time warping plus frequency and time masks applied to the log-mel spectrogram on the fly during training.

Why this method: turns an overfitting problem into a reading-with-gaps problem at almost no cost; it set state-of-the-art results on LibriSpeech and Switchboard at the time.

A ViT for sound 🧪 Research · 2021 Gong, Chung & Glass: AST, Audio Spectrogram Transformer

How: the spectrogram is split into overlapping 16×16 patches, linearly projected and fed to a transformer, starting from ImageNet ViT weights.

Why this method: if sound is a picture, the best picture model can be reused; it reached state-of-the-art audio classification without any convolution.

Mel spectrograms as the target for speech synthesis 🏢 Google · 2017 Shen et al.: Tacotron 2

How: 50 ms frames, 12.5 ms hop, an 80-channel mel filterbank from 125 Hz to 7.6 kHz, then log compression; a vocoder turns the predicted picture into sound.

Why this method: predicting a small, smooth picture is much easier than predicting 24,000 samples a second; Lesson 8.3 runs this in reverse.

🤯 Fun fact · the 30-second box

Whisper was trained only on 30-second chunks and “cannot consume longer audio inputs at once”, in its paper's words. A 10-second clip is padded with silence to fill the box (that's why the feature extractor above returned 3,000 frames for one second of audio). Long recordings are transcribed 30 seconds at a time, shifting the window using the timestamps the model predicts. The padding with silence matters again in Lesson 8.2, where silence is where Whisper sometimes invents sentences.

Check yourself

1. You compute a spectrogram of 16 kHz audio with a 25 ms window and a 10 ms hop. How many frequency bins per frame, and how many frames for 10 seconds?
25 ms × 16,000 = 400 samples, and an FFT of 400 samples gives 400 ÷ 2 + 1 = 201 bins (40 Hz apart). A 10 ms hop is 100 frames a second, so 10 s ≈ 1,000 frames. 80 is the number of rows after a mel filterbank, not the FFT's output.
2. A team feeds 44.1 kHz podcast audio to a 16 kHz speech model without resampling, and the transcripts are gibberish. A colleague suggests the model just needs fine-tuning on podcasts. What's going on?
Frequencies are computed from sample positions, so the wrong rate scales every pitch and every duration: voices look like slow, deep growls the model never saw. No error is raised. Always resample to the model's rate before feature extraction. (44.1 kHz audio has a Nyquist limit of 22 kHz, more than enough.)
3. Why do speech models use log-mel features instead of the linear magnitude spectrogram?
Both changes copy human hearing. Mel's triangles are narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones, so 80 rows cover what 201 linear bins did, with detail where speech needs it. The log compresses a range of thousands into tens of decibels, and doubling the volume becomes +6 dB everywhere. Neither removes noise or helps invertibility (both make inverting harder).
📝 TL;DR
  • Audio is a list of samples: 16,000 a second for speech. You can only capture frequencies up to half the sample rate (Nyquist); higher ones fold back as fake pitches.
  • A spectrogram = FFT of short overlapping windows (25 ms, every 10 ms): time on x, pitch on y, loudness as brightness. Window length trades time detail for pitch detail.
  • Mel filterbanks (80 or 128 rows) match pitch perception; the log matches loudness perception. Log-mel is the standard input to speech recognition (Whisper: 80 × 3,000 per 30 s).
  • Because it's an image, CNNs and ViT-style transformers read it directly. SpecAugment masks bands and time slices for cheap, strong regularization.
  • Phase is thrown away, so turning a mel picture back into sound needs a vocoder (8.3).
