Phase 7 · Lesson 3

GANs and mode collapse

Two networks play a game: one forges, one catches forgeries. Nobody ever tells the forger what a face looks like, yet by 2018 its faces fooled people. Then you'll train one live, watch it cover all eight targets, and make it collapse onto one with a single knob.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A binary classifier ends in a sigmoid (a 0–1 “probability of yes”) and trains with binary cross-entropy: loss = −log(probability given to the right answer) (Lessons 0.6, 1.1; math 6.3).
  • Backprop can push gradients through one network into another, all the way to its input (Lesson 1.2).
  • VAE outputs are blurry because pixel-wise losses reward averaging plausible answers (Lesson 7.2).
🎯 Hook

In 2014, Ian Goodfellow and colleagues proposed training a generator with no pixel loss at all. Instead, a second network, the discriminator, looks at real photos and the generator's fakes and tries to tell them apart. The generator's only feedback is “how badly did I fool the discriminator?”

Four years later, NVIDIA's StyleGAN was generating 1024×1024 portraits of people who don't exist, trained on 70,000 photos of faces (the FFHQ dataset). No blur: a discriminator punishes blur instantly, because real photos are never blurry.

But GANs had a famous weakness. Ask one to learn eight different things and it often learns one, perfectly, then hops to another, never settling. Why does a forger give up on variety, and why did diffusion models take over image generation anyway? You'll cause the collapse yourself below.

Where GANs live today: the adversarial loss that keeps Stable Diffusion's autoencoder and VQGAN's decoder sharp, the 1–4-step distilled generators (SDXL Turbo, FLUX.1 [schnell]), and super-resolution upscalers.

Numbers first: one round of the game

The discriminator D outputs a probability that its input is real. The generator G turns random noise z into a fake sample G(z). One round:

InputD says “real” with probabilityD's loss on it (wants real → 1, fake → 0)
a real photo xD(x) = 0.9−ln 0.9 = 0.105
a fake G(z)D(G(z)) = 0.2−ln(1 − 0.2) = 0.223

D's total loss is 0.328: it's doing well. Now G's turn. G wants D(G(z)) to go up. Its loss is the opposite of D's on the fake: it gets better as 0.2 rises toward 1. G can't change the real photos, and it can't change D's weights on its turn; it changes its own weights so that its fakes land where D says “real”. The gradient travels backwards through D (frozen), into the fake image, and on into G.

Then D trains again on the new fakes, G again, and so on. Neither network ever sees a “correct” image to copy. The discriminator is a learned loss function that keeps getting stricter.

💡 Big idea A GAN replaces a hand-written loss (“match these pixels”) with a learned critic. The discriminator learns what makes real data look real; the generator follows its gradient toward that. Because a sharp, realistic sample is the only thing that fools a good critic, GANs make sharp outputs. Because it's a two-player game rather than a single loss going downhill, training can oscillate, diverge or collapse.
🧠 Analogy: a counterfeiter and a bank teller

A counterfeiter prints bills; a teller checks them. Every rejected bill teaches the counterfeiter something (“the watermark was off”). Every bill that slipped through, once discovered, teaches the teller. Neither has a rulebook: they push each other. And the counterfeiter discovers a shortcut: if the teller only really checks 20-dollar bills, just print perfect twenties and nothing else. That shortcut is mode collapse.

🤔 Why this? Adversarial training
  1. The problem before Generators trained with pixel losses (autoencoders, VAEs) produce blur: when several sharp answers are plausible, the average of them scores best. Writing a loss that measures “looks real” by hand is impossible.
  2. What people tried Better likelihood models (slow to sample, or blurry); hand-made image statistics.
  3. Why this works Let a network learn the measure of realism. Blur is easy for a discriminator to spot, so the generator is pushed to commit to one sharp answer. Goodfellow et al. showed that with a perfect discriminator, the game's best outcome is a generator whose samples are distributed exactly like the data. Sampling is one forward pass: fast.
  4. What it costs, when it fails No single loss goes down; two losses fight, so training is unstable and very sensitive to learning rates and architecture. Nothing rewards covering every mode, only fooling D on what you do produce: mode collapse. No encoder, no likelihood, hard to tell when it's done.
  5. What refined it DCGAN's architecture rules (2015), the Wasserstein loss and gradient penalty (2017), spectral normalization (2018), StyleGAN (2018–2021). Then diffusion (7.4) took over generation, and GANs became a component: the adversarial term in autoencoders and in few-step distillation.

The loss, in words, then symbols

The discriminator does ordinary binary cross-entropy: “real → 1, fake → 0”. The original paper wrote the whole game as one value that D maximizes and G minimizes (a minimax game):

minG maxD   Ex ~ data[ log D(x) ] + Ez ~ noise[ log(1 − D(G(z))) ]

Read it: D wants log D(x) high on real data and log(1 − D(G(z))) high on fakes (that's minus its cross-entropy). G only appears in the second term and wants it low: “make D's ‘fake’ verdict wrong”.

There's a catch in that second term, spotted in the original paper. Early in training G is terrible, D rejects its fakes with confidence (D(G(z)) ≈ 0.01), and log(1 − 0.01) is nearly flat right there. G gets almost no gradient exactly when it needs one most. Their fix: let G maximize log D(G(z)) instead. Same goal (fool D), very different slope.

What you should see: with D's sigmoid output p = D(G(z)), the original loss sends back a gradient of size p, the non-saturating one 1 − p. At p = 0.01 that's 0.01 versus 0.99: 99× more signal when the generator is losing badly. Near p = 0.5 they're equal; once G is winning (p → 1) the original would give more, but by then G doesn't need the help.

🤔 Why this? The non-saturating generator loss
  1. The problem before Minimizing log(1 − D(G(z))) gives the generator a gradient proportional to D(G(z)). Early on, D easily rejects fakes (D(G(z)) ≈ 0.01), so the gradient is 0.01: the generator barely moves while the discriminator races ahead.
  2. What people tried Slowing D down, training G more often.
  3. Why this works Goodfellow et al. (2014): train G to maximize log D(G(z)), i.e. minimize −log D(G(z)). It has the same fixed point (both push D(G(z)) up), but its gradient is 1 − D(G(z)) ≈ 0.99 when G is losing. In code, it's just binary cross-entropy of D's output on fakes with the label “real”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It's a heuristic: it no longer corresponds exactly to the minimax game. It fixes the start of training, not instability, mode collapse, or a discriminator so perfect that its gradients point nowhere useful.
  5. What refined it The Wasserstein loss (below) replaces the probability with an unbounded score whose gradient doesn't saturate at all; hinge losses are a common modern variant.

Train a GAN live: eight targets on a ring

The classic GAN stress test (used in the 2016 Unrolled GAN paper): real data is a mixture of 8 Gaussian blobs arranged in a circle. A good generator should put samples on all eight. Both networks here are real MLPs training in your browser: G is 2 → 32 → 32 → 2 with ReLU (noise in, a 2D point out), D is 2 → 32 → 32 → 1 with Leaky ReLU (a DCGAN rule), both with Adam (β₁ = 0.5, from DCGAN), batch 64, the non-saturating loss.

What you should see (seeded, so you'll get this too): with equal learning rates the fakes first clump on two or three blobs, then spread around the ring and cover all 8 by about step 1,000. Halve D's learning rate and it still gets there, more slowly (all 8 by about step 1,800). At G 10× faster, the generator piles almost everything onto one blob at a time, and that blob keeps changing as you train (in our seeded run: blob 1, then 4, then 5, then 7…), never covering more than 3 in 3,000 steps. That's exactly what the Unrolled GAN paper describes: the generator “rotates through the modes of the data distribution. It never converges”.

Mode collapse: why the forger stops being creative

Look at the generator's goal: fool D on the samples I produce. Nothing in that sentence says “produce all kinds of samples”. If one blob currently looks most real to D, the cheapest way to fool D is to send every noise input there. D then learns “that blob is suspicious” and pushes the fakes away, and they all rush to the next blob. A slow or weak discriminator makes it worse: it can't punish the pile-up fast enough, so G keeps winning by not being diverse.

The fixes that stuck

DCGAN (Radford, Metz & Chintala, 2015) found a convolutional recipe that trains reliably, listed as rules: replace pooling with strided convolutions (and fractional-strided, i.e. transposed, convolutions in the generator); batch normalization in both networks; no fully connected hidden layers; ReLU in the generator with tanh at the output; Leaky ReLU in the discriminator. Plus Adam at learning rate 0.0002 and β₁ = 0.5, because the usual 0.9 made training oscillate.

🤔 Why this? The Wasserstein loss (WGAN, WGAN-GP)
  1. The problem before When the real and fake distributions barely overlap (common early on, in high dimensions), a well-trained discriminator separates them perfectly, its sigmoid saturates, and the gradient it gives G carries no information about which way real data is. Training stalls or swings wildly, and the loss values tell you nothing about quality.
  2. What people tried The non-saturating loss, noise added to D's inputs, careful architecture rules (DCGAN).
  3. Why this works Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou (2017) replaced the probability with an unbounded score: a critic f gives real samples high scores and fakes low ones, loss = mean f(fake) − mean f(real), no sigmoid. If f is kept smooth (its slope bounded), this difference estimates the Wasserstein distance, “how much mass must move how far” to turn the fake distribution into the real one. Even with no overlap, it shrinks smoothly as fakes move closer, so G always gets a useful direction. And the loss finally tracks sample quality.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The smoothness constraint is the hard part. The original clipped every critic weight to ±0.01, which cripples the critic or explodes gradients if c is wrong. They also trained the critic 5 times per generator step (with RMSProp), so it's slower.
  5. What refined it WGAN-GP (Gulrajani et al., 2017) replaced clipping with a gradient penalty that keeps the critic's input-gradient norm near 1 (weight λ = 10). Spectral normalization (Miyato et al., 2018) divides each layer's weights by their largest singular value, a cheap per-layer way to bound the slope; it became a standard for discriminators.

StyleGAN: the peak of GAN image quality

Karras, Laine & Aila's StyleGAN (2018) changed the generator, not the game. The noise z first goes through an 8-layer MLP “mapping network” to an intermediate code w. Then w controls the image at every resolution through AdaIN (adaptive instance normalization: each layer's features are normalized, then scaled and shifted by numbers computed from w). Separate per-pixel noise inputs add fine random detail like hair strands. Mixing the w of two faces at different layers swaps coarse traits (pose) and fine ones (skin). They released FFHQ, 70,000 high-quality 1024×1024 face photos. StyleGAN2 (2019) redesigned the normalization to fix artifacts; StyleGAN3 (2021) made it alias-free so textures move with the face rather than sticking to the screen.

Why diffusion took over (and where GANs stayed)

In 2021, Dhariwal and Nichol titled their paper “Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis”. On ImageNet their guided diffusion model reached an FID of 4.59 at 256×256, versus 6.95 for BigGAN-deep, the best GAN (FID, Fréchet inception distance, compares feature statistics of generated and real images; lower is better). They also matched BigGAN-deep with as few as 25 network passes per sample.

So GANs became a tool inside other systems. Few-step generation: Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (SDXL Turbo, 2023) trains a student to sample in 1–4 steps using a discriminator; FLUX.1 [schnell] is described as trained with latent adversarial diffusion distillation and runs in 1–4 steps. GigaGAN (2023) makes a 512-px image in 0.13 s. Super-resolution: Real-ESRGAN upscales photos with a GAN loss. Sharp decoders: VQGAN and latent diffusion's autoencoders add a patch-based discriminator to their reconstruction loss, so the decoder paints crisp pixels instead of a VAE's blur.

Why p? With p = σ(a), the slope of log(1 − σ(a)) with respect to a is −σ(a) = −p, and the slope of −log σ(a) is −(1 − σ(a)) = −(1 − p). Same direction (raise a), very different push when G is losing.

🤯 Fun fact · β₁ = 0.5

Many GAN codebases still use Adam with betas=(0.5, 0.999) instead of PyTorch's default 0.9. That's from DCGAN (2015): its authors report the suggested momentum of 0.9 “resulted in training oscillation and instability while reducing it to 0.5 helped”. Less momentum means each player reacts faster to the other's latest move. You'll see the same betas in the snippet below.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A GAN training step is two optimizer steps. The two details that matter: fake.detach() on D's step (don't update G with D's loss), and the non-saturating G loss = BCE of D(fake) against the label “real”. Same 8-blob ring as the playground:

import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
modes = torch.tensor([[math.cos(k * math.pi / 4), math.sin(k * math.pi / 4)] for k in range(8)])
def real_batch(n=256):                      # 8 Gaussians on a ring
    return modes[torch.randint(0, 8, (n,))] + 0.05 * torch.randn(n, 2)

G = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
D = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, 64), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 1))
opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=1e-3, betas=(0.5, 0.999))   # DCGAN's betas
opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=1e-3, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
ones, zeros = torch.ones(256, 1), torch.zeros(256, 1)

for step in range(2001):
    # 1) discriminator: real -> 1, fake -> 0 (detach: don't update G here)
    fake = G(torch.randn(256, 2))
    d_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(D(real_batch()), ones) + \
             F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(D(fake.detach()), zeros)
    opt_d.zero_grad(); d_loss.backward(); opt_d.step()
    # 2) generator, non-saturating: make D call the fakes real
    g_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(D(fake), ones)
    opt_g.zero_grad(); g_loss.backward(); opt_g.step()
    if step % 500 == 0:
        with torch.no_grad():
            s = G(torch.randn(2000, 2))
            near = torch.cdist(s, modes).min(1)
            covered = torch.bincount(near.indices[near.values < 0.2], minlength=8) >= 40
        print(f"step {step:4d}  D loss {d_loss.item():.2f}  G loss {g_loss.item():.2f}  modes covered {covered.sum().item()}/8")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, about 3 s):
# step    0  D loss 1.37  G loss 0.71  modes covered 0/8
# step  500  D loss 1.17  G loss 0.94  modes covered 3/8
# step 1000  D loss 1.18  G loss 0.94  modes covered 3/8
# step 1500  D loss 1.34  G loss 0.72  modes covered 8/8
# step 2000  D loss 1.34  G loss 0.72  modes covered 8/8

Notice the losses barely move: in a GAN, the loss numbers don't tell you how good the samples are. Watch samples and a metric (here mode coverage; for images FID) instead. For discriminators, torch.nn.utils.parametrizations.spectral_norm(layer) adds spectral normalization in one line. D's loss settling near 1.39 (= 2 ln 2) means it's down to coin-flipping on both halves, which is what an evenly matched game looks like.

🎤 Interview angle

“What is mode collapse, and how would you fix it?” A strong answer:

  • The game: D classifies real vs fake with BCE; G is trained through D to raise D(G(z)), usually with the non-saturating loss −log D(G(z)) because log(1 − D) gives tiny gradients when D wins early.
  • Mode collapse: G's objective only asks it to fool D on what it produces, so mapping many z to one convincing output is a cheap win. It often hops between modes as D catches up.
  • Fixes: Wasserstein loss with gradient penalty or spectral norm (non-saturating, smooth critic), balanced learning rates or two-timescale updates, minibatch statistics in D, architecture rules (DCGAN), and evaluating diversity (FID, recall) rather than trusting the losses.
  • Context: diffusion overtook GANs on quality and diversity (Dhariwal & Nichol 2021); GANs remain for one-step speed, super-resolution and as the sharpening loss inside autoencoders and distillation.
🏭 In the real world
The original game 📜 History · 2014 Goodfellow et al.: Generative Adversarial Networks

How: a generator and a discriminator trained in alternation on the minimax value; the paper also suggests training G to maximize log D(G(z)).

Why this method: a generator trained by backprop alone, with no Markov chains or explicit likelihood, whose optimum reproduces the data distribution.

Style-based faces 🏢 NVIDIA · 2018 Karras, Laine & Aila: A Style-Based Generator Architecture for GANs (StyleGAN)

How: an 8-layer mapping network, AdaIN style injection at every resolution, per-pixel noise inputs, mixing regularization; trained on the new 70,000-image FFHQ dataset at 1024².

Why this method: separates coarse and fine attributes, so the latent is easier to control, and pushed GAN face quality to its peak.

The paper that changed the default 🧪 Research · 2021 Dhariwal & Nichol: Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis

How: an improved diffusion U-Net plus classifier guidance (Lesson 7.7); FID 2.97 / 4.59 / 7.72 on ImageNet 128 / 256 / 512, versus BigGAN-deep's 6.02 / 6.95 / 8.43.

Why this method: better quality and better coverage of the data's variety, with a stable training objective.

A discriminator for speed 🏢 Stability AI · 2023 Sauer et al.: Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (SDXL Turbo)

How: a student generator learns from a diffusion teacher plus an adversarial loss, so it samples in just 1–4 steps.

Why this method: the adversarial term keeps few-step samples sharp, where plain distillation gets blurry. Black Forest Labs describes FLUX.1 [schnell] as trained with latent adversarial diffusion distillation.

Check yourself

1. The discriminator scores a fake at D(G(z)) = 0.05. What is the size of the generator's gradient (at D's logit) with the original loss log(1 − D) versus the non-saturating loss −log D?
With p = σ(a): d/da log(1 − σ(a)) = −p = −0.05, and d/da (−log σ(a)) = −(1 − p) = −0.95. Same direction, 0.95 ÷ 0.05 = 19× bigger push for the non-saturating loss, exactly when the generator is losing.
2. Your face GAN's samples are sharp, but after a day of training every sample looks like the same three people. Both losses look healthy. What's going on and what do you try first?
GAN losses don't measure diversity. Collapse means many z map to a few outputs. Smoother critics (WGAN-GP, spectral norm) and balanced updates are the standard fixes; minibatch features in D help it notice that every sample looks the same.
3. A product team needs an image generator that runs in under 50 ms per image on one GPU, conditioned on a few categories. Which statement is most accurate in 2026?
A GAN samples in one forward pass, which is why it still wins on latency. Modern practice often gets the best of both: train a diffusion model, then distill it to a few steps with an adversarial loss (ADD/SDXL Turbo, FLUX.1 [schnell]). Either way, check for collapsed diversity.
📝 TL;DR
  • GAN = generator vs discriminator. D does real/fake BCE; G is trained through D to make D say “real”. A learned critic punishes blur, so samples are sharp and sampling is one pass.
  • Use the non-saturating generator loss −log D(G(z)): its gradient is 1 − p instead of p, 99× stronger at p = 0.01.
  • Mode collapse: nothing rewards variety, so G piles onto whatever fools D, and hops between modes (you saw it at a 10:1 learning-rate ratio).
  • Fixes: DCGAN rules (strided convs, BN, Leaky ReLU in D, Adam β₁ = 0.5), Wasserstein loss + gradient penalty (λ = 10), spectral normalization. StyleGAN: mapping network + AdaIN + noise inputs.
  • Diffusion beat GANs on quality and diversity (FID 4.59 vs 6.95 on ImageNet 256, 2021). GANs remain for one-step speed, super-resolution and as the sharpening loss in autoencoders and distillation.
