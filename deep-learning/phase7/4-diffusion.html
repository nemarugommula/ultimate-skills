Phase 7 · Lesson 4

Diffusion models: noise in, image out

Destroy a picture with noise, one sprinkle at a time. Train a network to guess the noise. Then start from pure static and run the movie backwards. It sounds too simple to work; it now powers almost every image and video generator.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The forward process and the shortcut xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε, the noise-guessing MSE loss and the step-by-step sampler are derived in math 8.4b. This lesson builds the intuition and the engineering on top.
  • A denoising autoencoder learns to rebuild a clean input from a corrupted one (Lesson 7.1). VAEs blur because they average plausible answers (7.2).
  • A U-Net is an encoder–decoder CNN with skip connections that outputs an image the same size as its input (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2020, Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel trained a network to do one thing: look at a noisy image and say what noise was added. They used plain mean squared error, the loss from your first regression lesson. No adversary, no KL balancing act. Generating a picture meant asking that network the same question 1,000 times in a row.

It reached an FID of 3.17 on CIFAR-10, the best reported at the time. A year later, the same family beat the best GANs on ImageNet (Lesson 7.3). Today Stable Diffusion, FLUX, Google's Veo and OpenAI's Sora all build on this idea or its close cousin, flow matching (Lesson 7.6).

How can “guess the noise” be enough to invent a picture? And if one line of code destroys an image in a single jump, why does undoing it take a thousand steps? Below you'll noise a spiral to static and grow it back, find the fewest steps that still work, and watch an image decide what it is.

Numbers first: one pixel, destroyed on schedule

Take one pixel with value 0.8 (images are scaled to −1…1). The forward process mixes in a little Gaussian noise at each of T = 1,000 steps, following DDPM's original linear schedule. Thanks to a shortcut (variances of Gaussian noise add up), you can jump straight to any step t:

ᾱt (“alpha-bar”) is the share of the original signal's power that survives to step t. By the end, the picture is pure N(0, 1) static. That's the easy direction. The hard direction is a network εθ(xt, t) that looks at a noisy picture and the step number and predicts the noise ε that was mixed in. Training is one line, repeated on millions of (picture, random t, random noise) triples:

loss = ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε,  t ) ‖²   (plain MSE, “guess the noise I added”)

To generate, start from pure noise at t = 1,000, ask the network for its noise guess, remove a slice of it, step to t = 999, and repeat. The full formulas (and why the step adds back a little fresh noise) are in math 8.4b.

💡 Big idea A diffusion model is a denoising autoencoder trained at every noise level at once, then run in a loop. Each single step is a small, easy correction (“this static is a little too static”), so one network with a plain MSE loss can learn it. Chaining a thousand small corrections turns pure noise into a sample from the data distribution, with no adversary and no blur from one big guess.
🧠 Analogy: a sculptor and a block of marble

Ask a sculptor to carve a statue in one swing of the hammer and you get rubble. Ask “what's the one chip of stone that least belongs here?” a thousand times and a statue emerges. Early chips decide the rough shape (is it a horse or a person?); late chips decide the details (the eyelashes). The noise-predicting network is the sculptor's eye for “what doesn't belong”, asked once per step.

🤔 Why this? Denoising (noise prediction) as the training target
  1. The problem before VAEs map noise to an image in one decoder pass, so they average plausible answers and blur (7.2). GANs make sharp images in one pass but train as an unstable game and drop modes (7.3).
  2. What people tried Better VAEs, stabilized GANs; score matching (learning the direction toward likelier data) worked on toy data but struggled at high noise and high resolution.
  3. Why this works Predicting the noise for a given noisy picture is an ordinary supervised regression: the right answer is the noise we added ourselves, so the loss is plain MSE, as stable as training a classifier. Each reverse step only has to make a small correction, which a network can learn well; the hard “create a whole picture” problem is split into a thousand easy ones. Ho et al. found that dropping the full probabilistic loss's per-step weights and using this simple MSE gave better samples. Seen as a vector, −εθ points from the noisy point toward likelier data: it's the score, tying diffusion to score-based models (Song et al. 2020).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Generation needs many network calls (DDPM: 1,000; this is the big cost). A tiny error in each step can drift. Pixel-space diffusion at high resolution is expensive to train and run (Lesson 7.5 fixes that).
  5. What refined it DDIM (fewer steps, below), better noise schedules (below), latent diffusion (7.5), guidance (7.7), flow matching with straighter paths (7.6), and distillation to 1–4 steps (7.3, 7.6). Some models predict x₀ or a mix called v (“velocity”) instead of ε; same idea, better behaved at the noisiest steps.

Noise schedules: where to spend your steps

A noise schedule says how fast the signal fades: ᾱt for every t. DDPM's linear schedule (β from 0.0001 to 0.02) fades fast. Nichol & Dhariwal (2021) noticed that with it, the latents in the last quarter of the forward process are almost pure noise: those steps teach the model little and waste sampling time. They proposed a cosine schedule that fades slowly at both ends.

At t = 750 the linear schedule keeps 0.058 of the signal; cosine keeps 0.380. Under the linear schedule 327 of the 1,000 steps have less than 10% of the signal left; under cosine only 65. That's why cosine did so much better with 20 steps in the scrubber: more of its steps happen where the picture is still being shaped.

🤔 Why this? The noise schedule (linear → cosine)
  1. The problem before DDPM's linear schedule destroys small images quickly: the last quarter of the steps are almost pure noise. Training spends a quarter of its effort on steps that barely matter, and samplers that skip steps lose the steps that do matter.
  2. What people tried Tuning β ranges by hand per resolution.
  3. Why this works The cosine schedule ᾱt = cos²(((t/T + s)/(1 + s))·π/2), with s = 0.008, falls slowly at the start, almost linearly in the middle, and slowly at the end. Information is removed at a more even pace, so every step does some work. In our toy it cut the nearly-pure-noise steps from 327 to 65 and made 20-step sampling go from 43% to 94% on the shape.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The “right” schedule depends on resolution and data scale: a 1024-pixel image has so much redundancy that even heavy noise leaves its shape visible, so high-resolution models shift schedules toward more noise. Scaling the data changes the effective schedule too (see the Tools snippet, and SD's 0.18215 from 7.2).
  5. What refined it Thinking in terms of signal-to-noise ratio, ᾱ / (1 − ᾱ), instead of steps; continuous-time schedules; SD3's “logit-normal” sampling of training times to focus on the middle noise levels (7.6).

DDIM: the same model, far fewer steps

DDPM's sampler takes a step, then adds fresh random noise, as the math of running the noise process backwards requires. Song, Meng & Ermon (2020) showed you can use the same trained model with a different, deterministic update: predict the clean picture x̂₀ from the noise guess, then jump directly to a much earlier noise level along the same “direction”. No retraining, and the step size is up to you.

✏️ Worked example: one DDIM step by hand

A 1-number “image” at a step where ᾱ = 0.5 has the value xt = 0.5. The network guesses noise ε̂ = 0.3.

1. Guess the clean value. Undo the mix: x̂₀ = (xt − √(1 − ᾱ)·ε̂) / √ᾱ = (0.5 − 0.707 × 0.3) / 0.707 = 0.407.

2. Jump to a much less noisy level, say ᾱ′ = 0.9, re-using the same noise guess: x′ = √0.9 × 0.407 + √0.1 × 0.3 = 0.386 + 0.095 = 0.481.

One network call moved us from 50% signal to 90%. DDPM would have needed many small steps for the same distance, each adding fresh noise. DDIM's sampler has a knob η (“eta”): η = 0 is this deterministic version, η = 1 recovers DDPM.

🤔 Why this? DDIM (deterministic sampling with fewer steps)
  1. The problem before DDPM needed about 1,000 network calls per sample: minutes per image on 2020 hardware. Taking bigger DDPM steps degrades samples quickly.
  2. What people tried Shorter schedules at training time (needs retraining), skipping steps naively.
  3. Why this works DDIM uses the same training objective, so any pretrained DDPM model works. Its update uses the model's guess of x₀ to jump along a smooth, deterministic path, which tolerates big jumps much better. The paper reports high-quality samples 10× to 50× faster in wall-clock time. Being deterministic, the same starting noise always gives the same image, so you can interpolate between noises or run it in reverse to find the noise for a real image (“inversion”, used in image editing).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Still tens of steps for top quality; very few steps (under ~10) still blur or lose detail. Deterministic sampling can be slightly less diverse.
  5. What refined it Faster ODE solvers (DPM-Solver and friends), flow matching's straighter paths (7.6), and distillation down to 1–4 steps (consistency models, adversarial distillation).

The network inside: U-Net, timestep embedding, and soon transformers

The noise predictor must output an image the same size as its input, so DDPM used a U-Net (Lesson 4.3), similar to PixelCNN++'s, with self-attention at low resolutions. It also needs to know how noisy its input is: the step t is turned into a vector with the same sinusoidal trick as a transformer's positional encoding (Lesson 6.3), passed through a small MLP and added into every block. Without it, the network couldn't tell “a little noise on a detailed image” from “lots of noise on a smooth one”.

🤯 Fun fact · the perfect denoiser only copies

In the image playground, “generate” can only ever produce one of the ten training digits, exactly. That's not a bug of our toy: for a finite training set, the mathematically ideal denoiser regenerates training examples and nothing else. Real diffusion models produce new images precisely because they can't learn that ideal: a network with limited capacity, trained on millions of images, learns smoother arrows that generalize. Researchers measure how often big models still copy training images, and it's one reason dataset de-duplication matters.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A full diffusion model on a 2D spiral, in PyTorch: an MLP noise predictor with a sinusoidal timestep embedding, the MSE training loop, and the 1,000-step DDPM sampler. One gotcha is built in: the spiral is scaled up 3× so the data's spread is comparable to the noise's (without it, in our runs only about a fifth of samples landed within 0.05 of the spiral).

import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
s_ = torch.linspace(0.15, 1, 2000)                       # a 2D spiral: our "dataset"
spiral = torch.stack([0.9 * s_ * torch.cos(3.6 * math.pi * s_), 0.9 * s_ * torch.sin(3.6 * math.pi * s_)], 1)
data = 3 * spiral                                        # scale up so the data's spread is comparable to the noise's

T = 1000
beta = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)                     # DDPM's linear schedule
alpha_bar = torch.cumprod(1 - beta, 0)

class EpsNet(nn.Module):                                 # predicts the noise from (x_t, t)
    def __init__(self, h=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 + 16, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, 2))
    def forward(self, x, t):
        freqs = torch.exp(-math.log(1000) * torch.arange(8) / 8)   # sinusoidal timestep embedding
        ang = t[:, None].float() * freqs[None]
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, ang.sin(), ang.cos()], 1))

model = EpsNet()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, 6000)
for step in range(6000):
    x0 = data[torch.randint(0, len(data), (512,))]
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (512,))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    ab = alpha_bar[t][:, None]
    xt = ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps           # jump straight to step t
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)                  # guess the noise: plain MSE
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()

@torch.no_grad()
def ddpm_sample(n=1000):
    x = torch.randn(n, 2)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):                           # 1,000 small denoising steps
        eps = model(x, torch.full((n,), t))
        x = (x - beta[t] / (1 - alpha_bar[t]).sqrt() * eps) / (1 - beta[t]).sqrt()
        if t > 0: x += beta[t].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x)
    return x

dist = torch.cdist(ddpm_sample() / 3, spiral).min(1).values
print(f"samples within 0.05 of the spiral: {(dist < 0.05).float().mean():.0%}")

🎤 Interview angle

“Explain how a diffusion model is trained and sampled.” A strong answer:

  • Forward: mix Gaussian noise into data on a schedule; closed form xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε lets you jump to any t.
  • Train: random image, random t, random ε; network εθ(xt, t) predicts ε with MSE. A U-Net or transformer with a timestep embedding. It's a denoising autoencoder at every noise level; −ε points along the score.
  • Sample: start at N(0, I), repeatedly remove predicted noise (DDPM: ~1,000 stochastic steps; DDIM: same model, deterministic, tens of steps).
  • Knobs: noise schedule (linear vs cosine), prediction target (ε, x₀, v), number of steps; costs are dominated by network calls, so latent diffusion, better solvers and distillation exist.
🏭 In the real world
The paper that made it work 🧪 Research · 2020 Ho, Jain & Abbeel: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)

How: T = 1,000, β linear from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02, a U-Net with sinusoidal time embedding, the simplified MSE on the noise.

Why this method: the simple objective gave better samples than the full weighted bound; FID 3.17 and Inception score 9.46 on unconditional CIFAR-10.

Fewer steps, same model 🧪 Research · 2020 Song, Meng & Ermon: Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM)

How: a non-Markovian, deterministic sampler (η = 0) that uses DDPM-trained models unchanged.

Why this method: 10× to 50× faster wall-clock sampling at high quality, plus meaningful interpolation in the starting noise.

The cosine schedule 🧪 Research · 2021 Nichol & Dhariwal: Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

How: ᾱt = f(t)/f(0) with f(t) = cos²(((t/T + s)/(1 + s))·π/2), s = 0.008, plus learned variances.

Why this method: under the linear schedule the last quarter of latents are almost pure noise; cosine spreads the destruction out, improving sample quality and few-step sampling.

One view of all of it: the score 🧪 Research · 2020 Song et al.: Score-Based Generative Modeling through SDEs

How: treat noising as a continuous-time process; generation runs it backwards using the learned score. A deterministic “probability flow ODE” samples the same distribution.

Why this method: unifies score-based models and DDPM, explains DDIM as an ODE solver, and leads straight to flow matching (7.6).

Video, too 🏢 Google DeepMind · 2025 Veo 3 technical report

How: Veo 3 uses latent diffusion applied jointly to compressed video and audio latents.

Why this method: the same denoise-step-by-step recipe scales from 2D points to sound-synced video when it runs in a compressed latent space (Lesson 7.5).

🌉 How it connects

The math (multivariate Gaussians, the shortcut, the sampler) is math 8.4b. Diffusion is a multi-noise-level denoising autoencoder (7.1) that avoids the VAE's one-shot blur (7.2) and the GAN's unstable game (7.3). Its U-Net is 4.3's; its timestep embedding is 6.3's positional encoding. Next: run it in a compressed latent with text conditioning (7.5), straighten its paths for fewer steps (7.6), steer it with classifier-free guidance (7.7). Diffusion also drives robot policies and audio generation (Phase 8).

Check yourself

1. With DDPM's linear schedule, √ᾱ at t = 250 is 0.724 and √(1 − ᾱ) is 0.690. A pixel of value 0.8 gets noise ε = −0.5. What is its noisy value x₂₅₀?
The shortcut keeps √ᾱ of the signal and adds √(1 − ᾱ) of the noise: 0.579 + (−0.345) = 0.234. Note 0.724² + 0.690² = 1, so the overall size of the numbers stays steady while the signal fades.
2. Your diffusion model gives great samples at 1,000 DDPM steps but blurry, washed-out ones at 25 DDPM steps. You can't retrain. What's the best first move?
DDIM uses the same ε-prediction model with a deterministic update that tolerates large jumps, giving good samples in tens of steps (10×–50× faster in the paper). Where the steps fall on the schedule matters too, as the cosine-vs-linear scrubber showed.
3. A colleague says “at t = 1,000 the denoiser's best guess of the clean image is a random training image”. Using the 8×8 playground, what's wrong with that?
MSE rewards the mean of all answers consistent with the input. At pure static every digit is equally consistent, so x̂₀ is their average. Sampling resolves the choice gradually: coarse identity first, details last. That's why the early steps (high t) decide composition and the late ones decide texture.
📝 TL;DR
  • Forward: noise data on a schedule; jump to any step with xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε.
  • Train: a U-Net (or transformer) with a timestep embedding predicts ε with plain MSE. It's a denoising autoencoder at every noise level.
  • Sample: start from N(0, I) and remove predicted noise step by step. DDPM ≈ 1,000 stochastic steps; DDIM reuses the same model, deterministic, 10×–50× faster.
  • The schedule decides where steps are spent: cosine left 65 nearly-pure-noise steps vs linear's 327, and turned 20-step sampling from 43% to 94% on our spiral.
  • High t decides the big picture (x̂₀ starts as the average of everything), low t the details. The cost is many network calls, which latent diffusion, better solvers and distillation attack.
