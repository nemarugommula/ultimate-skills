Phase 7 · Lesson 1

Autoencoders: compress, then rebuild

Train a network to copy its input, but force the copy through a narrow slot in the middle. What survives the squeeze is what the data is really made of. This one idea sits inside Stable Diffusion, anomaly detectors and the first deep networks that trained at all.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An MLP (multi-layer perceptron: stacked linear layers with a bend like ReLU between them) can learn almost any function from examples (Lessons 0.3, 0.4).
  • MSE (mean squared error) scores a guess of numbers: average of (guess − target)² (Lesson 1.1).
  • An 8×8 grayscale picture is just 64 numbers between 0 (black) and 1 (white). A convolution layer reads such pictures cheaply (Lesson 3.2).
🎯 Hook

In 2006, Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov published a short paper in Science. They took 28×28 pictures of handwritten digits (784 numbers each) and trained a network to squeeze every picture down to just 30 numbers, then rebuild all 784 from those 30. Its average reconstruction error was 3.00. The classic statistics method for the same job, PCA, scored 13.87: more than four times worse.

Here's the puzzle. The network's only task was to copy its input. Copying is trivial: a layer of 784 wires would do it perfectly. So why did a network that's forbidden from copying (only 30 numbers fit through the middle) turn out to be so useful? And why can't the same network, run backwards from random numbers, invent new digits?

Who uses this today: Stable Diffusion and FLUX run their whole image generator inside an autoencoder's compressed space (Lesson 7.5); factories and banks flag anomalies as “things the autoencoder can't rebuild”; masked autoencoders pretrain vision models (Lesson 9.1).

Numbers first: a 64-number picture through a 4-number slot

Take one of our tiny 8×8 digits: 64 numbers. An autoencoder has two halves:

  • The encoder squeezes the 64 numbers into a short code z, say 4 numbers. This middle layer is the bottleneck, and its values are called the latent (hidden) representation.
  • The decoder takes those 4 numbers and tries to rebuild all 64. Its output is the reconstruction x̂ (“x-hat”).
x (64 numbers) → encoder → z (4 numbers) → decoder → x̂ (64 numbers)   ·   loss = average of (x̂ − x)²

That's 64 ÷ 4 = 16× compression. The loss is plain MSE, and the target is the input itself. No labels needed: every picture is its own answer key. That's why autoencoders count as unsupervised (or self-supervised) learning.

If the bottleneck were 64 wide, the network could learn the identity (copy every pixel straight through) and learn nothing. The narrow middle is the whole point: with only 4 slots, the network has to find the 4 numbers that describe a digit best. It can't afford to store noise, so it keeps patterns.

💡 Big idea An autoencoder learns a compressed description of its data by being graded on how well it can rebuild the input from that description. The bottleneck forces it to keep what's common and structured (strokes, shapes) and throw away what's rare and random (noise). The code in the middle, the latent, becomes a small, useful summary of each example.
🧠 Analogy: describing a face over the phone

A police sketch artist can't see the suspect; a witness describes them over the phone, but only gets 12 words. “Tall forehead, thin nose, round glasses…” The witness learns to pick the 12 words that carry the most information, and the artist learns to turn those 12 words into a face. Neither is ever told what “a good word” is: they're only graded on whether the final sketch looks like the person. Encoder = witness, bottleneck = the 12 words, decoder = the artist.

🤔 Why this? A bottleneck
  1. The problem before Pictures are huge lists of numbers (a 512×512 color photo is 786,432 of them), but most possible lists are static. Real images live on a tiny, structured sliver of that space. We want the handful of numbers that describe where on the sliver a picture is, without labels telling us what matters.
  2. What people tried Hand-made features (edge counts, color histograms), and PCA (principal component analysis): keep the k straight-line directions along which the data varies most (math 2.6). PCA is fast and exact, but it can only bend space with straight lines.
  3. Why this works Rebuilding the input from k numbers rewards the network for spending its k numbers on what is shared by many examples. A random pixel flip is different in every picture, so it isn't worth a slot. In the playground below, a 4-number code keeps 48% of the digits' variation; 11 numbers are enough to rebuild all ten digits recognizably (5.8× compression).
  4. What it costs, when it fails Too narrow and details are lost (blurry, wrong digits). Too wide, and a big enough network can simply copy, learning nothing useful (see “no bottleneck” below). The latent has no promised shape, so you can't sample from it (the last section). And MSE loss makes reconstructions blurry, because averaging plausible answers scores well.
  5. What refined it Denoising autoencoders (this lesson) add noise instead of relying only on width; VAEs (7.2) give the latent a shape you can sample from; VQ-VAEs (7.8) make it a grid of discrete tokens; the autoencoders inside latent diffusion add perceptual and GAN losses for sharpness (7.3, 7.5).

What you should see: at k = 1 the reconstructions are blurry ghosts and only 2 of 10 digits are recognizable. Recognition climbs as you widen the slot: 6 at k = 4, 9 at k = 7. The answer to the challenge is k = 11, the first width where all ten pass: 64 pixels described by 11 numbers. Above about k = 12 nothing visible improves. That flat tail is the bottleneck telling you how much real structure the data has.

✏️ Worked example: what the bottleneck costs and saves

Parameters. A linear autoencoder 64 → 11 → 64 has an encoder weight matrix of 64 × 11 = 704 numbers plus 11 biases, and a decoder of 11 × 64 = 704 plus 64 biases: 1,483 parameters in all.

Storage. Each picture now costs 11 numbers instead of 64: 64 ÷ 11 ≈ 5.8× smaller, as long as everyone shares the decoder.

Hinton's 2006 network was 784 → 1000 → 500 → 250 → 30, mirrored back up. Counting weights and biases layer by layer: encoder 785,000 + 500,500 + 125,250 + 7,530 = 1,418,280; decoder 1,419,034; 2,837,314 parameters to compress each digit 784 ÷ 30 ≈ 26×. Its trick (bends between layers) is what let it beat PCA's straight lines by 4×.

The PCA connection: a straight-line autoencoder is PCA

Remove every bend from the network and the encoder can only compute k weighted sums of the pixels. Baldi and Hornik's 1989 result: the best such autoencoder projects every picture onto the k directions of greatest variation, exactly the top-k principal components (math 2.6 SVD and PCA). The “variation kept” readout above is literally PCA's explained variance.

So why use a neural network at all? Because data doesn't lie on a flat sheet. A digit shifted one pixel left is a completely different list of 64 numbers; the set of all shifted 3s is a curved shape in 64-dimensional space. PCA must cover that curve with a flat sheet; an autoencoder with bends can follow it. That's the whole 3.00 vs 13.87 story from the hook.

Make it harder to cheat: the denoising autoencoder

Slide k to 64 (no bottleneck at all) and turn on some noise. The reconstruction copies the noise perfectly: zero improvement, because the identity is the best a 64-wide linear autoencoder can do. Now drop k to 8.

🔮 Predict: digit 3 with 6 flipped pixels, bottleneck k = 8. Will the output have more wrong pixels than the input, fewer, or the same?

What you should see: the 8-number code has no room for the random flips, so the decoder rebuilds a clean-looking 3. Across all ten digits with 6 flips each, the wrong pixels drop from 60 to 12 at k = 8 (to 7 at k = 12), and stay at 60 with k = 64. A narrow bottleneck is a denoiser for free. A denoising autoencoder makes this the training goal.

🤔 Why this? Denoising autoencoder (DAE)
  1. The problem before An autoencoder with a wide or very powerful middle can learn to copy: perfect reconstruction, useless features. Making the slot narrow works, but then you must guess the right width.
  2. What people tried Narrow bottlenecks; penalties that keep the code sparse (most code numbers zero) or smooth.
  3. Why this works Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio and Manzagol (2008): corrupt the input, but grade against the clean original. In their words, reconstruct a clean “repaired” input from a corrupted, partially destroyed one. Copying now loses points (it copies the damage), so the network must learn what digits look like in order to fill in what was destroyed. Their corruption set a fixed number of random inputs to 0. Stacking such layers was one of the ways people pretrained deep networks in 2008–2010.
  4. What it costs, when it fails You pick a noise type and amount. Too little and it's a plain autoencoder; too much and nothing is recoverable. Features are only as good as the corruption is relevant to your task.
  5. What refined it Masked autoencoders (hide 75% of image patches, Lesson 9.1) and BERT's masked words (6.7) are denoising autoencoders with “masking” as the noise. And diffusion models (7.4) are denoising autoencoders trained at every noise level at once, then run in a loop to generate.

So… can it generate? The holes in latent space

Here's the tempting idea: the decoder turns codes into pictures. Pick a random code, decode it, and you've invented a new digit! Let's look at where real digits actually sit in code space. What you should see: the codes form clumps (round digits like 0, 3, 6, 8, 9 share one, since two numbers can’t separate all ten), with big empty regions around them. Drag the probe into the empty top-left corner: a smudge. The decoder was only ever graded on the clumps; the empty space is a hole, and what comes out there is a blend of overlapping ghosts. Keep clicking: the first three batches run a little lucky (8 of 30), but only 31 of the first 200 random codes look like a training digit (about 15%; 14% over 500 draws), while real codes pass 99% of the time. Nothing in the loss told the encoder to fill the space evenly, or to put codes anywhere you'd know how to sample from.

Honest note: this decoder is linear, so its holes produce blends. Nonlinear autoencoders are worse: outside the training clumps their output can be anything, because no training example ever constrained it there.

🤯 Fun fact · a 1989 proof, a 2006 comeback

That a straight-line autoencoder can do no better than PCA was proved in 1989. For years that made autoencoders look pointless. Deep, bent ones were hard to train, until Hinton and Salakhutdinov's 2006 trick: pretrain one layer at a time, then fine-tune the whole stack. The paper helped restart interest in deep networks, years before AlexNet. Today we don't need that pretraining trick (better activations, initialization and normalization did it; Phases 0–2), but the autoencoder stayed.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

An autoencoder is two nn.Sequentials and an MSE loss whose target is the input. This trains three bottleneck widths on 2,000 synthetic 8×8 “bar” pictures (each with 1–3 random horizontal or vertical bars, so 16 possible bars):

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
X = torch.zeros(2000, 8, 8)                # fake pictures: 1-3 random bars each
for img in X:
    for _ in range(torch.randint(1, 4, ()).item()):
        i = torch.randint(0, 8, ()).item()
        if torch.rand(()) < 0.5: img[i, :] = 1
        else: img[:, i] = 1
X = X.view(2000, 64)

def autoencoder(k):                       # 64 -> k -> 64
    enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, k))
    dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(k, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 64), nn.Sigmoid())
    return nn.Sequential(enc, dec)

for k in [2, 8, 16]:
    ae = autoencoder(k)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(ae.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
    for step in range(1500):
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(ae(X), X)   # the target is the input itself
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    print(f"bottleneck {k:2d}: reconstruction MSE {loss.item():.4f}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, about 5 s):
# bottleneck  2: reconstruction MSE 0.0638
# bottleneck  8: reconstruction MSE 0.0067
# bottleneck 16: reconstruction MSE 0.0059

Make it a denoising autoencoder with one line: feed ae(X_noisy) but keep the target X. For real images, swap the linear layers for convolutions down (stride 2) and transposed convolutions or upsampling back up (Lessons 3.2, 4.3).

🎤 Interview angle

“What's an autoencoder, and why can't you generate images with one?” A strong answer:

  • Encoder → bottleneck → decoder, trained with reconstruction loss (MSE) where the target is the input. The bottleneck (or noise, for a DAE) is what stops it copying.
  • A linear AE recovers PCA's subspace; nonlinear ones follow curved data.
  • Uses: compression, anomaly detection by reconstruction error, denoising, pretraining, and the latent space inside latent diffusion.
  • As a generator it fails because nothing shapes the latent space: codes form clumps with holes between them, so a random code decodes to junk. The VAE fixes exactly this (7.2).
🏭 In the real world
Deep autoencoders beat PCA 📜 History · 2006 Hinton & Salakhutdinov: Reducing the Dimensionality of Data with Neural Networks

How: a 784-1000-500-250-30 autoencoder on handwritten digits, pretrained one layer at a time, then fine-tuned end to end.

Why this method: average squared reconstruction errors of 3.00 vs 8.01 (logistic PCA) and 13.87 (PCA): curved structure needs a bent network.

Denoising as a training signal 🧪 Research · 2008 Vincent et al.: Extracting and Composing Robust Features with Denoising Autoencoders

How: zero out a fixed number of random inputs, train the autoencoder to rebuild the clean version, stack the learned layers.

Why this method: copying the input no longer wins, so the features must capture structure. The idea lives on in masked autoencoders and in diffusion's noise-prediction loss.

Linear autoencoder = PCA 📜 History · 1989 Baldi & Hornik: Neural Networks and Principal Component Analysis

How: analyzed the error surface of a one-hidden-layer linear autoencoder.

Why this method: its unique minimum is an orthogonal projection onto the subspace spanned by the top principal eigenvectors, so the page above can compute the trained network exactly.

The compressor inside Stable Diffusion 🧰 Open source · 2022 Stable Diffusion v1-4 model card

How: an autoencoder with a downsampling factor of 8 maps an H × W × 3 image to an H/8 × W/8 × 4 latent; all the expensive generation happens there, then the decoder paints pixels.

Why this method: 48× fewer numbers per 512×512 image to generate (Lesson 7.5). It's a VAE-style autoencoder, the subject of the next lesson.

The linear case is PCA from math 2.6; the encoder and decoder are the MLPs and CNNs of Phases 0 and 3 (U-Net-style decoders in 4.3). Check yourself

1. An autoencoder squeezes 28×28 grayscale images into a 49-number code. What is the compression factor, and how many weights does the first layer have if the encoder is a single linear layer (ignore biases)?
28 × 28 = 784 inputs, and 784 ÷ 49 = 16× compression. A linear layer from 784 to 49 needs one weight per (input, output) pair: 784 × 49 = 38,416. The decoder needs the same again in the other direction.
2. You train an autoencoder with a 512-number bottleneck on 256-number inputs. Training loss reaches almost 0, but the codes are useless for anything. What happened?
With room for everything, copying is the perfect solution and nothing forces the network to find structure. Either shrink the slot so it can't copy, or make copying lose points by adding noise to the input while keeping the clean target (Vincent et al. 2008).
3. A teammate trains an autoencoder on product photos, then decodes random codes drawn from N(0, 1) to make “new products”. The outputs are smears. What's the best explanation?
The reconstruction loss only cares about codes of real images. Where those codes sit, how spread out they are and what lies between them is arbitrary, so there's no distribution you know how to sample from. The VAE adds a loss term that pulls codes into a N(0, 1) shape and fills the holes (Lesson 7.2).
📝 TL;DR
  • Autoencoder = encoder → narrow bottleneck (the latent code) → decoder, trained so the output matches the input (MSE). No labels needed.
  • The bottleneck forces it to keep shared structure and drop noise: 11 numbers rebuild all ten 8×8 digits; a linear AE is exactly PCA.
  • A denoising AE corrupts the input but grades against the clean one, so copying can't win. It's the ancestor of masked autoencoders and diffusion.
  • Uses: compression (Stable Diffusion's latents), anomaly detection by reconstruction error, denoising, pretraining, tokens (VQ-VAE).
  • Bad generator: codes form clumps with holes, so random codes decode to junk. Next lesson's VAE fixes that.
