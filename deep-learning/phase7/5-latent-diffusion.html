Phase 7 · Lesson 5

Latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion

Diffusing raw pixels is gorgeous and absurdly expensive. Squeeze the image 48× with an autoencoder first, diffuse in that small space, and let the picture read your prompt through cross-attention. That recipe put image generation on gaming laptops in 2022, and its descendants (SD3, FLUX) swapped the U-Net for a transformer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A diffusion model predicts the noise in xt with MSE and samples by denoising step by step; each step is one network call (Lesson 7.4).
  • A VAE-style autoencoder compresses an image to a small latent and decodes it back; latent diffusion uses one with only a slight KL (Lesson 7.2).
  • Attention: each query scores every key, softmaxes the scores and takes a weighted mix of values; cost grows with (number of tokens)² (Lesson 6.1). CLIP turns text into embeddings that line up with images (Lesson 4.6).
🎯 Hook

In December 2021, Robin Rombach and colleagues pointed out an uncomfortable number: training a powerful diffusion model directly on pixels “often takes hundreds of GPU days (e.g. 150–1000 V100 days)”, and generating with it is slow too. Their fix was to stop diffusing pixels.

On 22 August 2022, that recipe was released as Stable Diffusion (v1.4), open weights from CompVis, Stability AI and Runway: an 860-million-parameter U-Net plus a 123-million-parameter text encoder, small enough to run on a consumer graphics card. Anyone could now type a sentence and get a 512×512 image on their own machine.

Where did the savings come from, how does a U-Net “read” a sentence, and why did every big model since 2024 replace that U-Net with a transformer? You'll compute the savings yourself, then poke at a cross-attention map to see which words each region of an image listens to.

Numbers first: 786,432 vs 16,384

A 512×512 color image is 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers. Stable Diffusion's autoencoder shrinks each side by a factor f = 8 and keeps 4 channels: 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 numbers.

786,432 ÷ 16,384 = 48× fewer numbers for the diffusion model to denoise, at every one of its tens of steps

Most of what's in those 786,432 numbers is detail that a decoder can repaint reliably (smooth gradients, fine texture); the latent keeps what's hard: what is where, its shape, its color. Compression and generation are split between two networks that are each good at one job.

text → CLIP text encoder → 77 token vectors + noise 64×64×4 → U-Net × ~25–50 steps, reading the text by cross-attention → clean latent → VAE decoder → 512×512×3 image

The VAE encoder is only needed during training (to turn training images into latents) and for image-to-image editing. Plain text-to-image generation uses only the text encoder, the denoiser and the decoder.

💡 Big idea Separate “compressing an image” from “imagining one”. Train an autoencoder once to map images to a small latent grid and back (with perceptual and adversarial losses so the decoder paints sharp details). Then run the whole diffusion process in that grid. The denoiser sees 48× fewer numbers, so training and sampling get dramatically cheaper, and the decoder handles the pixels in one pass at the end.
🤔 Why this? Diffusion in a latent space
  1. The problem before Pixel-space diffusion spends most of its capacity on imperceptible detail and runs a huge network on every pixel, at every step: hundreds of GPU days to train, slow to sample, and high resolution meant cascades of separate upsampling models.
  2. What people tried Cascades (a small image, then diffusion super-resolution stages); two-stage models with discrete tokens (VQ-VAE + transformer, Lesson 7.8), whose aggressive compression lost detail.
  3. Why this works A good autoencoder removes the redundancy first, so the generative model only learns the semantic, hard part. Rombach et al. compared compression factors and found f = 4 to 16 “strike a good balance between efficiency and perceptually faithful results”. The latent is still a grid, so a convolutional U-Net works on it unchanged, and one decoder pass at the end costs almost nothing compared with 50 denoising passes.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The decoder sets a quality ceiling: anything the autoencoder can't represent (tiny text, small faces, fine patterns), the generator can't produce. With 4 channels, early SD struggled with text and hands. And you now train and version two models.
  5. What refined it More latent channels (SD3 compared 4, 8 and 16 and chose 16; FLUX's autoencoder also has 16), a transformer denoiser (DiT, below), and video autoencoders that compress time as well (Wan's 3D causal VAE squeezes 4 × 8 × 8: frames × height × width).

How a picture reads a prompt: cross-attention

The text encoder turns the prompt into a list of token vectors: for SD 1.x, CLIP's tokenizer pads or cuts every prompt to 77 tokens, each a 768-number vector. Inside the U-Net, at several resolutions, a cross-attention layer lets every latent position look up the words:

  • Queries come from the image: each of the 64 × 64 = 4,096 positions asks “what should I be?”
  • Keys and values come from the text: each of the 77 tokens says “here's what I'm about” and “here's what to add if you listen to me”.
  • Each position takes a softmax-weighted mix of the token values and adds it to its features. A region that matches “dog” pulls in dog information; the sky pulls in “sun” and “sky”.

It's exactly the encoder–decoder attention of the original transformer (Lesson 6.5), with an image as the decoder side. Below is a hand-built toy so you can see what such a map looks like.

What you should see: “dog” lights up the dog cells and almost nothing else; filler words like “a” and “on” get a thin, even share everywhere. Each cell's attention always adds up to 100% over the words still in the prompt, so when you delete “sun” its region doesn't go dark: its attention redistributes to the next-best matches (“sky”, then filler words). That's why removing a word changes, rather than deletes, part of an image. Lower the sharpness and every cell spreads its attention evenly: the prompt stops steering anything.

🤔 Why this? Cross-attention conditioning
  1. The problem before Early conditional generators took a single class label or one sentence vector, concatenated or added to the features. One vector for a whole sentence can't say “red cube left of a blue sphere”: where each thing goes is lost.
  2. What people tried Class embeddings added to the timestep embedding (fine for 1,000 ImageNet classes); a pooled sentence embedding.
  3. Why this works Attention lets each position pick its own words: the dog region reads “dog”, the sky reads “sun”. It handles any number of tokens, any order, and any modality that can be turned into tokens (text, layouts, segmentation maps); Rombach et al. presented it as a general-purpose conditioning mechanism. The cost is small: 4,096 queries × 77 keys = 315,392 scores per head, versus 16.8 million for self-attention over the same latent.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The model only knows what the text encoder can express: CLIP's 77-token limit truncates long prompts, and CLIP is weak at counting and word order, which is why early SD scrambled “a red cube on a blue sphere”. Binding attributes to the right object (“red” to the cube) stays hard.
  5. What refined it Bigger and more text encoders (SDXL: OpenCLIP ViT-bigG + CLIP ViT-L; Imagen and SD3: the T5-XXL language model); and MMDiT (SD3), which drops separate cross-attention and puts text and image tokens into one joint attention, each with its own weights.

What you should see: pixel-space 512×512 with one token per pixel is 262,144 tokens and 68.7 billion attention pairs per layer: hopeless. SD's latent (f = 8) is 4,096 positions, 16.8 million pairs. A DiT-style transformer with 2 × 2 patches cuts that to 1,024 tokens and about 1 million pairs. ✏️ Worked example: SD3 at 1024×1024

Latent: 1024 ÷ 8 = 128, so 128 × 128 × 16 = 262,144 numbers: 3,145,728 ÷ 262,144 = 12× compression (less than SD 1.x's 48×, because 16 channels keep more detail).

Tokens: 2 × 2 patches → 64 × 64 = 4,096 image tokens, each the 2 × 2 × 16 = 64 latent numbers of its patch, projected to the model's width.

Attention: 4,096² ≈ 16.8 million image–image pairs per head per layer, plus the text tokens joined into the same sequence (MMDiT). Doubling the resolution to 2048² would mean 16,384 tokens and 16× the attention cost.

From U-Net to diffusion transformers (DiT, MMDiT)

In December 2022, William Peebles and Saining Xie replaced the U-Net with a plain Vision Transformer (Lesson 4.5) on latent patches: the DiT. The timestep and class steer every block through adaLN-Zero (layer norm whose scale and shift are computed from the conditioning, initialized so each block starts as the identity). They found that sample quality tracked compute very consistently, and their largest model, DiT-XL/2, reached an FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256×256, the best at the time.

Stable Diffusion 3 (2024) took this further with MMDiT (multimodal DiT): text tokens and image tokens are concatenated into one sequence for attention, but each modality has its own weights, and the model is trained with rectified flow (Lesson 7.6). FLUX.1 (Black Forest Labs, August 2024) is described as a 12-billion-parameter rectified flow transformer. Video models do the same with spacetime patches (Lesson 7.8).

🤔 Why this? A transformer backbone (DiT) instead of a U-Net
  1. The problem before U-Nets have hand-designed resolution levels, channel counts and attention placements; scaling them up (SDXL's 2.6B U-Net was 3× SD's) means re-engineering the architecture, and gains were hard to predict.
  2. What people tried Bigger U-Nets with more attention blocks and more text encoders (SDXL).
  3. Why this works A transformer is one block repeated: scale width, depth or tokens and you get a predictable family, just like LLMs (Lesson 6.8). Peebles & Xie found a strong correlation between compute (Gflops) and FID. Attention mixes information globally from the first layer, text and image tokens can share one sequence (MMDiT), and the whole LLM toolbox (FlashAttention, parallelism, mixed precision) applies.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Attention is quadratic in tokens, so patch size and latent compression become critical (the calculator); no built-in multi-scale bias like a U-Net's, so it needs more data and compute to learn local structure; positional encodings must handle changing aspect ratios.
  5. What refined it MMDiT with joint text–image attention (SD3), large rectified-flow transformers (FLUX), spacetime-patch video DiTs (Sora's report calls Sora “a diffusion transformer”; Movie Gen, Wan), and efficient-attention tricks from Lesson 6.9.

Two tricks everyone uses: negative prompts and LoRA

Negative prompts. Text-to-image pipelines run the denoiser twice per step, with the prompt and without it, and push the result away from the “without” version (classifier-free guidance, Lesson 7.7). A negative prompt (“blurry, extra fingers”) simply replaces the empty prompt in that second call, so each step is pushed away from what you described. It's not a filter; it's a direction.

LoRA fine-tunes. To teach a model your product, style or face, you don't retrain 2–12 billion weights. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes them and learns small low-rank update matrices for the attention layers, often a few megabytes, that can be loaded, mixed and swapped at run time. It's the same LoRA as for LLMs (AI Engineer 7.1); the SD3 paper used rank-128 LoRAs for its own fine-tuning experiments.

🤯 Fun fact · the encoder is optional

Stable Diffusion is “a VAE”, yet plain text-to-image generation never runs the VAE's encoder: it starts from random latent noise, denoises, and only calls the decoder. The encoder is needed to make training latents, and for image-to-image editing, where your photo is encoded, noised to some t and denoised again: exactly the “denoise from here” button in Lesson 7.4.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Cross-attention is one call to F.scaled_dot_product_attention with queries from the image and keys/values from the text. Here at SD 1.x's real sizes (first U-Net level: 64 × 64 positions, 320 channels, 8 heads; CLIP: 77 tokens × 768):

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
B, H, W, C = 1, 64, 64, 320            # latent features in SD 1.x's first U-Net block
img = torch.randn(B, H * W, C)         # 4,096 image positions, 320 channels each
txt = torch.randn(B, 77, 768)          # 77 CLIP text-token embeddings, 768 dims each

heads, d = 8, C // 8
to_q, to_k, to_v = nn.Linear(C, C), nn.Linear(768, C), nn.Linear(768, C)
split = lambda x: x.view(B, -1, heads, d).transpose(1, 2)   # (B, heads, tokens, d)

q, k, v = split(to_q(img)), split(to_k(txt)), split(to_v(txt))
out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v)               # image queries, text keys/values
attn = (q @ k.transpose(-1, -2) / d ** 0.5).softmax(-1)     # the map, for inspection only
print("queries", tuple(q.shape), "keys", tuple(k.shape))
print("attention map", tuple(attn.shape), f"row sum {attn[0, 0, 0].sum().item():.2f}")
print("output", tuple(out.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, H * W, C).shape))
print(f"cross-attention scores per head: {4096 * 77:,}; self-attention over the same latent: {4096 ** 2:,}")

# What we got (torch 2.8, CPU):
# queries (1, 8, 4096, 40) keys (1, 8, 77, 40)
# attention map (1, 8, 4096, 77) row sum 1.00
# output (1, 4096, 320)
# cross-attention scores per head: 315,392; self-attention over the same latent: 16,777,216

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through what happens when Stable Diffusion turns a prompt into an image.” A strong answer:

  • Tokenize the prompt (77 tokens for CLIP) → text encoder → token embeddings. Start from Gaussian noise in latent space (64×64×4 for 512² in SD 1.x).
  • For ~25–50 steps: the denoiser (U-Net with cross-attention, or an MMDiT/DiT) predicts the noise, twice (with prompt and with empty/negative prompt) for classifier-free guidance; a scheduler (DDIM, Euler…) takes the step.
  • Decode the final latent with the VAE decoder to pixels. The encoder is used only for training and img2img.
  • Why latent: 48× fewer numbers, f = 4–16 balance; why transformers now: predictable scaling, joint attention; costs: autoencoder ceiling, quadratic attention, text-encoder limits.
🏭 In the real world
The latent diffusion recipe 🧪 Research · 2021 Rombach et al.: High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models

How: a perceptual + adversarial autoencoder (KL- or VQ-regularized), a U-Net denoiser in its latent, cross-attention for text, layouts and other conditions.

Why this method: pixel-space models cost 150–1000 V100 days; f = 4–16 latents keep quality while cutting compute drastically.

Stable Diffusion goes public 🧰 Open source · 2022 Stable Diffusion v1-4 model card

How: autoencoder with downsampling factor 8 (H × W × 3 → H/8 × W/8 × 4), a U-Net with cross-attention, a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder.

Why this method: small enough for consumer GPUs, so open weights spawned an ecosystem of fine-tunes, LoRAs and tools.

Transformers replace the U-Net 🧪 Research · 2022 Peebles & Xie: Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers (DiT)

How: patchify the latent, run a ViT with adaLN-Zero conditioning on timestep and class.

Why this method: FID tracked Gflops tightly; DiT-XL/2 hit 2.27 FID on ImageNet 256.

MMDiT and rectified flow at scale 🏢 Stability AI · 2024 Esser et al.: Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis (SD3)

How: separate weights for text and image tokens joined in one attention, 2 × 2 latent patches, a 16-channel autoencoder, three text encoders (CLIP L/14, OpenCLIP bigG/14, T5-XXL), models up to 8B.

Why this method: lets text and image representations interact in every layer while keeping each modality's own processing; scales smoothly.

FLUX 🏢 Black Forest Labs · 2024 FLUX.1 [dev] model card

How: a 12-billion-parameter rectified flow transformer, trained with guidance distillation (Lesson 7.7); [schnell] samples in 1–4 steps.

Why this method: the transformer + flow recipe of SD3 at larger scale, with distillation for practical speed.

Check yourself

1. A 768×768 RGB image goes through an f = 8 autoencoder with 4 latent channels. How many numbers does the diffusion model work on, and what's the compression?
768 ÷ 8 = 96 per side, times 4 channels = 36,864. Pixels: 768 × 768 × 3 = 1,769,472. 1,769,472 ÷ 36,864 = 48×: with f = 8 and 4 channels the ratio is 8 × 8 × 3 ÷ 4 = 48 at any resolution.
2. Your fine-tuned SD 1.x model renders product labels as garbled text, even though the denoiser's training loss is low. Which component most likely sets this limit?
Encode and decode a real photo of the label with the VAE alone: if the text is already garbled, no denoiser can fix it. That's the quality ceiling of latent diffusion, and why SD3 moved to 16 channels (and better text encoders helped too).
3. You add “in the style of watercolor” to a 70-word prompt for an SD 1.x model and nothing changes. What's the likely reason?
70 words is often more than 77 tokens once split into sub-words (Lesson 6.6). Everything after the cut is silently dropped, so the U-Net never sees it. Models with T5 encoders (Imagen, SD3, FLUX) accept much longer prompts.
📝 TL;DR
  • Latent diffusion = autoencoder (compress) + diffusion in the latent (imagine) + decoder (paint). 512×512×3 → 64×64×4 is 48× fewer numbers; f = 4–16 balances cost and fidelity.
  • Text enters through cross-attention: image positions are queries, the text encoder's tokens (77 for CLIP) are keys and values; each region picks its own words.
  • The decoder caps quality (text, faces): SD3 and FLUX use 16-channel latents.
  • DiT replaced the U-Net with a ViT on latent patches (FID 2.27); SD3's MMDiT joins text and image tokens in one attention; FLUX is a 12B rectified flow transformer. Tokens² attention makes patch size and compression decisive.
  • Negative prompts replace the empty prompt in classifier-free guidance (7.7); LoRA fine-tunes are small swappable adapters.
