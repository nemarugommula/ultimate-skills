Phase 7 · Lesson 2

VAEs and the reparameterization trick

Last lesson's autoencoder couldn't invent anything: its latent space was full of holes. Make the encoder output a little cloud instead of a point, pull every cloud toward one bell curve, and the holes fill in. Then one line of algebra lets gradients flow through the dice roll.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An autoencoder: encoder → bottleneck code z → decoder, trained to rebuild its input. A plain one leaves holes between the codes of real data (Lesson 7.1).
  • A Gaussian N(μ, σ²) is a bell curve with centre μ (“mu”) and spread σ (“sigma”); N(0, 1) is the standard one. Draw from it with μ + σ·(a standard draw) (math 4.3).
  • KL divergence measures how different two distributions are; 0 means identical (math 6.4). Backprop needs every step from loss to weight to have a slope (Lesson 1.2).
🎯 Hook

On 20 December 2013, Diederik Kingma and Max Welling posted “Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes”. Less than four weeks later, Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra at DeepMind independently posted the same core idea. Both papers had to solve one awkward problem: their network had a random sampling step in the middle, and you can't take the slope of a dice roll.

Both found the same one-line fix, now called the reparameterization trick. More than a decade later, a VAE-style autoencoder still sits in nearly every image generator: Stable Diffusion's turns a 512×512 photo into a 64×64×4 latent, and SD3 and FLUX use one with 16 latent channels.

How do you make a latent space you can sample from, and how do you backprop through randomness? In this lesson you'll walk a real VAE's latent space and collect all ten digits, watch a gradient die at a sampling step and come back with the trick, and break the VAE with a single dial.

Numbers first: encode to a cloud, not a point

A plain autoencoder maps the digit 3 to one point. A VAE (variational autoencoder) maps it to a small distribution: the encoder outputs two numbers per latent dimension, a centre μ and a spread σ. Our tiny trained VAE (2 latent dimensions) says, for the digit 3:

μ = [1.82, 0.23]    σ = [0.158, 0.083]    → “the code is somewhere in this little oval”

During training we sample one code from that oval and decode it. Draw a standard-normal ε (“epsilon”) = [0.5, −1]:

z = μ + σ · ε = [1.82 + 0.158 × 0.5, 0.23 + 0.083 × (−1)] = [1.90, 0.15]

The decoder must rebuild a 3 from any point in the oval, not just its centre. That one change makes the space between training codes meaningful: a little region around every code decodes to that digit.

🤔 Why this? A probabilistic encoder (μ, σ)
  1. The problem before A plain autoencoder's decoder is only trained at the exact code points of the training data. In Lesson 7.1 only about 15% of random codes decoded to anything digit-like: the rest fell into holes.
  2. What people tried Adding noise to the input (denoising AEs), penalties that keep codes small or sparse. These shape the codes a bit but never give you a distribution you know how to sample from.
  3. Why this works If the code is jittered by σ during training, the decoder learns that a whole neighbourhood means “3”. Neighbourhoods of different digits touch, so moving through the space morphs one digit into another instead of falling into a hole. The encoder is now a probability model, q(z | x): “given this picture, here's where its code probably is”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails On its own this does nothing: the encoder can shrink σ to 0.0001 and move the μ's far apart, and you're back to a plain autoencoder with holes. Something has to hold σ open and keep the clouds near each other. That's the KL term, next.
  5. What refined it The KL term (below) and β-VAE's dial on it; VQ-VAE (7.8) goes the other way and snaps codes to a discrete codebook.

The loss: rebuild well, but stay close to N(0, 1)

The VAE loss has two parts, added together:

loss = reconstruction error (rebuild x from a sampled z)  +  KL( N(μ, σ²) ‖ N(0, 1) )

In words: “rebuild the picture, and keep each picture's cloud looking like a standard bell curve”. For a Gaussian cloud compared with N(0, 1), the KL has a closed form, summed over latent dimensions (derived in math 6.4):

KL = ½ Σ ( μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ² )

Read it term by term. μ² pulls every cloud's centre toward 0, so codes can't fly apart. σ² − 1 − ln σ² is 0 when σ = 1 and grows if σ shrinks toward 0 (ln σ² goes to −∞), so clouds can't collapse into points. Together: every digit's cloud is pushed to overlap with one shared N(0, 1) ball, and the holes fill in.

✏️ Worked example: the KL bill for the digit 3

Dimension 1: μ = 1.82, σ = 0.158. μ² = 3.312, σ² = 0.025, ln σ² = −3.69. KL₁ = ½ (3.312 + 0.025 − 1 + 3.69) = 3.01.

Dimension 2: μ = 0.23, σ = 0.083. KL₂ = ½ (0.053 + 0.007 − 1 + 4.98) = 2.02. Total: about 5.03 nats (natural-log units of information).

Compare the extremes: a cloud that is N(0, 1) (μ = 0, σ = 1) costs exactly 0 but tells the decoder nothing about which digit it was. A code at μ = 3 with σ = 0.1 costs 6.31: very informative, very expensive. Over all 80 training pictures our VAE averages 5.65 nats of KL: that's the information it chose to pay for.

🤔 Why this? The KL term
  1. The problem before With reconstruction loss alone, the encoder shrinks σ toward 0 and spreads the μ's out (our plain autoencoder's codes ranged from −135 to +323), leaving gaps the decoder never saw.
  2. What people tried Just adding fixed noise to the codes; weight decay on the encoder. Neither tells you where to sample at generation time.
  3. Why this works The KL gives every picture's cloud the same target, N(0, 1). The μ² part keeps codes near the origin; the −ln σ² part keeps clouds wide. So at generation time you know exactly what to do: draw z from N(0, 1) and decode. In the playground below, about 76% of such random draws decode to a recognizable digit, and they cover all ten. The math view: the total loss is (minus) the ELBO, the “evidence lower bound” on the log-probability of the data, so minimizing it trains a real probability model.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The two terms fight: every nat of information about the picture costs KL. The decoder can't be sure which picture a code came from, so it hedges, and hedging means blur. Too strong a KL and the encoder gives up entirely: σ = 1, μ = 0 for every input, KL = 0, and the decoder outputs the same average blob (“posterior collapse”; try β = 20 below).
  5. What refined it β-VAE (a weight on the KL, below); the “slight KL penalty” autoencoders of latent diffusion (7.5), which care about compression, not sampling; and diffusion models (7.4), which replace one big jump from N(0, 1) with many small denoising steps.

The problem: you can't backprop through a dice roll

Training needs the slope of the loss with respect to μ and σ (they come out of the encoder, so their gradients train the encoder's weights). But the code is drawn at random: z ~ N(μ, σ²). If an autograd system sees “sample a number”, it gets back a plain number with no history. The chain from loss to μ is cut.

The trick: move the randomness outside. Draw ε ~ N(0, 1) first, from a fixed bell curve that has nothing to learn, then compute

z = μ + σ · ε   ⇒   ∂z/∂μ = 1,   ∂z/∂σ = ε

Same distribution of z (μ + σ · a standard draw is a draw from N(μ, σ²)), but now z is ordinary arithmetic on μ and σ. Gradients pass straight through the plus and the times, like any other layer.

🧠 Analogy: a dart thrower and a wobbly hand

You're coaching a dart thrower whose hand wobbles. “Your dart landed 3 cm left” is useless feedback if you think of the landing spot as pure luck. Rewrite it: landing spot = where you aimed + your wobble size × (this throw's random shake). Now the feedback works: the shake is luck, but aim and wobble size are things the thrower controls, and you know exactly how each one moved the dart. μ is the aim, σ the wobble size, ε the shake.

🤔 Why this? The reparameterization trick
  1. The problem before The loss depends on μ and σ only through a random sample, and “sample” has no derivative. Treat the sample as a constant and the encoder gets zero gradient: it never learns.
  2. What people tried The score-function estimator (also called REINFORCE, the same trick as policy gradients in RL 5.2): it never differentiates the sample; it multiplies the loss by the slope of the log-probability. It is unbiased, but extremely noisy.
  3. Why this works Writing z = μ + σ·ε puts the randomness in an input (ε) that needs no gradient, and makes z a smooth function of μ and σ. The gradient estimate now uses the slope of the loss, not just its value, so it is far less noisy: in the playground below, with 16 latent dimensions its spread is 2.0 versus 83 for the score-function estimator.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It needs a distribution you can write as “fixed noise pushed through a differentiable function” (Gaussians: yes; discrete choices: no) and a loss that's differentiable in z. Discrete codes (VQ-VAE, 7.8) need other tricks such as the straight-through estimator or Gumbel-softmax.
  5. What refined it The same move appears all over this phase: diffusion writes the noisy picture as x_t = √ᾱ·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ)·ε (7.4); flow matching interpolates x_t = (1 − t)·x₀ + t·x₁ (7.6). Fixed noise in, differentiable arithmetic after.

One dial: β-VAE, and why VAE pictures are blurry

Put a weight β (“beta”) on the KL term: loss = reconstruction + β · KL. β = 1 is the standard VAE. Higgins et al. (2017) found that β > 1 pushes the latent to be tidier: each latent number tends to capture one independent factor (say, stroke thickness or slant), which they called disentangled. The price is fidelity. Five versions of our VAE were trained with different β; the playground replays them.

🤔 Why this? β-VAE (a weight on the KL)
  1. The problem before A standard VAE's latent dimensions are tangled: moving along one axis changes several properties at once, which makes the latent hard to interpret or control.
  2. What people tried Supervised labels for each factor (expensive); hand-designed priors.
  3. Why this works Raising β makes every nat of information more expensive, so the encoder spends it on the few biggest, most independent factors of variation, and keeps the latent close to a tidy N(0, 1) ball. Higgins et al. showed β > 1 learned disentangled factors (position, scale, rotation of simple shapes) without labels. Lowering β (below 1) does the opposite: sharper reconstructions, a less regular latent.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Higher β = blurrier reconstructions; too high and the latent collapses (KL = 0). Disentanglement is not guaranteed on real, messy data, and it's hard to measure.
  5. What refined it In practice the dial is used downward: latent diffusion's autoencoders use only a “slight KL penalty” (a tiny β) because they need sharp reconstructions and a well-scaled latent, not sampling; the diffusion model (7.5) does the generating.

Why VAE outputs are blurry

Hand the decoder a code that could have come from a 3 or an 8 (their clouds overlap). Pixel-wise loss rewards the guess that's closest on average to both possible answers: half a 3 plus half an 8, a grey smear. A sharp 3 would be perfect half the time and badly wrong the other half, which scores worse. The KL forces the overlap, so a VAE always has to hedge somewhere. Fixes that keep the VAE's encoder but sharpen its decoder: perceptual losses (compare features of a pretrained CNN, not raw pixels) and an adversarial loss from a discriminator (next lesson). Stable Diffusion's autoencoder and VQGAN use both.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The whole VAE is two lines different from an autoencoder: the encoder outputs (μ, log σ²), and z = μ + σ·ε. Predicting log σ² instead of σ keeps σ positive with no constraint. Trained here on one-bar 8×8 pictures with a 2-number latent:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in=64, d_z=2, h=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_in, h), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h, 2 * d_z))
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_z, h), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h, d_in))

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.enc(x).chunk(2, dim=-1)    # encode to a distribution
        eps = torch.randn_like(mu)                     # the randomness lives outside...
        z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps         # ...so z is a smooth function of mu and sigma
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar, beta=1.0):
    rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction="sum") / len(x)
    kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(-1).mean()  # KL(N(mu, sigma^2) || N(0, 1))
    return rec + beta * kl, rec, kl

torch.manual_seed(0)
X = torch.zeros(2000, 8, 8)                       # one random bar per picture
for img in X:
    i = torch.randint(0, 8, ()).item()
    if torch.rand(()) < 0.5: img[i, :] = 1
    else: img[:, i] = 1
X = X.view(2000, 64)

for beta in [1.0, 4.0]:
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    vae = VAE(); opt = torch.optim.Adam(vae.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
    for step in range(1500):
        logits, mu, logvar = vae(X)
        loss, rec, kl = vae_loss(logits, X, mu, logvar, beta)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    z = torch.randn(5, 2)                              # generate: sample N(0, 1), decode
    print(f"beta={beta}: reconstruction {rec.item():.2f}  KL {kl.item():.2f}  "
          f"sample bar pixels {(torch.sigmoid(vae.dec(z)) > 0.5).sum(1).tolist()}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, about 6 s):
# beta=1.0: reconstruction 0.39  KL 3.84  sample bar pixels [8, 8, 3, 13, 8]
# beta=4.0: reconstruction 1.70  KL 3.10  sample bar pixels [8, 13, 6, 8, 8]

A clean bar has exactly 8 lit pixels, so most samples are real bars; a 13 is two bars blended (a cross). Higher β: worse reconstruction, less KL. 🎤 Interview angle

“Explain the reparameterization trick, and why VAE samples are blurry.” A strong answer:

  • VAE = probabilistic encoder q(z | x) = N(μ, σ²), decoder p(x | z), loss = reconstruction + KL(q ‖ N(0, 1)) (the negative ELBO). The KL fills the latent's holes so you can sample N(0, 1) and decode.
  • Sampling has no gradient. Write z = μ + σ·ε with ε ~ N(0, 1): randomness becomes an input, z becomes differentiable in μ and σ. Lower variance than the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator, which only needs the loss's value.
  • Blur: overlapping clouds mean a code is ambiguous, and pixel-wise losses reward averaging the possible answers. Fixes: perceptual and adversarial losses, or let diffusion do the generating.
  • β trades fidelity for a tidy, disentangled latent; too high → posterior collapse. Latent diffusion uses a slight KL.
🏭 In the real world
The original VAE 📜 History · 2013 Kingma & Welling: Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes

How: an encoder outputs μ and σ, samples z = μ + σ ⊙ ε with ε ~ N(0, I), and trains encoder and decoder together on a lower bound of the data's log-likelihood.

Why this method: it made learning a deep latent-variable model as easy as backprop with minibatches. Rezende, Mohamed & Wierstra found the same idea independently weeks later (2014).

Disentangled factors without labels 🧪 Research · 2017 Higgins et al.: β-VAE (ICLR 2017)

How: the same VAE with the KL weighted by β > 1.

Why this method: a single hyperparameter that, in their experiments, beat the plain VAE (β = 1) at disentangling factors like position, scale and rotation.

The compressor in latent diffusion 🧪 Research · 2021 Rombach et al.: High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models

How: the autoencoder is regularized either with “a slight KL-penalty towards a standard normal” or with vector quantization, and trained with perceptual and patch-GAN losses for sharpness.

Why this method: the diffusion model needs a compact, well-behaved latent and a decoder that paints sharp pixels, not a VAE that can sample by itself.

More latent channels for SD3 🏢 Stability AI · 2024 Esser et al.: Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers (Stable Diffusion 3)

How: compared autoencoders with 4, 8 and 16 latent channels and chose 16. FLUX's open code also uses a 16-channel autoencoder.

Why this method: more channels rebuild finer detail (text, faces); a big enough generator can still model the richer latent.

Check yourself

1. A VAE's encoder outputs μ = 0 and σ = 1 for one latent dimension. What is that dimension's KL cost, and what does it tell the decoder about the input?
The formula gives exactly 0 when the cloud equals N(0, 1). But then the code is pure noise, the same for every picture, so it carries no information: free and useless. Information about the input always costs KL. If every dimension looks like this, the latent has collapsed.
2. A teammate writes z = torch.distributions.Normal(mu, sigma).sample() in their VAE. Training runs, the loss falls a bit, but the encoder's weights never change. Why?
.sample() is the black-box sample from the playground: gradient exactly 0 for the encoder. rsample() (“reparameterized sample”) does z = μ + σ·ε internally, so autograd sees ordinary arithmetic. The decoder still learns, which is why the loss moves a little.
3. After training a VAE with β = 50, every generated image is the same grey blur and the KL term reads 0.00. What happened, and what do you change?
With a huge weight on KL, the cheapest solution is to encode nothing (KL = 0) and let the decoder output the average picture. Lower β, or warm it up from 0 so the encoder learns useful codes before the KL bites. Try β = 20 in the playground: the same thing happens.
📝 TL;DR
  • A VAE's encoder outputs a cloud (μ, σ) per input; the decoder rebuilds from a sample of it.
  • Loss = reconstruction + KL(N(μ, σ²) ‖ N(0, 1)) = reconstruction + ½Σ(μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ²). The KL pulls clouds together and keeps them wide, filling the holes, so you can generate by decoding z ~ N(0, 1).
  • Reparameterization trick: z = μ + σ·ε, ε ~ N(0, 1). Randomness becomes an input; gradients flow to μ and σ with low variance (2.0 vs 83 for REINFORCE at 16 dims in our demo).
  • β weights the KL: high β = tidy, disentangled, blurry, and eventually collapsed; low β = sharp but less sampleable.
  • VAEs are blurry because overlapping codes make the decoder average answers. Today their main job is the compressor inside latent diffusion (4 channels in SD 1.x, 16 in SD3 and FLUX).
