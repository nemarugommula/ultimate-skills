Phase 6 · Lesson 3

Positional encodings: sinusoidal → RoPE

Self-attention can't tell “dog bites man” from “man bites dog”. Here's how transformers learn where each word sits, ending with the rotation trick almost every open LLM uses today.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Self-attention scores every pair with q·k and mixes values (Lesson 6.1); multi-head runs several of these (6.2).
  • Rotating a 2D vector by angle a: [x, y] → [x cos a − y sin a, x sin a + y cos a]. Rotation keeps lengths; the dot product of two vectors depends only on their lengths and the angle between them (math 1.3).
🎯 Hook

Feed a transformer with no position information “dog bites man”, then “man bites dog”. The vector it computes for dog is exactly the same both times, to the last digit. Every word just gets reshuffled. To the bare attention layer, a sentence is a bag of words.

The fixes came in quick succession: sine waves (the 2017 Transformer), learned position tables (BERT, GPT-2), relative distances (2018), and in 2021 rotations. That last one, RoPE, is what Meta's Llama 3, Alibaba's Qwen3 and Google's Gemma 3 use (per their 2024–2025 technical reports).

How do you tell a model “this word is 3 places before that one” without breaking everything else? By the end you'll rotate vectors by hand and see why the dot product suddenly cares only about distance.

Numbers first: attention is permutation-blind

Look at what self-attention computes for one token: a score with every other token (q·k), a softmax, a weighted sum. Nothing in that recipe mentions where a token is. Shuffle the input rows and you get the same output rows, shuffled the same way. Mathematicians call that permutation equivariance; your users call it “the model can't read”.

What you should see: with no positions, any order gives a difference of exactly 0.000: “dog” sees the same bag of words. Turn sinusoidal positions on and swapping “dog” and “man” changes its vector, because “dog at position 0” and “dog at position 2” are now different inputs.

🤔 Why this? Positional encoding
  1. The problem before RNNs read in order, so order was free. Self-attention dropped the recurrence (6.1) and with it every sense of order: “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” give identical per-word vectors (difference 0.000 above).
  2. What people tried Convolutions (which see order inside their window), or keeping an RNN layer under the attention.
  3. Why this works Give each position its own signal and combine it with the token, so “dog@0” and “dog@2” differ. The attention machinery stays the same, fully parallel.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The model only knows the positions it trained on. Learned tables have no row for position 1,025 if you trained on 1,024; most schemes degrade past the training length (6.11).
  5. What refined it From adding a position vector to the input (learned or sinusoidal), to putting distance directly into the attention score (relative positions, RoPE, ALiBi), below.
💡 Big idea What attention really needs is relative position: “this word is 3 before me” matters more than “I am word 517”. The history of positional encodings is a march from absolute addresses toward scores that depend only on the distance between query and key, which RoPE achieves with a rotation.

Generation 1: a learned position table

The simplest idea: a second embedding table with one learned vector per position, added to the token embedding. BERT has 512 rows; GPT-2 has 1,024. It works well inside that range and is what both used. Its limit is literal: there is no row 1,025. You can't run GPT-2 on a longer text without inventing new rows it never trained.

Generation 2: sine waves (the original Transformer)

Vaswani et al. instead built a fixed pattern: dimension pair i of position pos holds sin and cos of pos / 100002i/d. In words: each pair of dimensions is a clock hand turning at its own speed. The first pair spins fast (a full turn about every 6 positions), the last pair barely moves over thousands. Read all the hands together and you know the position, like reading hours, minutes and seconds.

What you should see: the left columns flicker every few rows; the right columns barely change across all 100: fast clocks on the left, slow on the right. The similarity curve peaks at the chosen position and falls off on both sides, so nearby positions look alike. The base sets how fast that falls off. At 10,000, a position 40 away still looks 48% alike: slow clocks give a smooth sense of “far”. Drop the base to 10 and every clock is fast: similarity hits about 0 just 5 positions away and then wobbles around zero, so distance 10 and distance 40 look equally “unrelated”. Long-context models go the other way and raise the base (Llama 3: 500,000).

🤔 Why this? Sinusoidal position encodings
  1. The problem before A learned table only covers positions seen in training (512 rows for BERT, 1,024 for GPT-2) and costs a vector of parameters per position.
  2. What people tried Learned absolute embeddings, which Vaswani et al. also tested and found “nearly identical” in quality.
  3. Why this works No parameters, any length can be computed, and the waves have a neat property: for a fixed offset k, the encoding of pos + k is a linear function (a rotation of each clock hand) of the encoding of pos. The authors hoped this would let the model learn relative offsets and extrapolate to longer inputs.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The position is added to the word vector, so word meaning and position share the same numbers and the model must untangle them. And the hope of extrapolation didn't come true: Press et al. (2021) found sinusoidal models have “very weak extrapolation abilities” past their training length.
  5. What refined it Relative positions inside attention (Shaw et al. 2018), then RoPE, which keeps the rotating clocks but applies them to queries and keys instead of adding them to the input.

Generation 3: relative positions

Shaw, Uszkoreit and Vaswani (2018) asked: why tell the model absolute addresses at all? They added a learned vector for each distance (clipped at some maximum) directly into the attention computation. On English→German translation that beat absolute positions by 1.3 BLEU (0.3 on English→French), and adding absolute positions on top brought no further gain. The cost: extra per-distance terms inside every attention score, which complicates fast kernels.

Generation 4: RoPE, rotate the query and the key

Su et al.'s rotary position embedding (2021) keeps the clock hands but uses them differently. Nothing is added to the input. Instead, right before the dot product, each pair of numbers in a query at position m is rotated by angle m·θ, and each pair in a key at position n by n·θ (one θ per pair, fast to slow, like the sine waves).

Why does that help? Rotating both vectors changes their angles, but the angle between them changes by (n − m)·θ. A dot product only cares about lengths and the angle between, so the score depends on the words and their distance, never on the absolute positions.

(Rmθ q) · (Rnθ k) = q · R(n−m)θ k

What you should see: (3, 1), (10, 8) and (40, 38) all give the same score: both arrows turn, the angle between them doesn't. Push the distance far enough and the rotated key swings past 90° from the query: the score goes negative. With θ = 0.3 a distance of 21 is a full turn (21 × 0.3 ≈ 2π) and scores like distance 0; that's why real RoPE uses many pairs with different speeds. The slow ones only complete a turn after thousands of positions, so far-apart tokens can't be confused with neighbours.

🤔 Why this? RoPE (rotary position embedding)
  1. The problem before Added positions (learned or sinusoidal) mix position into the word vector and extrapolate poorly. Relative-position terms (Shaw et al.) work but add extra pieces to every attention score.
  2. What people tried Learned tables, sine waves, per-distance biases (Shaw 2018, the T5 bias).
  3. Why this works Rotating q by mθ and k by nθ makes q·k depend only on n − m: relative position for free, inside the normal dot product, with zero new parameters and no change to the attention kernel. It touches only Q and K, so values (what gets handed over) stay position-free. Pairs spin at different speeds, so both fine (next word) and coarse (500 words back) distances are visible.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Distances beyond the training length produce rotation angles the model never saw, so plain RoPE models still degrade past their trained context. A few extra multiplies per query and key.
  5. What refined it Raising the base “clock speed” (Llama 3 uses 500,000; Qwen3 and Gemma 3's global layers 1,000,000) and rescaling tricks to stretch a trained model to longer contexts (Lesson 6.11). Meta's Llama 4 (2025) even interleaves layers with no position encoding between its RoPE layers.

ALiBi, briefly: just penalize distance

Press, Smith and Lewis (2021) went even simpler: no position vectors at all, just subtract a penalty proportional to the distance from each attention score (a different slope per head). A 1.3B-parameter model trained on 1,024-token inputs matched the perplexity of a sinusoidal model trained on 2,048 when tested at 2,048, while training 11% faster. BLOOM (2022) used it. The large open models in this lesson's table chose RoPE instead, but ALiBi's lesson stuck: a recency bias helps extrapolation.

✏️ Worked example: RoPE on one pair, by hand

q = [1, 0] and k = [1, 0] (identical words), θ = 90° per position. Query at m = 1, key at n = 2:

  • Rotate q by 1 × 90°: [1, 0] → [0, 1]. Rotate k by 2 × 90° = 180°: [1, 0] → [−1, 0].
  • Score = 0 × (−1) + 1 × 0 = 0.
  • Now m = 5, n = 6 (same distance 1): q turns 450° (= 90°) → [0, 1]; k turns 540° (= 180°) → [−1, 0]. Score = 0 again.
  • Distance 0 (m = n): both turn the same, score = 1 × 1 = 1.

Same words, different distance, different score; same distance, anywhere in the text, same score. Real models use θi = base−2i/d radians for pair i: with d = 128 and base 10,000, the fastest pair turns 1 radian per position and the slowest about 0.0001.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

RoPE is a few lines: rotate each consecutive pair of numbers by position × its own frequency. (Libraries differ on which numbers form a pair; this version pairs neighbours.) The second half checks the permutation problem directly:

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

def rope(x, pos, base=10000.0):
    """Rotate pairs (x[0],x[1]), (x[2],x[3]), ... by angle pos * theta_i."""
    d = x.shape[-1]
    theta = base ** (-torch.arange(0, d, 2) / d)          # d/2 frequencies, fast -> slow
    ang = pos[:, None] * theta[None, :]                    # (n, d/2)
    cos, sin = ang.cos(), ang.sin()
    x1, x2 = x[..., 0::2], x[..., 1::2]
    out = torch.empty_like(x)
    out[..., 0::2] = x1 * cos - x2 * sin
    out[..., 1::2] = x1 * sin + x2 * cos
    return out

q, k = torch.randn(64), torch.randn(64)                   # one query, one key
def score(m, n):                                           # q at position m, k at position n
    return (rope(q[None], torch.tensor([float(m)])) @ rope(k[None], torch.tensor([float(n)])).T).item()

print(f"{score(3, 1):.4f} {score(10, 8):.4f} {score(1000, 998):.4f}")   # distance 2 every time
print(f"{score(3, 0):.4f}")                                             # distance 3: different

# without positions, attention can't see order: shuffle in -> same rows, shuffled, out
x = torch.randn(5, 16); W = torch.randn(16, 16)
att = lambda x: torch.softmax((x @ W) @ (x @ W).T / 4, -1) @ x
perm = torch.tensor([3, 0, 4, 1, 2])
print(torch.allclose(att(x)[perm], att(x[perm]), atol=1e-6))

🎤 Interview angle

“Why do transformers need positional encodings, and how does RoPE work?” A strong answer:

  • Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle inputs, outputs shuffle the same way; order is invisible.
  • Absolute options (learned, sinusoidal) add a position vector to the input; learned ones cap the length.
  • RoPE rotates each 2D pair of q and k by position × frequency; since rotations preserve dot products up to the relative angle, qm·kn depends only on n − m. No parameters; values untouched.
  • Extrapolation caveat: unseen distances still hurt; base-frequency increases and rescaling (or ALiBi) are the usual answers.
🏭 In the real world
RoPE with a 500,000 base 🏢 Meta · 2024 The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: rotary embeddings in every layer, with the base frequency raised from 10,000 to 500,000.

Why this method: the authors say the larger base “enables us to better support longer contexts”: slower clocks keep far-apart positions distinguishable.

RoPE plus long-context extensions 🏢 Alibaba · 2025 Qwen3 Technical Report

How: RoPE with the base raised from 10,000 to 1,000,000, plus YaRN and Dual Chunk Attention at inference for a four-fold longer context.

Why this method: RoPE's frequencies can be adjusted after training, which makes context extension a configuration change plus a little extra training rather than a new model.

Different bases for local and global layers 🏢 Google · 2025 Gemma 3 Technical Report

How: global attention layers use a RoPE base of 1M; sliding-window local layers keep 10k.

Why this method: local layers only ever see short distances, so fast clocks are fine there; global layers need slow clocks for long range.

ALiBi in a 176B open model 🧪 Research · 2022 BigScience: BLOOM

How: no position embeddings; attention scores are penalized in proportion to query–key distance.

Why this method: chosen for extrapolation, and the team found it “also led to smoother training”.

🤯 Fun fact · the hope that didn't pan out

Vaswani et al. picked sine waves over a learned table partly because they “may allow the model to extrapolate” to longer sequences. Four years later the ALiBi paper put that hope to the test, found sinusoidal transformers extrapolate very weakly, and opened with the same quote. A good example of a reasonable hypothesis in a famous paper turning out to be wrong.

Check yourself

1. RoPE with one pair and θ = 30° per position. The query is at position 4 and the key at position 7. By what angle does RoPE rotate the key relative to the query, and would positions 104 and 107 give the same score?
The query turns 4 × 30° = 120° and the key 7 × 30° = 210°, so the angle between them changes by 90°, which is (n − m) × θ. At 104 and 107 both turn much more, but the difference is again 3 × 30° = 90°, so the dot product is identical. That's RoPE's whole point: scores depend on distance only.
2. You fine-tune a BERT-style model (learned absolute positions, 512 rows) and want to classify 2,000-token documents. What happens if you just feed them in?
A learned absolute position table literally has one row per trained position. Libraries raise an error or silently truncate past 512. Options: split into 512-token chunks and combine predictions, or pick a long-context encoder (e.g. ModernBERT, 8,192 tokens, Lesson 6.5) or a RoPE-based model.
3. A teammate removes the positional encoding from a small translation transformer “to simplify it”. Training loss still goes down a bit. What will you most likely see in its translations?
Without positions the encoder sees a bag of words: permuting the input just permutes the outputs, so any meaning carried by order is invisible. The model can still learn which words to output (so loss improves), but order-dependent meaning is lost. (Causal decoders are a partial exception: the mask itself leaks some order information, but encoders get none.)
📝 TL;DR
  • Self-attention is permutation-blind: shuffle the words and each word gets exactly the same vector. Positions must be injected.
  • Learned absolute tables (BERT 512, GPT-2 1,024) cap the length. Sinusoids: clock hands at geometric speeds, no parameters, extrapolate weakly.
  • Relative positions (Shaw 2018) put distance into the score: +1.3 BLEU over absolute.
  • RoPE rotates q and k by position × frequency, so q·k depends only on the distance n − m. No parameters. Used by Llama 3, Qwen3, Gemma 3 (2024–25 reports).
  • ALiBi subtracts a distance penalty and extrapolates by design (BLOOM). Going past the trained length is still hard: Lesson 6.11.
