Phase 6 · Lesson 8

Scaling laws: Kaplan → Chinchilla

Before spending $100 million on one training run, labs predict its loss with a ruler on a log-log plot. This lesson is that ruler: how loss falls with size, data and compute, and how to split a budget between them.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A language model is pretrained by next-token prediction; its loss is the average −ln p(next token) (Lesson 6.7).
  • N = number of parameters (weights). D = number of training tokens. A transformer block is mostly big matrix multiplies (Lesson 6.4).
  • On a log scale, equal steps mean equal multiples (×10, ×10, ×10). A refresher on logs: math 0.3.
🎯 Hook

In 2021 DeepMind trained Gopher: 280 billion parameters, 300 billion tokens. A few months later the same team trained Chinchilla with about the same compute budget, but made the model 4× smaller (70 billion parameters) and fed it about 4× more text (1.4 trillion tokens).

The smaller model won, across the board. It beat Gopher, GPT-3 (175B) and others, scored 67.5% on the MMLU knowledge exam (more than 7 points above Gopher), and, being 4× smaller, was much cheaper to run.

So the field had been building models that were too big and undertrained. How do you know, before training, how big a model to build and how much data to feed it? That's what scaling laws answer. Every frontier lab uses them to plan its runs.

Numbers first: the cost of training, C ≈ 6·N·D

Every training token passes through every weight. Each weight costs about 2 FLOPs on the forward pass (one multiply, one add) and about 4 on the backward pass (gradients for the inputs and for the weight). So:

training compute C ≈ 6 · N · D FLOPs

The Llama 3 paper reports exactly 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs for that run, so the rule of thumb lands on the dot. It ignores the attention-score math, which is small next to the weights at normal context lengths (Lesson 6.11 shows when it isn't).

Kaplan 2020: loss is a straight line on a log-log plot

In 2020 Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI trained hundreds of transformers, from 768 to 1.5 billion (non-embedding) parameters, and plotted the final loss against size. On ordinary axes the curve flattens, which looks like “diminishing returns”. On log-log axes it's a straight line, across more than seven orders of magnitude:

L(N) = (Nc / N)α   with α ≈ 0.076, Nc ≈ 8.8 × 10¹³

In words: every 10× more parameters multiplies the loss by 10−0.076 ≈ 0.84, a 16% drop, as long as data and compute aren't the bottleneck. Data and compute give similar lines (α ≈ 0.095 for tokens, 0.050 for compute). A straight line on log-log is called a power law; its slope is the exponent.

🤔 Why this? Scaling laws (predict before you train)
  1. The problem before A frontier run costs months and tens of millions of dollars. Choosing size, data and learning rate by trial and error at full scale is impossible: you get one shot.
  2. What people tried Intuition, copying the last model ×10, and small ablations that didn't say how results would change with scale.
  3. Why this works Loss follows smooth power laws in N, D and C over many orders of magnitude, and details like depth vs width barely matter within a wide range (Kaplan et al.). So you train a ladder of small models (say 10M to 1B), fit the line, and read off the big run. Example: 10× params ⇒ loss × 0.84.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Laws predict loss, not which skills appear (Llama 3's team had to build a second fit from loss to benchmark accuracy). Exponents change with data, tokenizer and architecture, so you must refit for your setup. The fitting itself is fragile (see the fun fact).
  5. What refined it Chinchilla's joint law in N and D (next), laws for data repetition, for MoE (Lesson 6.10), and for inference-time compute (RL 8.12).

The Chinchilla correction: grow data as fast as the model

Kaplan's paper also asked: given a budget C, how should you split it? Its answer was “mostly into parameters”: N ∝ C0.73, D ∝ C0.27. That's why GPT-3 and Gopher saw under 2 tokens per parameter.

Hoffmann et al. (2022, the Chinchilla paper) redid the experiment with over 400 models, and crucially tuned the learning-rate schedule to each run's length (Kaplan's runs used one schedule, which made long runs on more data look worse). Three different methods all said the same thing: N ∝ C0.5 and D ∝ C0.5. Double the compute → 1.4× the params and 1.4× the tokens. In numbers: about 20 tokens per parameter (their table: 1B params → 20.2B tokens, 10B → 205B).

They also fitted one formula for loss as a function of both:

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ

In words: an irreducible floor E (the randomness of language itself), plus a penalty for a too-small model, plus a penalty for too little data. Fix C, and making N bigger shrinks the second term but forces D = C / 6N smaller, growing the third. Somewhere in between is the sweet spot.

🤔 Why this? Compute-optimal training (Chinchilla)
  1. The problem before Following Kaplan's split, 2020–2021 models grew to 175B–530B parameters on about 300B tokens: under 2 tokens per parameter. Gopher (280B) used 5.0e23 FLOPs and was beaten by a 70B model on the same budget.
  2. What people tried Making models ever bigger with the same data size; Kaplan's fits, which used one learning-rate schedule for all run lengths and so underrated long training on more data.
  3. Why this works Both shortfalls cost loss: a small model can't hold the knowledge, but a big model that saw too little data hasn't absorbed it. The two penalties trade off, and the minimum sits where both grow together, N ∝ √C and D ∝ √C, about 20 tokens per parameter. Same budget, Chinchilla: 4× smaller, 4.7× more tokens, lower loss.
  4. What it costs, when it fails “Compute-optimal” means optimal for training only. It ignores inference: a model that serves trillions of tokens should be smaller and trained longer (next box). It assumes unlimited fresh data, and the exact constants depend on the data and the fit.
  5. What refined it Over-training for cheap inference (Llama), data-constrained laws (repeating data), and refits like Epoch AI's. Lesson 10.x revisits this for planning real training jobs.

Over-training on purpose: Llama's choice

Training happens once; serving happens billions of times. Each generated token costs about 2·N FLOPs, so a 10× smaller model is about 10× cheaper to run forever. Meta's Llama 3 announcement put it plainly: the Chinchilla-optimal data for an 8B model is about 200B tokens, but they trained it on 15T, two orders of magnitude more, and it kept improving “log-linearly”.

🤔 Why this? Over-training smaller models
  1. The problem before A compute-optimal 70B model is great to train, but serving it costs 2 × 70B = 140 GFLOPs per token, plus the memory to hold it on GPUs.
  2. What people tried Distilling big models into small ones after training; quantizing (fewer bits per weight).
  3. Why this works The IsoFLOP curve is flat near the bottom, so moving left (smaller N, more D) costs little loss: in the playground, 3× smaller is within 0.02. If the model will serve trillions of tokens, the inference savings dwarf the extra training cost. Total cost = training + (tokens served × 2N).
  4. What it costs, when it fails You need far more data (15T tokens is close to the high-quality web), and far from the optimum the penalty grows: a tiny model can't absorb unlimited knowledge.
  5. What refined it Distillation from a larger teacher (AI Engineer 7.2), MoE models that are big in params but cheap per token (6.10), and inference-aware scaling laws.

Two walls: data and “emergence”

Data. If D must grow like √C, data runs out. Villalobos et al. (Epoch AI) estimate that, at current trends, training sets will match the whole stock of public human text between 2026 and 2032 (earlier if models are over-trained). One relief valve: Muennighoff et al. (2023) found that repeating data for up to about 4 epochs is almost as good as fresh data; beyond that the value of repeats decays fast. Others: synthetic data, code, other modalities.

Emergence. Wei et al. (2022) catalogued “emergent abilities”: tasks where small models score near zero and, past some scale, accuracy jumps, so they “cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating”. Schaeffer et al. (2023, “Are Emergent Abilities of LLMs a Mirage?”) argued many jumps come from the metric: an all-or-nothing score like exact match turns a smoothly improving per-token accuracy into a cliff. Both effects are real in places; the debate continues.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Scaling-law work is mostly careful bookkeeping and a log-log fit. Here both steps in NumPy: fit an exponent, then split a budget with a Chinchilla-style formula.

import numpy as np

# 1) Fit a power law: a straight line in log-log space
N = np.array([1e6, 3e6, 1e7, 3e7, 1e8, 3e8, 1e9])          # model sizes you trained
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
loss = (8.8e13 / N) ** 0.076 * np.exp(rng.normal(0, 0.005, N.size))  # Kaplan's L(N), plus noise
slope, icpt = np.polyfit(np.log(N), np.log(loss), 1)
print(f"fitted exponent {-slope:.3f}   predicted loss at 1e10: {np.exp(icpt) * 1e10 ** slope:.2f}")

# 2) Compute-optimal split of a FLOP budget, Chinchilla-style L(N, D) = E + A/N^a + B/D^b
E, A, B, a, b = 1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658     # Epoch AI's 2024 re-fit
L = lambda N, D: E + A / N**a + B / D**b
C = 6 * 280e9 * 300e9                                         # Gopher's budget, C = 6·N·D
Ns = np.logspace(9, 12, 3001)
best = Ns[np.argmin(L(Ns, C / (6 * Ns)))]
print(f"budget {C:.2e} FLOPs -> N* = {best/1e9:.0f}B params, D* = {C/6/best/1e12:.2f}T tokens "
      f"({C/6/best/best:.0f} tokens/param)")
print(f"Gopher 280B/0.3T loss {L(280e9, 300e9):.3f} vs optimum {L(best, C/6/best):.3f}")

# What we got:
# fitted exponent 0.075   predicted loss at 1e10: 2.00
# budget 5.04e+23 FLOPs -> N* = 67B params, D* = 1.25T tokens (18 tokens/param)
# Gopher 280B/0.3T loss 2.000 vs optimum 1.978

Real practice: fit on your data and tokenizer with a ladder of small runs, each with a learning-rate schedule matched to its own length (Chinchilla's key fix), and keep the largest runs out of the fit to test the prediction.

🎤 Interview angle

“You have 10²⁴ FLOPs. How big a model do you train?” A strong answer:

  • Uses C ≈ 6ND and Chinchilla's ~20 tokens/param: 10²⁴ = 6·N·20N ⇒ N ≈ √(10²⁴/120) ≈ 91B, D ≈ 1.8T tokens.
  • Then asks about inference: if it will serve heavily, go smaller and train longer (Llama 3 8B: ~1,900 tokens/param).
  • Asks whether that much unique data exists; repeating up to ~4 epochs is OK.
  • Says the constants must be refit on your data with a ladder of small runs, and that loss predicts benchmarks only roughly (emergence debate).
🏭 In the real world
The first scaling laws 🏢 OpenAI · 2020 Kaplan et al.: Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

How: hundreds of transformer runs; power laws in N, D and C over seven-plus orders of magnitude; C ≈ 6N FLOPs per token.

Why this method: made model planning predictive; it shaped the GPT-3 era's “scale the parameters” strategy.

Chinchilla 🏢 DeepMind · 2022 Hoffmann et al.: Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models

How: 400+ models from 70M to 16B+ params, three estimation methods, then a 70B model on 1.4T tokens with Gopher's budget.

Why this method: showed N and D should scale equally; Chinchilla beat the 4× larger Gopher.

Planning Llama 3 405B 🏢 Meta · 2024 The Llama 3 Herd of Models

How: scaling-law runs from 6e18 to 1e22 FLOPs picked a ~402B model for a 3.8e25 budget; a second fit mapped loss to benchmark accuracy. Small models were over-trained on ~15T tokens (blog).

Why this method: one shot at a huge run, so predict first; small models are what most people serve.

When data runs out 🧪 Research · 2023 Muennighoff et al.: Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models

How: trained models with repeated data; up to ~4 epochs gave negligible loss changes vs unique data.

Why this method: as fresh text runs low (Villalobos et al.), knowing how much reuse is safe matters.

🤯 Fun fact · the famous formula had a bug

In 2024 Epoch AI re-extracted the data from Chinchilla's plots and refit the formula. They found the published parametric fit didn't match the paper's other two methods, and its confidence intervals were so narrow they would need “over 600,000 experiments”, while the authors “likely only ran fewer than 500”. Their re-fit gives about 20 tokens per parameter, agreeing with how Chinchilla was actually trained. That's the “Epoch AI 2024 re-fit” button in the playground. Science works: even a landmark result gets checked.

Check yourself

1. A 7B-parameter model is trained on 2 trillion tokens. Roughly how much training compute is that?
C ≈ 6·N·D = 6 × 7 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10¹² = 84 × 10²¹ = 8.4 × 10²² FLOPs. (1.4 × 10²² forgets the factor 6: that's just N·D.) The 6 = 2 FLOPs per weight per token forward + 4 backward.
2. Your team's plan: double the model size, keep the same 300B-token dataset, and double the compute. A Chinchilla-minded reviewer objects. What's their point?
Chinchilla found N ∝ C0.5 and D ∝ C0.5: with 2× compute, use about 1.41× the params and 1.41× the tokens. Pre-Chinchilla models (GPT-3, Gopher) put almost all extra compute into parameters at ~300B tokens and were beaten by smaller models trained longer.
3. You'll serve a model to millions of users for a year. Why might you deliberately train a model far smaller than compute-optimal, on far more tokens?
Compute-optimal minimizes training cost for a target loss. Total cost includes serving: billions of tokens × 2N. Because the IsoFLOP curve is flat near its minimum, going 3–10× smaller costs a little loss, which more training tokens can buy back. That's the Llama 3 8B strategy (15T tokens, ~75× the Chinchilla amount).
📝 TL;DR
  • Training compute C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs (2 forward + 4 backward per weight per token).
  • Kaplan 2020: loss is a power law (straight line on log-log) in N, D and C; 10× params → loss × ~0.84. Fit small runs, predict big ones.
  • Chinchilla 2022: scale N and D equally (∝ √C), ~20 tokens per parameter. A 70B model beat 280B Gopher on the same budget.
  • Over-train small models when serving dominates (Llama 3 8B on 15T tokens): the optimum is flat.
  • Walls: public text may run out around 2026–2032 (repeat up to ~4 epochs); “emergent” jumps are partly a metric effect. Laws predict loss, not skills.
