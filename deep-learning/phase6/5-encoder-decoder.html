Phase 6 · Lesson 5

Encoder, decoder, encoder–decoder: BERT vs GPT vs T5

The same transformer block, wired three ways. One mask decides whether a model is built to understand, to generate, or to translate.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Self-attention with and without the causal mask (−∞ above the diagonal, Lesson 6.1); the transformer block (6.4).
  • Seq2seq: an encoder reads the source, a decoder writes the target, attending back to the encoder (Lesson 5.4).
🎯 Hook

In October 2018 Google released BERT and it set records on eleven language tasks at once, pushing the GLUE benchmark to 80.5% (a 7.7-point jump). A year later Google's T5 paper compared architectures head to head and found the original encoder–decoder “worked best”. Yet almost every big chatbot today, from GPT to Llama to Qwen, is decoder-only, the third design.

Meanwhile, the search box in your product probably still runs on an encoder: embedding models are the workhorse of retrieval and RAG.

Same block, three wirings. What does each one see, what is each good at, and why did the decoder take over general-purpose LLMs? You'll pick the right one for eight real tasks.

Numbers first: who can see whom

Take a 6-token input. The only real difference between the three designs is which attention scores are allowed:

💡 Big idea The mask is the architecture. Let every token see both sides and you get the best possible reading of a fixed text (encoder). Let tokens see only the past and you get a model that can write one token at a time (decoder). Keep both, joined by cross-attention, and you get a reader that hands its notes to a writer (encoder–decoder).
🧠 Analogy: an editor, a novelist and an interpreter

The editor (encoder) gets the whole page and can glance anywhere, so they understand every word in full context, but they don't write new pages. The novelist (decoder) writes left to right and can only reread what's already written. The interpreter (encoder–decoder) first listens to the whole foreign sentence like an editor, then speaks the translation like a novelist, glancing back at their notes on the source after every word.

The encoder: read both ways

BERT is a stack of transformer blocks with no mask. The word “bank” in “I sat by the river bank” can look at “river” on its left and at anything on its right. The catch: a model that sees everything can't be trained to predict the next word (it would just look). BERT instead hides 15% of the tokens and learns to fill them in (masked language modelling, Lesson 6.7).

🤔 Why this? A bidirectional encoder (BERT)
  1. The problem before Left-to-right language models (GPT-1, 2018) gave each word only its left context. For understanding tasks that's a handicap: whether “it” is the animal depends on words that come later.
  2. What people tried Concatenating a left-to-right and a right-to-left model (ELMo), which still never lets one layer see both sides at once.
  3. Why this works No mask: every token conditions on left and right context “in all layers”, as the BERT abstract puts it. Fine-tune with one extra output layer and it set records on 11 tasks (GLUE 80.5%, +7.7 points). For a fixed input, a full view is simply more information per token.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It can't generate text naturally: there's no left-to-right story to continue. Masked-token training only learns from the 15% of positions it hides. Classic BERT has a 512-token limit (learned positions, Lesson 6.3).
  5. What refined it RoBERTa, DeBERTa, sentence-embedding encoders (Sentence-BERT, 2019) and ModernBERT (2024: RoPE, 8,192 tokens, trained on 2 trillion tokens). Pretraining objectives: Lesson 6.7.

The decoder: write left to right

GPT is the same stack with the causal mask. Every position predicts the next token from what's before it, so one pass over a document gives a training signal at every position, and at generation time the model just keeps appending its own output.

🤔 Why this? A causal decoder (GPT)
  1. The problem before Encoders understand but can't write; RNN language models wrote, but slowly and with short memories (Phase 5).
  2. What people tried Encoder–decoders for every generation task, each trained for one job (translation, summarization).
  3. Why this works One objective (predict the next token) that works on any text, no labels; every position is a training example; and any task can be phrased as “continue this text”. GPT-1 (2018) was a 12-layer decoder-only transformer with masked self-attention; GPT-2 and GPT-3 scaled the same recipe.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each token sees only its left side, so for pure understanding of a fixed text it uses less context per token than an encoder of the same size. Generation is sequential: one forward pass per new token.
  5. What refined it Scale (Lesson 6.8), the KV cache to avoid recomputing the past (6.9), and instruction tuning and RLHF to turn a text-continuer into an assistant (RL 7.1).

The encoder–decoder: read, then write, with cross-attention

The original Transformer and T5 keep both halves. The encoder reads the source with full attention. The decoder writes with causal self-attention, and in every block it has one extra sublayer: cross-attention, where its queries come from the decoder and the keys and values come from the encoder's output. It's Bahdanau's attention from Lesson 5.4, rebuilt from transformer parts.

🤔 Why this? Cross-attention (encoder–decoder attention)
  1. The problem before In a translation decoder, each new word must consult the source sentence, which isn't part of the decoder's own sequence.
  2. What people tried Squeezing the source into one vector (the seq2seq bottleneck, Lesson 5.4), then RNN attention over encoder states.
  3. Why this works Queries from the decoder (“what do I need for the next word?”), keys and values from the encoder (“what the source says”): every target token can look at every source token, with the source read once, bidirectionally. T5 found this form “worked best” in its text-to-text comparisons, at about the same compute as a decoder-only model with half the parameters.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two stacks to build, train and serve; the input and output must be split into “source” and “target”, which fits translation and transcription better than open-ended chat with long, mixed histories.
  5. What refined it T5's “everything is text-to-text” framing; Whisper for speech; the same cross-attention idea in image generators and multimodal models (Phases 7 and 9).

What you should see: understanding a fixed text (classification, tagging, embeddings for search) → encoder; open-ended generation (chat, code completion) → decoder; a clear source turning into a target (translation, speech → text, fine-tuned summarization) → encoder–decoder. A big decoder can do almost all of them by prompting, which is half the story of why it took over.

Why decoder-only won for general LLMs

No single paper settles this; the common explanation combines several facts:

  • One simple objective on all text. Next-token prediction needs no labels and no source/target split, and uses every position of every document.
  • Every task fits the format. Classification, translation and Q&A all become “continue this prompt”, so one model serves everything (Lesson 6.7, and GPT-3's few-shot results).
  • Evidence at scale. Wang et al. (2022) trained 5-billion-parameter models of each design: causal decoder-only models trained on next-token prediction had “the strongest zero-shot generalization after purely unsupervised pretraining”. (With multitask fine-tuning, a non-causal model with masked-token training did best: the answer depends on the recipe.)
  • Simple to scale and serve. One stack, one mask, and a KV cache (6.9) that makes chat-style generation cheap to extend.

Encoders didn't lose: they moved to the jobs they're best at. ModernBERT's authors note encoders remain “the workhorse of numerous production pipelines”, especially retrieval and classification.

✏️ Worked example: what the masks cost for a translation

Translate a 10-token source into a 12-token target, per layer and per head:

AttentionRows × columnsScores
Encoder self-attention (full)10 × 10100
Decoder self-attention (causal)12 × 12, lower triangle12 × 13 / 2 = 78
Cross-attention (decoder → encoder)12 × 10120

A decoder-only model would instead put source and target in one 22-token sequence with one causal mask: 22 × 23 / 2 = 253 scores, versus 100 + 78 + 120 = 298, in one stack instead of two. Similar work, simpler plumbing.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

The masks and cross-attention are a few lines of PyTorch (runs in a second, no downloads):

import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
n_src, n_tgt, d = 6, 4, 32
causal = torch.triu(torch.ones(n_tgt, n_tgt, dtype=torch.bool), 1)   # decoder: hide the future
print(causal.int())                                                    # an encoder simply has no mask

enc_out = torch.randn(1, n_src, d)        # what the encoder made of the source sentence
dec_x   = torch.randn(1, n_tgt, d)        # the decoder's tokens so far
cross = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, 4, batch_first=True)
out, w = cross(query=dec_x, key=enc_out, value=enc_out)   # Q from decoder, K and V from encoder
print(out.shape, w.shape)                 # every target token looks at all 6 source tokens
print(w[0].sum(-1))                        # each row of weights sums to 1

from transformers import pipeline, AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM

fill = pipeline("fill-mask", model="answerdotai/ModernBERT-base")     # encoder

🎤 Interview angle

“BERT vs GPT vs T5: when would you use each?” A strong answer:

  • Names the mask: BERT full (bidirectional), GPT causal, T5 encoder full + decoder causal + cross-attention (Q from decoder, K/V from encoder).
  • Ties each to its objective: masked tokens, next token, text-to-text span filling.
  • Matches tasks: encoder for classification/NER/embeddings/retrieval; decoder for open generation and general assistants; encoder–decoder for translation, speech recognition, fine-tuned summarization.
  • Explains decoder-only dominance carefully: simple universal objective, prompting covers all tasks, strong zero-shot at scale (Wang et al. 2022), simple serving; not a proof that it's best for everything.
🏭 In the real world
Bidirectional encoder 🏢 Google · 2018 Devlin et al.: BERT

How: a 12- or 24-layer encoder (110M and 340M parameters) pretrained by filling in 15% masked tokens, then fine-tuned with one output layer per task.

Why this method: both-sided context in every layer; it set new records on eleven tasks.

Decoder-only pretraining 🏢 OpenAI · 2018 Radford et al.: Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training (GPT-1)

How: a 12-layer decoder-only transformer with masked self-attention, trained on next-token prediction, then fine-tuned.

Why this method: unlabeled text is plentiful and next-token prediction uses all of it; the line led to GPT-2, GPT-3 and today's assistants.

Everything as text-to-text 🏢 Google · 2019 Raffel et al.: T5

How: one encoder–decoder (up to 11B parameters) where every task is a text prefix, like “translate English to German: That is good.” → “Das ist gut.”

Why this method: in their comparison, “the encoder-decoder architecture with the denoising objective performed best” at equal compute.

Encoders for search 🧪 Research · 2019 Reimers & Gurevych: Sentence-BERT

How: fine-tune BERT so each sentence becomes one vector, compared by cosine similarity.

Why this method: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences took about 65 hours with BERT reading every pair, and about 5 seconds with precomputed embeddings.

Encoder–decoder for speech 🏢 OpenAI · 2022 Radford et al.: Whisper

How: an encoder reads the audio spectrogram; a decoder writes the transcript, cross-attending to the audio.

Why this method: a clear source (sound) and target (text) of different kinds: exactly the shape cross-attention was built for (Phase 8).

🤯 Fun fact · 65 hours vs 5 seconds

BERT can judge whether two sentences mean the same thing, but only by reading them together. To find the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences that's about 50 million pairs: roughly 65 hours. Sentence-BERT encodes each sentence once into a vector, and the same search takes about 5 seconds. Every vector database you'll meet relies on this trick.

Check yourself

1. A decoder-only model processes 1,000 tokens. How many attention scores per head and layer does its causal mask allow, compared with an encoder on the same input?
Causal: token i sees i tokens (itself and everything before), so the total is 1 + 2 + … + 1,000 = 1,000 × 1,001 / 2 = 500,500. An encoder allows all 1,000² = 1,000,000. (Many kernels still compute the full square and mask it; fused ones like FlashAttention skip the masked half.)
2. Your team needs to route 50 million support tickets a day into 30 categories, on CPUs, with tight latency. Someone proposes prompting a 70B decoder-only chatbot. What's the better default?
Classification of a fixed text is the encoder's home turf: full context, one forward pass, a tiny output layer, and models in the 100M range that run on CPUs. Decoder-only won for general-purpose assistants, not for every high-volume, single-task job. Prompting a 70B model 50 million times a day would cost far more for little gain.
3. In a translation model's cross-attention layer, where do the queries, keys and values come from?
Each target position asks a question about the source: so the query comes from the decoder's current state and is matched against keys made from the encoder's output, and the answer is a blend of the encoder's values. That's “encoder-decoder attention” in Vaswani et al., the transformer version of Bahdanau attention.
📝 TL;DR
  • Same block, three masks: encoder (full, BERT), decoder (causal, GPT), encoder–decoder (full + causal + cross-attention, T5, Whisper).
  • Cross-attention: queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder.
  • Encoders read best: classification, tagging, embeddings for search and RAG. Decoders write: chat, code, anything as “continue the text”.
  • Decoder-only won for general LLMs because of one universal objective, prompting for any task, strong zero-shot at scale and simple serving (the common explanation, not a theorem).
  • Greedy decoding can loop; sampling with a temperature adds variety; the KV cache avoids recomputing the past (6.9).
