Pretraining objectives: masked vs next-token

Nobody labels the internet. The trick behind every large language model is a game the text plays against itself: hide some words, guess them back. Which words you hide decides whether you get BERT, T5 or GPT.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A transformer turns a sequence of tokens into one vector per token, using attention to mix in context (Lessons 6.1 to 6.4). A causal mask stops a token from looking at later tokens (6.4, 6.5).
  • Encoder (BERT), decoder (GPT) and encoder–decoder (T5) are three ways to wire those blocks (Lesson 6.5). Text is chopped into tokens first (6.6).
  • The output layer gives a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and the loss is cross-entropy: −ln(probability of the right token) (Lesson 0.6, math 6.3).
🎯 Hook

In 2020 OpenAI trained GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter transformer, on 300 billion tokens of text. Its only job during training: guess the next token. Nobody showed it a single labelled arithmetic problem.

Then they gave it a few examples like “48 plus 76 = 124” and asked new ones. It got 100% of two-digit additions right. The 13-billion-parameter version, trained the same way, managed only about half.

Two years earlier, Google's BERT had been trained on a different game, fill in the blank, and it swept the language-understanding benchmarks, but you can't chat with it. Why does “guess the next word” grow into a general assistant, while “fill in the blank” makes a brilliant reader that can't write? You'll play both games below and find out.

Numbers first: free labels, by the trillion

Supervised learning needs labels, and labels cost money. ImageNet, the dataset that launched deep learning in vision (Lesson 0.4), has about 1.3 million hand-labelled training images.

Now take one sentence: “the cat sat on the mat”. Without any human, it gives you six training examples:

Every token of text is a free label. Llama 3's largest model was trained on 15.6 trillion tokens: that's millions of times more labelled examples than ImageNet, and the internet wrote them for free.

💡 Big idea Self-supervised learning: hide part of the data and ask the model to predict it from the rest. The data supplies its own labels, so you can train on all the text that exists. The pretraining objective is simply which part you hide. That one choice shapes what the model becomes.
🧠 Analogy: three ways to study a textbook

Fill-in-the-blank worksheet (BERT): cover random words, guess them using everything around them. Great for understanding, but you never practise writing a paragraph from scratch.

Write the next line (GPT): cover everything after the current word, guess one word at a time. Harder, but it's exactly the skill of writing.

Repair the torn page (T5): whole phrases are ripped out; you rewrite just the missing pieces.

🤔 Why this? Masked language modelling (BERT)
  1. The problem before Older pretrained language models read left to right, so the vector for “bank” in “the bank of the river” was computed before the model saw “river”. Combining a left-to-right and a right-to-left model (ELMo, 2018) helped, but each half was still blind to one side.
  2. What people tried Just letting a left-to-right model see both sides. That fails: if a token can see the whole sentence, predicting a word is trivial, because the word itself is in the input.
  3. Why this works Hide the word first, then let every other token see everything. BERT picks 15% of positions; of those, 80% become [MASK], 10% become a random token and 10% stay unchanged. The 10/10 part exists because [MASK] never appears when you later fine-tune, so the model must not learn “only fix the [MASK] spots”. Result: deep two-sided context, ideal for classification, search and tagging.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Only 15% of tokens get a loss, so each sentence teaches less (BERT's authors measured slightly slower convergence than left-to-right). It can't generate text naturally: it never learned to continue a sentence. Several masks in one sentence are predicted independently (“New [MASK] [MASK]” can't coordinate “York City”).
  5. What refined it RoBERTa (more data, new masks each epoch), ELECTRA (detect replaced tokens, so every token gets a loss), span corruption (T5, below). Encoders like this still power embeddings and rerankers (AI Engineer 2.1).
🤔 Why this? Next-token prediction (GPT)
  1. The problem before You want one model that writes: answers, code, stories. A fill-in-the-blank model has no natural way to produce a long text, and only 15% of its tokens train it.
  2. What people tried Recurrent language models (Lesson 5.2) did predict the next word, but they read one token at a time, so training on billions of tokens was slow and long-range memory faded.
  3. Why this works Factor the probability of a whole text into a chain: P(text) = P(t₁) · P(t₂ | t₁) · P(t₃ | t₁ t₂) · …. Learn each factor, and you can both score any text and generate by sampling one token at a time. Every token is a label (6 tokens → 6 losses, vs about 1 for BERT's 15%), and with a causal mask a transformer computes all of them in one parallel pass (below). Training and use are the same task, so no mismatch.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each token sees only its left side, so for pure “understand this sentence” tasks a same-size encoder can be stronger. Generation is one token at a time (slow; Lesson 6.9 speeds it up). And it learns to imitate text, including errors and toxicity: it is not trained to be helpful or truthful.
  5. What refined it Scale (Lesson 6.8), then instruction tuning and preference training after pretraining (below). Today almost every frontier LLM is a decoder trained this way.
🤔 Why this? Span corruption (T5)
  1. The problem before BERT predicts each masked token separately and can't output text; GPT generates but sees only one side of its input.
  2. What people tried The T5 team compared many objectives head-to-head on one model: prefix LM, BERT-style, deshuffling, dropping tokens, and spans of different lengths.
  3. Why this works Corrupt 15% of tokens in spans of about 3 tokens on average, and replace each span by one sentinel: “Thank you <X> me to your party <Y> week.” The decoder writes only “<X> for inviting <Y> last <Z>”. The encoder sees both sides, the decoder practises writing, and targets are short, so training is cheap. It frames every task as text in, text out.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Two stacks (encoder and decoder) to build and serve, and the pretraining task still isn't “continue this document”, so open-ended chat needs more training than a decoder does.
  5. What refined it UL2 mixed several corruption styles; but the field mostly consolidated on decoder-only next-token prediction for general LLMs, while encoder–decoders survive in translation and speech (Lessons 6.5, 8.x).

Teacher forcing: training every position at once

Here's a puzzle. Generation is slow and sequential: one token, then the next. So is training a GPT sequential too? No, and that's half the reason transformers won.

During training we already know the whole sentence. So at every position we feed the true previous tokens (not the model's own guesses). That's called teacher forcing. Shift the labels left by one, and position i predicts token i+1. The causal mask (Lesson 6.4) makes sure position i can't peek at token i+1 through attention. One forward pass, n predictions, n losses.

🤔 Why this? Teacher forcing + causal mask
  1. The problem before If training used the model's own previous guesses, every sentence would need n sequential forward passes, and one early mistake would derail every later prediction.
  2. What people tried RNN language models already used teacher forcing, but an RNN still processes positions one after another (Lesson 5.2), so the GPU waits.
  3. Why this works Feed the true tokens, shift labels by one, and use a triangular mask so position i only attends to positions ≤ i. A 2,048-token sequence gives 2,048 losses from one parallel pass, with exactly the information each prediction would have at generation time.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Exposure bias: in training the model always sees perfect history; when generating it sees its own (sometimes wrong) tokens and can drift. Forget or break the mask and the model cheats.
  5. What refined it Sampling strategies at generation time, and post-training with the model's own outputs (RL fine-tuning, below) partly address exposure bias. Lesson 6.9 makes generation fast with the KV cache.

Perplexity: “how many words is it torn between?”

A language model's loss is the average of −ln p(right token). Loss 2.3 is hard to feel. Exponentiate it and you get perplexity:

perplexity = emean loss = e−(1/n) Σ ln p(tᵢ)

In words: perplexity is the number of equally likely choices the model is effectively choosing between at each step. A model guessing uniformly over 1,000 tokens has loss ln 1000 = 6.91 and perplexity exactly 1,000. A perfect model has perplexity 1. (Cross-entropy and surprise are built from scratch in math 6.1 and math 6.3.)

What you should see: the “one disaster” preset (five tokens at 0.9, one at 0.001) gives mean loss 1.24 and perplexity 3.45, three times worse than five good tokens alone (1.11). The log punishes confident mistakes brutally: a single token at p = 0.001 costs 6.9 nats, as much as 66 tokens at 0.9. That's why language models learn to hedge rather than be confidently wrong.

Gotcha: perplexity depends on the tokenizer. A model with bigger tokens makes fewer, harder predictions per sentence, so compare perplexities only between models with the same tokenizer (Lesson 6.6), or use bits per byte.

Why does “guess the next word” grow into general ability?

Read this carefully, because it's the most important (and most argued-about) idea in the field. To predict the next token really well across all text, a model has to capture whatever produced that text:

  • “The capital of France is ___” → it has to store facts.
  • “48 + 76 = ___” → it gets lower loss if it can actually add.
  • A proof, a function body, a chess game → lower loss if it tracks the structure and the rules.
  • “Translate to French: cheese →” → the internet is full of such patterns, so it learns tasks it was never explicitly given.

The objective never changes; what changes with scale is how much of that structure fits into the model. That's the evidence from GPT-3's few-shot results in the hook, and Lesson 6.8 shows the loss falls smoothly and predictably as models grow.

The careful part: lower loss on text is not the same as being correct, honest or helpful. The model learns to imitate the internet, including wrong answers, and when asked a question it may continue with more questions. Nobody fully understands which abilities appear at which scale (the “emergence” debate is in 6.8). That's why there is a second stage.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Both objectives are just a choice of inputs and labels for F.cross_entropy. Label -100 means “no loss at this position”. In Hugging Face, DataCollatorForLanguageModeling(tokenizer, mlm=True) does BERT masking (defaults: mlm_probability=0.15, 80% mask, 10% random) and mlm=False gives next-token labels; AutoModelForCausalLM shifts the labels for you.

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
V, MASK = 1000, 4                      # vocabulary size, id of the [MASK] token
x = torch.randint(5, V, (8, 128))      # a batch: 8 sequences of 128 token ids

# 1) Masked LM (BERT): pick 15% of positions, then 80% [MASK] / 10% random / 10% unchanged
chosen = torch.rand(x.shape) < 0.15
labels = torch.where(chosen, x, -100)   # -100 = "no loss here" for F.cross_entropy
r = torch.rand(x.shape)
inp = x.clone()
inp[chosen & (r < 0.8)] = MASK
swap = chosen & (r >= 0.8) & (r < 0.9)
inp[swap] = torch.randint(5, V, (int(swap.sum()),))
print("positions with a loss:", int(chosen.sum()), "of", x.numel())

# 2) Next-token (GPT): the labels are the inputs shifted left by one. Every position has a loss.
logits = torch.zeros(8, 128, V)        # an untrained model that guesses uniformly
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, V), x[:, 1:].reshape(-1))
print(f"next-token loss {loss:.4f} = ln {V}; perplexity {loss.exp():.1f}")

Gotcha: in next-token training the logits at position i are compared with the token at i+1. Forget the shift and your model learns to copy its input, with a beautiful loss of ~0 (you saw it above). The lab (Lab 6) trains a tiny GPT with this exact loss.

🎤 Interview angle

“What's the difference between how BERT and GPT are pretrained, and why did the GPT approach win for chatbots?” A strong answer:

  • MLM: mask 15% (80/10/10), predict from both sides, loss on the masked tokens only; NTP: predict token i+1 from tokens ≤ i, loss on every token.
  • Explains teacher forcing + causal mask: all positions are trained in parallel in one pass.
  • NTP's training task is generation, so prompting turns one model into many tools; it scales smoothly (6.8).
  • Knows perplexity = eloss, and that it's tokenizer-dependent.
  • Names post-training (SFT, RLHF/DPO) as what turns a base model into an assistant, and that encoders still win for embeddings and classification at small sizes.
🏭 In the real world
BERT: masked LM 🧪 Research · 2018 Devlin et al.: BERT

How: a transformer encoder pretrained with 15% masking (80/10/10) on BooksCorpus (800M words) and English Wikipedia (2,500M words), then fine-tuned per task.

Why this method: two-sided context for understanding tasks; it set new records on GLUE and SQuAD.

GPT-3: next-token at scale 🧪 Research · 2020 Brown et al.: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners

How: a 175B decoder trained for 300B tokens on next-token prediction only; tasks are given as a few examples in the prompt.

Why this method: one objective, no task-specific labels, and abilities like few-shot arithmetic that smaller models lacked.

T5: span corruption, everything is text-to-text 🧪 Research · 2019 Raffel et al.: Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning

How: corrupt 15% of tokens in spans (mean length 3), the decoder writes back only the missing spans.

Why this method: in their systematic comparison it performed as well as the alternatives while producing shorter targets, so it trained faster.

InstructGPT: what comes after 🏢 OpenAI · 2022 Ouyang et al.: Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback

How: start from pretrained GPT-3, then supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations and RLHF on human rankings.

Why this method: the pretrained model knew a lot but didn't follow instructions; a 1.3B InstructGPT was preferred to the 175B base model.

🤯 Fun fact · BERT knew it was slower

BERT's authors tested the obvious worry: since only 15% of tokens get a loss, does masked LM learn slower than a left-to-right model that predicts every token? Their answer (Appendix C.1): it does converge “marginally slower”, but the gains from seeing both sides “far outweigh” the extra training cost. For understanding tasks at 2018 scale, the trade was worth it. For generation at 2020s scale, the field took the other side.

Check yourself

1. On a test set, a model gives the true tokens probabilities 0.5, 0.5, 0.25 and 0.25. What is its perplexity?
Mean loss = (0.693 + 0.693 + 1.386 + 1.386) ÷ 4 = 1.040, and e1.040 = 2.83. Equivalently the geometric mean of 2, 2, 4, 4: (2·2·4·4)1/4 = 641/4 = 2.83. Perplexity averages in log space, so it's below the plain average 3.
2. You train a small decoder on next-token prediction. After 100 steps the training loss is 0.01, but generated text is gibberish. What's the most likely bug?
A loss that low that fast means the answer leaked into the input. Without the causal mask (or with labels not shifted by one), predicting “the next token” is just copying a visible token. At generation time there's nothing to copy, so it fails. Always check: logits[:, :-1] vs tokens[:, 1:], and a triangular mask.
3. You need a small, fast model that turns support tickets into embeddings for search and classifies them into 20 categories. No text generation is needed. Which pretraining objective suits the base model best?
For understanding tasks without generation, an encoder that sees both sides of every token is the natural fit and is cheap to run. Next-token decoders can also do it (and big ones do it well), but at small sizes BERT-style encoders remain the standard for embeddings, rerankers and classifiers.
📝 TL;DR
  • Self-supervised pretraining hides part of raw text and predicts it: every token is a free label, so you can train on trillions.
  • Masked LM (BERT): 15% of tokens, 80/10/10, two-sided context, loss on the masked ones only. Great reader, can't write.
  • Next-token (GPT): predict token i+1 from tokens ≤ i, loss on every token. Teacher forcing + causal mask train all positions in one pass. Training = generation.
  • Span corruption (T5): replace short spans with sentinels, decoder writes them back.
  • Perplexity = emean loss = the effective number of choices per token; one confident mistake costs a lot. Compare only with the same tokenizer.
  • A pretrained base model imitates text; SFT and RLHF/DPO turn it into an assistant.
