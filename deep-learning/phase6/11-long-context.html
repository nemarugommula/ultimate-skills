Phase 6 · Lesson 11

Long context

Context windows went from 2,048 tokens (GPT-3) to a million (2026 open models). Getting there meant fighting a quadratic bill, a memory explosion, positions the model had never seen, and a model that reads the whole book but forgets the middle.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Attention compares every token with every earlier token: n² scores for n tokens (6.1). FlashAttention avoids storing them but still computes them (6.9).
  • RoPE (rotary position embedding) rotates each pair of numbers in a query or key by an angle proportional to the token's position, with fast- and slow-turning pairs, like the hands of a clock (Lesson 6.3).
  • The KV cache stores keys and values for every past token: 128 KiB per token for Llama 3 8B in bf16 (6.9).
🎯 Hook

In 2024 Google gave Gemini 1.5 a grammar manual for Kalamang, a language with fewer than 200 speakers, inside its prompt. No Kalamang in its training data. It learned to translate English to Kalamang about as well as a person who learned from the same material. Its report also claims near-perfect retrieval (> 99%) of planted facts at up to 10 million tokens.

A year earlier, Liu et al. had tested a model on questions with 20 documents in the prompt. When the document with the answer sat in the middle, GPT-3.5-Turbo did worse than with no documents at all (56.1% closed-book).

How do you stretch a model trained on 4,000 tokens to a million, what does it cost, and why can a model that “reads” a million tokens still miss a fact in the middle?

For Llama 3 8B (32 layers, d = 4,096) that equals the weights' 2N when n ≈ 61k tokens: past that, attention costs more than the whole rest of the model. Then there's position: the model was trained up to some length, and positions beyond it are new territory.

Positions the model has never seen

With RoPE, each pair of dimensions in a query or key turns by position × θi, where θi = base−2i/d (base usually 10,000). Fast pairs spin many times within the training length, so the model sees every angle. Slow pairs may turn less than a full circle in training, so the model only ever sees a slice of their dial. Go past the training length and those slow hands point at angles the model has never seen: attention scores go haywire and quality collapses, often abruptly.

Three fixes, from simplest to best:

  • Position interpolation (PI, Chen et al. 2023): divide every position by s = new length ÷ trained length, so all angles land in the trained range. Llama models stretched to 32,768 tokens with under 1,000 fine-tuning steps. Cost: the fast hands slow down too, so neighbouring tokens look more alike.
  • NTK-aware scaling (proposed online by “bloc97”, 2023): raise the RoPE base instead, which stretches slow pairs a lot and fast pairs barely at all. Not exact for middle pairs.
  • YaRN (Peng et al. 2023): per-pair treatment: leave fast pairs alone, interpolate slow pairs fully, blend in between, and slightly sharpen attention (a temperature of 0.1·ln s + 1). It needed about 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than earlier methods. Qwen3's model card tells you to switch it on with "rope_type": "yarn", "factor": 4.0 to go from 32k to 131k.

Position interpolation pulls everything back into range, but the fastest dial now turns at ¼ speed: nearby tokens blur together. 🤔 Why this? RoPE scaling (PI → NTK-aware → YaRN)
  1. The problem before Retraining at long length is expensive (attention is n²), but a RoPE model run past its training length sees unseen angles on its slow dimensions and its perplexity explodes.
  2. What people tried Plain extrapolation (just run longer: fails), learned or ALiBi-style positions, training from scratch on long data.
  3. Why this works Map the new, longer positions back onto angles the model knows. PI divides positions by s (e.g. 16k → looks like 4k), YaRN does it only for the slow dimensions that need it and leaves the fast, local ones sharp, plus a small attention temperature. A short fine-tune (hundreds to a few thousand steps) then adapts the model. In the playground: at 32k, PI and YaRN both give 0 dials out of range, but only YaRN keeps the fastest dial at full speed.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Still needs some long-document fine-tuning to be good; stretched short models can pass simple retrieval tests while failing harder long-range tasks (RULER, below). Static scaling slightly hurts short-context quality, which is why Qwen recommends enabling it only when needed.
  5. What refined it Training with a much larger RoPE base from the start (Llama 3 uses θ = 500,000), progressive long-context training stages, and sparse or compressed attention designs (DeepSeek-V4).

Sliding windows and attention sinks

Sliding-window attention (SWA): each token attends only to the previous W tokens. Mistral 7B used W = 4,096 across 32 layers. Information still travels further, one window per layer: after 32 layers, a token can be influenced by tokens up to 32 × 4,096 ≈ 131k positions back. A rolling buffer keeps only W keys and values per layer: on a 32k sequence that cut cache memory 8× without hurting quality in their tests.

🤔 Why this? Sliding-window attention
  1. The problem before Full attention's cost per token grows with n and its cache grows forever: 16 GiB per user at 128k for Llama 3 8B.
  2. What people tried Sparse patterns (Longformer: local windows + a few global tokens), compressing old tokens, recurrence across segments.
  3. Why this works Most of what a token needs is nearby. Capping the look-back at W makes per-token cost and cache size constant, and stacking layers still lets information hop W tokens per layer (Mistral: ~131k theoretical span from W = 4,096 × 32 layers).
  4. What it costs, when it fails A fact 100k tokens back must be relayed through many layers and often gets lost: exact long-range recall suffers. And naive window attention breaks once the very first tokens fall out of the window (next box).
  5. What refined it Hybrid models that interleave sliding-window layers with a few full-attention layers, attention sinks, and state-space layers (9.3).

StreamingLLM (Xiao et al. 2023) found a strange thing: window attention works until the first few tokens are evicted, then perplexity explodes. Those early tokens get huge attention scores even though they mean nothing special. Softmax must put its weight somewhere, and the first tokens, visible to every later token, became the model's dumping ground: an attention sink. Keep just 4 initial tokens' keys and values plus the recent window, and models like Llama 2 stayed stable for up to 4 million tokens, up to 22.2× faster than recomputing the window.

🤔 Why this? Attention sinks (StreamingLLM)
  1. The problem before A chatbot that runs forever needs a bounded cache. Sliding windows bound it, but quality collapses as soon as the cache drops the start of the text.
  2. What people tried Recomputing the window from scratch each step (correct but slow), or growing the cache until memory runs out.
  3. Why this works Many heads have nothing useful to attend to for a given token; softmax weights must still sum to 1, so trained models park that spare weight on the first tokens. Removing them shifts the whole distribution. Keeping them (4 tokens is enough) restores normal attention, at a cost of 4 extra cache slots.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It makes long streams stable, not remembered: anything outside the window and the sinks is gone. It's a fluency fix, not a memory fix.
  5. What refined it Training with a dedicated learnable sink token (suggested in the same paper) or a learned per-head sink; for real memory you need full long context or retrieval.

Ring attention (Liu, Zaharia & Abbeel, 2023) attacks the problem from the hardware side: split a very long sequence across GPUs arranged in a ring; each GPU keeps its block of queries and passes key/value blocks to its neighbour while computing blockwise attention, overlapping communication with math. Exact attention on sequences “up to device count times longer” than one device could hold. It's how training on millions of tokens becomes possible at all (Phase 10 covers sequence parallelism).

Using a long context well: lost in the middle

A long window is not the same as good use of it. Liu et al. (2023) moved the one relevant document through a list of distractors and measured accuracy. The result was a U-shape: best when the fact is at the start (primacy) or the end (recency), worst in the middle. For GPT-3.5-Turbo with 20 documents, the middle was below its 56.1% closed-book score.

Measuring long context: needles and beyond

The popular needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) test hides one sentence (“the magic number is 7481”) at some depth in a long filler text and asks for it. Plot depth × length as a grid; an all-green grid became the standard marketing image. Its limit: finding one distinctive string is easy for attention, a lexical match. RULER (NVIDIA, 2024) added multiple needles, multi-hop tracing, aggregation and question answering. Of 17 long-context models tested, almost all were nearly perfect on vanilla NIAH, but “only half of them” kept satisfactory performance at 32k, even though all claimed 32k or more. Lesson: an advertised context length is a ceiling, not a guarantee.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

In Hugging Face, RoPE scaling is a config field. This builds a small Llama-shaped config with YaRN (factor 4: 4,096 → 16,384) and inspects what it does to each rotating pair, with no model download:

import math
from transformers import LlamaConfig
from transformers.modeling_rope_utils import ROPE_INIT_FUNCTIONS

shape = dict(hidden_size=1024, num_attention_heads=8, rope_theta=10000.0)      # head dim 128 = 64 rotating pairs
plain = LlamaConfig(**shape, max_position_embeddings=4096)
yarn = LlamaConfig(**shape, max_position_embeddings=16384,                     # 4x the trained 4,096
                   rope_scaling={"rope_type": "yarn", "factor": 4.0, "original_max_position_embeddings": 4096})

f0, _ = ROPE_INIT_FUNCTIONS["default"](plain, "cpu")      # rotation speed of each pair (radians per token)
f1, attn = ROPE_INIT_FUNCTIONS["yarn"](yarn, "cpu")
print("attention scale", round(attn, 4), "= 0.1*ln(4)+1 =", round(0.1 * math.log(4) + 1, 4))
for i in [0, 16, 40, 63]:                                  # fastest ... slowest rotating pair
    print(f"pair {i:2d}: wavelength {2*math.pi/f0[i]:6.0f} tokens -> yarn speed / plain speed = {f1[i]/f0[i]:.3f}")

# What we got (transformers 4.57, CPU):
# attention scale 1.1386 = 0.1*ln(4)+1 = 1.1386
# pair  0: wavelength      6 tokens -> yarn speed / plain speed = 1.000
# pair 16: wavelength     63 tokens -> yarn speed / plain speed = 1.000
# pair 40: wavelength   1987 tokens -> yarn speed / plain speed = 0.423
# pair 63: wavelength  54410 tokens -> yarn speed / plain speed = 0.250

Exactly the playground's story: fast pairs untouched, slow pairs slowed by the full factor 4, a blend in between, and attention sharpened by 1.14. Serving engines (vLLM, SGLang) accept the same rope_scaling dict. Sliding windows: Mistral-family configs carry sliding_window; FlashAttention supports windowed attention natively.

🎤 Interview angle

“A customer wants to paste 500-page contracts into your model. What breaks and what do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Sizes it: ~250k tokens → n² prefill (tens of seconds+), KV cache of tens of GiB per user (2 · layers · kv_heads · head_dim · n · bytes).
  • Checks the model's trained and effective length (RULER-style evals, not just NIAH); RoPE scaling (YaRN) only with a long-context fine-tune.
  • Knows lost-in-the-middle: put key clauses first or last, or retrieve the relevant sections (RAG) and keep the prompt short.
  • Reduces serving cost: GQA/MLA models, FP8 KV cache, prefix caching for the same contract, sliding-window/hybrid architectures.
🏭 In the real world
Position interpolation and YaRN 🧪 Research · 2023 Chen et al.: Position Interpolation, Peng et al.: YaRN

How: squeeze positions (PI), or per-frequency interpolation + attention temperature (YaRN), then a short fine-tune.

Why this method: LLaMA to 32k with under 1,000 steps (PI); YaRN with 10× fewer tokens; now a one-line config in Qwen3 (32k → 131k).

Mistral 7B: sliding window + rolling cache 🏢 Mistral AI · 2023 Jiang et al.: Mistral 7B

How: W = 4,096 window over 32 layers (~131k theoretical span), cache as a ring buffer of size W.

Why this method: 8× less cache memory at 32k tokens without hurting quality, plus GQA for faster decoding.

StreamingLLM: attention sinks 🧪 Research · 2023 Xiao et al.: Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks

How: keep 4 initial tokens' KV + a recent window.

Why this method: stable language modelling up to 4M tokens with no fine-tuning; up to 22.2× faster than window recomputation.

Million-token open models 🏢 DeepSeek · 2026 DeepSeek-V4 model card

How: a hybrid of compressed sparse attention schemes for a 1M-token context.

Why this method: at 1M tokens, DeepSeek reports V4-Pro needs 27% of the per-token inference FLOPs and 10% of the KV cache of their V3.2.

Testing long context honestly 🧪 Research · 2024 Hsieh et al.: RULER, Liu et al.: Lost in the Middle

How: multi-needle, tracing, aggregation and QA tasks at growing lengths; moving the answer through the context.

Why this method: simple needle tests saturate; these expose effective context far below the advertised one.

🤯 Fun fact · GPT-3's whole window would fit in a footnote now

GPT-3 (2020) had a 2,048-token context. Llama 3.1 (2024) supports 128k: 64× more. DeepSeek-V4 (2026) supports 1M: about 500× GPT-3. Gemini 1.5's report went to 10M tokens in research tests. Meanwhile the hardest part moved from “can it fit?” to “does it actually use it?”, which is why RULER and lost-in-the-middle exist.

Check yourself

1. A model has 40 layers and attention width dattn = 5,120, with 13B parameters. At what context length do attention FLOPs per token (2 · layers · n · dattn) equal the weights' 2N?
Set 2 · 40 · n · 5,120 = 2 · 13 × 10⁹ → n = 13 × 10⁹ ÷ (40 × 5,120) = 13 × 10⁹ ÷ 204,800 ≈ 63,500. Below that, weights dominate per-token cost; above it, attention does. Prefill grows even faster, with n².
2. You run a RoPE model trained on 8k tokens at 32k with no changes. Output turns to gibberish after about 8k tokens. Which fix addresses the cause?
Past the trained length, the slow RoPE dimensions produce rotation angles the model never saw, so attention breaks. Interpolation methods map positions back into the trained range (YaRN keeps fast dimensions sharp). Attention sinks fix stability of sliding windows, not unseen positions; cache precision is unrelated.
3. Your RAG prompt has 25 retrieved passages; evals show answers are often wrong when the best passage ranks 8th to 18th. Cheapest first fix?
That's lost in the middle: models use the beginning and end of the context best. Moving the most relevant passages to the edges and trimming the list costs nothing and often fixes it. A bigger window doesn't change the U-shape; more passages push the answer further into the middle.
📝 TL;DR
  • Longer context: attention FLOPs per token ∝ n (beat the weights past ~60k for an 8B), prefill ∝ n², KV cache ∝ n (Llama 3 8B: 16 GiB at 128k).
  • Past the trained length, slow RoPE dimensions see unseen angles. Fix: position interpolation (squeeze all), NTK-aware (raise the base), YaRN (per-frequency + temperature), then a short fine-tune.
  • Sliding windows make cost and cache constant (Mistral W = 4,096); attention sinks (keep 4 first tokens) keep streaming stable; ring attention spreads exact attention across GPUs.
  • Models use the middle of long prompts worst (U-shape); needle tests overstate ability (RULER). Put key info at the edges, or retrieve.
