Phase 6 · Lesson 1

Self-attention, step by step: Q, K, V and √d

Every word looks at every other word, scores them with a dot product, and pulls in a blend of what they offer. That one move, repeated, is the engine of every large language model.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bahdanau attention (Lesson 5.4): a decoder RNN scores every encoder state, softmaxes the scores and takes a weighted average. Today we drop the RNN and keep only that idea.
  • Softmax turns a list of scores into positive weights that add up to 1 (Lesson 0.6, math 8.2).
  • A dot product q·k is big when two vectors point the same way (math 1.3). The math course already did one attention row by hand: math 8.4.
🎯 Hook

“The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired.” Swap tired for wide and “it” flips from the animal to the street. You did that without thinking. A translator can't skip it: in French, animal and street have different genders, so “it” becomes a different word.

Google's 2017 post introducing the Transformer used a sentence pair like this one, and showed the model's attention for “it” landing on the right noun in each case. The same year's paper, “Attention Is All You Need”, threw away recurrence entirely and still set a translation record (28.4 BLEU on English→German) after 3.5 days on 8 GPUs, “a small fraction of the training costs” of the previous best models.

Today GPT-style chatbots, open models like Llama, Qwen and Gemma, vision transformers and speech models like Whisper all run on this mechanism. How does a word “look at” other words, and why is there a mysterious √d in the formula? You'll steer “it” yourself in a few minutes.

Numbers first: from one decoder looking back, to everyone looking at everyone

In Lesson 5.4, only the decoder looked around: at each output step it scored the encoder's hidden states. The encoder itself was still an RNN that read one word at a time. For a 50-word sentence, information from word 1 had to survive 49 hand-offs to reach word 50.

Self-attention makes every token do the looking, at the same time. Each of the n tokens scores all n tokens (itself included), so one layer computes an n × n table of scores:

RNN layerSelf-attention layer
Steps that must run one after anothern (each word waits for the previous)1 (all rows at once: one big matrix multiply)
Hops between word 1 and word 50491
Work per layer (n tokens, width d)about n·d²about n²·d

These three rows are Table 1 of Vaswani et al. (2017). The last row is the catch: double the length and attention's work quadruples. Hold that thought until the end of the lesson.

💡 Big idea Self-attention is a soft lookup that every token runs over every token. Each token asks a question (its query), every token advertises what it has (its key), dot products decide the match, softmax turns matches into weights, and each token walks away with a weighted blend of everyone's values.
🧠 Analogy: a room where everyone asks and answers at once

Eleven people stand in a room, one per word. Each holds up a sign saying what they're looking for (query: “it” holds up “a noun I could refer to”) and wears a badge saying what they are (key: “animal” wears “living noun”). Everyone glances at every badge at once, listens most to the best matches, and writes down a blend of what those people say (value). No queue, no passing notes down a line: that's the difference from an RNN.

Q, K, V: three lenses on the same word

Each token starts as an embedding vector x (Lesson 5.1). Three learned weight matrices turn it into three different vectors:

  • query q = xWQ: what I'm looking for.
  • key k = xWK: what I offer (how I want to be found).
  • value v = xWV: what I hand over if you pick me.

Why three, not one? Because “what I'm looking for” and “what I am” are different things. The word “it” is a pronoun but is looking for a noun. If query and key were the same vector, every word would mostly match itself.

Then three steps: score (q·k for every pair), softmax each row into weights, mix the values with those weights. In the playground below the vectors have just 2 numbers so you can drag them; real heads use 64 or 128.

🤔 Why this? Self-attention instead of recurrence
  1. The problem before RNNs and LSTMs (Lessons 5.2–5.3) read one token at a time. A 1,000-token document needs 1,000 sequential steps, so a GPU (which wants thousands of things at once) sits mostly idle, and information between distant words passes through hundreds of hand-offs.
  2. What people tried LSTMs with attention bolted onto the decoder (Lesson 5.4), and convolutions over sequences (Lesson 5.5), which run in parallel but need a stack of O(log n) or more layers to connect distant words.
  3. Why this works Every token reads every token in one step, so the whole layer is a couple of matrix multiplies (GPUs love those), and any two words are one hop apart. The Transformer's base model trained in 12 hours on 8 GPUs and still beat previously published models on English→German.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The n × n score table: double the context, 4× the work and memory (playground at the end). And attention alone has no idea of word order: shuffle the words and each token gets the same blend (Lesson 6.3 fixes that).
  5. What refined it Several heads in parallel (6.2), positions (6.3), the full block with MLP, residuals and norms (6.4), and memory-saving tricks like the KV cache and FlashAttention (6.9).

The formula, now that you've felt it

In words: score every query against every key, divide by √d, softmax each row, use the weights to average the values. Stack the n queries as rows of a matrix Q (and keys as K, values as V), and the whole layer is one line:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT / √dk ) · V

Q KT is the n × n heatmap you just edited; dk is the length of each key (64 in the original paper). The math course derives this line slowly: math 8.4. What it skips is the next question: why that √d?

Why divide by √d? Because dot products grow with dimension

A dot product adds up d little products. If every entry of q and k is a random number with average 0 and spread 1, the paper notes that q·k has average 0 and variance d, so its typical size is √d. With d = 64 that's about ±8; with d = 1,024, about ±32.

✏️ Worked example: three scores at d = 64

Typical scores at d = 64 look like [16, 8, 0] (two spreads apart). Softmax them as they are, then divided by √64 = 8:

ScoresSoftmax weightsSlope p(1 − p) of the top weight
Unscaled[16, 8, 0][0.9997, 0.0003, 0.0000]0.0003
÷ √d[2, 1, 0][0.67, 0.24, 0.09]0.22

Unscaled, softmax has already decided: one key gets everything, and the slope that backprop needs is 660× smaller (0.22 ÷ 0.00034). The paper's own words: large dot products push softmax “into regions where it has extremely small gradients”. Dividing by √d brings the scores back to a spread of about 1, whatever d is.

What you should see: the typical score size tracks √d almost exactly (7.8 at d = 64, 31 at d = 1,024). Unscaled, the share of one-hot rows climbs from 8% at d = 16 to 35% at d = 64 (the per-head size of the original Transformer) and 80% at d = 1,024, while the gradient size sinks from 0.34 to 0.04. Scaled, nothing moves: 0% one-hot and a gradient size of 0.35 at every d. One division makes the layer behave the same at any width.

🤔 Why this? Scaling scores by 1/√dk
  1. The problem before Dot products grow with dimension (variance d). At d = 64 typical scores are ±8, softmax goes nearly one-hot, and its slope collapses (0.0003 instead of 0.22 in the worked example). Gradients stop reaching WQ and WK.
  2. What people tried Additive attention (Bahdanau's small MLP scorer, Lesson 5.4), which the paper notes beat unscaled dot products at large d, but is slower than one matrix multiply.
  3. Why this works Dividing by √d turns a spread of √d back into a spread of 1, at every width. It's free (one multiply) and keeps the fast matrix-multiply form. Think of it as a fixed softmax temperature chosen so the layer starts “undecided” instead of “already sure”.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It assumes entries of roughly unit size. During training, query and key vectors can still grow and softmax saturates anyway: training a 22-billion-parameter vision transformer, Dehghani et al. (2023) hit “extremely large values in attention logits” that made attention almost one-hot and destabilized training.
  5. What refined it Normalizing queries and keys before the dot product (“QK-norm”), introduced for that 22B model and used in Google's Gemma 3 (2025), keeps scores bounded; the normalization layers themselves are Lesson 2.3. Flash-style kernels (6.9) fold the scale into the same fused computation.

The causal mask: no peeking at the future

A GPT-style model is trained to predict the next token. If token 7 could read token 8, it would just copy the answer. So before the softmax, every score where the key comes after the query is set to −∞. Since e−∞ = 0, those words get exactly zero weight, and the heatmap becomes a lower triangle.

Go back to the first playground and press Causal mask. Then flip tired ↔ wide while steering “it”. Its row doesn't change at all: at position 8 it simply can't see the word that decides the answer. A GPT resolves this one token later: when it reaches “tired”, that row can look back at both nouns (click the “tired” row).

🤔 Why this? Causal (look-back-only) mask
  1. The problem before To train next-token prediction on a 1,000-token text you'd want 1,000 training examples from one pass. With full attention, the prediction at position 7 can read position 8: the loss drops to near zero and the model learns nothing useful.
  2. What people tried Running the model once per prefix (1,000 separate passes for 1,000 positions), which is exactly how slow RNN decoding felt.
  3. Why this works One mask (−∞ above the diagonal) lets every position train at once while each one only sees its past: a 1,000-token text gives 1,000 honest next-token predictions in one forward pass. At generation time it also means old tokens never need recomputing when a new one arrives.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each token only sees the left side, so early tokens have less context. For tasks where you have the whole text up front (classifying a review, embedding a document) that's a handicap; encoders like BERT drop the mask (Lesson 6.5).
  5. What refined it The “never recompute the past” property becomes the KV cache (Lesson 6.9). Encoder vs decoder vs both: Lesson 6.5.

The price: n² scores

Every token scores every token, so the table has n² entries per head, per layer. Stored in 16-bit numbers (2 bytes each), one 8,192 × 8,192 table is 134 MB. A model with 32 heads and 32 layers that kept all of them (training needs them for the backward pass) would need about 137 GB just for attention weights.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You rarely write attention by hand: F.scaled_dot_product_attention does score → scale → mask → softmax → mix in one fused call and picks a FlashAttention or memory-efficient kernel when it can. Writing it by hand once is still the best way to trust it:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
n, d = 5, 64                          # 5 tokens, 64 numbers per query/key
x = torch.randn(1, n, d)              # (batch, tokens, features)
Wq, Wk, Wv = (torch.randn(d, d) / d**0.5 for _ in range(3))
q, k, v = x @ Wq, x @ Wk, x @ Wv

# by hand: scores -> causal mask -> softmax -> mix values
scores = q @ k.transpose(-2, -1) / d**0.5            # (1, 5, 5)
mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(n, n, dtype=torch.bool), 1)
weights = scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf")).softmax(-1)
out_hand = weights @ v

# the fused PyTorch kernel
out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
print(torch.allclose(out, out_hand, atol=1e-6))
print(weights[0, 2].round(decimals=2))   # token 3 sees only tokens 1-3

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# True
# tensor([0.4500, 0.3100, 0.2400, 0.0000, 0.0000])

Gotcha: in the real thing q, k, v have shape (batch, heads, tokens, head_dim); the scale uses the head dimension, not the model width (Lesson 6.2). And pass is_causal=True rather than building your own mask when you can: it lets PyTorch pick the fastest kernel.

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through self-attention. Why the √d?” A strong answer:

  • Q = XWQ, K = XWK, V = XWV; scores QKᵀ (n × n), scale by 1/√dk, optional causal mask (−∞ above the diagonal), row-wise softmax, multiply by V.
  • √d: for unit-variance entries, q·k has variance dk; big scores saturate softmax and its gradients vanish. Scaling restores unit spread.
  • Costs: O(n²·d) compute and O(n²) memory per head; mentions FlashAttention / KV cache as the practical answers.
  • Knows attention is permutation-blind without positional information.
🏭 In the real world
The paper that dropped recurrence 📜 History · 2017 Vaswani et al.: Attention Is All You Need

How: encoder and decoder built only from self-attention (8 heads of size 64), feed-forward layers, residuals and normalization; the decoder uses the causal mask.

Why this method: parallel training. The big model hit 28.4 BLEU on English→German after 3.5 days on 8 P100 GPUs; the base model beat earlier published models after 12 hours.

Causal self-attention for text generation 🏢 OpenAI · 2019 Radford et al.: Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (GPT-2)

How: a stack of decoder-only blocks with masked self-attention over a 1,024-token context, trained to predict the next token.

Why this method: the causal mask turns every position of every document into a training example in one pass, which is what made scaling to billions of parameters practical.

Self-attention over image patches 🧪 Research · 2020 Dosovitskiy et al.: An Image is Worth 16x16 Words (ViT)

How: cut an image into 16 × 16 pixel patches, treat each patch as a token, and run a standard transformer encoder (full, unmasked attention).

Why this method: every patch can relate to every other patch from layer 1, instead of growing a receptive field slowly like a CNN (Phase 3).

Making n² fit in memory 🧪 Research · 2022 Dao et al.: FlashAttention

How: computes exact attention in tiles that fit in the GPU's fast on-chip memory, never writing the full n × n table out.

Why this method: the quadratic memory you just measured is the bottleneck; the paper reports a 3× speedup on GPT-2 at length 1K and longer usable contexts (Lesson 6.9).

The Attention paper has eight authors, and a footnote says “Equal contribution. Listing order is random.” The same footnote credits Jakob Uszkoreit with proposing to replace RNNs with self-attention. That one idea is now in nearly every model you use.

Check yourself

1. Query and key entries are random with mean 0 and variance 1, and each head has dk = 256. Roughly how big is a typical unscaled score q·k, and what is it after scaling?
A dot product sums 256 products, each with variance 1, so the sum has variance 256 and a typical size of √256 = 16. Dividing by √dk = 16 brings it back to about 1, which keeps softmax out of its flat, one-hot region.
2. You train a small GPT-style model and the training loss falls to almost 0 within minutes, but generated text is garbage. What's the most likely bug?
A near-zero loss that doesn't translate into good generation is the signature of a leak: with no causal mask, position i reads token i+1 and copies it. At generation time the future isn't there, so the model has learned nothing useful. Check that scores above the diagonal are set to −∞ (or pass is_causal=True).
3. An attention layer takes 4 ms on a 2,000-token input. Ignoring everything except the n² score computation, about how long on an 8,000-token input?
The score table has n² entries. Going from 2,000 to 8,000 tokens multiplies n by 4 and n² by 16, so 4 ms × 16 = 64 ms. Parallelism removes the sequential steps, not the total work. This is why long context is expensive (Lessons 6.9 and 6.11).
📝 TL;DR
  • Self-attention: every token makes a query (what I want), a key (what I offer) and a value (what I hand over); scores q·k → softmax → weighted sum of values.
  • One step connects any two tokens and runs fully in parallel, unlike an RNN's n sequential steps.
  • Divide scores by √dk: dot products have variance d, and big scores make softmax one-hot with vanishing gradients (35% of rows one-hot at d = 64 without it).
  • The causal mask (−∞ above the diagonal) lets decoders train all next-token predictions at once without peeking.
  • The price is n² time and memory per head and layer; attention also ignores word order until we add positions (6.3).
