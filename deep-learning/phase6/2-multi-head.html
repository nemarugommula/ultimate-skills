Phase 6 · Lesson 2

Multi-head attention

One attention can only blend one way at a time. Split it into several smaller heads and each one learns to look for something different, for the same price.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Self-attention: queries, keys and values; scores q·k / √d → softmax → weighted sum of values (Lesson 6.1).
  • A weight matrix of shape d × d has d² parameters, and multiplying by it costs about d² multiply-adds per token (math 2.2).
🎯 Hook

In 2019, Stanford researchers opened up BERT (a 12-layer model with 12 attention heads per layer) and looked at all 144 attention maps one by one. Nobody had told any head what to do. Yet head 8-11 linked determiners to their nouns (“the” → “cat”) with 94.3% accuracy, head 8-10 linked direct objects to their verbs with 86.8%, and a few early heads simply looked at the previous word more than half the time.

That same year two other groups found the opposite surprise: you can delete many heads from a trained model and it barely notices. In one translation model, removing 38 of 48 encoder heads cost only 0.15 BLEU.

So why have several heads at all, what do they cost, and why do some of them turn out to be spare? You'll build a “previous word” head and a “same kind of word” head yourself.

Numbers first: one head has to compromise

Take the token “mat” in “the cat sat on the mat”. It would like two very different pieces of information: which word came just before it (“the”, so it's a specific mat) and which other nouns are around (“cat”, the thing sitting on it).

One attention head produces one set of weights per token, and one weighted average. If it spreads 50% on “the” and 50% on “cat”, the output is a smudge of both, and the next layer can't tell which part came from where. Vaswani et al. put it in five words: with one head, “averaging inhibits this”.

💡 Big idea Multi-head attention runs h small attentions in parallel, each with its own WQ, WK, WV, on a slice of size d/h. Each head picks its own “who should I listen to?”. Their outputs are concatenated (glued side by side) and mixed by one output matrix WO. Same total width, same parameter count, h different ways of looking.
🧠 Analogy: a panel of specialist readers

Instead of one editor reading a sentence and writing one summary note per word, you hire eight junior editors, each with a narrower brief: one checks “what came just before?”, one checks “who's the subject?”, one tracks pronouns. Each writes a short note. You staple the eight notes together (concat) and a senior editor rewrites them into one note (WO). Eight short notes cost the same paper as one long one.

Build two specialist heads

Below, each head scores pairs of words with two learned ingredients: type match (are the query and key the same kind of word: noun, verb, determiner…?) and position match (is the key at a particular offset from the query?). For the position part, each token's vector already carries a little position code; how models get one is Lesson 6.3. Each head's sliders play the role of its WQ and WK.

🤔 Why this? Multiple heads instead of one big one
  1. The problem before One attention gives each token one softmax and one weighted average. A token that needs “the previous word” and “the subject” gets a blur of both, and the paper's ablation found a single head 0.9 BLEU worse than the best multi-head setting.
  2. What people tried One wide head (the blur problem), or stacking more layers so different layers handle different relations (slower and deeper).
  3. Why this works Splitting width d into h slices of d/h lets each slice have its own queries and keys, so each head can learn a different pattern: previous token, next token, determiner → noun, pronoun → antecedent. Outputs land in separate slots of the concatenated vector, so nothing gets averaged away. And it costs the same: h heads of size d/h use the same 4·d² weights as one head of size d.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Each head is narrower (64 numbers instead of 512), so a single head can express less. Attention maps take h·n² memory instead of n². Many heads end up redundant: Michel et al. (2019) could prune 20% (translation) and 40% (BERT) of heads with no noticeable loss. Too many heads also hurts: the paper saw quality drop with 16 and 32 heads.
  5. What refined it Sharing keys and values across heads to shrink memory at generation time: multi-query and grouped-query attention (GQA), Lesson 6.9. Head-level pruning and distillation for small, fast models.

The recipe: split, attend, concat, mix

In words: project every token to Q, K and V as usual, cut each into h slices, run attention separately on each slice, glue the h outputs back together, multiply by WO.

headi = Attention(X WQi, X WKi, X WVi)    MultiHead(X) = Concat(head1, …, headh) · WO

Each WQi is d × (d/h). In code nobody builds h little matrices: you build one d × d matrix and reshape its output into h slices. Same numbers, one fast multiply.

Are all those heads needed?

Head analysis gave two answers that sound contradictory and aren't:

  • Some heads are real specialists. Clark et al. (2019) found BERT heads that track syntax (determiner → noun 94.3%, direct object → verb 86.8%, passive auxiliary → verb 82.5%), coreference (65.1%), and four heads that put over half their attention on the previous token. Many others attend mostly to the separator token [SEP], which the authors read as a “no-op”: a place to park attention when the head's job doesn't apply.
  • Many heads are spare at test time. Michel, Levy and Neubig (2019) pruned 20% of a translation model's heads and 40% of BERT's with no noticeable loss, and for most layers one head alone was enough. Voita et al. (2019) removed 38 of 48 encoder heads for a 0.15 BLEU drop, and the heads that survived longest were the specialists.

Michel et al. also found hints that the extra heads matter during training: having many starting points makes it more likely that some become useful specialists. Like a team where a few people end up doing most of the work, but you didn't know in advance which few.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

nn.MultiheadAttention is the ready-made module; most modern code (nanoGPT, Hugging Face models) instead does one fused Q/K/V projection, a reshape, and F.scaled_dot_product_attention, which accepts a heads dimension directly:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
d, h, n = 768, 12, 10                        # GPT-2 small sizes
mha = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, h, batch_first=True)
print(sum(p.numel() for p in mha.parameters()))   # 4*d*d + 4*d

x = torch.randn(1, n, d)
out, w = mha(x, x, x, need_weights=True, average_attn_weights=False)
print(out.shape, w.shape)                    # per-head attention maps

# the same thing by hand: one big projection, then split into heads
qkv = nn.Linear(d, 3 * d)
q, k, v = qkv(x).split(d, dim=-1)
split = lambda t: t.view(1, n, h, d // h).transpose(1, 2)   # (batch, heads, n, 64)
o = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(split(q), split(k), split(v), is_causal=True)
o = o.transpose(1, 2).reshape(1, n, d)       # concat the 12 heads back to 768
print(o.shape)

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8):
# 2362368
# torch.Size([1, 10, 768]) torch.Size([1, 12, 10, 10])
# torch.Size([1, 10, 768])

Gotcha: after transpose, call reshape (or .contiguous().view), not view: the memory is no longer laid out in order. And the hand-written version above still needs a final nn.Linear(d, d) for WO. To look at real heads in a pretrained model, BertViz draws them (it downloads the model you pick).

🎤 Interview angle

“Why multi-head attention, and what does it cost?” A strong answer:

  • One head produces one softmax per token, so different relations get averaged together; h heads give h independent patterns in separate subspaces.
  • Same parameters (4d² + biases) and same score FLOPs as one full-width head, because each head has width d/h. Attention-map memory grows to h·n².
  • Concat + WO lets the heads' outputs be mixed back into the residual width.
  • Evidence: interpretable heads in BERT (Clark et al. 2019) and that many heads can be pruned (Michel et al., Voita et al. 2019); MQA/GQA as the memory fix.
🏭 In the real world
8 heads of 64 in the original Transformer 📜 History · 2017 Vaswani et al.: Attention Is All You Need

How: d = 512 split into h = 8 heads with dk = dv = 64; Table 3 varies h from 1 to 32.

Why this method: “the total computational cost is similar to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality”, and one head was 0.9 BLEU worse.

Reading BERT's 144 heads 🧪 Research · 2019 Clark et al.: What Does BERT Look At?

How: measured each head's attention against parsed sentences and coreference labels.

Why this method: attention weights are directly readable, so heads can be checked one by one: some match syntax relations with 76–94% accuracy, none was trained to.

Pruning most heads 🧪 Research · 2019 Voita et al.: Specialized Heads Do the Heavy Lifting, the Rest Can Be Pruned

How: learned gates switch heads off during fine-tuning, with a penalty for keeping heads on.

Why this method: 38 of 48 encoder heads removed for a 0.15 BLEU drop; positional, syntactic and rare-word heads were the last to go.

Heads that team up across layers 🏢 Anthropic · 2022 Olsson et al.: In-context Learning and Induction Heads

How: a “previous token head” in one layer copies each token's predecessor into it; an “induction head” in a later layer uses that to find where the current token appeared before and predict what followed it.

Why this method: this two-head circuit completes patterns like [A][B] … [A] → [B], a key part of how models learn from examples in the prompt.

🤯 Fun fact · you built half of a famous circuit

The “previous word” head you made in the playground is exactly the first half of an induction head circuit. Anthropic's researchers note that induction heads don't appear in one-layer models, because they need two heads in different layers working together: one shifts information forward by one token, the next one searches with it.

Check yourself

1. A layer has d = 1,024 and h = 16 heads. How wide is each head, and how many weights do WQ, WK, WV and WO have together (ignore biases)?
Each head gets d/h = 1,024 / 16 = 64 dimensions. The 16 heads together still project from 1,024 to 1,024 for Q, K and V, plus the 1,024 × 1,024 output matrix: 4 × 1,048,576 = 4,194,304. Splitting into heads reshapes the work; it doesn't multiply it.
2. You need a smaller, faster version of a trained model for a phone. Someone suggests deleting attention heads. Based on the 2019 pruning studies, what should you expect?
Michel et al. pruned 20% (translation) and 40% (BERT) of heads with no noticeable loss, and Voita et al. kept quality while removing 38 of 48 encoder heads using fine-tuned gates. Both saw performance drop sharply past a point, and specialist heads were the ones that mattered. Measure importance, prune gradually, and check quality.
3. You double the number of heads in a layer from 16 to 32 at the same d. Training memory goes up noticeably even though the parameter count didn't change. Why?
Weights stay at 4d² and score FLOPs stay at about n²·d, but every head has its own n × n table of weights to keep for the backward pass. Doubling h doubles that h·n² memory (unless a fused kernel like FlashAttention avoids storing it, Lesson 6.9). Each head actually gets narrower, so √(d/h) gets smaller.
📝 TL;DR
  • One head = one softmax per token, so different relations blur together. Multi-head attention runs h attentions on slices of size d/h.
  • Split → attend per head → concat → WO. Same 4d² parameters and ≈ n²·d score work as one full-width head; attention maps cost h·n².
  • Heads specialize without being told: previous token, determiner → noun (94.3% in BERT), coreference.
  • Many heads are redundant at test time (20–40% prunable; 38 of 48 in one study) but help during training.
  • Typical: 64 or 128 numbers per head; GPT-2 small 12 × 64, GPT-3 96 × 128. Sharing K/V across heads (GQA) comes in 6.9.
