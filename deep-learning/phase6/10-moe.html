Phase 6 · Lesson 10

Mixture of experts

What if a model had a trillion parameters but each word only woke up 3% of them? That's a mixture of experts: many specialist MLPs and a tiny router that picks a few for every token. It's how most of the largest open models are built today.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Every transformer block is attention followed by an MLP (multi-layer perceptron, often SwiGLU), and the MLP holds about two-thirds of the parameters (Lessons 6.4, 0.5).
  • Per-token compute ≈ 2 × the parameters actually used; training compute C ≈ 6·N·D (6.8).
  • Softmax turns scores into probabilities that sum to 1 (Lesson 0.6). Decoding is memory-bound: weights must be read from GPU memory (6.9).
🎯 Hook

In December 2023 Mistral AI released Mixtral 8x7B. It has 46.7 billion parameters, but each token uses only 12.9 billion of them, so it runs at roughly the speed and cost of a 13B model. Mistral reported it beat Llama 2 70B on most of their benchmarks.

By 2026 the idea had taken over open models: DeepSeek-V3 has 671B parameters and uses 37B per token; DeepSeek's V4-Pro preview (April 2026) has 1.6 trillion and uses 49B.

How can a model skip 97% of its weights on every token and still be smarter than a model that uses all of its own? And what goes wrong when every token wants the same expert? You'll build the router and break it.

Numbers first: total vs active parameters

A dense transformer uses every weight for every token. A mixture of experts (MoE) layer replaces the one MLP in each block with E separate MLPs, the experts, plus a router: a tiny linear layer that scores the experts for each token and sends the token to the top k. Attention, embeddings and norms stay shared.

✏️ Worked example: counting Mixtral's parameters

Exactly the published numbers. Per-token compute ≈ 2 × 12.9B ≈ 26 GFLOPs, like a 13B dense model. But to serve it you must hold all 46.7B in memory: 93 GB in bf16.

💡 Big idea Decouple knowledge from compute. Parameters store what the model knows; FLOPs per token are what it costs to think. A dense model ties them together. An MoE grows the parameters (more experts) while keeping the FLOPs per token fixed (still k experts per token), so you get a bigger brain at the same price per word, paid for in memory and engineering.
🧠 Analogy: a hospital, not a GP

A dense model is one doctor who must know everything and sees every patient. An MoE is a hospital: a receptionist (the router) glances at each patient and sends them to two specialists. The hospital knows far more than one doctor, and each patient still sees only two. The catch: if the receptionist sends everyone to cardiology, cardiology has a queue out the door and the other specialists sit idle.

🤔 Why this? Mixture of experts
  1. The problem before Scaling laws (6.8) say more parameters means lower loss, but in a dense model every extra parameter costs FLOPs on every token: 10× the params, 10× the training and serving compute.
  2. What people tried Just paying for bigger dense models; “conditional computation” ideas from the 1990s and 2010s that were hard to make fast on GPUs.
  3. Why this works Shazeer et al. (2017) made sparsely gated MoE layers practical with up to thousands of experts and up to 137B parameters between LSTM layers. Switch Transformer (2021) showed up to 7× faster pretraining than a dense T5 at the same FLOPs per token. Mixtral: 3.6× the total params of its active compute, beating a 70B dense model.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Memory: all experts must be loaded (Mixtral: 46.7B in memory to compute like 13B). Communication: experts are spread across GPUs, so tokens are shuffled between them every layer. Instability and imbalance: routers can collapse onto a few experts (playground below); Switch needed tricks such as computing the router in float32.
  5. What refined it Top-k routing (next), balancing losses, fine-grained and shared experts (DeepSeekMoE), auxiliary-loss-free balancing (DeepSeek-V3), and inference engines with expert parallelism.

The router: top-k routing

For each token vector x, the router computes one score per expert (s = x · Wr, a d × E matrix: for Mixtral just 4,096 × 8 numbers per layer), turns them into probabilities with softmax, and keeps the top k. The layer's output is the gate-weighted sum of those k experts' outputs:

y = Σi ∈ top-k gi(x) · Experti(x),   g = softmax(x · Wr) renormalised over the k chosen

In words: ask the router who's best for this token, send it to the best k, and blend their answers by how confident the router was. Because the gate gi multiplies the output, the gradient trains the router too: if an expert's answer helped, the router learns to send it similar tokens.

🤔 Why this? Top-k routing
  1. The problem before A “soft” mixture (weighted sum of all experts) is smooth and easy to train, but it runs every expert on every token: no savings at all.
  2. What people tried Shazeer's noisy top-k gating (add tunable noise to the scores, keep the top k) with k ≥ 2, so the router can compare experts and keep exploring.
  3. Why this works Only k experts run, so compute is k/E of the dense-all-experts cost (Mixtral: 2 of 8). Switch went to k = 1: the simplest routing and least communication, and it still trained well. Mixtral uses k = 2; DeepSeek-V3 picks 8 of 256 small experts.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Picking the top k is not differentiable, so experts the router never picks get no gradient and never improve: a rich-get-richer loop that can collapse routing. Bigger k = more compute and communication.
  5. What refined it Load-balancing losses and capacity limits (next), expert-choice routing (experts pick tokens), and fine-grained experts with large k.

Load balancing: keep every expert busy

Experts live on different GPUs and each processes its tokens in a fixed-size batch. Switch Transformer gives each expert a capacity:

capacity = capacity factor × (tokens in the batch × k ÷ number of experts)

If an expert gets more tokens than its capacity, the extra ones are dropped: they skip that expert and pass straight through on the residual connection, untransformed. So imbalance wastes both compute (idle experts) and quality (dropped tokens). The classic fix is an auxiliary balance loss added to the language-model loss:

Laux = α · E · Σi fi · Pi   (fi = share of tokens sent to expert i, Pi = average router probability for i)

In words: penalise experts that get both many tokens and high probability. It equals α exactly when routing is perfectly even (every fi = Pi = 1/E) and grows as routing piles up. Switch used α = 0.01.

🤔 Why this? Load balancing (aux loss, capacity, bias nudging)
  1. The problem before Routers collapse: favoured experts get more tokens, more gradient, get better and get even more tokens. In the playground, 2 of 8 experts end up with everything and 132 of 192 assignments are dropped.
  2. What people tried Noisy gating to force exploration (Shazeer 2017); importance and load losses; hard capacity limits that drop overflow tokens (GShard, Switch).
  3. Why this works The aux loss E·Σ fiPi is minimal (= 1) when the load is even, so its gradient lowers the router probability of overloaded experts. Capacity bounds the per-expert batch so GPUs stay in lockstep. DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free method adds a per-expert bias to the scores only for choosing the top k, and after each step nudges it down by γ if the expert was overloaded and up if underloaded. The gate weights still come from the original scores, so balancing doesn't distort the language-model gradient.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Forcing balance overrides some of the router's preferences (first-choice rate falls). Too strong an α hurts the main loss; too weak and collapse wins. Dropped tokens silently skip computation. Capacity factors above 1 waste compute on padding.
  5. What refined it Shared experts and fine-grained experts (below); DeepSeek-V3 reports it keeps a good balance through all of training and drops no tokens, in training or inference.

Fine-grained and shared experts (DeepSeekMoE)

DeepSeekMoE (2024) made two changes. Fine-grained experts: cut each expert into m smaller ones and route to m times as many. Compute is the same, but the number of possible expert combinations explodes: with 16 experts and top-2 there are 120 combinations; split each into 4 (64 experts, top-8) and there are about 4.4 billion. Shared experts: a few experts that every token always uses, to hold common knowledge, so the routed experts can specialise. DeepSeek-V3 has 1 shared + 256 routed experts per MoE layer and picks 8 routed experts per token.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

A complete top-k MoE layer with a Switch-style balance loss fits on one screen. Real implementations replace the Python loop over experts with grouped matrix multiplies and spread experts across GPUs (expert parallelism), but the logic is this:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class MoE(nn.Module):
    """Top-k mixture of experts replacing a transformer's MLP. x: (tokens, d)."""
    def __init__(self, d=64, d_ff=128, n_experts=8, k=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.k, self.E = k, n_experts
        self.router = nn.Linear(d, n_experts, bias=False)
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList(nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, d_ff), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d_ff, d)) for _ in range(n_experts))

    def forward(self, x):
        probs = self.router(x).softmax(-1)                      # (tokens, E)
        topw, topi = probs.topk(self.k, dim=-1)                 # each token's k experts
        topw = topw / topw.sum(-1, keepdim=True)                # renormalise the k gate weights
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for e in range(self.E):
            tok, slot = (topi == e).nonzero(as_tuple=True)      # which tokens chose expert e
            if tok.numel():
                out.index_add_(0, tok, topw[tok, slot, None] * self.experts[e](x[tok]))
        # Switch-style balance loss: E * sum_i (fraction of tokens sent to i) * (mean router prob of i)
        frac = F.one_hot(topi, self.E).float().sum(1).mean(0) / self.k
        aux = self.E * (frac * probs.mean(0)).sum()
        return out, aux, frac

torch.manual_seed(0)
moe = MoE()
x = torch.randn(512, 64)
y, aux, frac = moe(x)
(y.pow(2).mean() + 0.01 * aux).backward()               # task loss + alpha * balance loss
total = sum(p.numel() for p in moe.parameters())
active = sum(p.numel() for p in moe.router.parameters()) + moe.k * sum(p.numel() for p in moe.experts[0].parameters())
print("out", tuple(y.shape), f"aux {aux.item():.3f} (1.0 = perfectly balanced)")
print("load per expert:", [round(f, 3) for f in frac.tolist()])
print(f"params total {total:,}  active per token {active:,}")

At random init the router is nearly uniform, so the balance term starts near 1.0; collapse happens during training. In practice you load MoE checkpoints with Hugging Face AutoModelForCausalLM or serve them with vLLM / SGLang, which handle expert parallelism.

🎤 Interview angle

“What is a mixture-of-experts model, and why isn't everyone using it for everything?” A strong answer:

  • Router + E expert MLPs per layer, top-k per token; total vs active parameters (Mixtral 46.7B / 12.9B) and FLOPs ≈ 2 × active.
  • Why: more capacity per FLOP (Switch: up to 7× faster pretraining at equal FLOPs).
  • Load balancing: collapse, capacity factor and dropped tokens, the aux loss E·Σ f·P, and DeepSeek-V3's bias-based alternative.
  • Costs: memory scales with total params, expert-parallel communication, instability, and less benefit at small batch on a single GPU (you still read many experts' weights).
🏭 In the real world
The sparsely gated MoE layer 📜 History · 2017 Shazeer et al.: Outrageously Large Neural Networks

How: noisy top-k gating over up to thousands of experts, between LSTM layers, up to 137B parameters.

Why this method: “greater than 1000x improvements in model capacity with only minor losses in computational efficiency”.

Switch Transformer 🏢 Google · 2021 Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer: Switch Transformers

How: top-1 routing, expert capacity with dropped tokens passed through the residual, balance loss with α = 0.01, float32 router for stability.

Why this method: simplest routing with the least communication; up to 7× pretraining speedups over dense T5 at the same FLOPs.

Mixtral 8x7B 🏢 Mistral AI · 2023 Jiang et al.: Mixtral of Experts

How: Mistral 7B's architecture with each MLP replaced by 8 experts, top-2 per token per layer.

Why this method: 13B-class speed with 47B-class knowledge; open weights made MoE mainstream.

DeepSeekMoE → DeepSeek-V3 🏢 DeepSeek · 2024 DeepSeekMoE, DeepSeek-V3

How: fine-grained + shared experts, auxiliary-loss-free bias balancing, 671B total / 37B active, trained on 14.8T tokens.

Why this method: specialisation without balance-loss side effects; the full training took 2.788M H800 GPU hours.

🤯 Fun fact · experts aren't topic specialists

It's tempting to imagine a “biology expert” and a “code expert”. Mixtral's paper checked which experts tokens were sent to across topics (from arXiv papers to biology to philosophy) and found, in their words, no “obvious patterns in the assignment of experts based on the topic”. What they did see was syntax: indentation tokens in code always went to the same experts, and words like “self” in Python were often routed together. The experts specialise, just not the way a human org chart would.

Check yourself

1. An MoE model has 60 MoE layers, each with 64 experts of 50M parameters, top-4 routing, plus 6B of shared (attention, embedding) parameters. How many parameters are active per token?
Active experts: 4 × 50M × 60 layers = 12B, plus the 6B every token uses = 18B. (198B is the total: 64 × 50M × 60 + 6B. 12B forgets the shared part.) Per-token compute ≈ 2 × 18B = 36 GFLOPs, but memory must hold all 198B.
2. During MoE pretraining, the loss stalls and you see that 3 of 64 experts receive 70% of tokens while 40 experts receive almost none. What's happening and what do you change?
That's the rich-get-richer loop you saw in the playground. Overloaded experts also overflow their capacity, so many tokens are dropped and skip the layer. A balance loss (Switch's E·Σ f·P with α ≈ 0.01) or DeepSeek-V3's bias nudging spreads the load; a raised router LR would usually make collapse faster.
3. You must serve a model on a single 80 GB GPU at batch size 1. A 47B-total / 13B-active MoE and a 13B dense model score similarly on your task. Which is the better fit?
An MoE's per-token FLOPs match its active parameters, but every expert must sit in memory, and decoding is memory-bound (6.9). At ~2 bytes per parameter, 47B doesn't fit on 80 GB without quantization. MoEs shine when you have many GPUs and big batches; on one small device a dense model of the same active size is simpler and fits.
📝 TL;DR
  • MoE: replace each MLP with E experts and a router that sends each token to the top k. Total params ≫ active params (Mixtral 46.7B / 12.9B; DeepSeek-V3 671B / 37B).
  • Why: more capacity at the same FLOPs per token (Switch: up to 7× faster pretraining). Cost: memory for all experts, communication, instability.
  • Routing collapses without balancing. Capacity = factor × tokens·k/E; overflow tokens are dropped. Aux loss α·E·Σ f·P (Switch α = 0.01) or DeepSeek-V3's bias nudging (no aux loss, no dropped tokens).
  • Fine-grained experts (many small, larger k) and shared experts (always on) improve specialisation at the same compute.
