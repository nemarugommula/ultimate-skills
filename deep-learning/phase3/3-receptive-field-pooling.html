Phase 3 · Lesson 3

Receptive fields and pooling

One 3 × 3 filter sees nine pixels. Yet a network built only from 3 × 3 filters can recognise a dog that fills the whole photo. The trick is stacking, shrinking and summarising, and you can watch the view grow layer by layer.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A conv slides a k × k kernel; stride s jumps s pixels; “same” padding keeps the size; output = ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1 (Lesson 3.2).
  • A conv layer's parameters are k·k·C_in·C_out + C_out (Lesson 3.2).
🎯 Hook

VGG-16, a famous 2014 network, uses nothing but 3 × 3 convolutions: thirteen of them. Each one only ever looks at a 3 × 3 patch of the layer below. And yet a single unit in its last conv layer depends on a 196 × 196 pixel region of the input photo, nearly the whole 224 × 224 image.

Where does that extra view come from? And here's the twist: Luo et al. (2016) showed that most of a unit's attention sits in a small blob in the middle of that region, falling off like a bell curve. How far does a neuron really see, and how do you make it see further without a billion parameters? You'll build networks layer by layer and click on their neurons to find out.

Numbers first: stack 3 × 3 layers and count

The receptive field (RF) of a unit is the patch of the input image that can affect it. One 3 × 3 conv: 3 × 3. Put a second 3 × 3 on top: each of its 9 inputs saw a 3 × 3 patch, and neighbouring patches overlap, so together they cover 5 × 5. Each extra stride-1 layer adds 2:

Receptive field (pixels per side)35791121

Linear growth is slow: covering a 224-pixel image would take 112 layers. Stride and pooling change the game. After a stride-2 step, neighbouring units sit 2 input pixels apart, so the next 3 × 3 layer adds 2 × 2 = 4 pixels instead of 2. After two stride-2 steps, each layer adds 8. The growth becomes multiplicative.

In words: each layer adds (k − 1) steps, and one step is as wide as all the strides so far multiplied together. As a formula, with j (the “jump”) = product of the strides so far:

RFnew = RFold + (k − 1) · jold     jnew = jold · s

What you should see: three plain 3 × 3 layers see only 7 × 7. Four layers can cover all 32 × 32, but only if you stride early: three “3 × 3 stride 2” then a 5 × 5 reach a theoretical RF of 47, because the jump is already 8 by the last layer. Put the strides at the end instead and the same four layers see far less. One dilated layer on its own sees a checkerboard with holes (3 × 3 taps, 2 apart). In path-count view, six 3 × 3 layers give a bell shape: the center pixel has 141 routes to the cell per direction, the edge pixel just 1, and the middle half of the RF (by width) holds 85% of all paths. That's why the effective RF is smaller than the theoretical one.

🤔 Why this? Stacking small (3 × 3) kernels
  1. The problem before Early CNNs used big kernels to see a lot at once: AlexNet's first layer is 11 × 11 at stride 4. Big kernels are expensive: a 7 × 7 conv from C to C channels has 49C² weights.
  2. What people tried Big kernels with big strides early (cheap but coarse); fewer channels.
  3. Why this works The VGG paper's argument: two stacked 3 × 3 layers have the same 5 × 5 RF as one 5 × 5, and three have a 7 × 7 RF, but cost 3 · 9C² = 27C² instead of 49C² (the 7 × 7 needs 81% more). And you get three ReLUs instead of one, so the stack can compute more complex functions. At C = 64: two 3 × 3 layers = 73,856 parameters vs 102,464 for one 5 × 5.
  4. What it costs, when it fails More layers means more sequential steps (latency) and more activations to store for backprop (memory). Depth also makes training harder: VGG-19 was near the limit of what plain stacks could train before residuals.
  5. What refined it Residual connections made very deep 3 × 3 stacks trainable (Lessons 2.2 and 3.4). ConvNeXt (2022) went back to bigger 7 × 7 kernels, made cheap by depthwise convolution (3.4).
💡 Big idea Depth buys view. Each layer sees a small window of the layer below, but those windows compound: stacking adds to the receptive field, striding and pooling multiply it. That's how a CNN goes from edges (a few pixels) to parts (dozens) to whole objects (hundreds), the same hierarchy Hubel and Wiesel found in the visual cortex.

Pooling: summarise, then shrink

Pooling slides a window (usually 2 × 2, stride 2) over each channel separately and replaces it with one number: the maximum (max pooling) or the mean (average pooling). No weights, nothing to learn. The map shrinks to a quarter of the positions, and because pooling acts per channel, the channel count stays the same.

Max pooling only reports “the strongest response somewhere in this window”, so a move that stays inside the window is invisible. Shift the stripes right by one: all 64 input pixels change, yet the max-pooled map doesn't change at all. That's the small-shift tolerance pooling buys. With 4 × 4 windows the map is tiny (2 × 2) and even more forgiving, but the picture is gone.

🤔 Why this? Pooling (max and average)
  1. The problem before Conv layers are equivariant: shift the input and the feature map shifts. A classifier wants the opposite: the same answer if the cat moves a little. And full-resolution maps keep every later layer expensive.
  2. What people tried Subsampling (keep every other pixel), as in the Neocognitron's position-tolerant layers and LeNet's subsampling layers.
  3. Why this works A 2 × 2 max pool keeps the strongest response in each window: small shifts inside the window don't change it (4 of 16 cells changed for the cross, vs 12 for average), it quarters the positions for the next layer, and it doubles the jump, so receptive fields grow faster. Zero parameters. AlexNet used overlapping 3 × 3 windows at stride 2, which its authors report cut top-5 error by 0.3% compared with 2 × 2.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It throws away exact positions, which tasks like segmentation and keypoints need (Lesson 4.3). The invariance is partial: shifts that cross a window boundary still change the output. Max pooling's gradient goes only to the winning input in each window.
  5. What refined it Strided convolutions that learn their downsampling (the “all convolutional net”, 2014; ResNet), anti-aliased pooling (blur, then subsample) for steadier shift behaviour, and global average pooling at the very end (below).
🧠 Analogy: the neighbourhood report

Each block of a city sends one line upward. Max pooling: “the loudest thing on our block was a siren”. Average pooling: “our block was moderately noisy overall”. The city council doesn't need to know which house the siren was in to know there's an emergency in that area, and its reading pile is four times smaller.

Dilation: see wider without more weights

A dilated (or “atrous”, French for “with holes”) convolution spreads its kernel taps apart. A 3 × 3 kernel with dilation 2 still has 9 weights, but they sample every other pixel across a 5 × 5 area. Dilation d turns a k × k kernel into an effective size of d·(k − 1) + 1. Stack dilations 1, 2, 4, 8 and the RF grows exponentially: 3, 7, 15, 31, with no pooling and no loss of resolution (four plain 3 × 3 layers reach only 9).

🤔 Why this? Dilated (atrous) convolution
  1. The problem before Pooling and striding grow the RF but shrink the map. Segmentation needs a label for every pixel (Lesson 4.3) and audio models need context over thousands of samples: shrinking throws away the very resolution you need.
  2. What people tried Shrink, then upsample back (blurry details); very large kernels (expensive).
  3. Why this works Spacing out the taps gives a wide view for the same 9 weights and keeps full resolution. Yu and Koltun (2015) built dense prediction networks on dilated convolutions that “support exponential expansion of the receptive field without loss of resolution”. WaveNet (2016) stacked dilations 1, 2, 4, …, 512 for a receptive field of 1,024 audio samples per block.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Holes: one dilated layer sees a checkerboard, and stacking the same dilation keeps missing the same pixels (“gridding” artifacts). Full-resolution maps stay expensive in memory and compute.
  5. What refined it Mixing dilation rates (1, 2, 4, …) so the holes fill in; DeepLab's atrous spatial pyramid pooling (parallel branches at several rates) for segmentation (Lesson 4.3).

The effective receptive field is smaller than you think

The theoretical RF says which pixels can matter. But there's only one path from a corner pixel to the output unit, and dozens or hundreds through the middle. Add up the paths and the influence piles up in the center like a bell curve (you saw the counts 1, 6, 21, 50, 90, 126, 141, … in path-count view). Luo, Li, Urtasun and Zemel (2016) measured this in real networks: the effective receptive field has a roughly Gaussian shape, occupies only a fraction of the theoretical one, and, relative to it, shrinks as layers are added (its absolute size grows like the square root of the depth). It does grow during training as the network learns to use more context.

Practical upshot: if your objects are 200 pixels wide, a network whose theoretical RF is exactly 200 is probably not enough; aim well beyond, or add pooling or global context.

Global average pooling: the end of the network

After the last conv, you have a small map, say 7 × 7 × 512. The old way to classify: flatten to 25,088 numbers and attach dense layers. VGG-16's first dense layer alone is 25,088 × 4,096 ≈ 102.8 million weights, and its three dense layers hold 89% of its 138 million parameters. Global average pooling (GAP) instead averages each channel's whole map into one number: 7 × 7 × 512 → 512. Then one small dense layer maps 512 features to the classes.

With ResNet-50's 7 × 7 × 2048 final map, a flatten head would be about 100 million parameters, four times the whole network; ResNet-50's real GAP head is 2,049,000. Feed a bigger image and the map grows to 10 × 10: the flatten head expects exactly 25,088 numbers and breaks, while GAP averages whatever size it gets.

🤔 Why this? Global average pooling
  1. The problem before Flatten + dense heads are huge (VGG-16: 123.6M of its 138.4M parameters), overfit easily, needed heavy dropout, and lock the network to one input size.
  2. What people tried Dropout in the dense layers (AlexNet, VGG); smaller hidden layers.
  3. Why this works Network in Network (Lin, Chen & Yan, 2013) replaced the dense layers with one average per channel. Their paper lists the wins: there is “no parameter to optimize” in the pooling, it is less prone to overfitting, and it is more robust to spatial translations, since it ignores where a feature fired. Each channel becomes a “how much of feature c is in this image” score. GoogLeNet and ResNet both end this way.
  4. What it costs, when it fails It discards all spatial layout at the end: fine for “what is it”, useless for “where is it” (detection heads keep the map, Lesson 4.1). Averaging can dilute a small object's signal in a big image.
  5. What refined it It's now the default classifier head for CNNs (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt). It also makes class activation maps possible, the ancestor of Grad-CAM (Lesson 3.6). ViTs use a class token or average their patch tokens the same way (4.5).

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

nn.MaxPool2d(2), nn.AvgPool2d(2), nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1) (global average pooling for any input size), and nn.Conv2d(..., dilation=2, padding=2). You can measure a real network's receptive field with one backward pass: the input pixels with a non-zero gradient are exactly the ones the output can see.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
layers = []
for _ in range(10):                                  # ten 3×3 convs: theoretical receptive field 21×21
    layers += [nn.Conv2d(8, 8, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU()]
net = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(1, 8, 1), *layers)

x = torch.randn(16, 1, 64, 64, requires_grad=True)
net(x)[:, :, 32, 32].sum().backward()                # one output position, all channels
g = x.grad.abs().sum(dim=(0, 1))                     # how much each input pixel matters
seen = (g > 0).nonzero()
print("theoretical RF:", (seen[:, 0].max() - seen[:, 0].min() + 1).item(), "pixels wide")
core = g[32 - 4:32 + 5, 32 - 4:32 + 5].sum() / g.sum()
print(f"share of the gradient in the central 9x9: {core:.0%}  (9x9 is {81 / 441:.0%} of the RF's area)")

pool = nn.MaxPool2d(2)                               # 2×2 windows, stride 2
gap = nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1)                        # global average pooling: any H×W → 1×1
feat = torch.randn(1, 512, 7, 7)
print(pool(feat).shape, gap(feat).shape, gap(torch.randn(1, 512, 12, 9)).shape)

That 68% is the effective receptive field, measured: the central 18% of the area carries over two thirds of the influence. Gotcha: MaxPool2d(2) on an odd size floors (7 → 3), silently dropping the last row and column.

🎤 Interview angle

“Why does VGG use 3 × 3 kernels everywhere?” and “What's a receptive field?” A strong answer:

  • Defines the RF as the input region that can affect a unit, and computes it: RF += (k − 1) × product of earlier strides.
  • Gives the VGG argument with numbers: three 3 × 3 = 7 × 7 RF for 27C² vs 49C² weights, plus extra non-linearities.
  • Knows the effective RF is Gaussian-ish and smaller than the theoretical one (Luo et al.), so you need margin.
  • Contrasts pooling (cheap, partial shift tolerance, loses position), strided conv (learned downsampling), dilation (wide view at full resolution, gridding) and GAP (zero-parameter head, any input size).
🏭 In the real world
Deep stacks of 3 × 3 🧪 Research · 2014 Simonyan & Zisserman: Very Deep Convolutional Networks (VGG)

How: 16–19 layers, all 3 × 3 convs at stride 1, with 2 × 2 max pooling five times.

Why this method: same RF as big kernels with fewer parameters and more ReLUs; the team placed 1st in localisation and 2nd in classification at ILSVRC 2014.

Global average pooling heads 🧪 Research · 2013 Lin, Chen & Yan: Network In Network

How: one feature map per class, averaged into the class score; no dense layers at all.

Why this method: no parameters, less overfitting, robust to where features appear. ResNet's paper says its network “ends with a global average pooling layer and a 1000-way fully-connected layer”.

Dilations for raw audio 🧪 Research · 2016 van den Oord et al.: WaveNet

How: stacks of dilated causal convolutions with dilations 1, 2, 4, …, 512, repeated.

Why this method: speech at 16,000 samples per second needs context over thousands of samples; one dilation block reaches 1,024 with only 10 layers and no downsampling.

Atrous convolution for segmentation 🧪 Research · 2016 Chen et al.: DeepLab

How: replaces the last downsampling steps of a classifier with dilated (atrous) convolutions and runs several rates in parallel.

Why this method: per-pixel labels need a dense output map and wide context at the same time (Lesson 4.3).

🤯 Fun fact · ten layers, a thousand samples

WaveNet's dilation trick is the receptive-field formula on steroids. With kernel size 2, each layer adds (2 − 1) × dilation: 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 512 = 1,023, plus the starting sample = 1,024. Ten layers with plain kernels of size 2 would see 11 samples. The same idea, in 2D, is in the grower above: dilations 1, 2, 4, 8 of a 3 × 3 kernel see 31 × 31.

🌉 How it connects

The convolution mechanics are 3.2 (and math 8.3b); the path-count picture is the chain rule summing over paths (math 3.5). Next, 3.4 shows how real networks place their strides, pools and GAP. Detection and segmentation (4.1, 4.3) need RFs large enough for objects and maps fine enough for boxes and masks; feature pyramids and dilations are their answers. Vision Transformers (4.5) get a global receptive field from the very first layer by attention.

Check yourself

1. A network: 3 × 3 conv (stride 1), 2 × 2 max pool (stride 2), 3 × 3 conv (stride 1), 3 × 3 conv (stride 1). What's the theoretical receptive field of the last layer?
Start at 1. Conv: 1 + 2·1 = 3. Pool: 3 + 1·1 = 4, jump becomes 2. Conv: 4 + 2·2 = 8. Conv: 8 + 2·2 = 12. Without the pool (jump 1) three convs would give only 7.
2. A defect detector misses large scratches that span about 150 pixels, but finds small ones. Its last layer's theoretical receptive field is 140 pixels. What's the best first fix?
The units can't see a whole large scratch, and the effective RF (Gaussian-ish, Luo et al.) is only a fraction of the 140 theoretical pixels. Add stride/pooling earlier, dilated layers or global context. The pooling type and learning rate don't change what the units can see.
3. Your final conv map is 7 × 7 × 1024 and you need 100 classes. How many parameters does a global-average-pooling head (GAP then one dense layer) have?
GAP turns 7 × 7 × 1024 into 1,024 numbers with no parameters. Dense: 1,024 × 100 weights + 100 biases = 102,500. Flattening instead: 50,176 × 100 + 100 = 5,017,700, 49× more, and tied to the 7 × 7 size.
📝 TL;DR
  • Receptive field = the input region a unit can see. RF += (k − 1) × (product of earlier strides): stacking adds, striding and pooling multiply.
  • Two 3 × 3 = one 5 × 5 view, three = 7 × 7, for 27C² instead of 49C² and with more ReLUs (the VGG argument).
  • Max pooling keeps the strongest response per window: zero parameters, partial shift tolerance, quarter-size maps. Average pooling smooths.
  • Dilation widens the view at full resolution (1, 2, 4, 8 → 31 × 31) but leaves holes. The effective RF is a Gaussian-ish blob, much smaller than the theoretical one.
  • Global average pooling replaces huge flatten heads (VGG's 123.6M) with one number per channel: no parameters, any input size.
