Phase 3 · Lesson 4

The CNN family tree: LeNet → ConvNeXt

Twenty-five years of image networks, told as a chain of fixes: each famous architecture exists because the previous one hit a wall. Learn the walls and you can read any new model's paper in minutes.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Conv parameters = k·k·C_in·C_out + C_out; 1 × 1 convs mix channels; stride and pooling shrink maps (Lessons 3.2 and 3.3).
  • A residual connection adds a block's input to its output, y = x + F(x), giving gradients a shortcut (Lesson 2.2). BatchNorm (BN) rescales each channel's activations to a stable range (Lesson 2.3).
  • ImageNet: 1.2 million training photos in 1,000 classes. Top-5 error = how often the right label is not among the model's 5 best guesses (the yearly ILSVRC contest metric until 2017); top-1 accuracy = how often its single best guess is right.
🎯 Hook

In 2012 the best ImageNet entry got the right answer in its top five guesses 84.7% of the time (AlexNet, 15.3% top-5 error). Three years later, a network 19 times deeper got 96.4% (ResNet, 3.57%). Between them, the parameter count went from 60 million up to 138 million (VGG) and back down to 6.6 million (GoogLeNet) before settling around 25 million (ResNet-50).

Deeper but cheaper, more accurate with fewer weights: how? Each step fixed one specific problem. And in 2022, a team took a 2015 ResNet, changed it one design decision at a time, and got it to beat the Vision Transformers that were supposed to have replaced CNNs. What did each of these networks fix, and which ideas are still in the models you'll use in 2026?

What you should see: before 2017, VGG-16 is the heavyweight at 138 million parameters, and 89% of them sit in its three dense layers at the end. GoogLeNet, released the same year, beat it with 6.6 million by using 1 × 1 bottlenecks and global average pooling. From ResNet to ConvNeXt-T, depth and accuracy go up while parameters stay roughly flat or drop: better blocks, not just bigger networks. (ConvNeXt V2 Huge's 650M shows the other modern lever: scale plus pre-training.)

1998: LeNet-5, the template

🤔 Why this? LeNet-5: conv → pool → conv → pool → dense, trained by backprop
  1. The problem before Handwriting recognition used hand-designed features plus a classifier; the Neocognitron (1980) had the right layout but wasn't trained end to end by gradients.
  2. What people tried Dense networks on pixels (no shift tolerance, too many weights); feature engineering per task.
  3. Why this works Alternating convolutions (shared local feature detectors) and subsampling (shift tolerance), all trained by backpropagation, with dense layers at the end. About 60,000 parameters (61,706 in the common modern re-implementation). It set the template every CNN still follows.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Tiny 32 × 32 grey inputs; tanh-style activations; 1990s computers couldn't train it on big colour photos.
  5. What refined it 14 years of faster hardware and bigger datasets, then AlexNet.

2012: AlexNet, the big bang

🤔 Why this? AlexNet: ReLU + dropout + augmentation + GPUs
  1. The problem before LeNet-style networks hadn't scaled to 224 × 224 colour photos and 1,000 classes: too slow to train, too prone to overfitting. The 2011–2012 contest leaders used hand-made features (the runner-up in 2012 had 26.2% top-5 error).
  2. What people tried Better hand-crafted features (SIFT, Fisher vectors) with linear classifiers.
  3. Why this works Four practical changes: ReLU (slope 1, so gradients don't vanish; six times faster than tanh in their test, Lesson 0.4), dropout in the dense layers (Lesson 2.4), data augmentation with random crops, flips and colour shifts (Lesson 2.5), and training on two GPUs for five to six days. Result: 15.3% top-5 error, a gap the field had never seen.
  4. What it costs, when it fails 60 million parameters, most in the dense layers; big 11 × 11 stride-4 first-layer filters are coarse; the network was split across two GPUs only because each had 3 GB of memory.
  5. What refined it Smaller kernels and more depth (VGG), cheaper blocks (GoogLeNet).

2014: two answers to “go deeper”

🤔 Why this? VGG: only 3 × 3 convolutions, 16–19 layers
  1. The problem before AlexNet mixed 11 × 11, 5 × 5 and 3 × 3 kernels; nobody knew how far depth alone could go.
  2. What people tried Tuning kernel sizes and strides layer by layer.
  3. Why this works One rule: 3 × 3 convs everywhere, double the channels after each pool. Three stacked 3 × 3 see 7 × 7 for 27C² instead of 49C² weights, with three ReLUs (Lesson 3.3). Simple and uniform, so it became the go-to feature extractor for years.
  4. What it costs, when it fails 138M parameters (123.6M in the dense head) and 15.3 billion FLOPs (floating-point operations; here multiply-adds) for VGG-16. Depth stalled near 19: deeper plain stacks trained worse.
  5. What refined it Global average pooling instead of the dense head; residuals for depth.
🤔 Why this? Inception / GoogLeNet: parallel branches with 1 × 1 bottlenecks
  1. The problem before Which kernel size is right? Objects appear at many scales. Just stacking bigger layers makes compute explode.
  2. What people tried Picking one kernel size per layer; making networks wider and paying for it.
  3. Why this works Each Inception module runs 1 × 1, 3 × 3 and 5 × 5 convs and a pooling branch in parallel and concatenates them, so the network can use several scales at once. 1 × 1 convs shrink the channels before the expensive branches (Lesson 3.2), and global average pooling replaces the big dense head. 22 layers, 12× fewer parameters than AlexNet, 6.67% top-5: 1st place in 2014.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Hand-designed, irregular modules that are hard to modify; needed extra “auxiliary” classifiers partway up to help gradients reach early layers.
  5. What refined it Inception v2–v4 (adding BatchNorm and factorised kernels); ResNet's simpler fix for depth.

2015: ResNet, depth solved

He et al. noticed something strange: a plain 56-layer network had higher training error than a 20-layer one. Not overfitting: the deeper net was worse even on the training data. Extra layers could in principle just copy their input (and be no worse), but optimisation couldn't find that. Lesson 2.2 covers residuals in depth; here is the architecture view.

🤔 Why this? ResNet: residual blocks y = x + F(x), bottlenecks, BN
  1. The problem before The degradation problem: plain networks beyond about 20 layers got worse, even in training.
  2. What people tried Careful initialisation, BatchNorm, auxiliary classifiers (GoogLeNet), highway networks with learned gates.
  3. Why this works Each block learns only a correction F(x) on top of the identity, so “do nothing” is the easy default and gradients flow straight through the additions. Deep versions use a bottleneck block (1 × 1 down to 64 channels, 3 × 3, 1 × 1 back up to 256). Result: 152 layers, 8× deeper than VGG yet cheaper (11.3 vs 19.6 billion FLOPs for VGG-19), 3.57% top-5 and 1st place in ILSVRC 2015.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Very deep versions give diminishing returns per layer; activations of every block must be stored for backprop (memory).
  5. What refined it Pre-activation ResNets, ResNeXt (grouped convs), DenseNet; the same residual stream runs through every transformer (Phase 6). ResNet-50 is still a standard baseline in 2026.
🤔 Why this? DenseNet (2016): connect every layer to every later layer
  1. The problem before ResNet layers each re-learn features, and information still has to pass through many additions.
  2. What people tried ResNets with stochastic depth (randomly skipping blocks in training).
  3. Why this works Inside a dense block each layer receives the concatenation of all earlier feature maps: L layers have L(L+1)/2 direct connections. Each layer adds only a few new channels (the “growth rate”), because it can reuse everything before it. DenseNet-121 has 8.0M parameters; the paper reports accuracy comparable to ResNets with significantly fewer parameters and compute.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Concatenations make feature maps wide and memory-hungry during training; slower in practice than its FLOP count suggests.
  5. What refined it Its ideas (feature reuse, narrow layers) influenced later efficient designs; ResNets stayed more popular for their simplicity.

2017: MobileNet, CNNs for phones

A standard 3 × 3 conv does two jobs at once: it looks at neighbours (spatial) and mixes channels. Depthwise-separable convolution splits them: a depthwise 3 × 3 that filters each channel on its own (one 3 × 3 per channel, no mixing), then a pointwise 1 × 1 that mixes channels. In words: the cost ratio is one over the number of output channels, plus one over the kernel area:

separable ÷ standard = 1/N + 1/k²   (k = 3, N = 128: 1/128 + 1/9 = 0.119, so 8.4× cheaper)
✏️ Worked example: one layer, 56 × 56 × 128 → 56 × 56 × 128, 3 × 3
WeightsMultiply-adds
Standard 3 × 33·3·128·128 = 147,45656·56·128·128·9 = 462,422,016
Depthwise 3 × 33·3·128 = 1,15256·56·128·9 = 3,612,672
Pointwise 1 × 1128·128 = 16,38456·56·128·128 = 51,380,224
Separable total17,536 (8.4× fewer)54,992,896 (8.4× fewer)

Notice that the 1 × 1 now does 93% of the work. That's why later mobile networks focus on making the pointwise part cheaper.

What you should see: at k = 3 the saving approaches but never reaches 9× (1/k² = 1/9 is the floor): 7.9× at N = 64, 8.4× at 128, 8.7× at 256, matching MobileNet's “8 to 9 times less computation”. Bigger kernels save more (5 × 5 at N = 512: 23.8×), which is why ConvNeXt can afford 7 × 7 depthwise kernels. At k = 1 there's nothing to separate: you pay for an extra layer and it's slightly more expensive.

🤔 Why this? MobileNet: depthwise-separable convolutions
  1. The problem before Accurate CNNs needed billions of multiply-adds per image: too slow and power-hungry for phones and embedded cameras.
  2. What people tried Shrinking or pruning big networks after training; lower resolutions.
  3. Why this works Splitting spatial filtering from channel mixing cuts cost to 1/N + 1/k² of a standard conv. In the paper's own comparison, the same network with full convs had 29.3M parameters and 4,866M multiply-adds for 71.7% top-1; with separable convs 4.2M and 569M for 70.6%. Eight times cheaper for 1.1 points. Two dials (width and resolution multipliers) trade accuracy for speed further.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Depthwise convs do little arithmetic per byte of memory moved, so on GPUs they're often slower than their FLOP count suggests. Capacity drops at very small widths.
  5. What refined it MobileNetV2 (inverted residuals with linear bottlenecks, 2018), later MobileNets and EfficientNet's MBConv blocks; ConvNeXt's 7 × 7 depthwise layers.

2019: EfficientNet, scale everything together

To make a network more accurate you can make it deeper (more layers), wider (more channels) or feed it bigger images. Compute grows with depth d, with width² and with resolution² (doubling width or resolution quadruples the FLOPs). Tan and Le searched once for the best mix and found depth × 1.2, width × 1.1, resolution × 1.15 per step, chosen so that each step roughly doubles the FLOPs (1.2 × 1.1² × 1.15² ≈ 1.92). Repeat the step φ times to get B1…B7.

🤔 Why this? EfficientNet: compound scaling
  1. The problem before People scaled networks along one dimension at a time (ResNet-18 → 152 is depth only), and gains flatten out quickly along any single dimension.
  2. What people tried Hand-tuning depth, width and resolution separately; huge models (GPipe reached 84.3% top-1 with 557M parameters).
  3. Why this works Bigger images need more layers (bigger receptive field) and more channels (finer patterns), so grow all three in a fixed ratio from a small, searched baseline (B0: 5.3M parameters). EfficientNet-B7 matched GPipe's 84.3% while being 8.4× smaller and 6.1× faster at inference.
  4. What it costs, when it fails High resolutions and depthwise convs make training slow and memory-hungry on GPUs, even when FLOPs are low.
  5. What refined it EfficientNetV2 (faster training), and the lesson itself: scaling rules matter as much as blocks, an idea that returned with transformer scaling laws (Phase 6).

2022: ConvNeXt, a CNN for the transformer era

By 2021 Vision Transformers (ViT, Swin) were beating CNNs, and many concluded convolution was obsolete. Liu et al. asked a sharper question: which of the transformers' design choices actually matter? They took a ResNet-50 and changed one thing at a time, measuring each step on ImageNet.

StepImageNet top-1
Original ResNet-5076.1%
Modern training recipe only (longer training, stronger augmentation, AdamW)78.8%
Stage ratio (3, 4, 6, 3) → (3, 3, 9, 3)79.4%
…“patchify” stem (4 × 4, stride 4), depthwise 7 × 7 convs, inverted bottleneck, GELU, fewer norms and activations, LayerNorm (LN) instead of BN, separate downsampling layers82.0% (ConvNeXt-T; Swin-T: 81.3%)
🤔 Why this? ConvNeXt (and ConvNeXt V2)
  1. The problem before ViTs and Swin Transformers were winning, but it was unclear whether attention itself or their training recipes and block designs made the difference.
  2. What people tried Hybrid models adding attention to CNNs; just switching to transformers.
  3. Why this works Borrow the transformer's choices that work, keep convolution: a large 7 × 7 depthwise kernel (like a local attention window), one GELU and one LN per block, wide-narrow-wide blocks, and the modern training recipe. The training recipe alone was worth +2.7 points. Pure convolution, up to 87.8% top-1 (largest model, pre-trained on the 14M-image ImageNet-22K), and better than Swin on COCO detection and ADE20K segmentation.
  4. What it costs, when it fails Large-kernel depthwise convs are memory-bound on GPUs; like ViTs, the big models need large-scale pre-training to shine.
  5. What refined it ConvNeXt V2 (2023) pre-trains with a convolutional masked autoencoder (hide most of the image, learn to fill it in, Lesson 9.1) and adds Global Response Normalization (GRN); its sizes run from a 3.7M “Atto” (76.7%) to a 650M “Huge” (88.9%).
💡 Big idea The family tree is a list of bottlenecks: trainability (ReLU, then residuals), cost (3 × 3 stacks, 1 × 1 bottlenecks, depthwise-separable convs, global average pooling) and scaling (compound scaling, modern recipes). ConvNeXt's lesson for 2026: much of what looks like “a better architecture” is a better training recipe. Always compare models trained the same way.
🧠 Analogy: the evolution of the car

LeNet is the first engine on wheels. AlexNet is the first mass-produced car (the engine plus good tyres, brakes and roads: ReLU, dropout, GPUs). VGG builds a bigger, simpler engine; GoogLeNet a turbocharger. ResNet adds the gearbox that lets you go really fast without stalling. MobileNet is the scooter for crowded cities. EfficientNet is a rule for sizing all parts together. ConvNeXt is a classic car rebuilt with modern parts that keeps up with the new electric models.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

You almost never code these by hand: torchvision.models and timm have them all. weights=None builds the architecture with random weights (no download), which is enough to count parameters and test shapes. A depthwise conv is just groups=channels.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torchvision import models

count = lambda m: sum(p.numel() for p in m.parameters())
for name in ["alexnet", "vgg16", "resnet50", "mobilenet_v2", "efficientnet_b0", "convnext_tiny"]:
    net = getattr(models, name)(weights=None)          # architecture only, random weights, no download
    print(f"{name:16s} {count(net) / 1e6:6.1f}M parameters")

M, N = 128, 128                                          # depthwise-separable vs standard 3×3
standard = nn.Conv2d(M, N, 3, padding=1, bias=False)
separable = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(M, M, 3, padding=1, groups=M, bias=False),  # depthwise: one 3×3 filter per channel
    nn.Conv2d(M, N, 1, bias=False),                       # pointwise: 1×1 mixes the channels
)
x = torch.randn(1, M, 56, 56)
print(standard(x).shape == separable(x).shape, count(standard), count(separable))

🎤 Interview angle

“Walk me through the evolution of CNN architectures.” A strong answer is a chain of problems and fixes, with one number each:

  • AlexNet: ReLU, dropout, augmentation, GPUs: 15.3% vs 26.2% top-5. VGG: uniform 3 × 3 stacks, 138M parameters mostly in the head. GoogLeNet: parallel branches + 1 × 1 bottlenecks + GAP, 12× fewer parameters than AlexNet.
  • ResNet: the degradation problem (56 layers worse than 20 in training) fixed by identity shortcuts; 152 layers; bottleneck blocks.
  • MobileNet: depthwise-separable convs, cost ratio 1/N + 1/k² ≈ 8–9× cheaper at 3 × 3. EfficientNet: scale depth, width and resolution together.
  • ConvNeXt: modernised ResNet matches Swin; training recipes explain much of the gap. Bonus: know when you'd still pick a CNN over a ViT (small data, edge devices, high resolution).
🏭 In the real world
The degradation problem, solved 🧪 Research · 2015 He, Zhang, Ren & Sun: Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition

How: identity shortcuts around every block; won ILSVRC 2015 classification, and also 1st in ImageNet detection and localization and COCO detection and segmentation.

Why this method: lets you just add layers: a 152-layer net was easier to train than a 56-layer plain one.

Efficient on-device vision 🧪 Research · 2017 Howard et al. (Google): MobileNets

How: depthwise-separable convs with width and resolution multipliers, targeting mobile and embedded vision.

Why this method: 8× fewer multiply-adds for about 1 point of accuracy, and two dials to fit any device budget.

Scaling by rule 🧪 Research · 2019 Tan & Le (Google): EfficientNet

How: a small searched baseline (B0) scaled up with one compound coefficient into B1–B7.

Why this method: one family covers phones to servers, each member near the best accuracy for its cost.

Modernising the ConvNet 🧪 Research · 2022 Liu et al. (Meta AI): A ConvNet for the 2020s · ConvNeXt V2

How: a step-by-step ablation from ResNet-50 to ConvNeXt, then masked-autoencoder pre-training in V2.

Why this method: pure convolutions stay simple and efficient at high resolution, and the ablation shows which ideas carry the gains.

Every backbone, one line 🧰 Open source torchvision.models · timm

How: ResNet, MobileNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt (and ViTs) with ImageNet weights and their matching preprocessing.

Why this method: reproducing a paper's training recipe takes weeks of GPU time; downloading its weights takes seconds (Lesson 3.5).

Check yourself

1. A depthwise-separable conv with k = 3 and N = 256 output channels replaces a standard 3 × 3 conv. Roughly how many times fewer multiply-adds?
Ratio = 1/N + 1/k² = 1/256 + 1/9 ≈ 0.115, so 1 ÷ 0.115 ≈ 8.7× cheaper. The 1/N term is the depthwise part's share and the 1/k² term is the pointwise 1 × 1 part's share. Even with huge N, the pointwise part alone costs 1/9 of the standard conv, so at 3 × 3 the saving can never reach 9×.
2. A plain 50-layer CNN has higher training loss than your 20-layer version, with the same data and recipe. Which architecture idea addresses exactly this?
Worse training loss with more layers is the degradation problem, not overfitting, so dropout (a regulariser) won't help and GAP changes only the head. ResNet's shortcuts make “do nothing” the default and give gradients a direct path (Lessons 2.2 and 3.4).
3. Your team compares their new architecture (trained with AdamW, strong augmentation, 300 epochs) to a ResNet-50 number copied from the 2015 paper (76.1%), and claims a 5-point win. What's the problem?
ConvNeXt's ablation showed the training recipe alone took ResNet-50 from 76.1% to 78.8%, and torchvision's newer weights reach 80.9%. A fair comparison trains both models with the same data, augmentation, optimiser and epochs; otherwise you're measuring the recipe, not the architecture.
📝 TL;DR
  • LeNet (1998) set the template: conv, pool, dense, backprop. AlexNet (2012) scaled it with ReLU, dropout, augmentation and GPUs: 15.3% vs 26.2% top-5.
  • VGG (2014): only 3 × 3, 138M parameters (mostly the head). GoogLeNet (2014): parallel branches, 1 × 1 bottlenecks, GAP: 12× fewer parameters than AlexNet.
  • ResNet (2015): residuals fixed the degradation problem; 152 layers, 3.57% top-5. DenseNet: concatenate everything, reuse features.
  • MobileNet (2017): depthwise-separable convs cost 1/N + 1/k² (≈ 8–9× cheaper at 3 × 3). EfficientNet (2019): scale depth, width and resolution together.
  • ConvNeXt (2022): a modernised ResNet matches Swin; much of the gain is the training recipe. Compare architectures only under the same recipe.
