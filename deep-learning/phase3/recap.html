Recap: Convolutional networks

Why images need convolutions, how a conv layer works and what decides its shape, how far its units can see, the architectures that took CNNs from 60 thousand to 650 million parameters, how to reuse them with a few hundred labels, and how to look inside. Here's the phase on one page, a speed round, and a network to design yourself.

🧾 Cheat sheet

🖼️

3.1 Why not an MLP?

Dense on 224 × 224 × 3 → 1,000 = 150.5M weights. Conv 3 × 3, 64 filters = 1,792, at any size.

Two priors: locality and weight sharing. A shifted input is a new input to an MLP, but just a shifted feature map to a conv (equivariance).

🧰 nn.Conv2d, nn.Linear(..., device="meta") for counting

🔲

3.2 Convolution

out = ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1 · params = k·k·C_in·C_out + C_out · MACs = H·W·C_out·k·k·C_in

Dot product of kernel and patch at every position. “Same” p = (k − 1)/2; stride 2 halves. 1 × 1 convs mix channels. It's really cross-correlation.

🧰 F.conv2d, padding="same", groups=

🔭

3.3 Receptive fields & pooling

RF += (k − 1) × jump; jump ×= s. Three 3 × 3 = 7 × 7 for 27C² vs 49C².

Stacking adds, striding and pooling multiply, dilation widens without shrinking. The effective RF is a smaller Gaussian blob. GAP replaces huge dense heads.

🧰 nn.MaxPool2d, nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1), dilation=

🌳

3.4 The family tree

AlexNet 15.3% vs 26.2% → VGG 3 × 3 → GoogLeNet 1 × 1 → ResNet x + F(x) → MobileNet 1/N + 1/k² → EfficientNet → ConvNeXt

Each fixed one wall: trainability, cost, scaling. Training recipes matter as much as blocks: compare models under the same recipe.

🧰 torchvision.models, timm

🎁

3.5 Transfer learning

Little data + similar images → freeze, train the head. More data or a domain gap → fine-tune with a lower backbone LR.

Features go general → specific. Frozen features plateau on very different images. Watch BatchNorm in frozen parts; use the weights' own preprocessing.

🧰 weights=...DEFAULT, requires_grad=False, parameter groups, DINOv2

🔍

3.6 Looking inside

Layer 1 = edges and colour blobs. Occlusion = hide and measure. Grad-CAM = maps × average gradient → ReLU.

Explanations are experiments; sanity-check them. ImageNet CNNs lean on texture over shape.

🧰 forward hooks, Captum, pytorch-grad-cam

🗣️ Teach it back

Answer out loud, like in an interview: “Walk me through what happens to a 224 × 224 photo inside a ResNet-50, from pixels to the class probabilities. Why convolutions and not dense layers, how do the shapes change, how does the last layer get to ‘see’ the whole image, and how would you adapt the network to classify 500 photos of your own products?”

A strong answer covers: the parameter argument (150M dense vs thousands per conv) and the two priors; 3 × 3 kernels spanning all input channels, with “same” padding, and stride-2 steps that halve the map while channels double (224 → 112 → 56 → 28 → 14 → 7, 64 → … → 2,048); receptive fields compounding through depth and stride (and the effective RF being smaller); residual bottleneck blocks that make 50 layers trainable; global average pooling to a 2,048-vector and one dense layer to 1,000 logits, then softmax; and for 500 photos, pre-trained weights with their preprocessing, a new head, a frozen backbone first, then partial fine-tuning with a lower backbone LR, plus augmentation and a Grad-CAM check that it looks at the products.

🏗️ Build it yourself: a CNN for 64 × 64 images

✏️ The problem

You have 64 × 64 RGB images in 10 classes (think small product photos), and you're asked to design a CNN from scratch. Constraints: under 2 million parameters, the last conv layer must have a theoretical receptive field that covers the whole image, and the head must work if someone later feeds 96 × 96 images.

Write down, layer by layer: the output shape, the parameter count, and the receptive field. Then: the training recipe, and what you'd do instead if you only had 2,000 labelled images. Then open the reveal.

Numbers

  • Total: 1,174,826 parameters (checked with PyTorch). Compute: about 211 million multiply-adds per image.
  • Each conv: k·k·C_in·C_out + C_out, e.g. 3·3·128·256 + 256 = 295,168. Channels double each time the map halves, so each stage costs similar compute.
  • Receptive field: +2 × jump per 3 × 3, +1 × jump per pool; the jump doubles at each pool (1, 2, 4, 8). Last conv: 68, so a central unit can see the whole 64 × 64 image (its effective RF is smaller, which is fine here; add a stage if objects fill the frame and accuracy suffers).
  • Head: GAP + linear = 2,570 parameters, and it works at 96 × 96 (the maps become 12 × 12; GAP doesn't care). A flatten head would be 8·8·256·10 + 10 = 163,850 and would break at a new size.

Training recipe

  • Add BatchNorm after each conv (Lesson 2.3); He initialization for ReLU (1.4).
  • Cross-entropy loss; AdamW (lr ≈ 1e-3, weight decay ≈ 0.05) or SGD with momentum; warm-up then cosine decay (1.6).
  • Augmentation: random crops with padding, horizontal flips (if they make sense for the classes), mild colour jitter (2.5).
  • Before training for real: overfit one batch to near-zero loss (2.6); watch train vs validation curves; early-stop on validation.

Only 2,000 labelled images?

  • Don't train from scratch: take a pre-trained ResNet-18/50 or ConvNeXt-T from torchvision/timm, upsample to its input size (or use it at 64 × 64 with lower accuracy), replace the head with 10 outputs (3.5).
  • Start frozen (a linear probe), then unfreeze the last stage with a 10× lower LR if the frozen baseline plateaus. Keep BatchNorm in eval mode while frozen.

Classic mistakes

  • Forgetting padding, so maps shrink layer by layer and shapes no longer match the head.
  • A flatten + dense head with millions of parameters that overfits and locks the input size.
  • Too few downsampling steps: the receptive field never covers the objects.
  • Pre-trained weights fed images without their normalization (mean/std), or ImageNet-style flips for classes where left and right matter (e.g. arrows, text).

What this doesn't cover: finding where objects are and drawing boxes or masks around them. That's Phase 4 (4.1 detection basics, 4.3 segmentation).

🏆 Boss quiz

1. Input 128 × 128 × 3. Conv layer: 7 × 7 kernels, 64 filters, stride 2, padding 3. Output shape and parameter count?
Side: ⌊(128 + 6 − 7)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊127/2⌋ + 1 = 64; depth = 64 filters. Parameters: 7·7·3·64 = 9,408 weights + 64 biases = 9,472. That's the shape of ResNet's first layer, which drops the bias because BatchNorm follows it (Lessons 3.2 and 3.4).
2. Three 3 × 3 convs with stride 2, then one 3 × 3 conv with stride 1. What's the theoretical receptive field of the last layer?
RF 1 → 3 (jump 2) → 3 + 2·2 = 7 (jump 4) → 7 + 2·4 = 15 (jump 8) → 15 + 2·8 = 31. Striding early makes every later layer count more (Lesson 3.3).
3. Your ResNet-50 classifier is accurate but too slow and too big for a phone app. What's the most standard change?
Depthwise-separable convs cost 1/N + 1/k² of a standard conv (about 8–9× fewer multiply-adds at 3 × 3), and those families have a few million parameters (EfficientNet-B0: 5.3M vs 25.6M). VGG-16 is far bigger (138M); removing residuals hurts training (Lesson 3.4).
4. You fine-tune a whole pre-trained network on 600 images with one learning rate of 1e-2. Validation accuracy ends below what a frozen backbone with a linear head gave you. Best next step?
A high LR on the backbone wrecks good features while a random head is still learning, and 600 images overfit millions of weights. Discriminative LRs and gradual unfreezing fix exactly that (Lesson 3.5).
5. Grad-CAM on your “defective part” class consistently lights up a small timestamp in the corner of the photos. What's going on?
Heatmaps are evidence; test them with an intervention. If hiding the timestamp drops the “defective” score, the model learned the camera, not the defect. The fix is in the data (Lesson 3.6).
