Phase 3 · Lesson 5

Transfer learning and fine-tuning

Nobody trains a vision model from zero anymore. You borrow a network that already learned to see from millions of photos, and teach it your task with a few hundred. Here's how, and when it backfires.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A CNN backbone (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt) ends in global average pooling and one dense head that maps features to classes (Lessons 3.3, 3.4).
  • Overfitting: a model with many parameters and few examples memorises the training set and fails on new data (Lesson 2.4). The learning rate sets the step size of each update (Lesson 1.6).
🎯 Hook

Your manager hands you 300 labelled photos of factory parts (good, scratched, dented) and wants a classifier by Friday. A ResNet-50 has 25.6 million parameters. Three hundred photos to set 25 million numbers: that's about 85,000 parameters per photo. It should overfit instantly.

Teams ship exactly this every week. The trick: almost none of those 25 million numbers are learned from your photos. They were learned from ImageNet's 1.2 million photos, where the network figured out edges, textures and object parts, and in 2014 Yosinski et al. showed such features carry over to new tasks. In this lesson's live toy, a model with 8 labelled points goes from 48% accuracy (trained from scratch) to 100% (with a borrowed backbone). Which parts should you borrow, which should you retrain, and when does borrowing hurt?

Numbers first: what you actually train

Strategy (ResNet-50, 5 classes)Trainable parametersShare
Train from scratch23,518,277100%

Feature extraction: freeze everything, train a new head10,245 (2,048 × 5 + 5)0.04%

With a frozen backbone, you're training a 10,245-parameter linear classifier on top of 2,048 ready-made features per image. Thirty photos per class is plenty for that. The backbone works like a fixed, very good feature extractor.

💡 Big idea Features go from general to specific as you go up a CNN. Early layers detect edges, colours and textures that are useful for almost any photo; late layers detect things like “dog snout” that are tied to the original classes. Transfer learning reuses the general part and retrains the specific part, and the right amount to retrain depends on how much data you have and how different your images are.
🤔 Why this? Transfer learning (pre-train, then adapt)
  1. The problem before Deep CNNs need huge labelled datasets; most real tasks have hundreds or thousands of images, not millions. Trained from scratch on 300 photos, a 25M-parameter network memorises them.
  2. What people tried Small networks (too weak), hand-crafted features (brittle), heavy augmentation alone (helps, not enough).
  3. Why this works A network trained on a big, diverse dataset has already learned reusable visual building blocks. Yosinski et al. (2014) showed first-layer features (Gabor-like edge filters, colour blobs) are general across tasks, that transferability drops as the tasks grow apart, but that even features from distant tasks beat random ones, and that a transferred start helps generalisation even after fine-tuning. Kornblith et al. (2019) found that better ImageNet models are better feature extractors almost perfectly in step (correlation r = 0.99 for frozen features).
  4. What it costs, when it fails You inherit the backbone's size, input format and biases. When your images look nothing like the pre-training data (medical scans, satellite, microscopy), the benefit can shrink a lot (Raghu et al. 2019, below). With lots of data, training from scratch can catch up (He et al. 2018).
  5. What refined it Bigger and more diverse pre-training (ImageNet-22K, then self-supervised backbones like DINOv2 trained on 142M curated images without labels; Lessons 4.5 and 9.1); parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA for huge models (AI Engineer 7.1).

🧠 Analogy: hiring an experienced photographer

You need someone to sort photos of your products. A fresh graduate (from scratch) needs months of examples. An experienced photographer (pre-trained) already knows light, edges, materials and shapes; you only teach them your categories (a new head). If your “photos” are actually X-rays, their eye for composition helps less, so you also retrain some of their instincts (fine-tune more layers), gently, so they don't forget everything they knew (a small learning rate).

What you should see (seeded, so you'll get these too): with no gap and 8 labels, scratch gets 48%, the frozen backbone 100%, and fine-tuning everything only 63% (56% with the same LR as the head): with 8 points, retraining all 337 weights mostly fits noise and damages good features. The pattern is real-world advice in miniature: little data and similar images → freeze; a domain gap or more data → fine-tune more.

A toy: 2D points instead of photos, 16-unit layers instead of 25 million weights. The shape of the trade-off is what transfers to real projects, not the exact numbers.

🤔 Why this? Freezing layers
  1. The problem before Fine-tuning all 25.6M parameters on 300 images overfits, and every training step costs a full backward pass through the backbone.
  2. What people tried Strong regularisation and early stopping on full fine-tunes; tiny learning rates.
  3. Why this works Setting requires_grad = False on the backbone leaves only the head to learn (10,245 parameters for ResNet-50 with 5 classes), which a few hundred examples can fit without memorising. It's also much cheaper: no gradients for frozen layers, and you can even compute the features once and cache them. In the toy: 8 labels, 100% frozen vs 48% scratch.
  4. What it costs, when it fails The features are fixed: if they don't describe your images well, accuracy plateaus no matter how much data you add (the 57° gap). Gotcha: BatchNorm layers in a “frozen” backbone still update their running statistics in train() mode; keep them in eval() if you really want them frozen.
  5. What refined it Gradual unfreezing (train the head, then unfreeze the top block, then the next) from ULMFiT (Howard & Ruder 2018); linear probing then fine-tuning; adapters and LoRA for very large models.
🤔 Why this? Discriminative (layer-wise) learning rates
  1. The problem before The new head starts random and needs big steps; the backbone is already good and big steps wreck it (people call this “catastrophic forgetting”). One learning rate can't serve both.
  2. What people tried One small LR for everything (the head learns too slowly), or freezing (the backbone can't adapt at all).
  3. Why this works Give each part its own LR: high for the head, lower for late layers, lowest for early layers, which are the most general. ULMFiT used η/2.6 for each layer below the one above it. In PyTorch it's just parameter groups in the optimizer. In the toy, a backbone LR × 0.3 beat the same LR everywhere: 63% vs 56% with 8 labels, 93% vs 85% with 20.
  4. What it costs, when it fails More knobs (LR per group, how many groups); too low a backbone LR is the same as freezing.
  5. What refined it Layer-wise LR decay (a constant factor per layer) is standard when fine-tuning ViTs and ConvNeXts; warm-up plus cosine schedules (Lesson 1.6).
✏️ Worked example: three LRs for a ResNet fine-tune

Head LR 1e-3. ULMFiT-style factor 2.6 per group, three groups going down:

GroupLR
head (fc)1e-3
layer41e-3 ÷ 2.6 ≈ 3.8e-4
layer31e-3 ÷ 2.6² ≈ 1.5e-4
layer1–2, stemfrozen (or 1e-3 ÷ 2.6³ ≈ 5.7e-5)

Each step down is about 2.6× gentler, so the general early features barely move while the head learns fast.

When does it fail? The domain gap

A model pre-trained on everyday photos “expects” everyday photos: colour, natural lighting, objects in the middle. The further your images are from that, the less the late features help:

  • Medical imaging. Raghu et al. (2019) studied two large medical-imaging tasks and found that, surprisingly, “transfer offers little benefit to performance”, and small, lightweight models did about as well as big ImageNet architectures. Pre-training still sped up convergence, but the expected accuracy gain wasn't there.
  • Lots of target data. He, Girshick and Dollár (2018) trained COCO detectors from random initialisation and matched ImageNet-pre-trained ones, given longer training. Pre-training “speeds up convergence early in training, but does not necessarily” improve final accuracy.
  • Mismatched inputs. A model trained on RGB at 224 × 224 with ImageNet's normalisation gets confused by grey 16-bit scans at 1024 × 1024 fed in raw. Always apply the backbone's own preprocessing.

Current practice (2026)

Two libraries cover most vision transfer learning: torchvision (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, ViT and more with ImageNet weights; each weight object carries its own preprocessing via weights.transforms()) and timm (hundreds of architectures and checkpoints; timm.create_model(name, pretrained=True, num_classes=K) builds the model with a fresh K-class head). For the strongest general-purpose features, self-supervised backbones such as DINOv2 (Meta, 2023; trained on 142M curated images without labels, available through torch.hub) and its 2025 successor DINOv3 are often used frozen, with just a linear head: their paper's goal is features that work “without finetuning”. Those are Vision Transformers: Lessons 4.5 and 9.1 explain how they're built and trained.

🧰 Tools you'll actually use

Freeze with requires_grad = False, swap the head, and give the optimizer parameter groups with different LRs. This runs as-is (random weights); for real use swap in the commented line, which downloads the ImageNet weights.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torchvision import models

torch.manual_seed(0)
# Real use (downloads ~100 MB of ImageNet weights once):
#   weights = models.ResNet50_Weights.DEFAULT
#   net = models.resnet50(weights=weights); preprocess = weights.transforms()
net = models.resnet50(weights=None)                 # same architecture, random weights: no download here

# 1) Feature extraction: freeze everything, replace the head for 5 classes
for p in net.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False
net.fc = nn.Linear(net.fc.in_features, 5)           # new head, trainable by default
trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
print(f"trainable: {trainable:,} of {sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters()):,}")

# 2) Fine-tuning with discriminative learning rates: unfreeze the last stage, give it a 10x smaller LR
for p in net.layer4.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = True
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([
    {"params": net.layer4.parameters(), "lr": 1e-4},
    {"params": net.fc.parameters(), "lr": 1e-3},
], weight_decay=0.01)

net.train()
x, y = torch.randn(8, 3, 224, 224), torch.randint(0, 5, (8,))
loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(x), y)
loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"one step done, loss {loss.item():.2f}; layer3 got gradients? {net.layer3[0].conv1.weight.grad is not None}")

# What we got on a laptop CPU (torch 2.8, torchvision 0.23):
# trainable: 10,245 of 23,518,277
# one step done, loss 2.02; layer3 got gradients? False

The timm equivalent (also downloads weights): timm.create_model("convnext_tiny", pretrained=True, num_classes=5), with matching preprocessing from timm.data.resolve_data_config(model.pretrained_cfg) and timm.data.create_transform(...). DINOv2: torch.hub.load("facebookresearch/dinov2", "dinov2_vits14").

Gotchas: (1) frozen BatchNorm still updates its running stats in train() mode: call .eval() on frozen parts. (2) Use the weights' own preprocessing (resize, crop, normalise with ImageNet mean and std), or accuracy quietly drops. (3) Pass only trainable parameters to the optimizer, or use parameter groups as above.

🎤 Interview angle

“You have 500 labelled images for a new classification task. What do you do?” A strong answer:

  • Starts from a pre-trained backbone (torchvision/timm), with its own preprocessing, and a new head.
  • Chooses by data size × domain similarity: frozen backbone + linear head first (fast baseline), then unfreeze the top blocks with a lower LR (parameter groups) if the frozen one plateaus.
  • Mentions augmentation (2.5), early stopping on a validation set, and BatchNorm in eval mode when frozen.
  • Knows the failure cases: big domain gaps (medical, satellite) and lots of data, where transfer may help less than expected (Raghu et al. 2019; He et al. 2018), and checks it with a from-scratch baseline when data allows.
🏭 In the real world
The standard fine-tuning recipe 🧰 Open source PyTorch tutorial: Transfer Learning for Computer Vision

How: a pre-trained ResNet, either fine-tuned end to end or used as a fixed feature extractor with a new final layer.

Why this method: the official tutorial's two options are exactly the two ends of the freezing spectrum, on a dataset of a few hundred images.

Better backbones transfer better 🧪 Research · 2019 Kornblith, Shlens & Le (Google): Do Better ImageNet Models Transfer Better?

How: 16 architectures compared on 12 downstream image datasets, as frozen feature extractors and fine-tuned.

Why this method: ImageNet accuracy predicted transfer accuracy with r = 0.99 (frozen features) and 0.96 (fine-tuned): pick a stronger backbone and your task usually improves.

When transfer barely helps 🧪 Research · 2019 Raghu, Zhang, Kleinberg & Bengio: Transfusion (medical imaging)

How: ImageNet-pre-trained vs randomly initialised models on two large medical-imaging tasks.

Why this method matters: transfer gave little accuracy benefit there and small models did comparably; much of the gain came from over-parameterised models, not clever feature reuse. Measure, don't assume.

Frozen general-purpose features 🧪 Research · 2023 Oquab et al. (Meta AI): DINOv2 · code and weights

How: self-supervised pre-training on 142M curated images; downstream tasks use the frozen backbone with simple heads.

Why this method: its stated aim is features that “work across image distributions and tasks without finetuning”, so one backbone serves many projects (Lessons 4.5 and 9.1).

🤯 Fun fact · transfer learning's most famous number is 2.6

ULMFiT (2018), the paper that made fine-tuning standard in NLP before BERT and GPT, chose its layer-wise learning rates with a rule this simple: each layer gets the rate of the layer above it divided by 2.6, a value the authors found worked well empirically. Same trick, any modality: the closer to the input, the gentler the update.

Check yourself

1. You freeze a ConvNeXt-Tiny backbone whose pooled features have 768 dimensions and add a new linear head for 12 classes. How many parameters will training update?
Only the head trains: 768 × 12 weights + 12 biases = 9,228. The 28.6 million backbone parameters are frozen. That's why a few hundred images are enough for feature extraction.
2. A frozen ImageNet ResNet with a new head reaches 71% on your satellite-image task and stays at 71% even after you double the labelled data. What's the most likely issue and next step?
A plateau that more data doesn't move means the fixed features are the bottleneck, not the amount of data or the head. Satellite images look very different from ImageNet photos, so let the backbone adapt, gently, with discriminative learning rates (the toy's 57° gap showed the same plateau).
3. You fine-tune all layers of a pre-trained network on 400 images with the same learning rate (1e-2) for everything. Validation accuracy is worse than with a frozen backbone. Why?
The random new head needs big steps; the backbone needs small ones. With one big LR, early updates (driven by a random head's errors) damage good features, and many weights fit few images. Parameter groups with a lower backbone LR, or freezing first and unfreezing later, fix it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Transfer learning reuses a backbone pre-trained on a big dataset; you train a new head (and maybe more). Features go from general (edges) to specific (object parts) up the network.
  • Frozen backbone: train only the head (10,245 parameters for ResNet-50 → 5 classes). Best with little data and similar images.
  • Fine-tuning: unfreeze the top (or all) with a lower backbone learning rate via parameter groups (ULMFiT: ÷ 2.6 per layer).
  • Domain gaps (medical, satellite) and plenty of data shrink the benefit; frozen features plateau. Measure against a baseline.
  • 2026 practice: torchvision / timm weights with their own preprocessing; DINOv2-style self-supervised backbones as strong frozen features.
