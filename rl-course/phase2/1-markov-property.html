Phase 2 · Lesson 1

The Markov property: the present is enough

One assumption makes nearly all of RL possible: "where I am now" tells me everything I need to know.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • State = a snapshot of "where things are right now" (Lesson 0.2).
  • Probability = how often something happens in the long run, from 0 (never) to 1 (always) (Lesson 1.1).
🎯 Hook

In 1913, the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov went through the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's poem Eugene Onegin by hand, counting how often a vowel follows a vowel, a consonant follows a vowel, and so on. He found that the previous letter changes the odds a lot: right after a vowel, the next letter was a vowel only about 13% of the time; right after a consonant, about 66%. To predict the next letter, the current one already tells you a great deal.

In effect, that was one of the first statistical language models, about 110 years before ChatGPT.

Up or down? 🏀

Here's a photo of a ball in the air. Is it going up or down?

one photo

You can't tell. One photo doesn't contain enough information.

position + direction → going UP ⬆️

With position and velocity, you can predict exactly where the ball goes next, and it no longer matters how it got there. That's what makes a state Markov.

💡 Big idea A state is Markov if it contains everything needed to predict what happens next. Once you know the present, the past adds nothing.
🧸 Analogy · chess vs. a story

Chess: walk up to any game and look at the board, and you can choose the best move without knowing how the players got there. The board is (almost*) a Markov state.

A novel: open a book at page 200 and read one sentence. You can't predict the next sentence well without the plot so far. One sentence is not a Markov state.

*Nerd detail: castling rights, en passant and the repetition rule depend on history, so real chess engines add those few facts to the state. Adding missing information to the state is exactly how you make it Markov.

Math refresher Conditional probability: P(A | B)

You met the bar | in Phase 0: read it as "given". P(A | B) is the probability of A, given that we know B.

  • P(rain tomorrow) = 0.3, the overall chance.
  • P(rain tomorrow | rain today) = 0.6. Knowing today was rainy changes the odds.

Now the Markov property in symbols. Subscripts are just time stamps: s0 is where you were at step 0, st is "now", st+1 is "one step from now", and the dots mean "everything in between".

P( st+1 | st ) = P( st+1 | s0, s1, …, st ) "Predicting the next state from just the current one is as good as predicting it from the whole history."

In code: the function that predicts the future takes current_state, not history_list.

Markov or not? 🃏

A robot vacuum's state = its (x, y) position only. It's driving at speed.
Like the ball photo: without speed and direction you can't predict the next position. Add velocity → Markov.
Atari game, state = one single screen image.
One frame can't show which way the ball is moving. DeepMind's DQN (2013) fixed this by stacking the last 4 frames as the state, which you'll see in Phase 4.
Tic-tac-toe, state = the board.
The board tells you everything: whose turn it is (count the marks), what moves are legal, who can win.
An LLM, state = the full text so far (prompt + every generated token).
The model's next-token probabilities depend only on the text in its context, and the full text includes the entire history. So it's Markov by construction (see the LLM bridge below).

Markov chains: states that hop around

A Markov chain is a set of states plus probabilities of hopping from one to another. There are no actions and no rewards yet; it's just a system wandering by itself. Next lesson, adding actions and rewards turns it into an MDP.

🎮 Playground · The weather machine

Change the sliders and watch: no matter how it starts, the long-run fraction of sunny days settles to the same number. Each day depends only on the day before.

Where does "math predicts" come from? Take the default sliders: a sunny day stays sunny 80% of the time, a rainy day stays rainy 50%. Over a long stretch, the number of ☀️→🌧️ switches must equal the number of 🌧️→☀️ switches (every rainy spell that starts also ends). If a fraction x of days is sunny, switches to rain happen on x × 0.2 of all days and switches back to sun on (1 − x) × 0.5. Set them equal: 0.2x = 0.5 − 0.5x, so x = 0.5 / 0.7 ≈ 71.4%. Write p for P(☀️ → ☀️) and q for P(🌧️ → 🌧️), and the same balance x(1 − p) = (1 − x)(1 − q) solves to:

fraction sunny = (1 − q) / (2 − p − q) "the chance a rainy spell ends, divided by the chance that either kind of spell ends"

🌍 Where it's used
A Markov chain of credit ratings 🏭 In production · 1997 J.P. Morgan CreditMetrics: how a company's rating drifts

How: state = a company's credit rating (AAA, AA, … CCC, or default) · no actions, no rewards: just a one-year table of hop probabilities between ratings, multiplied together for longer horizons.

Why this method: today's rating is treated as enough to predict next year's. The authors checked it against real multi-year default rates and called the Markov chain “a reasonable modeling tool”.

Not Markov? Stack frames into the state 🧪 Research · 2013 DeepMind: Playing Atari from screen pixels

How: one screenshot hides which way the ball is moving, so state = the last 4 frames stacked · action = a joystick move · reward = change in game score.

Why this method: the paper notes a single screen makes the task “partially observed”. Four frames add the missing velocity cheaply, instead of feeding in the whole game so far.

🤯 Fun fact · Google was built on a Markov chain

Google's original PageRank (1998) imagined a "random surfer" clicking links forever: each web page is a state, each link a hop. A page's rank is the long-run fraction of time the surfer spends there, the same kind of number you just computed for sunny days.

🌉 LLM bridge · how LLMs make the state Markov

Old language models (n-grams) used only the last 2–4 words as the state. That's not Markov for language: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." To know what "it" means you need words from much further back.

Transformers solved this by making the state the entire context, hundreds of thousands of tokens. That's why RL can treat an LLM as an MDP: the context contains the full history.

Agents break it again. When a coding agent can't see a file it hasn't opened, or a long task overflows its context window, it's partially observable: it can't see the full state, like the ball photo without the velocity (an RL term: a POMDP). That's why agents take notes, keep memory files and re-read code: they're rebuilding a Markov state. (The AGENTS.md file in this course folder is one of those notes!)

Check yourself ✅

1. What does "Markov" mean in one sentence?
Given the present, the past is irrelevant, because the present already summarizes it.
2. Your state isn't Markov. What's the usual fix?
Ball → add velocity. Atari → stack frames. Chess → add castling rights. Agents → notes and memory. In deep RL, a recurrent network or a transformer can also learn its own summary of history.
3. A Markov chain has states and transition probabilities. What's missing compared to an RL problem?
A Markov chain just wanders on its own. Add actions (choices) and rewards (scores) and you get a Markov Decision Process, which is the next lesson.
📝 TL;DR
  • Markov: the present state is enough to predict the future. History adds nothing.
  • Not Markov? Put the missing information into the state (velocity, frame stacks, memory).
  • Markov chain = states + hop probabilities. It has long-run behavior you can compute.
  • LLMs are Markov because the state is the whole context. Agents with limited context are only partially observable.
