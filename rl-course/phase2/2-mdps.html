Phase 2 · Lesson 2

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)

The standard way to write down any RL problem, from a vacuum robot to ChatGPT.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Markov chain = states + probabilities of hopping between them (last lesson).
  • State, action, reward, policy (Lesson 0.2) and the discount γ: future rewards count a bit less, e.g. ×0.9 per step (Lesson 0.3).
🎯 Hook

In the 1990s, Robert Crites and Andrew Barto trained an RL system to control a bank of elevators in a simulated 10-floor building. It beat the standard dispatching algorithms of the time. To do that, they first had to write the problem down as an MDP. Every RL success story starts the same way.

💡 Big idea An MDP = a Markov chain + actions (you get to choose) + rewards (you get scored). It's the rulebook of the world: what can happen, how likely it is, and how much it's worth.
🧠 Analogy: a board game's rulebook

Before you can play (or write an AI for) a board game, you need its rulebook: what positions exist (states), what moves you can make (actions), what happens after a move, including dice rolls (transitions), how points are scored (rewards), and whether points now beat points later (γ). An MDP is exactly that rulebook, written precisely enough for a computer.

The 5 ingredients

S

States
where you can be

A

Actions
what you can do

P

Transitions
P(s'|s,a): where you'll end up

R

Rewards
what each step is worth

γ

Discount
how much the future counts

MDP = ( S, A, P, R, γ ) s' (read "s-prime") = the next state. P(s' | s, a) = "the chance of landing in s', given I was in s and did a".

Notice what P depends on: just s and a, not the history. That's the Markov property from the last lesson in action.

Meet the recycling robot ♻️🤖

A classic example from Sutton & Barto. A robot collects empty cans. Its battery is either High or Low.

  • search for cans: +2 per step, but drains the battery. If it searches on a low battery and the battery dies, a human has to rescue it: −3, and it's carried back to charge.
  • wait for someone to bring a can: +1, no battery drain.
  • recharge (only when Low): 0 reward, battery back to High.

One more word before you play. A policy π(a|s) (read "pi of a given s") is the agent's rulebook: "in state s, take action a with this probability". A deterministic policy always picks the same action in a state, e.g. "High → search, Low → recharge". With 2 states, and 2 or 3 actions in each, there are only 2 × 3 = 6 possible deterministic policies, so we could just try them all. Our little gridworld has 20 non-terminal cells with 4 actions each: 420 ≈ 1.1 trillion policies. Trying them all isn't an option. We need something smarter, and the next 4 lessons build it.

Math refresher Probability distributions and transition tables

A probability distribution is a list of outcomes with probabilities that add up to 1 (something must happen). For "High battery + search":

sas'P(s'|s,a)r
HighsearchHigh0.8+2
HighsearchLow0.2+2

0.8 + 0.2 = 1 ✓. In code, the whole MDP is just a nested dictionary. This is the exact format Gymnasium's FrozenLake uses, and the one you'll build in Lab 2:

# P[state][action] = list of (probability, next_state, reward)
P = {
  "High": {"search":   [(0.8, "High", 2), (0.2, "Low", 2)],
           "wait":     [(1.0, "High", 1)]},
  "Low":  {"search":   [(0.6, "Low", 2), (0.4, "High", -3)],
           "wait":     [(1.0, "Low", 1)],
           "recharge": [(1.0, "High", 0)]},
}

If you know this table, you know everything about the world. Having it is called "having a model". In this phase we assume we do. In Phase 3 we won't.

Deterministic world

Each action has exactly one outcome (probability 1). Chess, and our gridworld with a normal floor.

🎲

Stochastic world

Actions have several possible outcomes. The slippery floor, the recycling robot, real robots, games with dice.

🗺️ Grid environments: our gridworld as an MDP

Since Phase 0 you've been driving a robot around a small grid. Here is that exact world written down as the 5 ingredients. Every grid environment in RL (and there are many) is described the same way.

IngredientOur gridworld
S statesEach open cell (row, col): 25 cells minus 3 walls = 22 states. Two of them, 💎 and 🔥, are terminal: the episode ends there.
A actions↑ → ↓ ←, the same 4 in every cell.
P transitionsNormal floor: you move where you point. Walking into a wall or the edge = you stay put. Slippery floor: 20% of the time your action is replaced by a random one.
R rewards−1 for an ordinary step, +10 for stepping onto 💎, −10 for stepping into 🔥. The +10 and −10 replace that step's −1 (they aren't added to it). (Plus a 50-step time limit so episodes always end.)
γ discountWe use 0.9 or 1, depending on the lesson.
✏️ Worked example: one row of P

The robot is at the start (4,0), bottom-left, on the slippery floor, and presses ↑. What can happen?

  • 80%: the action works → (3,0).
  • 20%: a random action replaces it, each direction 5%: ↑ → (3,0), → → (4,1), ↓ or ← → bump the edge and stay at (4,0).

Add them up: P((3,0)) = 0.80 + 0.05 = 0.85, P((4,1)) = 0.05, P((4,0)) = 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10. Total 1.00 ✓. That's one row of the rulebook; the full P has one such row for each of the 20 × 4 (non-terminal cell, action) pairs.

Why researchers love grid worlds: they're small enough to compute the exact right answer (the next lessons do), fast enough to run thousands of experiments on a laptop, and easy to see. Classic ones you'll meet: FrozenLake (Gymnasium's slippery lake), Cliff Walking (Lesson 3.3), MiniGrid (key-and-door puzzles), and DeepMind's AI Safety Gridworlds (2017), tiny worlds that test whether an agent avoids side effects or lets itself be switched off.

🌍 Where it's used
An MDP for road repair 🏭 In production · 1980 Arizona DOT: statewide pavement management

How: state = condition of each mile of highway · action = which repair, if any · P = how roads wear under traffic and weather · reward = −cost, while meeting quality standards.

Why this method: road wear is random and slow, so a fixed schedule over- or under-repairs. Planning with wear probabilities saved $14 million in year one, almost a third of the preservation budget.

Building the model P from data 🏭 In production · 2022 Amazon: Deep Inventory Management

How: state = stock on hand, orders still in transit, demand signals · action = how many units to buy this week · reward = cash flow (sales minus costs), discounted by γ.

Why this method: orders arrive after a random delay, so today's buy shapes later weeks. Amazon built P from historical data; a 26-week trial on 10,000 products cut stock ~12% without revenue loss.

🤯 Fun fact

Richard Bellman's 1957 paper that formalized all this was titled "A Markovian Decision Process". That's where the name comes from. MDPs are now used far beyond AI: inventory management, medical treatment planning, power grids, and choosing when to replace machines in a factory.

🌉 LLM bridge · ChatGPT as an MDP
SEvery possible text: prompt + tokens so far
AThe vocabulary (~100,000+ tokens)
PDeterministic and trivial: the next state is just the old text with the chosen token appended
R0 for every token, then a score at the end (reward model, unit tests, or "is the answer correct?")
γUsually 1

The dynamics are the easiest part. What's hard is the size: with 100,000 tokens and 1,000-token answers there are 100,0001000 = 105000 possible states (each of 1,000 positions can hold any of 100,000 tokens, so you multiply 100,000 by itself 1,000 times) (the universe has about 1080 atoms). That's why LLM RL relies on sampling and neural networks rather than tables (Phases 3–5).

Check yourself ✅

1. What does P(s' | s, a) tell you?
P is the world's rule for what happens next. (The probability the agent picks an action is the policy π(a|s), which is the agent's rule.)
2. In the recycling robot, which outcomes of "Low + search" must add up to 1?
For each (state, action) pair, its outcome probabilities form one distribution: 0.6 + 0.4 = 1.
3. For an LLM, what's the hard part of the MDP?
Transitions are trivial (append a token). The challenges are size (105000 states) and sparse, delayed reward, which is why credit assignment (Lesson 0.1's "which of my steps deserved the credit or blame?") is so hard for LLMs.
📝 TL;DR
  • MDP = (S, A, P, R, γ): the complete rulebook of an RL problem.
  • P(s'|s,a) depends only on the current state and action (Markov).
  • Knowing P and R = having a model. In code: P[s][a] = [(prob, s', r), …].
  • Policies multiply fast (420 for a tiny grid), so we need a smarter tool than trying them all: values.
