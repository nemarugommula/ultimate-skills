Phase 6 · Lesson 3

Model-based RL: Dyna, MuZero, Dreamer

Learn how the world works, then practise in your imagination. It's far cheaper than learning everything the hard way.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • A model = the world's rulebook: P(s'|s,a) (where you land) and R (what you get), like the recycling robot's table in Lesson 2.2.
  • Q-learning update: move Q(s,a) toward r + γ × max Q(s', ·) (Lesson 3.4).
🎯 Hook

In Lab 4, your DQN spent 50,000 real CartPole steps learning to balance a pole, and between steps it did no thinking at all. A real robot would wear out first. Compare a chess player: before a big match, a chess player replays positions in their head and imagines "if I play this, they'll play that…". They learn from games that never happened. In 2020, DeepMind's MuZero mastered Go, chess, shogi and 57 Atari games by learning its own internal model of each game and planning inside it, without ever being told the rules.

💡 Big idea Model-free RL (everything since Phase 3) learns values or policies straight from experience. Model-based RL also learns a model, "if I do a in s, I'll probably get r and land in s'", and uses it to plan: generating extra, imagined experience for free.
🧠 Analogy: habits vs a map

Model-free learning is like knowing your way to work by habit: you've driven it so often that "turn left at the bakery" is automatic, but if a road closes you're lost. Model-based learning is like carrying a map: it takes effort to build and to read, but you can plan a new route instantly, even to places you've never been. Brains seem to use both (Lesson 6.8).

✏️ Worked example: learning a model is just counting

The recycling robot tried "search" with a High battery 10 times. It stayed High 8 times and dropped to Low 2 times, earning +2 each time. Its learned model: P(High | High, search) ≈ 8/10 = 0.8, P(Low | High, search) ≈ 0.2, reward ≈ +2. That's exactly the table from Lesson 2.2, recovered from experience. With a model in hand, the robot can "imagine" outcomes and improve its plan without spending any more battery.

Dyna-Q: the simplest version (Sutton, 1990)

for each real step:
    a = epsilon_greedy(Q, s); s2, r = env.step(a)
    Q[s][a] += alpha * (r + gamma * max(Q[s2]) - Q[s][a])      # 1. learn from real experience
    model[(s, a)] = (r, s2)                                       # 2. remember what happened
    for _ in range(n):                                            # 3. plan: replay imagined steps
        (ps, pa), (pr, ps2) = random_choice(model)
        Q[ps][pa] += alpha * (pr + gamma * max(Q[ps2]) - Q[ps][pa])

That's Q-learning plus "dream about past experiences n extra times per real step". Each real step now feeds n + 1 updates instead of 1. Dreamed updates are worth less than fresh experience (they only rehearse what you've already seen), but they cost no real steps.

Notice the model is tiny: it stores only the last outcome of each (s, a). In this deterministic maze, that is the model: the same move always lands in the same cell. In a slippery world, you'd store counts instead (as in the recycling-robot example above) and, when dreaming, sample s′ from them.

Modern model-based RL

🌳

AlphaZero (2017) → MuZero (2020)

AlphaZero was given the rules and planned with tree search ("if I play this, they might reply that, then I…", exploring the most promising branches; how it works is Lesson 6.3b). MuZero learns its own model and runs tree search inside it. The surprise: its model never learns to draw the board. It only learns whatever helps predict value, reward and the next good move.

💭

Dreamer (2020–2025)

Learns a world model from pixels and trains its policy entirely in imagination. DreamerV3 was the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data (published in Nature, 2025).

🌍

World models

Video-generating models such as DeepMind's Genie 3 (2025) create interactive worlds from a prompt. Researchers hope to use them as unlimited training grounds for agents.

⚠️ The catch: model errors get exploited

A planner will happily exploit mistakes in its model: "according to my model, walking through this wall gives +100!" (the same lesson as TD3's critic and RLHF's reward model). Good model-based methods plan only a few steps ahead, use ensembles of models (several models trained separately) and trust only plans the models agree on, or stay cautious where data is thin.

Why isn't everything model-based?

Suppose your model is wrong on just 1% of steps. A 100-step dream is error-free only 0.99100 ≈ 37% of the time, so about 63% of dreams contain at least one mistake, and every step after a mistake builds on a wrong state. Errors compound the longer you dream. Meanwhile, wherever real data is cheap (Atari emulators, fast physics simulators), model-free methods were simpler to get working and often ended up at least as good. Model-based RL wins when real experience is expensive.

Sim-to-real: the model everyone already uses

In practice, the most-used "model" isn't learned at all: it's a hand-built simulator. Robots are trained for millions of simulated hours, then sent into the real world. The gap between the two (the reality gap) has two famous fixes:

  • Domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017): randomize textures, lighting and camera angles wildly in simulation, so the real world looks like "just another variation". Their object detector, trained only on simulated images with non-realistic random textures, located real objects to within 1.5 cm, well enough for a robot to grasp them.
  • Teacher–student training (Lee et al., 2020, ANYmal): a "teacher" policy that can see the true terrain and friction in simulation trains a "student" that only feels its own body (joint angles and an inertial sensor). The student walked the ANYmal robot through mud, snow and rubble it never saw in training, with no real-world training.

Learned models plan by dreaming whole episodes. The next lesson (6.3b) plans differently: search a few moves ahead from the current position, right before each move.

Math refresher Sample efficiency

Sample efficiency = how much real experience you need to learn. It matters most when experience is expensive: a real robot that breaks, a self-driving car, a human user's time. Model-based methods trade compute (planning in imagination) for samples (real interactions).

🌍 Where it's used
A learned model, used to plan 🏭 In production · 2018 DeepMind: autonomous data-centre cooling at Google

How: every 5 minutes, state = readings from thousands of sensors · neural nets predict how each candidate action (cooling settings) changes future energy use · it picks the lowest-energy one (reward = −energy) that passes safety checks.

Why this method: you can't freely experiment on a live data centre. A model lets it compare settings in imagination and vet each against safety rules first. Savings reached about 30% on average.

A learned model + tree search (MuZero) 🏭 In production · 2022 DeepMind: MuZero compresses YouTube videos

How: state = the video encoded so far · action = the compression level (QP) for the next frame · reward = win or lose against its own past results: higher quality while staying within the size budget.

Why this method: how one frame's setting affects later frames is too tangled to write down; MuZero learns its own model and plans with it. About 4% smaller videos, on part of YouTube's traffic.

Training in imagination (Dreamer) 🧪 Research · 2022 DayDreamer: world models on real robots

How: a world model learns to predict the next state and reward from camera and joint readings; the policy practises inside those predictions, then sends real motor actions.

Why this method: no simulator, and real steps are slow, so each one is stretched into many dreamed ones. A four-legged robot learned to roll over, stand up and walk in 1 hour.

Sim-to-real, patched with real data 🧪 Research · 2023 Swift: a drone beats champion pilots (Nature)

How: state = position and gate corners, estimated from camera + motion sensor · action = total thrust and turning rates · reward = progress to the next gate + keeping it in view, minus crashing.

Why this method: you can't crash real drones millions of times. Train in simulation, then fit small correction models from about 50 seconds of real flight. It won 15 of 25 races against three champions.

🤯 Fun fact

Rats seem to do something like Dyna. Neuroscientists recording from the hippocampus found that during rest, rats replay recent paths through a maze, sometimes backwards from the reward (Foster & Wilson, 2006), and sometimes even paths they never actually took (Gupta et al., 2010). That looks a lot like replaying a learned model to plan.

🌉 LLM bridge · planning with a model you already have

For text, the "model" is trivial: append a token. The planning happens in the chain of thought, where reasoning models simulate "if I try this approach, then…" before answering. LLMs also act as world models for agents ("what will this shell command do?"), and some research trains agents in LLM-simulated environments to save real interactions. Dyna's trade of compute for experience is a big theme in modern AI.

Check yourself ✅

1. What does Dyna-Q add to Q-learning?
Remember (s, a) → (r, s'), then replay those memories n times per real step.
2. The main advantage of model-based RL?
Planning gets more out of every real sample. Final performance can be limited by model errors.
3. What's MuZero's key difference from AlphaZero?
MuZero plans with a learned model, so it works even where the rules aren't available (like Atari).
📝 TL;DR
  • Model-based = learn P and R (a model), then plan with imagined experience.
  • Dyna-Q: Q-learning + n replayed model steps per real step. Huge sample-efficiency gains.
  • MuZero (learned model + tree search), Dreamer (training in imagination), world models.
  • Beware model exploitation and compounding errors. Plan short, use ensembles, stay uncertain.
  • Simulators are the most-used model: cross the reality gap with domain randomization or teacher–student training.
