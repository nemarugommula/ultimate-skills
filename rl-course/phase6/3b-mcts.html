Planning by search: MCTS & AlphaZero

A value network gives you a hunch. Search checks the hunch by playing out the future in your head, right before you move.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • UCB: pick the option with the best "average + uncertainty bonus"; the bonus shrinks as you try it more (Lesson 1.4).
  • Policy iteration: evaluate your policy, improve it greedily, repeat until it stops changing (Lesson 2.5).
  • Value and policy networks: a neural network that reads a state and guesses its value or the action probabilities (Lessons 4.2–4.3, 5.1).
🎯 Hook

In Lab 4, your DQN picked every action from one glance at its Q-network: a hunch, never double-checked. Strong chess players don't play their first hunch. They check it a few moves deep. DeepMind's AlphaZero (2017) did the same: before every move it ran 800 quick "what if" simulations, guided by its network. Given only the rules, it reached superhuman play in chess, shogi and Go within 24 hours of self-play training.

💡 Big idea Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) plans at decision time. From the current position, it simulates many possible futures, spending most simulations on the moves that look best so far, while still checking the others. The move it visited most is the one it plays. The network's guesses steer the search, and the search corrects the network's guesses.
🧠 Analogy: a grandmaster's candidate moves

A grandmaster glances at the board and intuition suggests two or three candidate moves (that's the policy network's prior). Then they calculate: "if I go here, they go there, then I…", spending most of their thinking time on the most promising line but glancing at the others too. At the end of each line they judge the position by feel (that's the value network). The move that survives the most checking gets played.

Why search at all?

Say your value network rates a position at +0.6 ("probably winning"), but three moves later the opponent has a forced win. The network's +0.6 was an average over positions like this one; it can easily miss a specific trap. Search actually plays the trap out and sees the −1. A value function is a hunch about the future; search looks at the future, using the game's rules (or a learned model, as MuZero does in 6.3).

One simulation = four steps

👇

1. Select

Start at the current position and walk down the tree of moves you've already explored, at each step picking the move with the best PUCT score (below).

🌱

2. Expand

When you reach a position you haven't explored yet, add it to the tree and list its legal moves, each with a prior P from the policy network.

🔮

3. Evaluate

Guess how good the new position is. AlphaZero asks its value network. Classic MCTS plays random moves to the end of the game instead (a rollout).

⬆️

4. Back up

Walk back up the path: add 1 to each node's visit count N and add the result to its running total, flipping the sign at each level (a win for me is a loss for my opponent).

PUCT: UCB with a hunch

Selection uses a cousin of UCB (Lesson 1.4's "average + a bonus for being unsure"). Each move's score is how good it has looked so far, plus an exploration bonus. The bonus is bigger when the policy network likes the move (high P), grows slowly as the parent gets more visits, and shrinks as this move gets tried. In symbols (√ = square root, N(s) = visits to the current position, N(s, a) = visits to move a, c = an exploration knob, usually around 1–2):

score(a) = Q(s, a) + c · P(s, a) · √N(s) / (1 + N(s, a)) "average result of this move so far, plus a bonus for moves the network likes and search hasn't checked much"

Q starts at 0 for an unvisited move. With a flat prior (every P equal) this is basically UCB applied at every node of the tree: that version, called UCT, is what made MCTS famous. AlphaZero's twist is the P: the policy network tells search where to look first.

✏️ Worked example: one PUCT selection by hand

c = 1. The current position has been visited 9 times, so √N(s) = √9 = 3. Three moves:

movevisits Naverage Qprior Pscore = Q + 1 · P · 3 / (1 + N)
A6+0.50.50.5 + 0.5 × 3/7 ≈ 0.71
B3+0.20.30.2 + 0.3 × 3/4 ≈ 0.43
C00 (untried)0.20 + 0.2 × 3/1 = 0.60

Search picks A. Suppose A keeps averaging 0.5. After 3 more visits (A: N = 9; position: 12 visits, √12 ≈ 3.46), A's score is 0.5 + 0.5 × 3.46/10 ≈ 0.67 while C's is 0.2 × 3.46/1 ≈ 0.69. Now C finally gets its first look. Every visit shrinks a move's own bonus and grows everyone else's, so nothing is ignored forever.

🎮 Playground · MCTS plays "race to 10"

Two players take turns adding 1, 2 or 3 to a running total. Whoever makes it exactly 10 wins. The total is 3 and it's 🤖's turn. (Spoiler you can check: reaching 6 wins, because whatever the opponent adds, you add the rest to make 10.) There's no network here: P is even (⅓ each) and evaluation is a random rollout. Bars = share of simulations spent on each first move.

With the even prior, the visits pile onto +3 fast: in our test over 200 runs, +3 was the most-visited move in 92% of runs after 100 simulations and in all of them after 200. Tick the misleading prior and search wastes early simulations on +1 (only 28% of runs had +3 on top after 100 simulations), but the bad results flow back up and the bonus pushes it to check the alternatives: 93% after 500, and all 200 runs after 1,000. A wrong hunch slows search down; it doesn't fool it.

AlphaZero: search teaches the network, the network guides search

  1. Play a game against yourself, choosing every move by MCTS (800 simulations each, guided by the current network).
  2. Train the network on every position from that game: the policy learns to imitate the search's visit shares (if search spent 70% of visits on move A, the target for A is 0.7), and the value learns to predict who actually won.
  3. Repeat, with the better network making the next search stronger.

Search is a better policy than the raw network, because it double-checked. Training makes the network a cheap copy of that better policy, so the next search starts from better hunches. In symbols (πsearch = visit shares, z = final result, +1 win / −1 loss, p and v = the network's outputs):

loss = (z − v)² − Σa πsearch(a) · log p(a) "predict the winner, and put high probability where search spent its visits"

That's policy iteration from Lesson 2.5 (evaluate, then improve greedily, repeat) with a neural network: search is the improvement step, and training locks the improvement in.

Math refresher Why play the most-visited move, not the highest Q?

A move tried twice can have a lucky Q of +1. That's the winner's curse from Lesson 4.4: the max of noisy estimates is too optimistic. Visit counts are safer, because PUCT only keeps returning to a move if it keeps looking good, so many visits = good and well checked. During self-play training, AlphaZero picks moves in proportion to their visits, which adds variety to its games; in real matches it plays the most-visited move.

Search in the wild

🧮

AlphaTensor (Nature, 2022)

Turned "find a matrix multiplication recipe" into a one-player game and played it AlphaZero-style. For 4×4 matrices with entries mod 2, it found a recipe with 47 multiplications, beating the 49 from applying Strassen's 1969 method twice.

🔢

AlphaDev (Nature, 2023)

Played "write a faster assembly routine" as a game. Its sorting routines for short lists were merged into LLVM's libc++, one of the main standard C++ libraries: the first change to those routines in over a decade.

📐

AlphaProof (Nature, 2025)

AlphaZero-style RL inside the Lean proof checker, which verifies every step. Together with AlphaGeometry 2 it scored 28/42 at the 2024 Math Olympiad, a silver-medal score, though some problems took days of computation.

🌳

MuZero (2020)

Same search, but with no rulebook: it learns its own model of the game and searches inside that (Lesson 6.3).

🌍 Where it's used
Search guided by policy and value networks 📜 History · 2016 AlphaGo (Nature), then 4–1 against Lee Sedol

How: state = the board · action = a move · reward = +1 win, −1 loss. A policy net (first trained on human games) gives the priors P; a value net judges positions; search combines them.

Why this method: Go has too many moves to check them all. The priors focus the search on a few candidates, and the value net judges positions without relying only on random playouts.

AlphaZero-style search over programs 🏭 In production · 2023 DeepMind: AlphaDev's faster sorting routines

How: state = the assembly program so far + the CPU's contents · action = add one instruction · reward = sorts correctly, and runs fast.

Why this method: one wrong instruction breaks the program, and the space of sequences is enormous; search checks moves ahead before committing. The routines in libc++ now run trillions of times a day.

MCTS for chemistry 🧪 Research · 2018 Segler et al.: planning chemical syntheses (Nature)

How: state = the molecules still to be made · action = undo one known reaction, splitting a molecule into simpler ones · reward = success when only readily available starting chemicals remain.

Why this method: each molecule splits many ways. A policy net trained on 12 million published reactions guides the search: 30× faster, twice as many molecules solved; chemists couldn't tell its routes from published ones.

🤯 Fun fact

The name "Monte Carlo tree search" was coined in 2006 by Rémi Coulom, whose Go program Crazy Stone won the 9×9 Go gold medal at that year's Computer Olympiad in Turin. The same year, Kocsis and Szepesvári published UCT, the "UCB in every node" rule. A decade later, the same family of ideas was inside AlphaGo.

🌉 LLM bridge · test-time search

For an LLM, the "moves" are reasoning steps and the "rules" are trivial (append text), so you can search over answers too. Best-of-N (Lesson 7.7) is the simplest search: sample N answers, keep the one a judge scores highest. Verifier-guided search (Lesson 8.5) goes further: a process reward model scores partial solutions, playing the value network's role, so you can expand promising steps and prune bad ones. Spending more compute this way at answer time is called test-time compute. And AlphaZero's loop (search finds better answers, train the model to produce them directly) is the same shape as expert iteration in 7.7.

Check yourself ✅

1. In PUCT, what does the prior P(s, a) from the policy network do?
P scales the bonus. Q still records what simulations actually found, and the final move is the most-visited one.
2. What is AlphaZero's policy network trained to imitate?
Search improves on the network's hunch; training distills that improvement back into the network. That's policy iteration with a neural net.
3. In the playground, the misleading prior bets 80% on +1. Why does search still end up preferring +3?
The prior only shapes the bonus. Real results flow back through Q, and the √N(s) term forces neglected moves to be checked eventually.
📝 TL;DR
  • MCTS plans at decision time: select (PUCT) → expand → evaluate → back up, many times; play the most-visited move.
  • PUCT = Q + c · P · √N(s) / (1 + N(s,a)): UCB with a prior from a policy network.
  • AlphaZero: search makes a better policy; train the policy to imitate visit shares and the value to predict the winner; repeat (policy iteration).
  • In the wild: AlphaTensor, AlphaDev, AlphaProof, MuZero. For LLMs: best-of-N, verifier-guided search, test-time compute.
