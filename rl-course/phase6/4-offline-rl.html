Offline RL: learning from a fixed log

No more trial and error, only a dataset someone else collected. The main danger is trusting guesses about things the data never showed.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Q-learning's max: the target uses the highest Q-value of the next state (Lesson 3.4).
  • Maximization bias: the max of noisy guesses is too optimistic (Lesson 4.4).
🎯 Hook

A hospital has 10 years of records: patient state, treatment given, outcome. Could RL learn a better treatment policy from them? Experimenting on patients isn't an option. You only get the log. Recommendation systems, self-driving fleets and robot logs have the same constraint, and so does every fixed preference dataset used to align LLMs.

💡 Big idea Offline RL learns a policy from a fixed dataset with no new interaction. The core danger is distribution shift: Q-learning's max happily picks actions the data rarely or never showed, where the network's estimate is just a guess, often a wildly optimistic one. The fix is pessimism: don't trust what you haven't seen.
🧠 Analogy: learning medicine from old hospital records

You have 10 years of records: which treatment each patient got and how they did. Treatment A was given 600 times, with solid results. Treatment Z appears twice, and both patients happened to recover. A naive learner concludes "Z is a miracle cure!" A sensible doctor says "two cases tells me almost nothing", and you can't run new trials to check. That's offline RL: distribution shift means your new policy wants to go where the data is thin, and you can't go back and collect more.

Why is this worse than the overestimation you met in Lab 4? Online, if DQN overrates treatment Z, it tries Z, sees the disappointing result, and that new transition in the buffer pulls the estimate back down. Overestimates get tested and corrected. Offline, that feedback loop is cut: nobody ever tries Z, so the overrated estimate is never checked, and the greedy policy keeps choosing it.

🏥 In the wild: the "AI Clinician" (Komorowski et al., Nature Medicine 2018) learned sepsis treatment policies (IV fluid and vasopressor doses) from tens of thousands of ICU records. A 2019 critique argued it could recommend harmful doses, partly because its evaluation gave almost no weight to the cases where it disagreed with the doctors, which are exactly the cases the log can't tell you about. That's this lesson's problem in a real hospital.

🎮 Playground · Choosing from a log

One situation, 10 possible actions. The dataset (a cautious doctor's records) contains many examples of a few actions and almost none of the rest. Bars = estimated value from the data (rarely-tried actions have wild estimates). Dashed = true value (hidden from the learner). Number under each bar = how many times it appears in the log.

With α = 0 (plain "pick the max"), the learner often picks a rarely-seen action whose estimate is high by luck. Its true value is usually worse. Turn up α (score = estimate − α/√(count+1)) and it sticks to actions the data actually supports. In our offline test over 20,000 datasets (the 🧪 button runs 1,000, so its numbers wobble a little), the average true value of the chosen action rose from 0.46 (α = 0) to 0.64 (α = 2) and 0.67 (α = 3).

✏️ Worked example: plain max vs a pessimistic score

The log shows action A tried 600 times (average outcome 0.5) and action Z tried 2 times (average 0.9).

  • Plain max: pick Z (0.9 > 0.5). Based on two lucky samples.
  • Pessimistic score = average − 1/√(times tried): A = 0.5 − 1/√600 ≈ 0.46; Z = 0.9 − 1/√2 ≈ 0.19. Pick A.

Pessimism says: "Z might be great, but I can't check, so I'll trust what's well supported." That's the spirit of CQL and friends.

Before you deploy: off-policy evaluation

You've learned a new policy from the log. Is it better than what the doctors did? You can't run it on patients to find out, so you estimate its value from the old records by re-weighting them. Example: the doctors gave treatment B to 20% of patients; your new policy would give it to 50%. So each logged B patient counts 0.5/0.2 = 2.5×, and each A patient 0.5/0.8 = 0.625×. If A patients had an average outcome of 0.5 and B patients 0.9, the estimate is 0.8 × 0.625 × 0.5 + 0.2 × 2.5 × 0.9 = 0.25 + 0.45 = 0.70, exactly the new policy's value (0.5 × 0.5 + 0.5 × 0.9). This is importance weighting, with the same new/old probability ratio PPO clips (Lesson 5.6):

value(πnew) ≈ average over the log of [ πnew(a|s) / πold(a|s) ] × outcome "count each record more if the new policy likes its action more than the old one did"

The catch is the same as everywhere in this lesson: if the new policy loves an action the doctors almost never chose (0.5/0.01 = 50×), a handful of records dominate the estimate, and it gets very noisy. Doubly robust estimators (Dudík et al., 2011) reduce that noise by combining a learned model's guess of the outcome with the weighted correction.

The main offline RL methods

⬇️

CQL (2020)

Conservative Q-Learning: add a loss that pushes Q-values down for actions not in the data and up for actions in the data, which gives pessimistic, safe estimates.

🚧

IQL (2021)

Implicit Q-Learning: never evaluates unseen actions at all. It learns values only from dataset actions, then extracts a policy by imitating the better-than-average ones.

🧾

Decision Transformer (2021)

Treat RL as sequence modeling: train a transformer on (return-to-go, state, action) sequences, where return-to-go = "the reward still to be collected from here". Then prompt it with "give me actions worth +50 in total" and it imitates the trajectories that earned that much. An early sign of RL and LLM ideas merging.

Math refresher Why rare actions get extreme estimates

An estimate from n samples has noise of about σ/√n (Phase 0 recap). With n = 1 the noise is as big as the signal. Among many rarely-seen actions, one of them will look great by luck: the winner's curse from Lesson 4.4. Online RL can go try that action and find out. Offline RL can't, so it has to be pessimistic.

🌍 Where it's used
Learning from logs + off-policy evaluation 🏭 In production · 2018 Facebook's Horizon: which notifications to send

How: state = features of the person and the candidate notification · action = send or drop · reward = later activity on Facebook, minus a penalty for each notification sent.

Why this method: the model is retrained daily from logs, never a simulator. Re-weighting logged outcomes (doubly robust estimates) predicted its value before it replaced the older supervised system.

Pessimism (CQL) on someone else's robot data 🧪 Research · 2022 PTR: pre-training robots with offline RL

How: state = camera image · action = arm and gripper motion · reward = +1 at the end of successful demos. CQL pushes Q down on actions the logs don't show.

Why this method: the big dataset (toy kitchens, many tasks) can't be re-collected; plain Q-learning would overrate unseen actions. A real robot then learned new tasks from as few as 10 demos.

Offline RL from hospital records 🧪 Research · 2018 The AI Clinician for sepsis (Nature Medicine)

How: state = one of 750 patient-state clusters from ICU records · action = doses of IV fluids and vasopressors · reward = survival at 90 days.

Why this method: experimenting on patients is off the table. It was limited to doses clinicians often chose (stay near the data) and scored by weighted importance sampling. The authors say it isn't ready for clinical use.

🤯 Fun fact

A 2019 study (Fujimoto et al., "Off-Policy Deep RL without Exploration") showed that standard off-policy algorithms like DDPG can fail completely when trained on a fixed dataset, even a dataset of expert behavior. That surprising result, caused by extrapolation error, helped launch offline RL as its own field.

🌉 LLM bridge · offline vs online alignment

DPO (Phase 7) learns from a fixed preference dataset: offline. Several 2024 studies found that online or iterative variants, which generate fresh answers from the current model and get them judged, beat purely offline training. The reason is the one in this lesson: the model drifts away from the data it was trained on, into regions where the offline signal says nothing. It's one reason RLHF and RLVR remain on-policy.

Check yourself ✅

1. What makes offline RL hard?
Without the ability to try those actions, errors in their estimates never get corrected.
2. What does CQL do?
Conservative estimates mean the policy prefers what the data supports.
3. Why might iterative or online DPO beat offline DPO?
It's the distribution shift problem again: on-policy data covers where the policy actually is.
📝 TL;DR
  • Offline RL: learn from a fixed log, no interaction. Needed for healthcare, robot logs and recommendations.
  • Danger: distribution shift and extrapolation error on unseen actions. Online, overestimates get tried and corrected; offline they don't.
  • Off-policy evaluation: re-weight logged outcomes by πnew/πold (doubly robust estimators cut the noise).
  • Fix: pessimism (CQL), avoiding unseen actions (IQL), or sequence modeling (Decision Transformer).
  • Same tension in LLMs: offline DPO vs on-policy RLHF/RLVR.
