Phase 6 · Lesson 7

Imitation & inverse RL

Learning from an expert's examples instead of from rewards, and why copying alone breaks down.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Supervised learning = learning from examples with correct answers (Lesson 0.1).
  • Policy = a rule for which action to take in each state; reward function = the score the environment gives (Lesson 0.2).
🎯 Hook

In 1989, Dean Pomerleau's ALVINN, a tiny neural network with a 30×32-pixel camera input, learned to steer a van along roads at Carnegie Mellon just by watching a human drive. It was one of the first self-driving neural networks. It also showed the classic weakness of copying: the human never drove badly, so the network never learned how to recover.

💡 Big idea Behavior cloning (BC) = supervised learning on expert (state → action) pairs. It's simple and works surprisingly often, but small mistakes take the agent to states the expert never visited, where it has no idea what to do, so the mistakes compound. DAgger fixes this by asking the expert to label the states the learner actually ends up in.
🎮 Playground · Keep the car on the road

Lane position over time (0 = lane center, ±1 = off the road). The road curves, which pushes the car to the right, and steering is noisy. The expert always steers back toward the center. The learner only knows what's in its training data.

Our test over many trials: the expert crashed 0% of the time, behavior cloning about 13%, and DAgger about 0.1%. BC's policy is fine near the center, where the expert spent its time. Once noise pushes it to the edge, it reaches positions with no training data, steers nowhere useful, and drifts off.

Why errors compound

A cloned policy with a small per-step error rate ε doesn't just make ε mistakes. Each mistake moves it into unfamiliar territory where it makes more mistakes. Ross & Bagnell showed the expected cost can grow with the square of the episode length (∝ εT²), rather than linearly. Think of a snowball: every small slip puts the car somewhere slightly stranger, where slips are more likely, so errors pile up faster and faster. DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) brings it back to linear:

data = expert_demos()
for round in range(N):
    policy = train_supervised(data)
    visited = run(policy)                       # go where the LEARNER goes
    data += [(s, expert_action(s)) for s in visited]   # ask the expert: "what should I do here?"

Why it works: the learner is now trained on the states it actually visits, mistakes included, so its training inputs finally match its test inputs. The price: the expert must label states they aren't controlling ("the car is drifting left; what would you do?") without the feel of actually driving. That's tiring and unnatural for people, so DAgger is rare with human experts. It's easy when the expert is a program, such as a planner or a privileged teacher policy in simulation (like 6.3's teacher–student training).

✏️ Worked example: how small errors add up

A cloned driver makes a mistake on 1% of steps. Over a 100-step drive, the chance of no mistake at all is 0.99100 ≈ 37%. So 63% of drives contain at least one slip, and after a slip the car is somewhere the expert never was, where the error rate is much higher than 1%. That's the snowball.

DAgger adds expert labels for exactly those off-course situations, so the learner's error rate stays low even after a slip.

Inverse RL: learn the reward, not the actions

Behavior cloning copies what the expert did. Inverse RL (IRL) asks why: "what reward would make this behavior the smart thing to do?" Then it runs ordinary RL on that inferred reward.

🧠 Analogy: figuring out what someone cares about

You watch a friend walk to a café. There's a short path along a busy road, and a longer path through the park. They take the park every time. You don't conclude "they like turning left at the fountain" (that's copying actions). You conclude "they dislike traffic more than they mind a few extra minutes". That's inverse RL: infer the values behind the behavior. And once you know their values, you can predict what they'd do in a city they've never visited.

🎮 Playground · Be the reward detective

The expert (blue dashed path) walked from 🤖 to 💎 the long way, staying away from the lava. Your job: guess the reward they're optimizing. Each step costs c, and each step on a cell next to lava costs an extra k ("nervousness"). Orange = the best path under your guess. Does your guess explain the expert?

Two lessons from the detective game:

  • Many rewards explain the same behavior. Any nervousness above a threshold fits the demo equally well, and the silly "reward = 0 everywhere" fits every behavior (all paths are equally good, so the expert's is "optimal" too). Andrew Ng and Stuart Russell pointed out this ambiguity when they introduced IRL in 2000. More demonstrations in varied situations, and tie-breaking principles, narrow it down.
  • The inferred reward transfers. Once you know "they're nervous near lava", you can predict their path in a brand-new map, which copied actions can't do.
🚁

Apprenticeship learning (2004–2010)

Pieter Abbeel and Andrew Ng's methods infer a reward whose optimal behavior matches the expert's. Stanford's autonomous helicopter learned aerobatic stunts, such as flips and rolls, from an expert pilot's demonstrations this way.

🚕

Maximum-entropy IRL (2008)

Breaks the ambiguity with a principle: among rewards that explain the data, prefer the one that assumes the expert is otherwise as random as possible (no extra hidden preferences). Ziebart and colleagues used it to learn Pittsburgh taxi drivers' route preferences from GPS traces.

🎭

GAIL (2016)

Skips writing down a reward at all. One network plays detective ("expert or imitation?"), and the agent earns reward only when the detective can't tell its behavior apart from the expert's. As the detective gets sharper, the imitation must get closer.

🦾

Imitation + RL today

Behavior cloning on human demonstrations is the workhorse of robot learning. The frontier adds RL on top: Waymo added an RL loss to imitation of 100k+ miles of driving (BC-SAC) and cut failures on the hardest scenarios by over 38%. Physical Intelligence's π*0.6 (Nov 2025) adds RL from the robot's own attempts plus human corrections; the company reports more than 2× throughput on some of its hardest tasks (espresso, laundry, boxes).

Math refresher Distribution shift, again

Supervised learning assumes test inputs look like training inputs. In sequential decision making, your own actions choose your next input. A slightly different policy visits slightly different states, and the gap grows over time. It's the same root cause as offline RL's problems (Lesson 6.4).

🌍 Where it's used
Behavior cloning + made-up mistakes 🧪 Research · 2018 Waymo's ChauffeurNet

How: state = a top-down drawing of the road, traffic lights and nearby cars · action = the car's path for the next few moments · expert = 30 million real driving examples (about 60 days).

Why this method: plain cloning wasn't enough even with 30 million examples. Shifting expert paths off course showed it drift-and-recover states (DAgger's idea without a live expert), plus penalties for collisions and leaving the road.

DAgger with a human pilot 🧪 Research · 2013 Ross et al.: a drone flies itself through a forest

How: state = features from one cheap camera image · action = left-right speed · the expert pilot labels the drone's own flights afterwards, watching the video slowed down.

Why this method: pilots steer around trees early, so plain demos never show near-misses. DAgger collected exactly those states. It avoided over 680 trees across more than 3 km of flights.

Inverse RL at planet scale 🧪 Research · 2023 Google: inverse RL for Google Maps routes

How: state = where you are on the road network, heading to a destination · action = the next road segment · demos = routes people actually travelled · learned reward per road segment.

Why this method: “fastest route” misses what travellers actually value. Inferring the reward from real trips matched their routes 16–24% more often, and won in live Google Maps A/B tests.

🤯 Fun fact

To teach ALVINN to recover, Pomerleau generated synthetic training images showing the road shifted and rotated, as if the van had drifted, each labeled with the correcting steering command. It's an early version of the same fix as DAgger: show the learner off-course states and what to do there.

🌉 LLM bridge · SFT is behavior cloning

Supervised fine-tuning on human-written answers is behavior cloning on text: copy the expert, token by token. LLMs inherit its problems. During training the model always continues from perfect text, but at generation time it continues from its own earlier tokens, mistakes included (researchers call this exposure bias). It never practised recovering from its own errors. That's reason #3 from Lesson 0.4 for why RL after SFT helps: RL trains on the model's own outputs. And learning a reward from human comparisons (Phase 7's reward models) is a practical form of inverse RL.

Check yourself ✅

1. Why does behavior cloning drift off course?
Distribution shift: the learner's own mistakes change what it sees next.
2. What does DAgger collect?
Training data gets matched to where the learner actually goes, including its mistakes.
Bonus. In the detective game, why does "reward = 0 everywhere" technically explain the expert?
IRL's ambiguity problem: many rewards (including useless ones) are consistent with the same behavior. Extra data and principles like maximum entropy are needed to pick a sensible one.
3. Which LLM training stage is basically behavior cloning?
SFT copies expert text. RL stages train on the model's own samples, which addresses exposure bias.
📝 TL;DR
  • Behavior cloning = supervised learning on expert actions. Simple, but errors compound (∝ εT²).
  • DAgger: have the expert label the learner's own states, so training matches test and the cost drops back to ∝ εT. Awkward with human experts, easy with programmatic ones.
  • Today: behavior cloning plus an RL loss or RL from the robot's own experience (BC-SAC, π*0.6).
  • Inverse RL / GAIL: infer the reward or match expert behavior, which generalizes better.
  • SFT = behavior cloning for text, with exposure bias. RL on the model's own outputs fixes this.
