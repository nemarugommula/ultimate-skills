Phase 6 · Lesson 8

RL in the brain: dopamine & the striatum

The TD error you computed in Phase 3 turned out to be written in the firing of real neurons. Here's what neuroscience found, and where the analogy stops.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • TD error δ = reward + value of the next moment − value of this moment: "did things turn out better or worse than I expected?" (Lesson 3.2).
  • Actor-critic: a critic predicts value and computes δ; an actor makes actions more likely when δ > 0 (Lesson 5.4).
  • Model-free vs model-based: habits learned by trial and error vs planning with a map of the world (Lesson 6.3).
🎯 Hook

In the early 1990s, Wolfram Schultz recorded single dopamine neurons in monkeys. A light came on, and a few seconds later a drop of juice arrived. At first the neurons fired when the juice arrived. After training, they fired when the light came on and stayed quiet at the juice. And when the juice was secretly withheld, they went silent at the exact moment it should have come. In 1997, Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague showed this is precisely what a TD error does.

💡 Big idea Bursts of dopamine don't mean "this is pleasant". They mean "better than expected": a reward prediction error, like δ. Dopamine neurons broadcast that signal to the striatum (a structure deep in the brain that helps choose actions), where it strengthens whatever connections led to the good surprise. That's a biological actor-critic.
🧠 Analogy: your brain's surprise meter

The first time a friend surprises you with pizza, you're thrilled (big positive surprise). If they bring pizza every Friday, by the third Friday you feel the excitement when you see their car pull up, not when the box opens: the car became the predictor. And the Friday they show up empty-handed feels worse than an ordinary day with no pizza at all. Excitement moves to the earliest reliable clue, and a missing expected reward hurts. That's exactly the dopamine pattern.

🎮 Playground · Rebuild Schultz's experiment with TD learning

Each trial: a light comes on at time 4, juice arrives at time 14. A TD learner (Lesson 3.2) learns how much reward to expect at each moment after the light. The plot shows its TD error δ, the model's "dopamine", across one trial. Before the light, nothing predicts it, so it always arrives as a surprise.

Slide from trial 1 to trial 60: the burst moves from the juice back to the light. Then tick "withhold the juice" on a late trial: a dip below zero appears exactly when the juice was due. Those are the three classic findings, reproduced by the one-line TD update. (One caveat: in between, this simple model shows a bump creeping gradually backward from the juice to the light over trials. Classic recordings didn't clearly show that creeping bump; a 2022 mouse study (Amo et al., Nature Neuroscience) did report a gradual backward shift, so the timing details are still debated.)

✏️ Worked example: the three findings as three δs

After training, the learner expects 1 unit of juice once the light is on: V(after light) = 1. Before the light, it expects nothing: V = 0. With γ = 1:

  • Light appears: δ = 0 + V(after light) − V(before) = 0 + 1 − 0 = +1 → burst at the light.
  • Juice arrives as expected: δ = 1 (juice) + 0 (trial over) − 1 (expected) = 0 → no burst.
  • Juice withheld: δ = 0 + 0 − 1 = −1 → dip below baseline.

Two pathways: "Go" and "No-Go"

Inside the striatum, two groups of neurons respond to dopamine in opposite ways. Roughly: the "Go" pathway learns from positive surprises ("do that again") and the "No-Go" pathway learns from negative ones ("don't do that"). In a well-known 2004 study (Frank, Seeberger and O'Reilly), people with Parkinson's disease, which destroys dopamine neurons, learned better from mistakes than from successes when off their medication, and the reverse when on dopamine-boosting medication. Changing the dopamine level changed which kind of feedback they learned from, as the RL model predicts.

Math refresher The same update, in neurons

In code (Lesson 3.2): V[s] += alpha * delta, and in actor-critic the actor's weights change by α × δ × (how much that connection was active). Neuroscientists call the biological version a three-factor learning rule: a connection changes when (1) the input neuron fired, (2) the output neuron fired, and (3) dopamine (the δ signal) arrived shortly after. Factors 1 and 2 say "this connection was involved"; factor 3 says "and it turned out better (or worse) than expected".

⚠️ Where the analogy stops
  • Dopamine also relates to movement, motivation, novelty and effort, not only prediction errors.
  • "Ventral = critic, dorsal = actor" is a useful simplification, not a wiring diagram.
  • The brain is not running DQN or PPO. The deep, shared idea is learning from prediction errors.

🌍 Where it's used
Dopamine ≈ TD error, many at once 🧪 Research · 2020 Dabney et al.: a distributional code for value (Nature)

How: mice got water rewards of random sizes (0.1 to 20 μL). Each dopamine neuron's burst behaved like its own δ, but some neurons were optimistic and others pessimistic about what was coming.

Why this method: with one shared TD error, every neuron would switch from dip to burst at the same reward size. They didn't, which fits distributional RL (Lesson 4.4): together they encode the whole spread.

Go / No-Go learning in Parkinson's disease 🧪 Research · 2004 Frank et al.: ‘By carrot or by stick’ (Science)

How: patients learned which symbols pay off more often · in the model, positive δ (dopamine bursts) train the “Go” pathway and negative δ (dips) train “No-Go”.

Why this method: the model predicted that low dopamine favors learning from bad outcomes, and medication flips that. Patients off medication avoided bad choices better; on medication they chased good ones better.

Model-based vs model-free in psychiatry 🧪 Research · 2016 Gillan et al.: compulsivity and goal-directed control (eLife)

How: a two-step choice game separates planning with a model (“this choice leads there”) from repeating whatever was just rewarded. Fitting an RL model gives each person a planning score.

Why this method: questionnaires alone can't measure how someone learns. In nearly 2,000 people online, weaker planning tracked a compulsivity dimension, not any single diagnosis like OCD.

🤯 Fun fact · dopamine isn't the "pleasure chemical"

Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson found that rats whose dopamine was depleted still made "tasty!" facial reactions to sugar, but would no longer work to get it. Dopamine seems to drive wanting and learning, not liking. One influential computational model of addiction (Redish, 2004) builds on the RL view: some drugs raise dopamine directly, creating a "better than expected" signal that never shrinks no matter how well the reward is predicted, so the learned value of the drug keeps climbing.

🌉 Connecting the dots

The same "reality minus expectation" signal runs through this whole course: the bandit update (1.2), TD learning (3.2), advantages and actor-critic (5.3–5.4), and GRPO's "better than the group average" (8.3). The idea was discovered in machines first (Sutton's TD learning, 1988) and then found in brains. It's one of the best examples of AI and neuroscience informing each other.

Check yourself ✅

1. After training, juice reliably follows a light. When do dopamine neurons burst?
The juice is fully predicted, so δ = 0 there. The light arrives unpredictably and announces juice, so the surprise moves to the light.
2. The juice is expected but doesn't come. What does the TD model predict for dopamine at that moment?
δ = 0 (no reward) + 0 − V(expected juice) < 0: worse than expected.
3. In the actor-critic picture of the striatum, what plays the role of the critic's teaching signal?
Dopamine carries δ to the striatum, where it adjusts both value predictions and action choices.
📝 TL;DR
  • Dopamine bursts ≈ TD error: "better than expected". Predicted rewards cause no burst; missing rewards cause a dip.
  • Dopamine teaches the striatum: roughly, ventral ≈ critic, dorsal ≈ actor; Go/No-Go pathways learn from positive/negative surprises.
  • Habits vs planning ≈ model-free vs model-based; hippocampal replay ≈ experience replay.
  • A powerful analogy with limits: dopamine does more than δ, and brains don't run PPO.
