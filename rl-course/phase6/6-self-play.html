Phase 6 · Lesson 6

Self-play & multi-agent RL

The best sparring partner is a copy of yourself that keeps getting stronger, as long as you avoid going in circles.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Rock-paper-scissors and Nash equilibrium: the unexploitable strategy is ⅓ each (Lesson 5.1).
  • Markov property: the same state + action should lead to the same kind of outcome (Lesson 2.1).
🎯 Hook

OpenAI Five played about 180 years' worth of Dota 2 games against itself every day during training. In 2019 it beat OG, the reigning world champions. Around the same time, OpenAI's hide-and-seek agents, trained only by playing against each other, invented tool use: building forts out of boxes, then using ramps to climb over the walls, then locking ramps away. Nobody programmed any of it.

💡 Big idea In self-play, the agent's opponent is (a copy of) itself. As it improves, its opponent improves too, so the challenge always matches its level: an automatic curriculum. The danger is cycling: getting better against your latest self while forgetting how to beat older strategies.
🧠 Analogy: a sparring partner who grows with you

Tennis players improve fastest against opponents at their own level: too weak and you learn nothing, too strong and you lose every point. Self-play gives you a partner who is always exactly your level, because it is you. The risk: you and your partner can drift into weird habits that only work against each other.

🎮 Playground · Two learners playing rock-paper-scissors

Two players start with lopsided strategies. Each round, each one nudges its probabilities toward whatever would have beaten the other's current strategy (each round, slightly raise the probability of whichever move would have won). Look at Player 1's latest strategy, then at the average of all its past strategies.

The latest strategy never settles. It keeps chasing the opponent in a loop: heavy on rock, then paper, then scissors, and the swings get wider over time (in our run, the peak probability grew from about 0.77 in the first 150 rounds to 0.997 by round 1,500): it spirals outward toward going all-in on one move. The average of all past strategies converges to ⅓ each, the unexploitable Nash equilibrium from Lesson 5.1. Why does averaging work? Each latest strategy is a counter to something specific, so it's always exploitable. The average mixes all those counters together, and an even ⅓/⅓/⅓ mix has no weakness left to exploit. That's why real systems keep a pool of past opponents or average over past policies instead of trusting only the latest one. (Researchers call this "last-iterate vs average-iterate" convergence. The update rule in this playground is multiplicative weights, a "no-regret" learner whose time-average provably converges in games like this. A different classic rule, fictitious play, best-responds to the opponent's average strategy instead of its latest one.)

When does self-play work? Best in symmetric, competitive games (two players, one wins, one loses, same rules for both, like Go). There your copy is a fair opponent, and beating it means you really got better. In a cooperative game, or one with humans at the table, two copies of you can invent private conventions that work only with each other. That's why the Diplomacy agent below also learned from human games.

✏️ Worked example: chasing in circles

Your current strategy leans rock (50% rock, 25% paper, 25% scissors). The best counter is all paper, so your copy learns paper. The best counter to paper is scissors, so you learn scissors. Then rock beats scissors… The latest strategy goes round in circles forever.

But average the strategies over a full cycle (rock phase, paper phase, scissors phase) and you get about ⅓ each, the unbeatable mix. That's why systems keep a pool of past selves.

Landmarks

🎲

TD-Gammon (1992)

The original self-play success, already met in Lesson 3.2: TD learning against itself reached near world-champion level at backgammon.

♟️

AlphaZero (2017)

Pure self-play with tree search (Lesson 6.3b). In about a day of training it surpassed world-champion programs in chess, shogi and Go.

🃏

Pluribus (2019)

Beat elite pros at six-player no-limit Texas hold'em, using self-play plus a search a few moves ahead, not deep RL. Meta reports it would cost under $150 of cloud compute to train.

🤝

CICERO (Science, 2022)

Played the negotiation game Diplomacy in natural language against people online. It scored more than double the human average and ranked in the top 10% of players who played more than one game. Pure self-play wasn't enough here: it combined a language model trained on human games with planning.

🌌

AlphaStar (2019)

StarCraft II Grandmaster. A whole league: main agents, plus "exploiter" agents whose only job is to find weaknesses in the main ones. That's the fix for cycling at scale.

🙈

Hide-and-seek (2019)

Six phases of emergent strategy and counter-strategy, including exploiting a physics bug ("box surfing"). Reward hacking again.

Math refresher Why multi-agent RL is harder
  • Non-stationarity: other agents keep learning, so "the environment" keeps changing. The Markov assumption from Phase 2 quietly breaks: the same move in the same position stops having the same consequences, because the opponent changed.
  • Credit assignment across a team: the team won, but whose actions mattered?
  • Non-transitivity: A beats B and B beats C doesn't mean A beats C (rock-paper-scissors). A single "skill score" can mislead.
📺 Watch (3 min)
  • Multi-Agent Hide and Seek (OpenAI). Self-play in action: hiders learn to build forts, seekers learn to use ramps, hiders learn to hide the ramps, and seekers discover "box surfing", a strategy the researchers didn't know their environment allowed. Nobody programmed any of it.
🌍 Where it's used
Self-play as an automatic curriculum 🧪 Research · 2019 OpenAI Five (Dota 2)

How: state = the part of the map its team can see · action = one of thousands of commands, every 4th frame (~20,000 per game) · reward = winning, plus signals like gold and deaths, minus what the other team earned.

Why this method: no scripted opponent improves as fast as you do. It played 80% of games against its latest self and 20% against older versions, so old tricks weren't forgotten.

A league against cycling 🧪 Research · 2019 DeepMind: AlphaStar (StarCraft II)

How: state = a camera view with roughly what a human sees · action = unit commands, capped at 22 per 5 seconds · reward = mainly winning, against opponents picked from the league.

Why this method: plain self-play kept forgetting and cycling. Exploiter agents hunted the main agents' weaknesses, and past strategies stayed in the pool. Result: Grandmaster, above 99.8% of active players.

Self-play beyond games: driving 🧪 Research · 2025 Apple: robust driving emerges from self-play

How: every car, cyclist and pedestrian runs the same policy · state = nearby road users' positions and speeds (not their goals) · action = steering and acceleration changes · reward = reach the goal, no collisions, stay in lane, obey signals.

Why this method: no human driving data needed; the traffic around you improves as you do. After 1.6 billion simulated km, it averaged over 3 million km between incidents in simulation.

🤯 Fun fact

AlphaGo Zero's self-play rediscovered centuries of human Go knowledge (standard opening patterns called joseki) in a few days of training, then abandoned some of them for new variations that professionals have since studied and adopted.

🌉 LLM bridge · LLMs playing themselves
  • SPIN (Self-Play Fine-Tuning, 2024): the model learns to tell its own outputs apart from human-written ones, improving without new human labels.
  • Proposer–solver self-play: one role invents problems, another solves them, and a code executor checks the answers. Example: Absolute Zero (2025), "reinforced self-play reasoning with zero data".
  • Debate and red-teaming: models argue against each other or attack each other to surface flaws.

The same risks apply: models can collude, cycle, or exploit the checker.

Check yourself ✅

1. What's the main appeal of self-play?
As you improve, your sparring partner improves, so you're always challenged at the right level.
2. Why does naive self-play cycle in rock-paper-scissors?
Non-transitive games have no "best pure strategy". Responding to an average or a pool of opponents breaks the loop.
3. What did AlphaStar's league add?
A diverse pool of opponents keeps learning robust instead of cyclic.
📝 TL;DR
  • Self-play: train against copies of yourself, giving an automatic curriculum (AlphaZero, OpenAI Five).
  • Risk: cycling and forgetting (the latest strategy can even spiral outward). Fix: opponent pools, leagues, averaging over past strategies.
  • Works best in symmetric, competitive games; with humans or teammates, add human data (CICERO).
  • Multi-agent RL: non-stationarity, team credit assignment, non-transitivity.
  • LLMs: SPIN, proposer–solver self-play, debate, red-teaming.
