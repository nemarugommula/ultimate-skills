Phase 6 · Lesson 1

Continuous actions: DDPG & TD3

When the action is "how much torque" rather than "left or right", you can't take an argmax over a list. So climb the Q-function instead.

🗺️ Phase 6 map: five assumptions, removed one at a time

Your DQN and PPO agents (Labs 4–5) quietly assumed five things. Each Phase 6 lesson removes one:

  • A short list of actions, or fresh data you can afford to throw away → 6.1 DDPG/TD3, 6.2 SAC.
  • Millions of cheap tries → 6.3 learn a model and practise in imagination; 6.3b plan by search before each move.
  • Being allowed to try at all → 6.4 offline RL: learn from a fixed log.
  • Stumbling on reward by luck → 6.5 exploration bonuses.
  • A hand-written reward or a fixed opponent → 6.6 self-play, 6.7 imitation and inverse RL.

Then 6.8 asks whether nature solved it first: dopamine looks a lot like a TD error.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • DQN: a neural network critic Q(s, a), trained toward r + γ × the next value, with replay and a target network (Lesson 4.3).
  • Maximization bias: taking the max of noisy estimates is too optimistic (Lesson 4.4).
🎯 Hook

In Lab 5, PPO needed 60,000 CartPole steps, and it threw each batch of 1,024 away after 10 passes (it's on-policy: old data came from an old policy). In a simulator that's free. A real robot arm gets maybe 1,000 tries an hour, so you'd want to reuse every one from a replay buffer, as DQN does. But DQN picks by argmax over a list, and a joint's torque can be anything between −2 and +2 newton-metres. In 2015, DeepMind's DDPG combined the two with a small idea: keep DQN's replay buffer, and train a second network to find the best action for you.

Didn't Phase 5 already handle continuous actions? Yes: a Gaussian policy ("aim for μ, give or take σ", Lesson 5.1) trained with PPO (5.6) controls torques fine, and it's the default in fast simulators. DDPG's selling point is being off-policy: it learns from any old data in the buffer, so every expensive real try gets reused many times. And why not chop the torque into bins for DQN? 10 bins for one joint is fine. A 7-joint arm needs a choice per joint: 10 × 10 × … × 10 = 107, ten million actions for the argmax to scan in every state.

💡 Big idea Learn a critic Q(s, a) as in DQN. Then train an actor network μ(s) that outputs one action, by gradient ascent on the critic: nudge the action in the direction that makes Q bigger. The actor learns to compute the argmax.
🧠 Analogy: a hiker and a guide

The critic is a guide who can say "the ground rises if you step a bit to the left". The actor is the hiker who just takes that step. Repeat, and the hiker climbs to wherever the guide thinks the summit is. (If the guide is wrong about a bump, the hiker happily climbs a fake hill: that's the weakness TD3 fixes below.)

In two steps: (1) ask the critic "which way should I nudge the action to raise Q?"; the slope of Q with respect to the action, written ∇aQ, answers that. (2) Nudge the actor's weights so its output moves that way. In symbols (∇ = "slope of", |a=μ(s) = "measured at the action the actor currently picks"):

actor update: θμ ← θμ + α · ∇a Q(s, a)|a=μ(s) · ∇θ μ(s) "Ask the critic which way to move the action to raise Q, then change the actor's weights to move its output that way" (the chain rule from Lesson 4.2)

DDPG = DQN ideas + an actor

  • Replay buffer and target networks (soft-updated: θ− ← 0.995θ− + 0.005θ every step. The target network is a lagging copy that moves just 0.5% toward the live network each step, so the targets drift slowly instead of jumping).
  • Exploration: add noise to the actor's action (it's deterministic, so it would never explore otherwise).
  • Critic target: r + γ Qtarget(s', μtarget(s')).

The weakness: the actor climbs whatever the critic believes. If the critic has a spurious bump (an overestimate), the actor heads straight for it. It's maximization bias (Lesson 4.4) again, now applied by gradient ascent.

🎮 Playground · An actor climbing a noisy critic

Dashed = the true Q(a) for one state (best action ≈ 0.8). Two learned critics = truth + random errors. The actor (dot) starts at a = 0 and climbs either one critic (DDPG-style) or the minimum of two critics (TD3-style).

Over 3,000 random critics in our test, the actor that climbed a single critic believed its action was worth +0.44 more than it really was, on average. Climbing min(Q₁, Q₂) brought that to −0.07: slightly pessimistic, but honest. The true value reached was about the same (1.18 vs 1.20).

✏️ Worked example: one actor step, and the twin-critic trick

Actor step: in some state the actor outputs a torque of 0.5. The critic says "Q goes up by about 2 per unit of extra torque here" (slope +2). With step size 0.01, the actor's weights are nudged so its output moves to about 0.5 + 0.01 × 2 = 0.52. Repeat everywhere, and the actor learns to output the torque the critic likes best.

Twin critics (TD3): two critics score the next state-action: 5.3 and 4.9. The target uses the smaller, 4.9. A fake bump that fools one critic rarely fools both, so the actor stops chasing illusions.

TD3 (2018): three fixes

👯

1. Twin critics

Learn two Q-networks and use the smaller one in the target. Errors would have to fool both at once, so overestimation drops (like Double DQN).

⏱️

2. Delayed actor updates

Update the actor (and targets) every 2 critic steps, so it follows a steadier critic.

🌫️

3. Target smoothing

Add small noise to the target action, so the target averages Q over a small neighborhood of actions. A spike narrower than the noise gets averaged away; a broad, real hill survives.

Math refresher Gaussian policies for continuous actions

Stochastic continuous policies usually output a mean μ and a standard deviation σ, then sample a ~ Normal(μ, σ²): "aim around μ, with a spread of σ". To keep actions inside limits like [−2, 2], squash with tanh. PPO, SAC (next lesson) and robot controllers all use this.

🌍 Where it's used
An actor climbing a critic (DDPG) 🧪 Research · 2018 Wayve: a real car learns to drive in a day

How: state = one camera image + current speed and steering angle · action = a steering angle and a target speed, both continuous · reward = forward speed; the episode ends when the safety driver takes over.

Why this method: every real try costs a safety driver's time, so replay reuses each one, and the actor outputs smooth steering directly. Lane-following took under 20 trials.

Twin critics: trust the smaller estimate 🧪 Inside · 2018 Google's QT-Opt: robots learning to grasp

How: state = camera image + gripper status · action = gripper motion, rotation, open/close · reward = 1 if an object is lifted, else 0. Each Q-target takes the min of two lagged critics.

Why this method: 580,000 real grasps on 7 robots is too expensive to rerun if training goes unstable; the min of two critics (TD3's fix) stops one critic's overestimate leaking into every target.

🤯 Fun fact

TD3's paper title says it plainly: "Addressing Function Approximation Error in Actor-Critic Methods" (Fujimoto et al., 2018). Its main contribution was showing that DDPG's overestimation was real and measurable, and that simple fixes beat much fancier methods on the standard MuJoCo robot benchmarks.

🌉 LLM bridge · optimizing against a learned judge

An actor exploiting a critic's spurious bump is the same failure as an LLM exploiting a reward model's blind spots: long-winded answers, flattery, or weird phrasings the reward model happens to score highly. Common remedies match TD3's: ensembles of reward models (be pessimistic, take the min or the mean), smoothing, and not trusting the learned judge too far, enforced with a KL penalty (Phase 7).

Check yourself ✅

1. How does DDPG choose a continuous action?
The actor approximates argmaxa Q(s,a) for each state.
2. Why does TD3 use the minimum of two critics?
Being pessimistic about value estimates counters the upward bias of maximizing.
3. DDPG's actor is deterministic. How does it explore?
Off-policy learning means noisy exploratory actions are fine. The critic still learns about the deterministic actor.
📝 TL;DR
  • Continuous actions: an actor μ(s) is trained by gradient ascent on the critic Q(s, a).
  • DDPG = actor + critic + replay + soft target networks + action noise.
  • The actor exploits critic errors. TD3: twin critics (min), delayed actor, target smoothing.
  • Same lesson as reward-model exploitation in LLMs.
