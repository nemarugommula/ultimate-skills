Phase 7 · Lesson 6

RLAIF & Constitutional AI

Human labels are slow and expensive. Can an AI give the feedback instead, guided by a written set of principles?

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Reward models learn from "A is better than B" labels (Lesson 7.3).
  • RLHF then optimizes the policy against that reward model (Lesson 7.4).
🎯 Hook

In December 2022, Anthropic published Constitutional AI: train a harmless assistant without any human labels for harmfulness. Instead, they wrote a short list of principles (a "constitution") and had the model critique and judge its own answers against them. Claude was trained using this approach.

💡 Big idea RLAIF = RL from AI Feedback: an LLM (the judge) compares answers and produces the preference labels, so feedback scales with compute instead of human hours. Constitutional AI makes the judge's criteria explicit and editable: written principles instead of thousands of individual labeling decisions.
🧠 Analogy: a teaching assistant with the professor's rubric

A professor can't grade 10,000 essays. So they write a clear rubric and hand it to teaching assistants. Grading scales up, and anyone can read the rubric to see what's being rewarded. In Constitutional AI, the AI judge is the teaching assistant and the "constitution" is the rubric. The catch: assistants have their own quirks (like favoring the first essay they read).

Why this works at all: checking is easier than creating

You can tell a good joke from a bad one far more reliably than you can write a good one. Models are the same: a model often judges answers better than it writes them. RLAIF turns that gap into skill: the model's judging lead becomes a reward, and RL pulls its writing up toward its judgment. The limit: the judge can't reward values it doesn't have. AI feedback scales up whatever standards it is given, which is why the human-written constitution matters: it's where people decide what "better" means.

Constitutional AI's two phases

✍️

1. Supervised: critique → revise

The model answers a tricky prompt, critiques its own answer against a principle ("Identify ways this response is harmful…"), rewrites it, and is fine-tuned on the revisions.

⚖️

2. RL from AI feedback

The model generates answer pairs, and an AI judge picks the better one according to the constitution. Train a preference model on those labels, then do RL against it (Lesson 7.4).

✏️ Worked example: one critique → revise cycle
  1. Prompt: "My neighbor's dog barks all night. How do I get back at them?"
  2. First draft: "Blast loud music at their window at 3 a.m. so they know how it feels."
  3. Critique (the model, asked to check its draft against a principle like "Identify ways this response is harmful"): "This escalates the conflict and could get the user into legal trouble. It doesn't solve the barking."
  4. Revision: "Revenge usually makes it worse. Try a friendly note or a chat first; if that fails, check your local noise rules or ask a mediator. Earplugs or white noise can help in the meantime."

The pair (prompt, revision) becomes one SFT example. In the second (RL) phase, the fine-tuned model writes two fresh answers and the judge compares them; if it says A is better with probability 0.9, that's a 0.9 / 0.1 soft label for the preference model. No human labeled anything.

AI judges have biases

Studies of LLM-as-a-judge (e.g., Zheng et al., 2023, the MT-Bench paper) found systematic biases:

  • Position bias: preferring whichever answer is shown first (or second).
  • Verbosity bias: preferring longer answers.
  • Self-preference: rating outputs from its own model family higher.
🎮 Playground · Fixing position bias by swapping order

1,000 answer pairs where we know which answer is truly better. The judge is fairly good, but leans toward the first answer it sees. Single pass: ask once (A first). Swap test: ask twice with the order swapped, keep the label only if both verdicts agree, and drop the rest as "unsure".

With a strong position bias, the single-pass judge gets many pairs wrong just because of order. The swap test throws away inconsistent verdicts and is much more accurate on the labels it keeps. The cost is fewer labels and twice the compute. Real pipelines use tricks like this, plus judge ensembles and rubrics.

Math refresher Why AI feedback can be "good enough"

RL needs a signal that correlates with the goal, not a perfect one. If the AI judge agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other (~70–75% on many tasks), its labels are about as useful, and you can get millions of them. Google's 2023 RLAIF study found policies trained with AI feedback were rated comparably to those trained with human feedback on tasks like summarization.

🌍 Where it's used
Constitutional AI 🏭 In production · 2023 Anthropic: Claude's constitution (2023)

How: state = a user's prompt · action = Claude's reply · reward = a preference model trained on AI choices of the more harmless reply, judged against written principles.

Why this method: AI labels scale with compute and spare people from rating harmful text. Written principles make the target readable and editable.

AI preference labels at scale 🧪 Research · 2023 UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023)

How: GPT-4 rated answers from many models: over 1 million ratings across 250k conversations. The ratings became preference pairs and reward-model data for open chat models.

Why this method: open human preference datasets were small and narrow. An AI judge made a large, varied set cheaply, and Zephyr (Lesson 7.5) was trained on it.

LLM judges and their biases 🧪 Research · 2023 Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023)

How: GPT-4 picks the better of two chatbot answers. It agreed with humans over 80% of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other.

Why this method: far cheaper than people, but the study measured position, verbosity and self-preference biases. Hence swapping the answer order and averaging.

🤯 Fun fact

Claude's earlier constitution (published 2023) was a list of short principles drawn from varied sources, including the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights. In January 2026 Anthropic replaced it with a much longer document that explains its reasoning rather than just listing rules, keeps a few hard limits, and ranks priorities for conflicts: broadly safe, then broadly ethical, then following Anthropic's guidelines, then genuinely helpful. Claude also uses it to generate synthetic training data. It's public and free to reuse, so anyone can read what the model is trained to value.

🌉 Where this leads (Phase 8)

AI feedback grew into generative reward models (judges that reason before scoring) and rubric-based rewards (per-question checklists scored by a judge), which is how RL extends beyond math and code into medicine, science, writing and agent behavior. The core risk stays the same: the policy will exploit whatever the judge gets wrong.

Written rules also moved into the model's own reasoning. OpenAI's deliberative alignment (Dec 2024) trains its reasoning models, partly with RL, to recall the written safety spec and reason over it before answering, instead of only absorbing it indirectly through labels.

🛡️ Sidebar: safe / constrained RL

Sometimes a rule shouldn't be traded off against reward at all: "keep harmful answers under 1%", "never exceed the robot's torque limit". Constrained RL maximizes reward subject to a cost staying below a limit. A common recipe uses a Lagrange multiplier λ, which works like a price on breaking the rule: the agent maximizes reward − λ × cost, and λ rises for as long as the cost is over the limit (and eases back once it's under). Break the rule and it gets more expensive until breaking it no longer pays. CPO (Achiam et al., 2017) is the classic constrained version of trust-region policy optimization.

Check yourself ✅

1. What replaces human labelers in RLAIF?
The rest of the pipeline (preference model + RL, or DPO) stays the same.
2. What's in Constitutional AI's supervised phase?
Self-critique and revision create the SFT data. The RL phase then uses AI preference labels.
3. A judge prefers the first answer it sees. Cheapest robust fix?
Swapping cancels the order effect. Inconsistent pairs reveal where the judge is unsure.
📝 TL;DR
  • RLAIF: AI judges produce the preference labels, so feedback scales with compute. It works because judging is easier than writing, but it can't add values the judge (and its constitution) lacks.
  • Constitutional AI: written principles → self-critique and revision (SFT) → AI preference labels → RL.
  • Judges have position, verbosity and self-preference biases. Swap tests, ensembles and rubrics help.
  • This leads to generative reward models and rubrics as rewards (Phase 8).
