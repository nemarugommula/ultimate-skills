Phase 7 · Lesson 4

RLHF with PPO & the KL penalty

Optimize the reward model's score, but on a leash. Without the leash, the policy finds the reward model's blind spots.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • PPO: improve the policy in small, clipped steps (Lesson 5.6).
  • Reward model: a network that scores answers, learned from comparisons (last lesson).
  • KL: a number measuring how differently two models behave; 0 = identical.
🎯 Hook

In early RLHF experiments, optimizing a learned reward model too hard sometimes produced degenerate text the reward model loved and humans hated. OpenAI researchers (Gao et al., 2022) measured it carefully, using a large "gold" reward model as a stand-in for human judges (real humans would be too slow and costly to ask that often): as optimization pressure grows, the reward model's score keeps rising, but the true quality rises, peaks, then falls. It's Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

💡 Big idea RLHF maximizes the reward model's score minus a penalty for drifting from the original (reference) model: KL(πθ ‖ πref). The policy stays in the region where the reward model was trained and is trustworthy, and keeps the fluency and knowledge of the original.
🧠 Analogy: a dog on a leash

The reward model is a treat-giver with blind spots. Let the dog (policy) roam freely and it finds the spot where treats fall for silly reasons. A leash (the KL penalty) lets it explore nearby, where the treat-giver's judgment is reliable, but not wander off into weird territory. β is how short the leash is.

The sentence first: score the answers with the reward model, then subtract β × "how far the model has drifted from the original". In symbols (𝔼x, y~π = "on average over prompts x and answers y sampled from the policy", ‖ = "compared with"):

maxθ 𝔼x, y~πθ[ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x) ) β = leash strength. The optimum (Lesson 6.2!): π*(y|x) ∝ πref(y|x) · exp(r(x,y)/β)

Sweep the leash: reward model score vs true quality

Start with a strong leash (right) and slowly loosen it (left). First, probability moves toward genuinely good answers and both curves rise. Loosen further and the policy piles onto the answers the reward model overrates ("long flattering" and "RM-gaming"). The reward model score keeps climbing while true quality collapses. The best β is in the middle, and you can't find it by watching the proxy score alone.

✏️ Worked example: why the leash stops a hack

β = 0.5. For a single answer y, the penalty uses its log-ratio log π(y)/πref(y): how much likelier the policy now makes this answer than the original model did.

  • A normal good answer: reward model 3.0, log-ratio 2.0 (the policy makes it e2 ≈ 7× likelier than the original) → total 3.0 − 0.5 × 2.0 = 2.0.
  • A weird answer that exploits the reward model ("You're a genius!!!" repeated): reward model 4.0, but log-ratio 8.0 (e8 ≈ 3,000× likelier than the original would ever write it) → total 4.0 − 0.5 × 8.0 = 0.0.

Without the leash, the hack wins (4.0 > 3.0). With it, the normal answer wins. Make β too small and the hack wins again; too large and the model can't improve at all. Average these log-ratios over the answers the policy actually samples and you get the KL: KL is a property of the whole distribution, the log-ratio is one answer's share of it.

How it's implemented (InstructGPT-style)

# per-token reward for one sampled response y (PPO treats each token as a step)
kl_t   = logp_policy[t] - logp_ref[t]                     # per-token log-ratio (a KL estimate)
reward = -beta * kl_t                                     # a small penalty on every token…
reward[last_token] += reward_model(prompt, response)      # …plus the reward-model score at the end
advantages = GAE(reward, critic_values, gamma=1, lam=0.95)   # Lesson 5.5
ppo_update(policy, critic, advantages)                    # Lesson 5.6, with 4 models in memory: policy, critic, reward model, reference

Why per-token? The per-token log-ratios add up to the whole answer's log-ratio (logs turn the chain-rule product of token probabilities into a sum, Lesson 7.1). E.g. three tokens with log-ratios 0.5, 0.0, 1.5 make an answer with log-ratio 2.0, so PPO can charge each token its own share of the drift.

The four models: the policy (trained), the critic (trained), the reward model (frozen), the reference model (frozen). That's heavy: a 7B model in 16-bit numbers takes about 14 GB per copy (7 billion × 2 bytes), so four copies need ~56 GB before you even add optimizer states. That cost motivated DPO (next lesson) and GRPO (Phase 8).

Math refresher KL divergence in one picture

KL(π ‖ πref) = Σy π(y) · log( π(y) / πref(y) ): the average extra "surprise" if you expected πref but got π. It's 0 when they're identical, and it grows quickly when π puts mass where πref had almost none (like the RM-gaming answer above, which the reference model thought was 1% likely).

🌍 Where it's used
PPO with a per-token KL leash 🏭 In production · 2022 InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022)

How: policy = the SFT model being tuned · reward = a 6B reward model's score at the end, minus a KL penalty at every token against the SFT model · the value model starts as a copy of the reward model.

Why this method: without the leash the policy drifts into text the reward model overrates. Per-token penalties keep it fluent while the end score pulls it toward preferred answers.

How reward grows with KL 🧪 Research · 2022 Anthropic: a helpful & harmless assistant with RLHF

How: preference models and RL policies were retrained weekly on fresh human comparisons. The reward rose roughly in a straight line with the square root of the KL from the starting model.

Why this method: fresh comparisons keep the reward model accurate on what the current policy writes. The square-root law means each extra unit of reward costs ever more drift.

Dropping the KL term for checkable rewards 🧪 Research · 2025 Yu et al. (2025): an open reasoning-RL system

How: state = a competition maths problem · action = a long reasoning answer · reward = right or wrong, checked by a rule · no KL penalty.

Why this method: the leash guards against a flawed learned scorer. A rule can't be flattered, and the authors argue long reasoning has to drift far from the starting model anyway.

🤯 Fun fact

RLHF slightly hurt InstructGPT on some standard benchmarks, an "alignment tax". OpenAI's fix, PPO-ptx, mixed a bit of the original pretraining loss into the PPO updates, so the model kept practising "predict the internet" while learning to be helpful.

A cautionary tale from OpenAI's 2019 RLHF paper (Ziegler et al.): a bug flipped the sign of both the reward and the KL penalty, and because the leash still pulled toward natural language, the model wrote fluent, maximally bad text (the explicit content labelers had been told to rate lowest), which the sleeping authors only discovered once training had finished.

🌉 Today's practice
  • For subjective goals (helpfulness, tone, safety) with learned reward models, a KL penalty or some other anchor remains essential.
  • For verifiable rewards (math answers, unit tests), the reward can't be fooled the same way, and several 2025 recipes (e.g. DAPO) dropped the KL term entirely to let reasoning drift far from the start. Others keep a small one for stability. It's an active trade-off (Phase 8).

Check yourself ✅

1. What does the KL penalty in RLHF prevent?
The reward model is only accurate near the data it was trained on, which came from outputs like the reference model's.
2. As β → 0, the RLHF optimum π* ∝ πref·exp(r/β) becomes…
No leash means pure reward maximization, which exploits the reward model's errors. Large β keeps π* ≈ πref.
3. Gao et al. (2022) found that with more optimization against a reward model…
Reward over-optimization: Goodhart's law, measured. Bigger reward models delay the peak but don't remove it.
📝 TL;DR
  • RLHF objective: 𝔼[rφ] − β·KL(π ‖ πref). Optimum π* ∝ πref·exp(r/β).
  • Implemented with PPO: per-token KL penalty + reward-model score at the end, GAE, 4 models.
  • Over-optimization: proxy ↑, true quality ↑ then ↓. The leash β trades reward against staying trustworthy.
  • With verifiable rewards, some recipes drop the KL term.
