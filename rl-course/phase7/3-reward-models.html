Phase 7 · Lesson 3

Reward models & Bradley–Terry

People find it hard to score an answer "7.3 out of 10", but easy to say "A is better than B". Here's how comparisons become a reward function.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Sigmoid σ: the S-shaped curve that turns any number into a probability between 0 and 1 (Lesson 4.2).
  • Supervised learning = adjust a model so it predicts labeled examples well (Lesson 0.1).
🎯 Hook

Chess ratings (Elo) and LLM reward models rest on the same idea. Bradley and Terry proposed a model in 1952 for ranking items from pairwise comparisons, and Arpad Elo later built chess ratings on the same principle: the bigger your rating gap, the more likely you win. Today it's how human "this answer is better" clicks become the reward that trains assistants, and how leaderboards like LMArena rank models.

💡 Big idea Give each answer a hidden score r. The chance a person prefers A over B depends only on the score difference: P(A ≻ B) = σ(rA − rB). Train a network rφ(prompt, answer) to make the observed human choices likely. That network is the reward model.
🧠 Analogy: chess ratings

Chess players get a rating, and the bigger the rating gap, the more likely the stronger player wins. Nobody watches each player alone and assigns a score: the ratings are worked out only from who beat whom. A reward model does the same for answers: from thousands of "A beat B" judgments, it learns a hidden score for every answer. (A ≻ B just means "A is preferred to B".)

P(A ≻ B) = σ( r(x, A) − r(x, B) ),   σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z) Loss = −log σ(rchosen − rrejected): "make the chosen answer's score higher than the rejected one's"
🎮 Playground · The Bradley–Terry curve

Equal scores → 50/50. A gap of 1 → 73%. A gap of 3 → 95%. Only differences matter, so adding 100 to every score changes nothing. A reward model's absolute numbers mean nothing by themselves (one reason GRPO normalizes rewards within a group).

Math refresher The sigmoid σ, and how the reward model is built

σ(z) squashes any number into (0, 1): σ(0) = 0.5, σ(large) → 1, σ(very negative) → 0. It's the S-shaped curve that turns any number into a probability (the same shape as the sigmoid neuron in Lesson 4.2).

In practice the reward model is an LLM (often the SFT model) with its token-prediction layer replaced by a single linear "score" head on the last token's hidden state (Lesson 4.1's linear function on learned features). Why the last token? Each position only reads the text before it, so only the last position has read the whole prompt + answer:

r_chosen   = reward_model(prompt + chosen)       # one number
r_rejected = reward_model(prompt + rejected)
loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected).mean()
✏️ Worked example: one comparison, one loss

The reward model scores answer A at 2.0 and answer B at 0.5. Its predicted chance that a person prefers A: σ(2.0 − 0.5) = σ(1.5) ≈ 0.82.

  • The labeler picked A: loss = −log 0.82 ≈ 0.20. Small: the model was already mostly right.
  • The labeler picked B: loss = −log 0.18 ≈ 1.71. Big: training pushes B's score up and A's down.

Reward models learn what labelers do, including their biases

🎮 Playground · You be the labeler

Question: "What's the boiling point of water at sea level?" For each pair, click the answer you prefer. After 8 pairs, we fit a reward model to your choices and show what it learned to value.

🎮 Playground · Train a tiny reward model from biased labelers

Answers have three features: correct (0/1), polite (0–1), long (0–1). True quality = 2·correct + 0.5·polite; length doesn't matter. Labelers compare 600 pairs, but they're slightly charmed by longer answers (bias slider). We fit a Bradley–Terry reward model (logistic regression) to their choices and look at the weights it learns.

With any bias above zero, the reward model gives long a positive weight, even though length has nothing to do with quality. RL against this reward model will then make answers longer, because that's the cheapest way to raise the score. This is the well-documented length bias of RLHF, and it's reward hacking with a clear cause: the reward model faithfully learned a human bias.

The same thing happens with flattery. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update after it became overly agreeable; its write-up said an added reward signal from users' thumbs-up/down was one cause, because that feedback can favor answers that simply agree with the user. (Full story in Lesson 8.6.)

🌍 Where it's used
Two Bradley–Terry reward models 🏭 In production · 2023 Meta: Llama 2-Chat

How: two reward models (helpfulness, safety), each an LLM with a score head trained on ‘which reply is better?’ pairs, with an extra margin when labelers said one was much better.

Why this method: helpfulness and safety sometimes pull in opposite directions, and Meta found a single reward model struggled to do well on both. Two scorers keep the trade-off explicit.

Bradley–Terry for ranking chatbots 🏭 In production · 2023 Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024)

How: a user sees answers from two anonymous models and votes. Bradley–Terry turns the votes into one score per model: the chance A beats B depends only on their score difference.

Why this method: people rarely agree on a 1–10 rating, but ‘which is better?’ is easy to answer, and the same pairwise maths that trains reward models ranks the models.

Reward models learn labeler biases 🧪 Research · 2023 Singhal et al.: length and RLHF

How: in three RLHF setups, most of the reward gain came from longer answers. A reward based only on length reproduced most of the improvement over the SFT model.

Why this method: a length-only reward is the cheapest control there is. If it matches your reward model's gains, the model mostly rewards length; check before RL amplifies it.

🤯 Fun fact

Humans disagree a lot. In the InstructGPT paper, labelers agreed with each other on which answer was better only about 73% of the time, and the reward model's accuracy on held-out comparisons was in the same range. A reward model can't be much more consistent than the people it learns from.

🌉 Where reward models are today
  • Scalar reward models (this lesson) remain standard for subjective qualities like helpfulness and tone.
  • Generative reward models / LLM judges write a critique, then a verdict, and are often more accurate (Phase 8).
  • Verifiers replace reward models wherever the answer can be checked (RLVR, Phase 8).
  • Rubric-based rewards score answers against explicit checklists, extending RL to medicine, science and writing (Phase 8).

Check yourself ✅

1. Reward model scores: A = 2.0, B = 1.0. Bradley–Terry P(A preferred)?
σ(2 − 1) = σ(1) = 1/(1 + e−1) ≈ 0.731.
2. Why collect comparisons instead of absolute 1–10 scores?
Different people use 1–10 scales very differently. Relative judgments are easier and more reliable.
3. Labelers subtly prefer longer answers. What happens after RL against the learned reward model?
The reward model encodes the bias, and RL exploits it. Common mitigations: length-controlled evaluation, length penalties, debiased data.
📝 TL;DR
  • Bradley–Terry: P(A ≻ B) = σ(rA − rB). Only differences matter.
  • Reward model = LLM + scalar head, trained with −log σ(rchosen − rrejected).
  • It learns labelers' preferences, biases included (length). RL then amplifies those biases.
  • Human agreement (~73%) caps reward-model accuracy.
