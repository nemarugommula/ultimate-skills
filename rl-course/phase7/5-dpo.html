Phase 7 · Lesson 5

DPO and the preference family

A clever piece of algebra turns RLHF into a simple classification-style loss: no reward model, no sampling loop, no PPO.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Bradley–Terry: P(A preferred) = σ(score A − score B), loss = −log of that (Lesson 7.3).
  • RLHF's goal: high reward while staying close to the reference model (Lesson 7.4).
🎯 Hook

The 2023 Stanford paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" (Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell et al.) won an outstanding-paper runner-up award at NeurIPS 2023. Within a year, DPO and its relatives were among the most widely used ways to preference-tune open models, because they replaced a fragile four-model PPO pipeline with one loss function.

💡 Big idea We know RLHF's optimal policy: π* ∝ πref·exp(r/β). Flip it around and the reward is hidden inside the policy: r = β·log(π/πref) + const. Substitute that into Bradley–Terry and you can train the policy directly on preference pairs. The model is its own reward model.
🧠 Analogy: skipping the middleman

RLHF is like hiring a food critic (reward model) to rate dishes, then training the chef to please the critic. DPO notices that, mathematically, the ideal chef and the critic are two views of the same thing. So it hands the chef the original "dish A beat dish B" cards directly and says: make A a bit more like you, B a bit less. No critic, no RL loop.

Remember Bradley–Terry (7.3)? Same shape: −log σ(score difference). The only change is what counts as the "score": β × how much more the new model likes the answer than the reference model did.

LDPO = − log σ( β [ log πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] ) "Raise the chosen answer's probability (relative to the reference) more than the rejected one's." yw = winner, yl = loser.
Math refresher The derivation in 4 lines
  1. RLHF optimum (Lesson 7.4): π*(y|x) = πref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) / Z(x). Z(x) is a normalizing constant that's impossible to compute (you'd need to score every possible answer to the prompt).
  2. Solve for the reward. Log rules you need: log(a·b) = log a + log b, log(a/b) = log a − log b, and log(ex) = x. Take the log of step 1: log π* = log πref + r/β − log Z. Move terms across and multiply by β: r(x,y) = β·log(π*(y|x)/πref(y|x)) + β·log Z(x).
  3. Bradley–Terry only uses a difference r(x,yw) − r(x,yl), so the nasty β·log Z(x) cancels.
  4. Plug into −log σ(rw − rl) with πθ in place of π*, and you get the DPO loss. 🎉

In numbers: the reference model gave an answer 10% probability and the policy now gives it 20%. Its implicit reward (ignoring the shared β·log Z) is β·log(0.20 / 0.10) = 0.1 × ln 2 ≈ 0.07 at β = 0.1. Doubling an answer's probability = +0.07 reward.

chosen_ratio   = policy_logp(x, y_w) - ref_logp(x, y_w)    # sequence log-probs (Lesson 7.1)
rejected_ratio = policy_logp(x, y_l) - ref_logp(x, y_l)
loss = -F.logsigmoid(beta * (chosen_ratio - rejected_ratio)).mean()

Notice the gradient weight σ(−margin): when the model already ranks the pair correctly by a wide margin, the weight is near 0 and the example barely matters. When it ranks them the wrong way, the weight is near 1. DPO concentrates on the pairs it currently gets wrong, like a well-designed curriculum.

✏️ Worked example: the DPO loss in numbers

β = 0.1. At the start the policy equals the reference, so both log-ratios are 0, the margin is 0, and the loss is −log σ(0) = log 2 ≈ 0.69.

After some training, the policy likes the chosen answer more than the reference did (log-ratio +1.0) and the rejected one less (−1.0). Margin = 0.1 × (1.0 − (−1.0)) = 0.2. Loss = −log σ(0.2) ≈ −log 0.55 ≈ 0.60. Lower loss = the model now prefers the chosen answer more strongly than the reference did, which is exactly the goal.

The preference-optimization family

MethodWhat it changes, in plain EnglishWhy
DPO (2023)The loss aboveRLHF without reward model or RL loop
IPO (2023)Stops pushing once the chosen answer leads by a set margin (a squared loss toward a target gap)DPO pushes forever: on clean data where the same answer always wins, it drives the rejected answer's probability toward 0, however big β is
KTO (2024)Learns from a single 👍 or 👎 per answer, no pairs. Named after Kahneman and Tversky, whose prospect theory says losses hurt more than equal gainsUnpaired thumbs-up/down feedback is far easier to collect
SimPO (2024)Scores an answer by its average per-token log-prob instead of the sum, adds a target margin, and drops the reference modelWith an average, padding an answer with extra tokens can't inflate the margin; no reference model is cheaper
ORPO (2024)Ordinary SFT on the chosen answer, plus a penalty that lowers the rejected answer's odds; no reference modelOne training stage instead of SFT then DPO
Online / iterative DPOGenerate fresh pairs from the current model each round, judge them, trainFixes offline distribution shift (Lesson 6.4); often clearly better
⚠️ Known DPO quirks (good research questions)
  • Likelihood displacement: DPO often lowers the probability of both chosen and rejected answers (rejected faster), so the margin grows while the chosen answer becomes less likely. Example: chosen 0.30 → 0.20, rejected 0.10 → 0.02. The gap grew, but your favorite answer still got less likely. Probability mass can leak to unexpected outputs.
  • Offline: it only ever sees the dataset's answers, so it can't learn from its own new mistakes the way on-policy RL can.
  • Results are sensitive to β, data quality and how closely the pairs match the model's own outputs.

🌍 Where it's used
DPO on AI-ranked pairs 🧪 Research · 2023 Hugging Face: Zephyr-7B

How: policy = a 7B model after SFT · pairs = the answer GPT-4 rated best vs a random lower-rated one · the DPO loss trains on the pairs directly: no reward model, no sampling.

Why this method: a few hours of training with no RL loop and no human labels, and it beat Llama 2-Chat 70B on MT-Bench, a chat benchmark.

SFT then DPO in a shipped model 🏭 In production · 2023 Mistral: Mixtral 8x7B Instruct

How: SFT on an instruction dataset gives the policy, then DPO on a paired feedback dataset. Mistral served it behind its mistral-small API endpoint (in beta).

Why this method: DPO needs only the policy and a frozen reference copy, instead of PPO's four models plus a sampling loop. That's much simpler on a large mixture-of-experts model.

DPO at 70B scale 🧪 Research · 2023 Ai2: Tülu 2 + DPO

How: Llama 2 models fine-tuned on an instruction mix, then DPO on preference pairs. The 70B model was the largest DPO-trained model reported at the time.

Why this method: it showed the simple offline loss stays stable at 70B, and every checkpoint, dataset and training script was released so others could check.

🤯 Fun fact

Meta's Llama 3 post-training used DPO rather than PPO for its preference stage, citing stability and lower compute at scale, but ran it iteratively over six rounds with fresh data each time. That's a practical compromise between offline simplicity and on-policy freshness.

🌉 Where DPO stands now

DPO-style methods remain popular for style, tone and helpfulness tuning, where preference data is the signal. For reasoning (math, code, agents), labs moved to on-policy RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO and its variants, Phase 8), because exploring and checking your own fresh attempts beats learning from a fixed list of pairs.

DPO's breakout moment: Zephyr-7B (Hugging Face, Oct 2023) ran DPO on UltraFeedback, preference pairs ranked by GPT-4 instead of humans, and beat Llama-2-70B-chat, a model 10× its size trained with RLHF, on the MT-Bench chat benchmark. Who needs human labelers when an AI can judge? That's the next lesson.

Check yourself ✅

1. What does DPO eliminate compared with RLHF-PPO?
DPO still uses preference pairs and a reference model (SimPO and ORPO drop the reference). The reward is implicit: β·log π/πref.
2. Why does the partition function Z(x) disappear in DPO?
Both answers share the same prompt x, so the same Z(x), which cancels in rw − rl.
3. The model already prefers the chosen answer by a large implicit margin. How much does this pair contribute to the gradient?
Correctly ranked pairs with large margins contribute little. Mis-ranked pairs dominate the update.
📝 TL;DR
  • Reward hidden in the policy: r = β log(π/πref) + const. Plug into Bradley–Terry → the DPO loss.
  • L = −log σ(β[Δchosen − Δrejected]). Simple, stable, offline.
  • Family: IPO, KTO, SimPO, ORPO, online/iterative DPO.
  • Quirks: likelihood displacement, offline drift. Reasoning moved to on-policy RLVR.
