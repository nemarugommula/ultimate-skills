Phase 5 · Lesson 4

Actor-critic & A2C

Two networks working together: one acts, one judges. The skeleton of PPO and of RLHF.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • TD error δ = reward + γ × V(next state) − V(this state): "how much better did things turn out than I expected?" (Lesson 3.2).
  • REINFORCE: make actions more likely in proportion to how good their outcome was (Lesson 5.2).
  • Advantage = how much better an action was than usual; subtracting a baseline makes learning far less noisy (Lesson 5.3).
🎯 Hook

A figure skater (the actor) performs while a coach (the critic) watches and says after each move, "that was better than I expected" or "worse than I expected". The skater adjusts right away instead of waiting for the final score. The coach is learning too, getting better at predicting how routines turn out. That's actor-critic.

💡 Big idea Actor = the policy πθ. Critic = a value function Vw. After every step the critic computes the TD error δ = r + γV(s') − V(s), which serves as the advantage: δ > 0 means "better than expected", so the actor makes that action more likely. Both learn at the same time.
🧠 Analogy: the GPS arrival time

Your GPS shows "arrive in 30 min" (the critic's V). You take a shortcut, drive 2 minutes, and it now says 25. That's 3 minutes better than expected (30 − 2 − 25), so the shortcut was a good call: δ > 0, without waiting to arrive. But if the GPS is broken, you'll learn bad shortcuts. The actor is only as good as the critic grading it.

for each step:
    a = sample(actor(s)); s2, r, done = env.step(a)
    delta = r + gamma * critic(s2) * (1 - done) - critic(s)      # TD error = advantage estimate
    critic_loss = delta ** 2                                    # critic: predict better (TD learning)
    actor_loss  = -delta.detach() * log_prob(actor(s), a)           # actor: REINFORCE with A = δ (detach = treat δ as a fixed number here)
    s = s2
✏️ Worked example: two steps, by hand

γ = 0.9. The critic currently thinks V(here) = 2.

  • Step 1: the actor moves right, gets r = 1, and lands where the critic says V = 3. δ = 1 + 0.9 × 3 − 2 = +1.7: better than expected. The actor makes "right" more likely here; the critic raises V(here) toward 2 + 1.7.
  • Step 2 (another visit): the actor moves down, gets r = 0, and lands where V = 1. δ = 0 + 0.9 × 1 − 2 = −1.1: worse than expected. The actor makes "down" less likely; the critic lowers its estimate a bit.

The actor never needs to wait for the episode to end, and it's never confused by "all rewards are positive": δ is already centered on "what I expected".

Math refresher Why δ is a good advantage estimate

The advantage is A(s,a) = Q(s,a) − V(s). One sample of Q(s,a) is "the reward I got + the value of where I landed": r + γV(s'). Subtract the baseline V(s) and you get δ. It's biased if the critic is wrong, but has much lower variance than REINFORCE's full return. MC vs TD again (Lesson 3.2), now for policy gradients.

Two ways actor-critic goes wrong

  • The critic falls behind. δ grades each action against the critic's idea of "usual". If the actor changes faster than the critic can track, it's graded against an outdated "usual" (a GPS still showing yesterday's traffic). So the critic must learn at least as fast as the actor: implementations give it an equal or bigger step size, or update the actor less often (TD3, Lesson 6.1, updates the actor only every 2 critic steps).
  • The actor stops exploring. Early on, one action gets a few lucky δs and the policy goes 99% on it. Now the other actions are almost never sampled, so it never finds out they might be better, and the update (1 − π) on the favorite shrinks to almost 0. The fix is an entropy bonus. Entropy H(π) measures how spread out the choices are: 0.69 for [50%, 50%], only 0.06 for [99%, 1%]. Subtract a little of it from the loss (loss = actor_loss − 0.01 × H, as in Lab 5) and the policy is paid to stay a bit uncertain until the evidence is strong.

A2C and A3C: run many copies

One agent's consecutive steps are highly correlated (the same problem the replay buffer solved for DQN). Policy gradients are on-policy, so we can't replay old data (unless we correct for it, Lesson 5.6): the gradient formula is only valid for data collected by the current policy, and old data came from a different one. The fix is to run many environments in parallel and average their gradients.

🧵

A3C (2016)

Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic: many CPU threads, each with its own environment, sending updates to a shared network whenever they're ready.

🧱

A2C

The synchronous version: step all environments together, then do one batched update. Simpler, uses GPUs better, and works about as well.

🌍 Where it's used
Actor-critic in a shipped game 🏭 In production · 2023 Sony AI: GT Sophy races players in Gran Turismo 7

How: state = the game's description of the car, track and rivals · action = steering, throttle and brake, 10 times a second · reward = going fast, with penalties for hitting walls or other cars.

Why this method: steering and pedals are continuous, so an actor outputs them while a critic judges how good they were. Since November 2023 it races PS5 players on 340+ cars and nine tracks.

Small, fast actor; big critic 🧪 Research · 2022 DeepMind + EPFL: shaping plasma in a fusion reactor

How: state = magnetic measurements + the target shape · action = voltage commands for all the control coils · reward = how closely the plasma matches its targets. Trained in a simulator, then run on the real TCV tokamak.

Why this method: the actor must run 10,000 times a second, so it is a small, fast network. The critic is only needed during training, so it can be a much larger recurrent network.

🤯 Fun fact

The A3C paper (Mnih et al., 2016) beat DQN on Atari while training for half the time on a single multi-core CPU instead of a GPU. Parallel environments gave the decorrelation that DQN had needed a big replay buffer for.

Actor-critic methods now run real systems. Sony AI's GT Sophy beat top Gran Turismo drivers using QR-SAC, an actor-critic (Nature 2022). DeepMind shaped the plasma inside a fusion reactor (a tokamak) with MPO, another actor-critic (Nature 2022).

🌉 LLM bridge · the four models of RLHF

Classic PPO-based RLHF (InstructGPT, Phase 7) keeps four big models in memory: the actor (the LLM being trained), the critic (a value model, often as big as the actor), the reward model, and a frozen reference model. The critic alone can double the training memory, which is a big reason GRPO (Phase 8) replaced it with "average reward of a group of samples".

Check yourself ✅

1. What does the critic do in actor-critic?
The critic is a learned baseline that also bootstraps, giving a low-variance advantage after every step.
2. r = 1, V(s') = 4, V(s) = 5, γ = 1. What does the actor do with this action?
δ = 0 means no surprise and no update. Like dopamine for a fully predicted reward (Lesson 1.2).
3. Why do A2C and A3C run many environments in parallel?
On-policy methods can't replay old data, so parallel workers provide the diversity instead.
📝 TL;DR
  • Actor = policy, critic = value function. δ = r + γV(s') − V(s) is the advantage estimate.
  • Critic learns by TD. Actor learns by REINFORCE with A = δ. Both update every step.
  • A3C/A2C: parallel environments for decorrelated on-policy data.
  • RLHF with PPO = actor-critic plus a reward model and a reference model, which is memory-hungry.
