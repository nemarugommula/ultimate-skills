Phase 5 · Lesson 6

Trust regions: TRPO → PPO

How to take big learning steps without wrecking your policy. The algorithm behind OpenAI Five and ChatGPT's RLHF.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Policy gradient with advantages: push actions up when the advantage Â is positive, down when negative (Lessons 5.2–5.3).
  • Ratio ρ (rho) = new probability ÷ old probability of an action: 1.3 means "30% more likely than before". (We write ρ so it doesn't clash with the reward r.)
🎯 Hook

PPO came out in 2017. Within two years it had trained OpenAI Five, which beat the Dota 2 world champions, and in 2022 it was the RL algorithm behind InstructGPT and ChatGPT. Its key idea fits in one line: don't let the policy change too much in one update.

The problem: one bad step can ruin everything

📉

In supervised learning…

a bad step is annoying, but the dataset doesn't change. The next steps fix it.

💥

In RL…

a bad step gives a bad policy, which collects bad data, which produces more bad steps. You can fall off a cliff and never recover.

You'll see this in Lab 5. REINFORCE on CartPole with a step size of 0.01 instead of 0.001 often climbs to about 200, then collapses to about 10 and never recovers. One oversized step made the policy nearly certain of the wrong action, and from then on it only collected data that confirmed it.

We also want to reuse each expensive batch of experience for several gradient steps. But after the first step, the data came from an older policy. PPO handles both problems.

Math refresher Importance sampling: correcting for "old" data

Numbers first. The old policy picked action a 30% of the time; the new policy would pick it 60% of the time. So under the new policy, a would show up twice as often as it does in our old data. Fix: count each old sample of a double. The ratio is 0.6 / 0.3 = 2.

In general, reweight each old sample by how much more (or less) likely the new policy makes it:

ratio ρt(θ) = πθ(at|st) / πold(at|st) ρ (rho) = 1: no change. ρ = 1.5: the new policy makes this action 50% more likely. ρ = 0.5: half as likely.

We can't recompute the true gradient on old data, so we use a stand-in (a "surrogate") objective: reweight each old sample by ρ, i.e. maximize ρ × Â. At ρ = 1 (nothing has changed yet), the slope of ρ × Â is Â × ∇log π: exactly REINFORCE with an advantage again (Lessons 5.2–5.3). It gets less trustworthy as ρ wanders away from 1, because the old data no longer represents the new policy.

TRPO (2015): a speed limit

Trust Region Policy Optimization maximizes the surrogate subject to the new policy staying close to the old one, measured by KL divergence (a "distance" between probability distributions). For example, an old policy that was 50/50 between two actions and a new one at 90/10 are KL ≈ 0.51 apart (0.5 × log(0.5/0.9) + 0.5 × log(0.5/0.1) = −0.29 + 0.80); identical policies are 0 apart. TRPO's rule: KL(πold ‖ πθ) ≤ δ, read "the KL from the old policy to the new one must stay below a small limit δ" (the ‖ just separates the two distributions; δ here is a limit like 0.01, not a TD error). You'll meet this number again as RLHF's leash in Phase 7. TRPO enforces this speed limit exactly, but checking "am I still inside the safe zone?" needs heavy second-order math: expensive, fiddly and painful to implement on big networks. People loved the idea and hated the code.

PPO (2017): just clip it

💡 Big idea Proximal Policy Optimization gets the same effect with one line: clip the ratio to [1 − ε, 1 + ε] (typically ε = 0.2). Once an action's probability has moved more than 20% in the helpful direction, that sample's gradient switches off: there's no more reward for pushing it further. That makes it safe to do several epochs of updates on the same batch.
🧠 Analogy: renovating one room at a time

If a house mostly works, you don't knock it all down at once based on one inspection report: the report was written about the old house, and big changes make it unreliable. You renovate in steps, re-inspect, renovate again. PPO's clip is that rule: stop rewarding any change beyond about 20% per round, then collect fresh data.

LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t[ min( ρt(θ) Ât,   clip(ρt(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε) Ât ) ] "take the more pessimistic of the clipped and unclipped objective". 𝔼t = average over the samples in the batch; ε (epsilon) = the allowed wiggle room, usually 0.2.

Soft limit, not a wall: the clip only stops this sample from pushing further. Other samples in the batch (the same action in similar states) can still drag the probability past 20%. Engstrom et al. (2020, in Explore more) found ratios often end up outside the range in practice.

✏️ Worked example: when does the clip switch the gradient off?

ε = 0.2, so the allowed ratio range is [0.8, 1.2].

  • Good action (Â = +2), ratio 1.1: min(1.1 × 2, 1.1 × 2) = 2.2. Inside the range → keep pushing it up.
  • Good action (Â = +2), ratio 1.5: min(1.5 × 2 = 3.0, 1.2 × 2 = 2.4) = 2.4, a flat value → no more push. It already grew 50%; that's enough for this round.
  • Bad action (Â = −2), ratio 0.7: min(−1.4, 0.8 × (−2) = −1.6) = −1.6, flat → it's already 30% less likely; stop pushing.

The full PPO recipe

for iteration in range(N):
    batch = run_policy(parallel_envs, steps=2048)              # on-policy data (A2C-style)
    adv, value_targets = GAE(batch, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95)        # Lesson 5.5
    for epoch in range(10):                                    # 10 passes over the same batch!
        for mb in minibatches(batch, size=64):
            ratio = exp(logp_new(mb) - mb.logp_old)
            policy_loss = -min(ratio * adv, clip(ratio, 0.8, 1.2) * adv).mean()
            value_loss  = (V(mb.s) - value_targets) ** 2               # critic
            loss = policy_loss + 0.5 * value_loss - 0.01 * entropy    # entropy bonus = keep exploring
            step(loss)
🤖 Sidebar · Sim-to-real: how PPO reached real robots

Real robots are slow and fragile, so you train in a simulator. But no simulator is exact. The trick is domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017): train across many randomly varied simulated worlds (friction, masses, colors, delays), so the real world looks like just one more variation.

  • Rubik's Cube (OpenAI, 2019): the Dactyl robot hand was trained with PPO plus automatic domain randomization: widen the randomness each time the policy masters the current range. It solved 15-move scrambles 60% of the time and the hardest 26-move ones 20% (paper; a standard solver chose the moves, RL did the finger work).
  • ANYmal (ETH Zurich, 2021): PPO with thousands of simulated robots in parallel on one GPU learned to walk on flat ground in under 4 minutes and on rough terrain in about 20, then transferred to the real four-legged robot (Rudin et al.).

🌍 Where it's used
PPO at massive scale 🧪 Research · 2019 OpenAI Five: Dota 2

How: state = arrays of game data (not pixels) · action = one of 8,000–80,000 choices per step · reward = winning, plus signals like characters dying and resources collected, minus the enemy team's reward.

Why this method: with 10 months of continual training on batches of about 3 million steps, one wrecked update could waste weeks; PPO's capped steps are cheap insurance. It beat world champions OG in 2019.

PPO-clip for chip layout 🏭 In production · 2021 Google AlphaChip: placing blocks on TPU chips

How: state = the chip canvas and the blocks placed so far · action = where to put the next block · reward = −(estimated wire length + congestion), given once the layout is done.

Why this method: a long chain of placements with one score at the end is noisy, so cautious clipped updates help. Google says it shaped its last three TPU generations, though outside researchers dispute how much it beats other tools.

PPO + a KL leash on an LLM 🏭 In production · 2022 OpenAI InstructGPT

How: state = a customer's prompt · action = the whole response · reward = a learned score of human preference, minus a per-token KL penalty for drifting from the starting model.

Why this method: the learned score can be fooled, so capped steps plus the KL leash limit over-optimizing it. Labelers preferred the 1.3B model to the 175B GPT-3, and InstructGPT became OpenAI's default API model.

🤯 Fun fact

PPO looks simple, but a famous 2022 blog post, "The 37 Implementation Details of Proximal Policy Optimization" (Huang et al., ICLR blog track), showed that its performance depends on many small choices: advantage normalization, orthogonal initialization, learning-rate annealing, value clipping and more. Reproducing RL papers is hard, and that's a Phase 9 topic.

🌉 LLM bridge · from PPO to RLHF to GRPO
  • RLHF (2022): PPO on an LLM, with reward = reward-model score − β · KL(π ‖ πreference), where KL measures how far the model has drifted from the original and β sets how much drifting costs. The KL term is another "don't drift too far" leash, against the original model rather than last step's policy (Phase 7).
  • GRPO (2024): keeps PPO's clipped ratio and KL penalty, but drops the critic and uses group-normalized rewards as the advantage (Phase 8).
  • DAPO (2025): uses an asymmetric clip (1 − 0.2, 1 + 0.28) so that rare, low-probability tokens have more room to grow, which helps keep exploration alive (Phase 8).

Almost every LLM RL algorithm you'll meet is "PPO, with one or two pieces changed". Learn this lesson well.

📝 TL;DR
  • RL updates must be cautious: a bad policy collects bad data.
  • Ratio ρ = πnew/πold lets us reuse data. At ρ = 1 it's plain REINFORCE. TRPO constrains KL with second-order math.
  • PPO: min(ρÂ, clip(ρ, 1±ε)Â). Simple, stable, several epochs per batch. Plus a value loss and an entropy bonus.
  • RLHF = PPO + KL to a reference model. GRPO = PPO without the critic.
