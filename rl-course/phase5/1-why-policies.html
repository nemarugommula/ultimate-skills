Phase 5 · Lesson 1

Why learn the policy directly?

Instead of learning values and acting by argmax, learn the probabilities of each action and adjust them directly.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Policy π(a|s) = the probability of picking action a in state s (Lesson 0.2).
  • Value-based methods (Phases 3–4) learn Q and then pick the action with the highest Q (that's "argmax").
🎯 Hook

In 2017, the poker AI Libratus beat four top professionals at no-limit Texas hold'em. (It wasn't trained with this phase's methods: it used a game-theory algorithm called counterfactual regret minimization, CFR.) Its lesson still applies here: a good poker strategy must mix. Any agent can bluff ("always bet big with this weak hand"), but if it always does the same thing in the same spot, opponents read it instantly. The winning move is "bluff here 30% of the time". An argmax agent is deterministic, so it can't mix. A policy that outputs probabilities can.

💡 Big idea Policy-based methods learn a parameterized policy πθ(a|s) (read: "the probability that the policy with weights θ picks action a in state s"; usually a neural network that outputs action probabilities) and adjust θ directly to get more reward. No argmax over Q-values needed.
🧠 Analogy: instincts vs scorecards

A value-based agent is like a tennis player who, before every swing, computes a score for every possible shot and picks the best. A policy-based agent is like a player who has simply developed good instincts: given the situation, certain shots feel right, with some variety. Practice tunes the instincts directly. For many problems (especially huge or continuous choices), tuning instincts is easier than scoring everything.

Four reasons to learn the policy (and one catch)

🎚️

1. Continuous actions

A robot arm chooses a torque from infinitely many values. You can't take an argmax over infinitely many actions, but a network can output "mean 2.3 Nm, std 0.4".

🎲

2. The best policy may be random

Rock-paper-scissors, poker, and situations where you can't tell two states apart (e.g. a poker hand where you can't see the opponent's cards). Argmax is always deterministic. (Playground below.)

🧈

3. Smoother learning

A tiny change in Q can flip the argmax and change behavior completely. Nudging probabilities changes behavior gradually and more stably.

🎯

4. Sometimes simpler

"Push the handle left" can be far easier to learn than the exact long-term value of every possible push.

⚠️

The catch

Plain policy learning is noisy (one lucky episode can steer it), needs fresh data from the current policy every time (on-policy: no replay buffer), and can get stuck once it stops trying alternatives. Lessons 5.3–5.6 fix these one by one.

🎮 Playground · Rock-paper-scissors against an opponent who studies you

Set your strategy (probabilities). Your opponent knows it and plays the best counter. Win = +1, lose = −1, draw = 0.

Any lean toward one option gets exploited. The only strategy that can't be exploited is ⅓ each, a random policy. A Q-learner with a deterministic argmax would lose every round to a smart opponent.

Math refresher Softmax: turning scores into probabilities

A policy network outputs a score (a "logit" or preference) h for each action. Softmax turns scores into probabilities that are positive and sum to 1:

π(a) = eh(a) / Σb eh(b) "exponentiate every score (makes them positive), then divide by the total (makes them sum to 1)"

Bigger score → more probable, but never 100%, so the policy keeps exploring. Dividing scores by a temperature before softmax makes the policy sharper (T < 1) or flatter (T > 1).

Continuous actions: a bell-curve policy

Softmax handles a menu of actions. For a robot joint, the network instead outputs two numbers, say μ = 2.3 Nm and σ = 0.4 ("aim for 2.3, give or take 0.4"), and the torque is drawn from a bell curve (a Gaussian) centered there. μ (mu) is the mean, σ (sigma) the spread. Learning nudges μ toward torques that worked, and shrinks σ as the agent grows confident.

Policy-gradient methods need the log-probability of the action taken ("log" turns tiny probabilities into manageable negative numbers; refresher in 5.2). For a bell curve it's simple: the penalty grows with the squared distance from the mean, measured in spreads.

log π(a) = −(a − μ)² / (2σ²) − log σ + const "how far the action landed from the aim, in units of spread, squared and halved; minus a term for how wide the curve is". const is a fixed number that never changes, so it never affects learning.
✏️ Worked example: three torques, one policy

With μ = 2.3 and σ = 0.4, the −log σ term is the same for every action, so compare only the first term:

  • a = 2.3 (on target): −0² / 0.32 = 0.
  • a = 2.7 (one spread away): −0.4² / 0.32 = −0.5.
  • a = 3.1 (two spreads away): −0.8² / 0.32 = −2.0.

Twice as far away costs four times as much: torques near the aim are much likelier. If 2.7 earned a big reward, learning raises its log-probability, which means sliding μ toward 2.7.

🌍 Where it's used
Continuous actions: the policy outputs the numbers 🧪 Research · 2023 Swift (Univ. of Zurich): drone racing against world champions

How: state = the drone's position, speed and orientation, estimated from camera + motion sensors · action = total thrust and spin rates (real numbers) · reward = progress toward the next gate + keeping it in view.

Why this method: thrust and spin rates are real numbers chosen many times a second; a policy network outputs them directly instead of searching for a max over infinitely many. Swift won 15 of 25 races.

Stochastic (deliberately random) policies 🧪 Research · 2022 DeepMind DeepNash: Stratego, a game of hidden pieces

How: state = the board as DeepNash sees it (enemy pieces hidden) · action = the starting layout of its 40 pieces, then each move · reward = win or lose · the policy is random on purpose.

Why this method: with hidden information, a predictable player gets exploited. It generates billions of different setups and sometimes bluffs, reaching the top 3 of the Gravon platform's human rankings.

Probabilities over many moves 📜 History · 2016 DeepMind AlphaGo's policy network

How: state = the Go board · policy = a network giving every legal move (action) a probability · reward = win or lose. Trained first to copy expert games, then by self-play.

Why this method: Go has far too many move sequences to try them all; move probabilities let its lookahead focus on the few promising ones. AlphaGo beat European champion Fan Hui 5–0.

🤯 Fun fact

Game theorists proved long ago that in games like rock-paper-scissors the unbeatable strategy (a Nash equilibrium) is often random. John Nash showed in 1950 that every finite game has such an equilibrium if randomized strategies are allowed, work that later earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics.

🌉 LLM bridge · an LLM is a softmax policy

Look at the math refresher again: scores → softmax → probabilities, with a temperature knob. That is exactly what an LLM does over its ~100,000-token vocabulary at every step. It's already a parameterized stochastic policy πθ(token | text so far). That's why LLM RL (RLHF, GRPO) is built on policy-gradient methods, the topic of this phase, rather than on DQN-style value methods.

Check yourself ✅

1. Why is DQN awkward for a robot arm with continuous torques?
A policy network can directly output a distribution over continuous actions (e.g., a mean and a std).
2. Softmax of scores [2, 0]?
e² ≈ 7.39, e⁰ = 1. 7.39 / 8.39 ≈ 0.88 and 1 / 8.39 ≈ 0.12.
3. In rock-paper-scissors against a perfect opponent, what's your best policy?
Any bias is exploitable. The uniform random policy guarantees an expected result of 0. Only a stochastic policy can represent it.
📝 TL;DR
  • Policy methods learn πθ(a|s) directly instead of acting by argmax on values.
  • Wins: continuous and huge action spaces, stochastic optimal policies, smoother learning.
  • Softmax turns scores into probabilities. Temperature controls sharpness.
  • An LLM is a softmax policy, so LLM RL uses policy gradients.
