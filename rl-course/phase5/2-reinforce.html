REINFORCE & the log-derivative trick

The simplest policy-gradient algorithm: make actions that led to high reward more likely. Every LLM RL method builds on it.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Return G = how well things went from that point on (Lesson 0.3).
  • Softmax policy: each action has a score; softmax turns scores into probabilities (last lesson).
  • Gradient = "which way to nudge the weights to make a number go up (or down)" (Lesson 4.2).
🎯 Hook

Ronald Williams named his 1992 algorithm with a backronym: REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor × Offset Reinforcement × Characteristic Eligibility. Nobody remembers the long version, but the idea underneath it is in PPO, GRPO and nearly every RL method used for LLMs today.

💡 Big idea Try an action, see the return G, then push up the log-probability of that action in proportion to G. Good outcomes make their actions more likely and bad outcomes make theirs less likely. It's Thorndike's Law of Effect (Lesson 0.1) as a gradient.

What are we trying to raise? Call it J(θ): the average total reward the policy earns, over many episodes. Before any symbols, here is the update in action.

✏️ Worked example: two REINFORCE updates by hand

3 actions A, B, C, all scores 0, so each has probability ⅓. Step size α = 0.3. The update for each action b: scoreb += α × G × ((1 if b was chosen else 0) − π(b)).

The good action C gained probability, the bad action A lost some, and B (never tried) drifted only slightly. Notice the sizes: the chosen action moves the most, and the others move in proportion to their probability.

Math refresher Logs in one minute (you'll see them everywhere from here on)

log x answers "e (≈ 2.718) to what power gives x?". So log 1 = 0, log 0.5 ≈ −0.69, log 0.1 ≈ −2.30. Any probability below 1 has a negative log, and the rarer it is, the more negative.

The key rule: log(a × b) = log a + log b. Logs turn products into sums.

Why we use logs of probabilities: a 1,000-token answer where each token had probability 0.5 has probability 0.51000 ≈ 10−301, too tiny for a computer to store reliably. Its log is just 1,000 × (−0.69) = −693. Sums are also easier to take gradients of than long products.

The rule from the example, written for any policy: nudge the weights in the direction that makes the action you took more likely, scaled by how well it went, and average over many tries. That result is called the policy gradient theorem:

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼π[ G · ∇θ log πθ(a|s) ] "The direction that increases expected reward = the average of (how good the outcome was) × (the direction that makes that action more likely)". ∇θ reads "the direction to nudge the weights θ"; 𝔼π[…] reads "the average over episodes played with π".

Which G? For the action at step t, G is the reward-to-go: the rewards from step t onward, not the whole episode's total. An action can't affect rewards that came before it, so counting them would only add noise.

for each episode:
    states, actions, rewards = play(policy)             # sample with the current policy
    returns = discounted_returns(rewards, gamma)        # Lesson 0.3's backward loop = reward-to-go
    loss = -sum(G * log_prob(policy, s, a) for s, a, G in zip(states, actions, returns))
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()                   # minus sign: optimizers minimize

This "loss" isn't an error measure like 4.2's. It can go up while the policy improves, and its value means little. It exists only so that autograd's loss.backward() produces exactly the gradient above.

Math refresher Where the log comes from (the log-derivative trick)

Goal: maximize J(θ), the average reward: J = Σ over all actions of π(a)·R(a), i.e. probability of each action × its reward, added up. (𝔼[R] is shorthand for that average.) We want ∇J, but we can only sample actions, not sum over all of them (100,000 tokens!).

Step 1: ∇J = Σa ∇π(a) · R(a). This is a sum we can't compute.

Step 2, the trick: a calculus identity says ∇log π = ∇π / π, so ∇π = π · ∇log π. Substitute:

∇J = Σa π(a) · ∇log π(a) · R(a) = 𝔼a~π[ R(a) · ∇log π(a) ]

Step 3: a sum weighted by π is an expectation, so we can estimate it by sampling actions from π and averaging. Monte Carlo again!

Why divide by π? Sampling already gives common actions a head start: they show up more often. The ∇π / π inside ∇log π cancels that head start. With the same lucky reward, a 10% action's sample is pushed 1/0.1 = 10 units and a 90% action's 1/0.9 ≈ 1.1 units: 9× harder. But the 90% action gets sampled 9× more often, so on average each action is pushed by its true worth, not by how often it happens to be picked.

Why no model of the world is needed. A whole episode's probability is a product, so its log is a sum: log P(episode) = Σ log π(a|s) (your choices) + Σ log p(s′|s, a) (the environment's physics). The physics terms don't contain θ, so their gradient is 0. They drop out, and you never need to know how the world works.

For softmax policies the gradient is simple: ∂ log π(a) / ∂h(b) = (1 if b is the action you took, else 0) − π(b). In code: (b == a ? 1 : 0) - pi[b]. That means raise the score of the action taken and lower everyone else's in proportion to their probability.

🎮 Playground · REINFORCE learns a 3-armed bandit

A softmax policy over 3 slot machines. True average rewards: A = −1, B = 0, C = +1 (each pull adds noise). The policy starts uniform (⅓ each). Each pull: score_b += α · G · ((b == a ? 1 : 0) − π(b)).

Press "Pull once" a few times and read the message. A positive G pushes the chosen action's probability up, a negative G pushes it down, and the size of the push scales with |G|. Then run 300 pulls: π(C) climbs toward 1. Try α = 0.5. It learns faster, but sometimes commits to the wrong arm early because of a lucky pull. That instability is where we're headed (PPO, Lesson 5.6).

🧠 Analogy: a jar of marbles

Imagine your policy is a jar of colored marbles, one color per action. To act, you draw a marble at random. If the action turned out well, you add a few marbles of that color; if it went badly, you take some out. Over time the jar fills with the colors that work, but the other colors never vanish entirely, so you keep exploring a little. That's REINFORCE.

🎬 Animation · The marble jar, live

30 marbles, one color per action: 🔴 A (true average reward −1), 🔵 B (0), 🟢 C (+1). Each step draws one marble (the action), gets a noisy reward, and applies the REINFORCE update (α = 0.3). The jar is then refilled in proportion to the new probabilities.

REINFORCE vs. supervised learning

👩‍🏫

Supervised (SFT)

Loss = −log π(correct answer). Push up the label, always with weight 1.

🎮

REINFORCE

Loss = −G · log π(my own sampled action). Push up what I did, weighted by how well it went. If G < 0, push it down.

REINFORCE is a reward-weighted version of supervised learning on your own samples. Remember that sentence: it's the most useful mental model for RL on LLMs.

🌍 Where it's used
REINFORCE at YouTube scale 🏭 In production · 2019 YouTube: choosing candidate videos to recommend

How: state = your watch history, summarized by a network · action = one video out of millions (a softmax policy) · reward = your activity, added up over the next 4–10 hours.

Why this method: REINFORCE is just reward-weighted training of the softmax they already had, and it scales to millions of videos. Learning from an older system's logs needed a correction (Lesson 5.6's ratio).

REINFORCE when the action is a design 🧪 Research · 2017 Google Brain: neural architecture search

How: policy = a small network that writes a design one choice at a time · state = the choices so far · action = filter sizes, strides, skip connections · reward = the built network's validation accuracy, at the end only.

Why this method: accuracy can't be differentiated with respect to design choices, but REINFORCE needs only a score. With 800 networks training at once, its CIFAR-10 design rivaled the best human-made ones.

🤯 Fun fact

Plain REINFORCE came back for LLMs in 2024. Cohere's paper "Back to Basics: Revisiting REINFORCE Style Optimization for Learning from Human Feedback in LLMs" showed that simple REINFORCE variants (like RLOO, Phase 8) can match or beat PPO for RLHF while being simpler and cheaper. Sometimes the 30-year-old idea wins.

🌉 LLM bridge · REINFORCE on text

For an LLM: the "action" is a whole response y (a sequence of tokens), log π(y | prompt) = the sum of the log-probabilities of its tokens, and G = the reward for that response. The update is loss = −reward × Σ log π(token): a correct math answer gets all its tokens pushed up, and a wrong one gets them pushed down. GRPO, the algorithm behind DeepSeek-R1, is this plus a baseline (next lesson) and PPO-style clipping (Lesson 5.6).

Check yourself ✅

1. In REINFORCE, what happens to an action that led to a negative return?
The update is G × ∇log π(a). Negative G flips the direction.
2. Why do we need the log-derivative trick?
∇Σπ(a)R(a) can't be computed directly over huge action spaces. Rewriting it as 𝔼[R ∇log π] means sampling works.
3. The best one-line description of REINFORCE?
Loss = −G · log π(a): SFT's loss, but on your own action and weighted by the outcome.
