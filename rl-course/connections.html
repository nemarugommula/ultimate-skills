RL's neighbours: the fields RL connects to 🕸️

RL didn't come from nowhere. Engineers, economists, statisticians, psychologists and biologists each found part of it first, under different names. Learn their words and you can read their papers, and borrow their tools.

💡 The one idea they share Every field below studies some version of “choose, see what happens, adjust, so that things go well over time”. They differ in what they assume you know (a model of the world or not), what they call the score (cost, utility, fitness, satisfaction), and whether they care most about the decision, the evidence, or the mechanism.

🎛️ Control theory

What it shares: the same loop. A thermostat reads the temperature (the state), switches the heater (the action), and tries to keep you comfortable over time. Control engineers usually start from a model of the physics and minimize a cost; RL usually starts with no model and maximizes a reward, learning by trial and error. Flip the sign of the cost and most of the math is the same.

Their wordCourse wordIn plain English

cost-to-go J(x)value V(s) (with a minus sign)Total future cost from here if you act well.

LQR (linear–quadratic regulator)solving Bellman exactly, with a modelThe textbook case: straight-line physics plus a squared-error cost. The best policy is then “action = a fixed matrix × state”, computed by dynamic programming.
MPC (model predictive control)planning with a model, replanning each stepPlan the next few actions with a model, do only the first, then replan. Like the decision-time planning of Lessons 6.3–6.3b.

Real anchors

  • 📜 History · 1950s The term “optimal control” came into use in the late 1950s; Richard Bellman's approach used a system's state and a value function to write what we now call the Bellman equation. That's one of the three threads that became RL. Sutton & Barto, §1.7
  • 🏭 In production · 2018 Google data-centre cooling: every five minutes the system takes a snapshot from thousands of sensors, neural networks predict how candidate actions change future energy use, and it picks the actions that use the least energy while meeting safety constraints, which the local control system checks again. That is MPC-style control with a learned model. DeepMind: safety-first AI for data-centre cooling
  • 🧪 Research · 2022 A deep-RL policy trained in a simulator controlled the magnetic coils of a real fusion reactor (the TCV tokamak in Lausanne), holding the plasma steady and sculpting it into different shapes. DeepMind blog · Nature paper
  • 📖 Read next Recht: A Tour of Reinforcement Learning: The View from Continuous Control (RL explained by a control theorist, LQR as the test case) · Bertsekas: Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control (books and lectures, including MPC and RL)

📦 Operations research

What it shares: OR is where dynamic programming and MDPs were born. Inventory, scheduling, routing and pricing are all sequential decisions under uncertainty: order too little today and you lose sales next week. OR usually has a model (a demand forecast, a simulator) and, when the state is too big for exact dynamic programming, calls the RL toolkit approximate dynamic programming.

value function, cost-to-govalue V(s)Same word, same meaning.
policy, e.g. “order up to level S”policy πA rule: always refill stock to S units.

Real anchors

  • 📜 History · 1950s Bellman picked the name “dynamic programming” partly as camouflage: the Secretary of Defense at the time, Wilson, hated the word “research”, and Bellman wanted a name that “not even a Congressman could object to”. Bellman (1957) also introduced MDPs, and Howard (1960) devised policy iteration. Dreyfus: Richard Bellman on the Birth of Dynamic Programming (2002) · Sutton & Barto, §1.7
  • 🏭 In production · 2022 Amazon researchers trained inventory policies (state = stock, demand history, uncertain vendor lead times · action = how much to order · reward = profit) in a simulator built from historical data. Their model-based method beat model-free RL and classical newsvendor baselines in simulations and real-world deployments. Deep Inventory Management (arXiv)
  • 📖 Read next Powell: Reinforcement Learning and Stochastic Optimization (2022), an OR professor's unified framework for sequential decisions that puts RL, control and OR in one vocabulary.

⚖️ Economics and game theory

What it shares: economics models agents who maximize expected utility and discount the future, exactly like an RL agent maximizing discounted return. Game theory adds other agents: your best move depends on theirs. Self-play and multi-agent RL are game theory with learning bolted on.

Nash equilibriumthe fixed point self-play aims forNobody can do better by changing only their own strategy.

Real anchors

  • 📜 History · 1950 John Nash's short paper proving that every finite game has an equilibrium point. Nash: Equilibrium Points in N-Person Games (PNAS)
  • 📜 History · 2020 The economics Nobel went to Milgrom and Wilson “for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats”, used for things that are hard to sell the usual way, such as radio frequencies. Ad auctions are the environment bidding agents learn in. Nobel press release
  • 🧪 Research · 2018 Alibaba framed ad bidding as multi-agent RL (state = market and campaign status · action = the bid · reward = revenue or return on spend). On their industry-scale real data, coordinated bidding agents beat single-agent and bandit approaches. Real-Time Bidding with Multi-Agent RL (arXiv)
  • 🧪 Research · 2007–2019 CFR (counterfactual regret minimization) has each player keep track of how much it regrets not having played each action, then play actions in proportion to that regret, over and over in self-play. In two-player zero-sum games, its average strategy approaches an equilibrium. CFR-based self-play gave Libratus, which beat top professionals at two-player no-limit poker, and then Pluribus, which beat pros at six-player poker using less than $150 of cloud compute. Zinkevich et al. 2007 · Libratus (Science) · Meta: Pluribus
  • 🧪 Research · 2020 The AI Economist: a two-level RL setup in which worker agents and a tax-setting “government” agent learn together in a simulated economy. The learned tax policies improved the equality–productivity trade-off by 16% over baselines, including the Saez framework. Zheng et al. (arXiv)

🧪 Statistics and experiment design

What it shares: a bandit is an adaptive A/B test. A classic A/B test splits traffic 50/50 for a fixed time, then ships the winner: explore first, exploit later. A bandit shifts traffic toward the better variant as evidence comes in, so it loses less to the worse one (lower regret), at the price of a less clean comparison. Statistics also gives RL its evaluation toolkit: error bars, confidence intervals, and re-weighting logged data.

Real anchors

  • 📜 History · 1933 and 1952 In statistics, bandits are filed under “sequential design of experiments”, introduced by Thompson (1933) and Robbins (1952). Thompson's paper asked which of two treatments is better given two small samples: the origin of Thompson sampling. Thompson 1933 (Biometrika) · Robbins 1952 (Bull. AMS) · Sutton & Barto, ch. 2 notes
  • 🏭 In production · 2017 Netflix picks the artwork you see with a contextual bandit (state = you + the title · action = which image · reward = did you play it). Before going live, they score new algorithms offline with “replay” on logged, randomly explored impressions: off-policy evaluation in practice. Netflix TechBlog
  • 🏭 In production · 2016 Microsoft's Decision Service runs contextual bandits as a loop of explore → log → learn → deploy, logging the probability of every choice so that offline evaluation is unbiased. Live deployments reported 25–30% click-through improvements. Agarwal et al. (arXiv)
  • 📖 Read next Russo et al.: A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling.

🐈 Psychology

What it shares: the name and the core idea. Edward Thorndike put hungry cats in puzzle boxes with food in sight outside, and recorded how long each one took to find the escape (pull a loop, press a lever), trial after trial, until the right move came at once. His law of effect (1911) is a policy update in one sentence:

“Of several responses made to the same situation, those which are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things being equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be more likely to recur.” (Thorndike, Animal Intelligence, ch. 5)
Their wordCourse wordIn plain English
situation, discriminative stimulusstate sThe cue that tells the animal which rule applies.
response, operantaction aWhat the animal does.
satisfaction, reinforcerreward rFood, escape, praise.
“more likely to recur”raise π(a | s)The policy update.
shapingreward shaping, curriculaReward closer and closer approximations of the behavior you want (Lesson 6.5's sparse-reward problem).
prediction error (Rescorla–Wagner)Q ← Q + α(R − Q)Learn only from surprise (Lesson 1.2).
habits vs goal-directed behaviormodel-free vs model-basedCached reflexes vs thinking ahead (Lesson 6.8).

Real anchors (all summarized in Sutton & Barto, §1.7 and ch. 14)

  • 📜 History · 1927 The word “reinforcement”, in this sense, first appeared in the English translation of Pavlov's monograph on conditioned reflexes, well after Thorndike's law of effect.
  • 📜 History · 1938 B. F. Skinner's operant-conditioning experiments. Later he saw how powerful shaping is while trying to teach a pigeon to bowl by swiping a wooden ball with its beak: after waiting a long time for a swipe worth rewarding, they rewarded anything that slightly resembled one, then closer and closer versions. Within minutes the ball was bouncing off the walls of the box.
  • 📜 History · 1972 The Rescorla–Wagner model explains conditioning with a prediction error (“surprise”). Sutton & Barto point out it is essentially the same as the least-mean-squares learning rule from engineering, the ancestor of the course's one-line update.

🧠 Neuroscience

What it shares: in the mid-1990s, researchers noticed that some dopamine neurons fire like RL's TD error: a burst when a reward is better than expected, nothing when it was fully predicted, and a dip when an expected reward doesn't come. An algorithm designed by computer scientists turned out to describe a signal in the brain.

diverse optimism across neuronsdistributional RLPredict the whole spread of outcomes, not just the average (Lesson 4.4).

Real anchors

  • 📜 History · 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague: dopamine neurons in primates appear to signal errors in predicting reward, and TD learning explains their behavior. A neural substrate of prediction and reward (Science)
  • 🧪 Research · 2020 DeepMind and a Harvard lab analysed dopamine cells in mice receiving rewards of unpredictable size, and found evidence that different neurons encode more optimistic or more pessimistic predictions: a distributional code, as in distributional RL. DeepMind blog · Dabney et al. (Nature)
  • 📖 Read next Sutton & Barto, ch. 15 (Neuroscience).

🧬 Evolution and evolution strategies

What it shares: evolution improves behavior across generations even though no individual learns: try many variants, keep the ones that do best, mutate them, repeat. Evolution strategies (ES) do this to a policy's weights. They only need each episode's total score: no value function, no credit assignment step by step. Sutton & Barto call these “evolutionary methods” and note they work well when good policies are easy to find or when the agent can't sense the full state.

Their wordCourse wordIn plain English

populationmany noisy copies of θEach one plays an episode.
mutationrandom noise added to θExploration in weight space, not action space.
selectionmove θ toward the copies that scored wellA gradient estimate built from scores alone.
generationone training iterationEvaluate, select, mutate.

Real anchors

  • 🧪 Research · 2017 OpenAI showed ES scales extremely well with machines because workers only exchange single numbers: on 80 machines with 1,440 CPU cores it trained a 3D humanoid to walk in 10 minutes (their A3C baseline on 32 cores: about 10 hours). Salimans et al. (arXiv) · OpenAI blog
  • 🧪 Research · 2025 ES for LLMs: full-parameter fine-tuning of billion-parameter models with evolution strategies. The authors report better tolerance of long-horizon and delayed rewards, less reward hacking and more stable training than the RL setups they compared against. A claim to watch, not yet a standard recipe. Qiu et al. (arXiv)

📊 Supervised and unsupervised ML

What it shares: the same neural networks, losses and optimizers. How RL differs: (1) supervised learning is told the right answer; RL only gets a score for what it did, so it has to explore; (2) RL's data depends on its own actions, so the dataset shifts as it learns; (3) the score may arrive many steps after the action that earned it. Unsupervised learning gets no feedback at all and finds structure in data.

“The most important feature distinguishing reinforcement learning from other types of learning is that it uses training information that evaluates the actions taken rather than instructs by giving correct actions.” (Sutton & Barto, ch. 2)

  • 🧪 Research · 2022 InstructGPT stacks all three: a model pretrained on text, then fine-tuned on human demonstrations (supervised), then trained with RL on human preference scores. Ouyang et al. (arXiv)

🦉 Philosophy and AI safety

What it shares: if an agent maximizes exactly what you reward, then writing the reward means choosing its goals. Philosophers ask whether every goal can be written as a reward. Safety researchers study what goes wrong when the written reward and the intended goal differ.

The reward hypothesis: “all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (reward).” (Sutton's reward-hypothesis page)

OpenAI: Faulty reward functions in the wild
  • 🧪 Research · 2016 A research agenda that named five accident risks: avoiding side effects, avoiding reward hacking, scalable supervision, safe exploration, and distributional shift. Amodei et al.: Concrete Problems in AI Safety

📝 TL;DR
  • Control and OR gave RL the Bellman equation, with a model and a cost; RL drops the model and learns from experience.
  • Economics and game theory give utility, discounting and equilibria; self-play and CFR put learning into games.
  • Statistics gives bandits (adaptive A/B tests) and the error bars to judge results; psychology gave the name and the law of effect; neuroscience found the TD error in dopamine.
  • Evolution strategies optimize from scores alone; supervised learning gets answers, RL only gets grades; safety asks whether the grade is what we meant.
