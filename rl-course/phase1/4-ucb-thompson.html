Phase 1 · Lesson 4

Smarter exploration: UCB & Thompson sampling

Don't explore at random. Explore the things you're unsure about.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • ε-greedy: explore at random with probability ε (Lesson 1.3).
  • N(a) = how many times action a has been tried so far; ln = the natural logarithm, a function that grows very slowly (ln 100 ≈ 4.6, ln 10,000 ≈ 9.2).
🎯 Hook

In 1933, William R. Thompson proposed a clever way to decide which medical treatment to give the next patient. The idea was mostly ignored for about 80 years. Around 2010, researchers at Yahoo and Microsoft rediscovered it and found it beat nearly everything else for choosing ads and news articles. Today it's one of the most popular bandit algorithms. You'll build it in this lesson.

What's wrong with ε-greedy?

When ε-greedy explores, it picks completely at random. It's just as likely to re-try a machine it has tested 500 times and knows is bad as one it has tried only twice. That's wasteful.

💡 Big idea Explore based on uncertainty. An action you've barely tried might be great, so it's worth checking. An action you've tried a lot and know is bad isn't worth re-trying.
🧠 Analogy: 2 reviews vs 500 reviews

Restaurant A has 4.2 stars from 500 reviews. Restaurant B has 4.0 stars from 2 reviews. A is reliably good. B is a mystery: it could easily be a 4.8 or a 3.0. UCB says "give B a chance, because it might be great and you know little about it". Once B has many reviews, its bonus disappears and it's judged on its real score.

You've already seen a crude version of this: optimistic starting values (Lesson 1.3: start every estimate too high so greedy tries everything; with 1/N each machine gets just one try, so it needs a fixed α or a pretend first sample, and it only helps early). UCB keeps that optimism going for the whole run, sized by how unsure you are.

Trick 1: UCB, "optimism in the face of uncertainty"

UCB = Upper Confidence Bound. For each action, ask: "How good could this plausibly be?" Then pick the action with the highest plausible value.

Stage 1, the idea: score(a) = Q(a) + bonus(a). "What I've seen so far, plus a 'but maybe I'm wrong' bonus." The bonus is big for actions tried only a few times and shrinks as N(a), the number of times you've tried a, grows.

Stage 2, the bonus exactly: bonus(a) = c · √(ln t / N(a)), where t = total pulls so far and c = how adventurous you are. Picking the action with the biggest score (that's what argmax means) gives the full rule:

At = argmaxa [ Q(a) + c · √( ln t / N(a) ) ] "pick the action whose (estimate + uncertainty bonus) is biggest"
  • Q(a): what we've seen so far (exploit part)
  • The bonus: big when N(a) (times tried) is small, which is the explore part
  • t: total steps so far. While an action sits unpicked, its N(a) stays put but ln t keeps creeping up, so its bonus slowly grows. Neglected actions get re-checked, but only rarely, because ln t grows very slowly
  • c: how much you value exploration (the dial)
Math refresher √ and ln: why the bonus has this shape

1/√N: uncertainty shrinks with more samples

When you average N noisy samples, your uncertainty shrinks like 1/√N. It's the standard error from Lesson 0.5 (σ/√n: how much an average wobbles from run to run), which we bookmarked for exactly this moment. Going from 1 to 4 samples halves it, but halving it again takes 16, and again 64:

ln t: the world's slowest-growing function

ln (natural log) answers "e to what power gives t?" (e ≈ 2.718). What matters here: it grows very slowly.

So as time passes, the bonus of an unvisited action creeps up slowly. Eventually it gets checked again, but rarely.

✏️ Worked example: one UCB decision

After t = 100 pulls (ln 100 ≈ 4.6), with c = 0.5:

  • Machine A: Q = 0.60, tried 80 times. Bonus = 0.5 × √(4.6 / 80) ≈ 0.12. Score ≈ 0.72.
  • Machine B: Q = 0.50, tried 5 times. Bonus = 0.5 × √(4.6 / 5) ≈ 0.48. Score ≈ 0.98.

UCB picks B, even though its estimate is lower, because we're still very unsure about it. If B really is worse, a few more pulls will shrink its bonus and A takes over again.

Why optimism, not pessimism?

Because optimism fixes its own mistakes and pessimism doesn't. Overrate a machine → you try it → you find out it's worse → its bonus shrinks. Mistake corrected. Underrate a machine → you never try it → you never find out. Mistake kept forever (that's the greedy trap from 1.3).

Trick 2: Thompson sampling, "act on a plausible guess"

Instead of keeping one number per action, keep a belief: a spread of how likely each win rate is. Then, at every step:

  1. For each machine, draw one random guess from its belief.
  2. Play the machine with the highest guess.
  3. Update that machine's belief with the result.

A machine you're unsure about has a wide belief, so sometimes its random guess is very high and it gets explored. A machine you're sure is bad has a narrow, low belief, so it almost never wins the draw. Exploration comes from the uncertainty itself.

Why this is the right amount of exploring: Thompson plays each machine with exactly the probability that it is the best one, given what you've seen so far. If the evidence says machine C has a 30% chance of being the best, C gets played about 30% of the time. As the evidence firms up, that chance heads to 0% or 100% on its own.

Math refresher The Beta distribution = a belief about a win rate

For win/lose machines, the belief is a Beta(wins + 1, losses + 1) curve (the +1s mean "start with no opinion": a flat curve before any data). You don't need its formula, just how it behaves:

  • 0 wins, 0 losses → flat: "no idea, any win rate is possible".
  • The peak sits near wins / (wins + losses).
  • More data → narrower: "I'm more and more sure".

In numbers: 3 wins, 1 loss → Beta(4, 2). It peaks at 0.75, but it's wide: there's still about a 9% chance the true win rate is 0.4 or lower. 30 wins, 10 losses → Beta(31, 11). Same peak at 0.75, but now the chance of 0.4 or lower is about 4 in a million. Same ratio, much more certainty.

Each update is just wins += 1 or losses += 1, about as simple as learning gets.

The race 🏁

Let's compare everything on win/lose machines, averaged over many runs. We plot cumulative regret (total coins lost compared with always playing the best machine). Lower is better, and a curve that flattens out means the algorithm has stopped making mistakes.

🎮 Playground · Greedy vs ε-greedy vs UCB vs Thompson
  • Greedy: a straight line up. It gets stuck, so it keeps losing at the same rate.
  • ε-greedy: also roughly a straight line, because it keeps exploring at random forever.
  • UCB & Thompson: curves that bend and flatten, growing only about logarithmically. Mathematicians (Lai & Robbins, 1985) proved that this is the best rate any algorithm can achieve.

And the WWII problem from Lesson 1.1? In 1979 John Gittins found an exact answer for the discounted version (future pulls count less by γ, Lesson 0.3): give each machine one score, its Gittins index, computed from that machine's own history alone, and always play the highest. Whittle's "intellectual sabotage" quote comes from his comments on that very paper. The index is expensive to compute, so in practice people use UCB and Thompson.

⚠️ Real-world gotcha: UCB's c matters

Textbook UCB1 uses c ≈ √2 ≈ 1.4, the value that comes with a mathematical guarantee. But over a short game like this one (1,000 steps), c = 1.4 or 2 explores so much that it can do worse than ε-greedy. We use c = 0.5 here. You'll measure this yourself in Lab 1. The lesson: guarantees about the long run don't always tell you what works best over a short run. Always test.

Contextual bandits: bandits that read the room

A news site has 20 articles (arms) to put at the top. The best one for a sports fan in Mumbai isn't the best for a gardener in Leeds. So before each pull you see a context: who the user is, what page they're on, the time of day. You learn a separate estimate (or a model that predicts one) for each context, and pick an arm for this context. That's a contextual bandit.

This is how news, ads and artwork personalization run in practice: Microsoft's Decision Service on MSN and Netflix's artwork picker (Lesson 1.1) are both contextual bandits, exploring with the same tricks as above (MSN's used plain ε-greedy).

🌍 Where it's used
UCB inside game search 🧪 Research · 2017 AlphaZero (chess, shogi, Go)

How: state = a board position in its search tree · arms = the legal moves · Q = average result of the simulated games through that move · bonus = big for moves its network likes but the search has rarely tried.

Why this method: in training it had only 800 simulated games per move, far too few to try everything. An uncertainty bonus spends them on moves that might be best.

Thompson sampling in production 🏭 In production · 2017 Amazon: choosing a page layout

How: arms = 48 versions of a message (title, image, bullet points, two buttons) · reward = the customer buys the promoted service · Thompson sampling, plus a model of how the parts interact.

Why this method: 48 arms is too many for a slow, equal-split test. Thompson moved traffic to good layouts while still checking others: after one week, 21% more purchases than the median layout.

Thompson sampling with a context, for health 🧪 Research · 2019 HeartSteps: nudging people to walk

How: context = location, recent step count, time of day · action = send a walking suggestion or not, 5 times a day · reward = steps in the next 30 minutes.

Why this method: each person gives only a few data points a day. Thompson starts from a belief built from an earlier study, so it explores sensibly from day one, then adapts to each person.

A contextual bandit with a UCB bonus 🧪 Research · 2010 Yahoo! front page news (LinUCB)

How: context = features of the user and each article · arms = the stories for the top spot · reward = a click · pick the highest estimate + uncertainty bonus.

Why this method: stories come and go fast, so most have little data, and the bonus tests fresh ones quickly. On 33 million logged visits it got 12.5% more clicks than a bandit without context.

🤯 Fun fact

UCB-style thinking powered a big AI breakthrough before deep learning. UCT (UCB applied to Trees, 2006) made Monte Carlo Tree Search work, which suddenly made computer Go programs much stronger. A variant of it (PUCT, UCB adapted for tree search with a learned prior) is the search inside AlphaGo and AlphaZero.

🌉 LLM bridge · the "does RL teach anything new?" debate

Here's a live research question that's really about exploration. A 2025 paper (Yue et al., "Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?") measured pass@k: does at least one of k sampled answers solve the problem?

  • At k = 1, RL-trained models clearly beat their base models.
  • At large k (say 256 tries), the base model often caught up or even won.

Their reading: RL mostly sharpens the policy toward answers the base model could already find (exploiting), rather than discovering new ones (exploring). Later work, such as longer RL training and better exploration methods, argues that RL can expand what models can do. It's the explore–exploit dilemma at the frontier of AI, and you'll dig into it in Phase 8.

Check yourself ✅

1. UCB's bonus for an action is large when…
The bonus grows like √(ln t / N(a)). Small N(a) means high uncertainty, which means a big bonus.
2. In Thompson sampling, why does a machine with a wide belief get explored?
Uncertainty → spread-out guesses → sometimes the highest. No separate exploration setting needed.
3. Cumulative regret that keeps growing in a straight line means…
Good algorithms (UCB, Thompson) have regret that flattens out (grows like log t). A straight line means a constant rate of mistakes, like ε-greedy with a fixed ε.
📝 TL;DR
  • Explore based on uncertainty, not at random.
  • UCB: pick argmax [ Q + c√(ln t / N) ]: estimate + uncertainty bonus.
  • Thompson: keep a belief per action, sample a guess from each, play the best guess.
  • Both achieve regret that grows like log t, the best possible. ε-greedy's regret grows linearly.
  • Contextual bandit = bandit + a context per pull. Let actions change the next state and you have an MDP (Phase 2).
