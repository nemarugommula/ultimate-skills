Phase 4 · Lesson 4

Double, Dueling, PER & Rainbow

Six small upgrades to DQN, why each one helps, and what happened when someone combined them all.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • DQN's target: r + γ × the highest Q-value of the next state, computed by the target network (Lesson 4.3).
🎯 Hook

You ask 10 friends to guess the weight of a cow, and every guess is a bit off at random. If you then go with the biggest guess, you'll overestimate almost every time. DQN does exactly this in every update, with its max over noisy Q-values. Fixing it is the first upgrade in this lesson.

1. Double DQN: fixing the optimism

💡 Big idea The max of noisy estimates is biased upward (maximization bias). Double DQN uses one network to choose the best next action and the other network to score it, so the noise that made the action look best doesn't also inflate its score.
🧠 Analogy: the luckiest-looking fund

Ten investment funds are all equally good, but last year's returns were noisy. If you pick the one with the best record, you'll usually be disappointed next year: it looked best partly through luck. Taking the max of noisy numbers picks out the luckiest one, so the max is systematically too high. Double DQN asks a second opinion to score the winner, which doesn't share the first one's luck.

🎮 Playground · Maximization bias

Every action's true value is 0. Each Q estimate is the average of a few noisy samples. We repeat this 2,000 times and compare: the average max of the estimates (DQN-style) vs the double estimator (pick the argmax with one set of samples, score it with an independent set).

More actions → worse bias (more chances for one lucky estimate). More samples → less noise → less bias. The double estimator stays near the truth.

DQN target: r + γ Qtarget(s', argmaxa' Qtarget(s',a'))
Double DQN: r + γ Qtarget(s', argmaxa' Qonline(s',a')) A one-line change: the online network picks the action, the target network scores it.
✏️ Worked example: why the max is too optimistic

Two actions are truly worth 1.0 each. The network's noisy estimates: 1.3 and 0.8. Plain DQN's target uses the max: 1.3, an overestimate of 0.3. Noise in the other direction (0.7 instead of 1.3) would usually be ignored, because the max picks the high one. So errors only push upward.

Double DQN: the online network picks action 1 (its 1.3 looks best), but the target network, with its own independent noise, scores that action: say 0.9. Sometimes too high, sometimes too low, but not systematically inflated.

2–6: the other upgrades

🎭

Dueling networks

Split the head into V(s) ("how good is this situation?") + A(s,a) ("how much better is each action?", called the advantage; Phase 5 makes it central). In many states the action barely matters (no enemies on screen), and V can be learned without trying every action.

❌ Without it: with 4 actions, the network learns "this situation is bad" 4 separate times, each from only the data for that one action.

⭐

Prioritized replay (PER)

Replay surprising transitions (large TD error |δ|, the "surprise" from 4.1) more often. Like studying the questions you got wrong rather than re-reading what you already know. Replaying some samples more often skews the average, so PER scales their updates down with importance weights to undo that bias.

❌ Without it: a rare +100 transition is replayed no more often than each of the 10,000 boring ones around it.

👣

Multi-step returns

Use n-step targets (Lesson 3.5) instead of 1-step, so rewards spread backward faster.

❌ Without it: a reward at the end of a 100-step corridor creeps back one step per round of updates, so it takes about 100 rounds to reach the start.

📊

Distributional RL (C51)

Learn the whole distribution of returns (e.g. "50% chance of +10, 50% of −10"), not just its average. A richer learning signal. In 2020 an RL controller built on this idea (quantile-regression distributional RL) steered Loon's stratospheric internet balloons, beat Loon's hand-built controller in a 39-day experiment over the Pacific, and was deployed (Bellemare et al., Nature; the equatorial winds may have helped).

❌ Without it: "always 0" and "a coin flip between +10 and −10" look identical (both average 0), so safe and risky choices blur together.

🔊

Noisy networks

Replace ε-greedy with learnable noise in the weights, so the network learns where to explore.

❌ Without it: ε-greedy flails randomly at the same rate everywhere, even in situations it has already mastered.

🔬 Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018)

DeepMind combined all six (Double, Dueling, PER, multi-step, distributional, noisy) into Rainbow, which clearly beat every individual variant on Atari. Their ablation (remove one piece at a time) found that prioritized replay and multi-step returns mattered most. Ablations like this are a core research skill: they tell you why something works, not just that it works.

Math refresher Why is the max biased? (Jensen's inequality, informally)

In words: "the average of the best guesses ≥ the best of the averages". In symbols: 𝔼[max(X1, X2, …)] ≥ max(𝔼[X1], 𝔼[X2], …), where 𝔼[·] just means "average over many tries". Even if every estimate is right on average, picking the largest one tends to pick one that's above average by luck. That's also known as the winner's curse: the winner of an auction is usually the bidder who overestimated the most.

🌍 Where it's used
Double Q-learning (against optimism) 🧪 Research · 2018 Google QT-Opt: robot grasping

How: the grasping robot from Lesson 4.3 (state = camera image, reward = object lifted). Its target = r + the smaller of two lagged Q-networks' estimates (“clipped double Q”), a cousin of Double DQN.

Why this method: a max over noisy Q-values read from camera images is optimistic. The authors found the clipped version learned faster in simulation and was crucial on the real robots.

Distributional RL 🏭 In production · 2020 Loon: steering internet balloons with the wind

How: state = wind readings and forecasts, sun angle, battery power · action = go up or go down · reward = staying within 50 km of a target spot. The network outputs 51 quantiles of the return, not one average.

Why this method: winds are uncertain and forecasts often wrong, so knowing the spread of outcomes helps. Loon called it the first RL in a production aerospace system, as far as it knew.

Dueling + prioritized replay + multi-step (grouped) 🧪 Research · 2018 DeepMind Ape-X: 360 Atari players, one learner

How: state = game frames · action = joystick moves · reward = score. 360 actors fill one shared buffer; the learner replays surprising transitions first, with multi-step double-Q targets and a dueling network.

Why this method: 360 actors make data faster than one learner can use it, so prioritizing spends compute where it teaches most. Agent57 (fun fact below) still learns from such a prioritized shared buffer.

🤯 Fun fact

In 2020, DeepMind's Agent57 became the first agent to beat the human benchmark on all 57 games of the standard Atari suite, including notoriously hard exploration games like Montezuma's Revenge and Pitfall. It took seven years of the improvements in this lesson, plus smarter exploration (Phase 6).

🌉 LLM bridge · the same bias breaks reward models

Generate 64 answers, score them with a learned reward model, keep the top one (Best-of-N, Phase 7). The winner's score is biased upward: it's partly the best answer and partly the answer the reward model most overrated. The harder you optimize against a noisy reward model, the more you're picking its mistakes. This is reward over-optimization, one of the central problems in RLHF (Phases 7–8). It's the same math as maximization bias.

Check yourself ✅

1. Why does DQN overestimate Q-values?
Noise up or down is equally likely, but the max preferentially selects the upward noise.
2. How does Double DQN fix it?
Decoupling selection from evaluation means the noise that picked the action doesn't also inflate its value.
3. Rainbow's ablation found which components mattered most?
Removing PER or multi-step hurt the most. Worth remembering: not every published trick matters equally.
📝 TL;DR
  • Max over noisy estimates → overestimation. Double DQN decouples selection and evaluation.
  • Dueling (V + A), PER (replay surprising data), multi-step, distributional, noisy nets.
  • Rainbow = all six. PER and multi-step matter most. Ablations reveal what matters.
  • The same bias shows up as reward over-optimization in LLMs.
