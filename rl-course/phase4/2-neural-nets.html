Neural nets & gradient descent, a refresher

No deep-learning background needed: one idea (walk downhill) plus one rule (chain rule). That's everything deep RL uses, in 15 minutes.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Slope (gradient): if nudging a weight up makes the loss go up, the slope is positive, so move the weight down. That's all the calculus you need here.
🎯 Hook

GPT-style models, AlphaGo's networks and the DQN that played Atari are all trained with the same simple loop: make a guess, measure how wrong it is, nudge every weight a little in the direction that makes it less wrong, and repeat. Once that loop clicks, deep RL is mostly about choosing what to be less wrong about.

Gradient descent: walk downhill

🧸 Analogy · lost on a foggy hill

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley. You can't see it, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and take another step. The slope is the gradient. Your step size is the learning rate.

w ← w − α · ∇L(w) "move each weight a little against its slope". L is the loss: a number measuring how wrong we are. ∇L (read "grad L") is the list of slopes, one per weight.
Math refresher Derivatives and the chain rule, in one minute

Derivative = slope = "if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how much does the output move?" For L(w) = (w − 3)², the slope is 2(w − 3): it's 0 at the bottom (w = 3), positive to the right, negative to the left. Moving against the slope always heads toward the bottom.

Chain rule = multiply the rates along a chain. If a car goes 60 km per hour and fuel burns 0.1 L per km, fuel burns 60 × 0.1 = 6 L per hour. A neural network is a long chain of simple steps, and backpropagation applies the chain rule backwards through it, which gives every weight's slope in one pass.

🎮 Playground · Pick a learning rate

Try 0.05 (slow crawl), 0.4 (fast), 0.95 (bouncing back and forth across the valley), and 1.05 (bouncing outward: it diverges). Choosing the step size is the same speed-vs-stability trade-off as α in the bandit update (Lesson 1.2).

✏️ Worked example: two steps downhill

Loss L(w) = (w − 3)²: smallest at w = 3. Start at w = 0. The slope of this loss is 2(w − 3).

  • At w = 0 the slope is −6 (going right lowers the loss). With α = 0.1: w = 0 − 0.1 × (−6) = 0.6.
  • At w = 0.6 the slope is −4.8: w = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08.

Each step moves toward 3, and steps shrink as the slope flattens near the bottom. A neural network does exactly this, for millions of weights at once.

From loss to the TD update. Replace 3 with a target and w with a prediction: L = (prediction − target)². Its slope is 2(prediction − target) × (slope of the prediction). For 4.1's linear v̂ = w · x, the slope of the prediction is just x, and prediction − target = −δ (minus the TD error). So the slope is −2δ·x, and stepping against it gives w ← w + 2αδ·x. That's 4.1's TD update (the 2 is folded into α). DQN's loss is the same, with a network as the prediction.

What's a neural network?

🔘

A neuron

A weighted sum of its inputs (like the linear function from 4.1), passed through a bend, e.g. ReLU(x) = max(0, x), which just chops off negatives. One line of code, and it's what lets networks draw curves instead of only straight lines.

🧱

Layers

Many neurons side by side form a layer, and layers stack. Each layer builds new features out of the previous layer's features.

〰️

Why the bend matters

Stacking linear layers just gives another linear layer. The nonlinear bend is what lets a network draw curves, and learn its own features.

✏️ Worked example: backprop by hand on a 2-weight network

Network: y = w₂ · tanh(w₁ · x). (tanh squashes any number into −1…1. Its slope is 1 − tanh², so it's steep near 0 and flat far away.) Take x = 1, w₁ = 0.5, w₂ = 2, and target 2.

  • Forward: h = tanh(0.5) = 0.462. y = 2 × 0.462 = 0.924. Loss L = (0.924 − 2)² = 1.16.
  • Backward, one link at a time. ∂L/∂y (read "how much L moves per nudge of y") = 2(0.924 − 2) = −2.15. ∂y/∂h = w₂ = 2. ∂h/∂w₁ = (1 − 0.462²) × x = 0.786.
  • Chain rule: multiply along the chain: ∂L/∂w₁ = −2.15 × 2 × 0.786 = −3.38. Negative, so raise w₁.
  • Step with α = 0.1: w₁ = 0.5 + 0.338 = 0.838. Now y = 1.37 and the loss drops from 1.16 to 0.40.

Things that matter in practice

  • Minibatches: compute the gradient on a small random batch (e.g. 32 examples) rather than all the data. It's faster, and the noise even helps, like taking a slightly different route downhill each time so you don't get stuck in the same rut. This is SGD, stochastic gradient descent.
  • Adam: a smarter optimizer that adapts the step size for each weight. It's the default in deep RL and LLM training.
  • Supervised vs RL: in supervised learning the targets are fixed labels. In RL the targets (like r + γ max Q) are produced by the network itself and move as it learns, like trying to hit a target that flinches every time you swing. That's the root of deep RL's instability (next lesson).
🔥 The same loop in PyTorch (what the labs use) 
pred = net(x)                            # forward: the playground's forward()
loss = ((pred - target) ** 2).mean()     # how wrong we are
opt.zero_grad()                          # wipe last step's slopes (they add up otherwise)
loss.backward()                          # backprop: every weight's slope, via the chain rule
opt.step()                               # w ← w − α·slope (Adam: a smarter version)
with torch.no_grad():                    # no slopes through this block: a fixed target
    target = r + gamma * net(s2).max(1).values   # x.detach() does the same for one tensor

Two more you'll meet. q.gather(1, a.unsqueeze(1)) (Lab 4): from a batch × actions table of Q-values, pick each row's value for the action actually taken. Categorical(logits=scores) (Lab 5): turns a network's raw scores into probabilities you can .sample() and ask for .log_prob(a).

🌍 Where it's used
A neural network predicts value from raw sensor readings 🏭 In production · 2018 Google: data-centre cooling on autopilot

How: every five minutes, state = a snapshot from thousands of sensors · action = cooling settings · deep networks predict each option's future energy (reward = −energy); the cheapest safe option wins.

Why this method: thousands of interacting readings are far too many to hand-pick features from (Lesson 4.1). The networks learn their own from historical data; savings averaged about 30%.

A network that learns its own features 📜 History · 1992 TD-Gammon (Tesauro, backgammon)

How: state = how many checkers sit on each point · the network scores the position after every legal move and the action is the best one · reward only at game end · trained by TD on self-play.

Why this method: fed only the raw board, its hidden layer discovered useful backgammon features by itself. With 40 hidden units and 200,000 self-play games it roughly matched a network trained on expert games.

🤯 Fun fact

Backpropagation was popularized in a 1986 Nature paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (the core idea had appeared earlier). Hinton later shared the 2018 Turing Award for deep learning and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for this line of work.

🌉 LLM bridge · same loop, giant scale

Pretraining an LLM is this playground at enormous scale: billions of weights, trillions of tokens, loss = "how surprised was I by the next token". RL fine-tuning keeps the same machinery (backprop, Adam) and only changes the loss: instead of "predict the next token", it becomes "make high-reward answers more likely" (Phase 5). Most of what makes LLM RL hard is about what that loss should be.

Check yourself ✅

1. Gradient descent moves weights…
The gradient points uphill (where the loss increases), so we step the opposite way: w ← w − α∇L.
2. Your loss jumps around and then shoots up to infinity. First thing to try?
Overshooting the valley is the classic sign of a learning rate that's too large.
3. Why is deep RL less stable than supervised learning?
Chasing a target that moves every time you step is hard. DQN's tricks (next lesson) slow the target down.
📝 TL;DR
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − α∇L. Too small α = slow, too big = diverges.
  • Neural net = stacked (weighted sum → nonlinearity) layers that learn their own features.
  • Backprop = chain rule backwards, giving every weight's slope at once. Adam is the usual optimizer.
  • In RL the targets move, which is the root of instability.
