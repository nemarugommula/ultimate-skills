Phase 4 cheat sheet + Lab 4

Deep RL on one page, then you train a DQN to balance a pole.

🧾 Cheat sheet

Function approximation

Replace tables with v̂(s, w) or Q(s, a; θ). Generalizes to unseen states. Linear: w · x(s). Semi-gradient TD: w ← w + α δ x(s).

Neural nets

Stacked (weighted sum → nonlinearity) layers that learn features. Trained by gradient descent w ← w − α∇L, with backprop and Adam. Too big a step size diverges.

Deadly triad

Function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy → values can diverge. In RL, targets move as the network learns.

DQN

Loss (r + γ maxa' Q(s',a'; θ−) − Q(s,a; θ))². Replay buffer: random, reusable, decorrelated minibatches. Target network θ−: slow-moving targets. ε decays over time.

Maximization bias

𝔼[max of noisy estimates] > max of the true values. Double DQN: online network picks the action, target network scores it.

Rainbow

Double + Dueling (V + A) + PER + multi-step + distributional + noisy nets. PER and multi-step matter most.

LLM links

Value and reward heads = linear layer on LLM features. Frozen reference model ≈ the anchor idea. Reward over-optimization = maximization bias.

🗂️ Case files: five real problems, one phase later

The same five problems follow you through the course (meet them). Here's what this phase changes for each.

🌡️ The cooling plant

Now: a neural network reads thousands of sensor readings and predicts how much energy each candidate setting will use; pick the cheapest safe one. That is the heart of DeepMind's 2016–2018 system (Lesson 4.2).

Still breaks: dozens of continuous knobs turn “score every setting, take the max” into a big search every few minutes, and learning by trial and error on a live plant is risky.

📱 The feed

Now: features of users and items let one Q-network generalize to people it has never seen. Facebook used DQN this way to decide whether to send each notification (Lesson 4.3).

Still breaks: with millions of items, one Q-value per item plus a max is clumsy, and the logs only show reactions to what the old system chose.

🦾 The robot hand

Now: a Q-network reads camera images, a replay buffer reuses every real attempt, and target networks keep it stable. QT-Opt reached 96% grasp success on unseen objects this way.

Still breaks: every step needs a search for the best of infinitely many motor commands: workable for a simple gripper, hopeless for a hand with 20 joints.

♟️ The board game

Now: a network judges a position straight from the raw board, as TD-Gammon's did, so similar positions share what they learn instead of each needing its own table cell.

Still breaks: judging positions isn't enough for Go: with up to 361 legal moves you also want something that proposes good moves and looks ahead.

🧑‍💻 The coding agent

Now: the agent's brain would be a huge neural network (an LLM) reading the repository, with in principle a Q-value for every possible next token.

Still breaks: a max over tens of thousands of tokens at every step, with targets that keep moving, is unstable at this scale; you'd rather nudge the model's own probabilities directly.

🧩 Frame it yourself

🧩 New problem

An office tower has 10 floors and 4 elevators. In the morning rush, people pile up in the lobby; at lunch they travel between floors. Each time a moving car approaches a floor where someone is waiting, the controller must decide: stop here, or continue past. Today a hand-written rule decides, and some people wait over a minute.

Before you open the answer, write down: the state, the action, the reward, episodic or continuing (and γ), and which method from this phase (or earlier) you'd try first.

Reveal One good framing + the classic mistakes

State: which hall-call buttons are lit and for how long, each car's position, direction and load, and the time of day. Action: for the car making a decision, stop or continue (2 choices). Reward: −(sum of the squared waiting times of everyone waiting). Episodic? No: the building runs all day, so it's continuing, with γ < 1.

Method: a Q-network (DQN-style) per car, trained in a building simulator. Billions of button-and-position combinations rule out a table, but there are only 2 actions, so “pick the larger Q” is easy. Replay reuses cheap simulated hours; a target network keeps the targets steady.

Classic mistakes: rewarding average wait only (it lets a few people wait forever: squaring punishes long waits); leaving out how long each call has waited (not Markov); training on the real building; forgetting that the other cars move at the same time.

This is a classic: Crites & Barto (1995) used this exact setup, with Q-values stored in neural networks and 60,000 hours of simulated elevator time, and beat the best hand-written dispatching rules they knew of, in simulation.

⚠️ Normal deep-RL behavior, not your bug

Your learning curve will be bumpy: CartPole DQN often reaches 500, then suddenly drops and recovers. Different seeds give quite different results. This is why researchers report many seeds and error bars (Phase 0 recap), and why Phase 5's policy-gradient methods became popular.

🔬 One subtle detail worth knowing

The lab stores done = terminated, not terminated or truncated. When CartPole hits its 500-step time limit, the pole didn't fall, so the future still has value and we should keep bootstrapping. Getting this wrong is a classic bug that quietly hurts performance. It's one of the "implementation details" that separate working RL code from broken RL code.

Boss quiz 👾

Next state has online Q = [5, 9] and target Q = [6, 4]. r = 1, γ = 1. What are the DQN and Double DQN targets?
DQN: 1 + max(target) = 1 + 6 = 7. Double: the online network picks argmax [5, 9] = action 2, and the target network scores it at 4, so 1 + 4 = 5.
Your DQN's Q-values climb into the thousands while returns stay low. Most likely?
Values far above any achievable return are a symptom of maximization bias and deadly-triad instability.
Why can DQN reuse old experience but plain SARSA can't?
Off-policy learning is what makes the replay buffer valid.
🚀 What's next: Phase 5

🏁 Milestone: you've trained a neural network to act from raw experience: the same recipe (replay buffer + target network) DeepMind used to play Atari in 2015.

DQN learns values and acts by argmax. That struggles with continuous actions and huge action spaces, like ~100,000 tokens. Phase 5 learns the policy directly: REINFORCE, baselines, actor-critic, GAE, and finally PPO, the algorithm behind ChatGPT's RLHF.
