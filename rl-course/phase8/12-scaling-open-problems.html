Phase 8B · Lesson 12

Scaling RL: compute curves

Pretraining has scaling laws. Does RL? The first big study says yes, roughly, and it changes how methods should be compared.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The GRPO fixes (Lesson 8.4) and the infrastructure choices (Lesson 8.11) that this lesson compares at scale.
🎯 Hook

Meta researchers spent more than 400,000 GPU-hours on one question: as you spend more compute on RL, how does performance grow, and which design choices actually matter at scale? Their paper (ScaleRL, "The Art of Scaling Reinforcement Learning Compute for LLMs", ICLR 2026) found that RL performance follows a predictable S-shaped curve, and that two methods' curves can cross.

💡 Big idea Fit each RL recipe's validation reward vs compute with a saturating curve. Two numbers then describe a method: its ceiling (asymptote A: where it ends up) and its efficiency (B and Cmid: how fast it gets there). Many tricks change only efficiency. Comparing methods at one small compute budget can pick the wrong winner.
🧠 Analogy: learning the guitar

Your first 10 hours of practice make a huge difference, the next 100 hours help less, and eventually you plateau at your ceiling. Two students: one improves fast but plateaus early; the other starts slow but ends up much better. Who's "better" depends entirely on when you check. RL methods behave the same way.

In words: reward starts at R0, climbs, then flattens toward a ceiling A. The formula below is just a standard way to draw that S-shaped curve:

R(C) − R0 = (A − R0) · 1 / (1 + (Cmid/C)B) A = asymptotic reward (ceiling), Cmid = compute where half the gain is reached, B = steepness (efficiency)
🎮 Playground · Fast starter vs high ceiling

Method X looks better in a short experiment, but Method Y wins with enough compute. A paper that only runs to C = 5 would crown the wrong method. ScaleRL's fix: run long enough to fit the curve, then compare asymptotes and efficiency separately. Their combined recipe reached A ≈ 0.61 on their benchmark, above the popular recipes they tested.

What ScaleRL found mattered

  • Loss type: losses that keep learning from tokens that moved a lot (CISPO) or check change per whole answer (GSPO) reached higher ceilings than the per-token clipping + divide-by-std style (8.4).
  • Numerics: computing the last layer in extra precision (the "FP32 LM head", 8.11) substantially raised the ceiling.
  • Aggregation: prompt-level loss averaging worked best (8.4).
  • Data: skip prompts where every answer got the same score (they teach nothing, 8.3), and stop re-sampling prompts the model already solves >90% of the time.
  • Systems: an asynchronous pipeline improved efficiency.
  • Some popular tricks mostly changed efficiency, not the ceiling.

How big is RL getting?

For years, RL fine-tuning was a small slice of a model's training compute. Labs now say it isn't. OpenAI, on o3 (April 2025): large-scale RL shows the same "more compute = better performance" trend as pretraining, and they "pushed an additional order of magnitude" in RL training compute and thinking time. xAI, on Grok 4 (July 2025): RL training "at pretraining scale" on its 200,000-GPU cluster. Exact compute shares aren't published, so treat these as the labs' own descriptions. But they explain why curves like ScaleRL's matter: when RL budgets are this large, picking a recipe from a short run is an expensive mistake.

Math refresher Reading log-scale compute plots

The x-axis here is logarithmic: each step right multiplies compute (1 → 10 → 100). An S-curve on a log axis means early doublings give big gains and later doublings give less and less, until the ceiling. Always ask: is a reported improvement a shift left (efficiency) or a shift up (ceiling)?

🌍 Where it's used
RL's growing share of compute 🏭 In production · 2025 DeepSeek-V3.2

How: one merged RL stage (reasoning, agent and alignment rewards together) with a post-training budget above 10% of the pretraining cost. Published figures like this are rare.

Why this method: DeepSeek saw scores keep improving as the RL budget grew over months, and expects more from more compute: a curve that hasn't reached its ceiling yet.

Just keep training 🧪 Research · 2025 Olmo 3.1 Think 32B (AI2)

How: AI2 continued its best RL run from 750 to 2,300 steps (21 more days on 224 GPUs): AIME up 4+ points, IFBench up 20, AlpacaEval down 5.

Why this method: the curve hadn't flattened, so more steps still paid. They stopped for budget, not a ceiling. Note one score fell: always check the whole eval suite.

🤯 Fun fact

ScaleRL checked that its curves were genuinely predictive: they fitted curves on the early part of training runs, extrapolated, then kept training. The extended runs landed close to the predictions for stable recipes. That's the kind of predictability that made pretraining scaling laws so useful.

Check yourself ✅

1. In ScaleRL's curve, what does A represent?
B and Cmid describe how fast you get there. A is where you end up.
2. Method X beats Y after 1,000 GPU-hours. Can you conclude X is better?
Curves can cross. Compare asymptotes and efficiency separately.
3. A new trick shifts a method's curve to the left on the log-compute plot, but it flattens out at the same height. What did the trick change?
Left = same reward sooner (efficiency, Cmid). Up = a higher ceiling (A). Both are useful, but they're different claims.
📝 TL;DR
  • RL reward vs compute follows a predictable S-curve: ceiling A, efficiency B and Cmid.
  • Curves can cross, so compare methods at scale or by fitted asymptotes.
  • ScaleRL: CISPO/GSPO-style loss, FP32 LM head, prompt-level averaging, data filtering, async pipeline.
  • RL's share of compute is growing: OpenAI (o3) and xAI (Grok 4) describe RL runs approaching pretraining scale.
