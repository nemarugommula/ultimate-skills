Phase 8A · Lesson 8

Does RL create new skills? The pass@k debate

RL-trained models win at 1 try. Base models sometimes win at 256 tries. What does that say about what RL actually teaches?

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Sharpening / entropy collapse: RL makes the model more confident and less varied (last lesson).
  • Chance of at least one success in k tries = 1 − (chance of failing every time).
🎯 Hook

A NeurIPS 2025 paper with a provocative title, "Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?" (Yue et al.), found that with enough samples (k = 256), base models often solved more problems than their RL-trained versions. RL made models more reliable, they argued, but didn't expand what they could solve at all. The paper started one of 2025's biggest debates.

💡 Big idea pass@k = the chance that at least one of k samples is correct. pass@1 measures reliability. pass@k at large k approximates the boundary of what the model can solve at all. If RL only sharpens the distribution (Lesson 8.7), pass@1 rises while large-k pass@k can fall, because problems the base model solved only occasionally get dropped.
🧠 Analogy: a more reliable student, or a smarter one?

After a semester of drills, a student solves problems they could already sometimes solve, but now every time. Did they learn anything new, or just become reliable? Give both versions of the student 256 tries on a hard problem: the old, inconsistent student might stumble on the answer once, while the drilled one keeps giving the same wrong method. That's the pass@k debate.

🤔 Predict first With pure sharpening, who wins at k = 1024?

Before touching the sliders: RL pushes likely-solved problems toward 100% and rarely-solved ones toward 0%. At k = 1 RL clearly wins. At k = 1024 (a thousand tries per problem), does RL still win, tie, or lose? Commit to a guess.

Reveal

It loses. With a thousand tries, the base model eventually hits most problems it solves even 1% of the time, while sharpening pushed some of those toward 0%. The curves cross. Now drag "genuine expansion" up and watch the crossing disappear.

🎮 Playground · Sharpening vs expanding

100 problems. The base model solves each problem i with some probability pi (many hard problems have small pi). "RL" is modelled as sharpening: problems the model usually solves get pushed toward 100%, and rarely-solved ones get pushed toward 0. Optionally add some genuine expansion: a few previously unsolvable problems become solvable.

Pure sharpening (expansion = 0) reproduces the paper's crossover: RL wins at small k, base wins at large k. Add even a little genuine expansion and the RL curve stays above at every k. In real models both effects happen, and which one dominates depends on the training setup.

Math refresher Computing pass@k properly

If a problem's per-sample success rate is p, then pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k. In practice you don't know p, so you draw n ≥ k samples, count c correct, and use the unbiased estimator from OpenAI's Codex paper (Chen et al., 2021):

pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k) C(n, k) = "how many ways can I pick k items out of n" (C(4, 2) = 6). So C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) = the chance that a random pick of k samples contains only wrong ones, and 1 minus that = the chance at least one is right.

Tiny example: 4 samples, 1 correct (n = 4, c = 1), k = 2. Ways to pick 2 of 4: 6. Ways to pick 2 of the 3 wrong ones: 3. So pass@2 = 1 − 3/6 = 0.5. Check by hand: half of all pairs include the correct one. ✓

Average it over problems. Never estimate pass@k by drawing exactly k samples once: it's far too noisy.

The evidence on each side

📉

"RL mostly sharpens"

Yue et al. (2025): base wins at large k across model families and RL algorithms; RL's correct reasoning paths already appear in the base model's samples; distillation from a stronger teacher did add new abilities.

📈

"RL can expand"

Prolonged RL (NVIDIA's ProRL, 2025: long training with KL control and periodic resets of the reference model) reported RL models beating base models across pass@k, including tasks where the base model fails however many times it tries.

🔍

"Measure it better"

On answers like numbers, pass@1024 can be inflated by lucky guessing. Cover@τ (Dragoi et al., 2025; τ, "tau", is just a threshold like 20%) counts a problem as solved only if at least a fraction τ of samples are correct.

🛠️

"Agents are different"

Pass@(k,T) (Zhai et al., Apr 2026: pass@k for agents, with k attempts of up to T tool-using turns each) found that RL on multi-step tool use genuinely enlarged what agents could solve on compositional tasks: the RL curve pulled above the base model's and the gap widened at large k, while supervised fine-tuning on the same data made things worse. On simpler tasks, RL behaved like the math results above. Their summary: both camps are right, on different task types.

Why distillation beats RL for small models

Numbers first: the DeepSeek-R1 paper ran large-scale RL directly on a 32B base model for over 10,000 steps and reached 47.0% on AIME 2024. Simply fine-tuning the same base model on R1's reasoning traces (distillation, Lesson 7.7) reached 72.6%.

That's the sharpening story again. RL can only reinforce paths the small model already samples; distillation hands it good reasoning paths it would rarely find on its own. The paper's conclusion: distilling a strong model into small ones is cheap and effective, while pushing past the frontier still needs RL on strong base models.

🌍 Where it's used
Reporting pass@1 from many samples 🧪 Inside · 2025 DeepSeek-R1's evaluation setup

How: for each question, sample k = 4 to 64 answers at temperature 0.6, check each, and report pass@1 = the average correctness (the estimator with k = 1).

Why this method: greedy decoding on long reasoning repeated itself and swung a lot between checkpoints. Averaging many samples gives a steadier number than one lucky or unlucky run.

Distillation instead of RL for small models 🏭 In production · 2025 Qwen3 small models (0.6B–14B)

How: the student policy writes its own answers, then is trained to match the big teacher's token probabilities on them. No reward needed: the teacher is the signal.

Why this method: on Qwen3-8B it beat RL with about a tenth of the GPU hours, and raised pass@64 on AIME. RL from the same start didn't move pass@64 at all.

Rewarding pass@k directly 🧪 Research · 2025 Chen et al.: “Pass@k Training”

How: reward = whether at least one of k sampled answers is correct, credited to the group, instead of each answer's own 0/1.

Why this method: a pass@1 reward pushes the policy toward safe, conservative answers. Paying for “any of k” rewards trying different paths, and improved exploration in their runs.

🤯 Fun fact

This debate is the exploration problem (Lessons 1.3, 6.5, 8.7) in new clothes. An agent can only reinforce behaviors it samples. If the right reasoning path has probability 10−9, no amount of policy gradient on that problem will find it without better exploration, curricula, or a teacher.

🌉 What a researcher takes away
  • Always report pass@1 and pass@k (with the unbiased estimator), and consider guess-robust metrics.
  • Watch entropy and diversity during training (8.7).
  • "Is RL teaching new skills or eliciting old ones?" is a great open research question, and you can test it on small models.

📝 TL;DR
  • pass@k = P(at least one of k correct). Use the unbiased estimator 1 − C(n−c,k)/C(n,k).
  • RL often sharpens: pass@1 ↑, large-k coverage can ↓ (Yue et al., 2025).
  • Counter-evidence: prolonged RL (ProRL), and especially agentic tool-use RL (Pass@(k,T)), can expand the boundary.
  • For small models, distilling a strong model's reasoning beats running RL on them directly (R1: 72.6% vs 47.0% on AIME).
  • Report both, beware guessing, and treat it as an open question.
