Phase 8A · Lesson 4

Fixing GRPO: Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, CISPO & more

After R1, the community took GRPO apart and found that several "harmless" implementation details weren't harmless. Here's the 2025–26 state of the art.

🗓️ Last reviewed: Oct 2026. This area changes monthly; check the dates on anything you read.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • GRPO: sample a group of answers, advantage = reward compared with the group's average (last lesson).
  • PPO's clip: a cap on how much a token's probability may grow per update (Lesson 5.6).
🎯 Hook

R1's responses got longer and longer during training, and it was read as the model "learning to think". Then the Dr. GRPO paper ("GRPO Done Right", 2025) showed that part of the growth came from a quiet bias in how the loss is averaged, one that rewards rambling when the answer is wrong. A one-line change removed it.

💡 Big idea Most post-R1 "new algorithms" are GRPO with one or two knobs turned: how the loss is averaged over tokens, whether advantages are divided by the group's spread (std), how big a step the clip allows, and which prompts get trained on (plus, sometimes, a small tweak to the reward itself). Each knob decides which tokens get pushed and how hard, so small-looking changes matter a lot.
🧠 Analogy

Think of a teacher grading essays. GRPO's original rule: each essay gets one grade, and that grade is split evenly across its words. A 20-word wrong essay loses a lot per word; an 800-word wrong essay loses almost nothing per word. Students quickly learn: if you don't know the answer, write a lot. The fixes in this lesson are mostly about grading fairly.

Fix 1: the length bias (Dr. GRPO, DAPO)

🎮 Playground · Who gets punished more: a short or a long wrong answer?

Two wrong answers in the same group, both with advantage −1. Compare the gradient push per token under three ways of averaging the loss.

  • Original GRPO (sample-level): average over each answer's own tokens first. An 800-token wrong answer spreads its −1 over 800 tokens: −1/800 ≈ −0.001 per token, versus −1/20 = −0.05 per token for a 20-token wrong answer. Being wrong at length is 40× cheaper per token, and lengths creep up.
  • DAPO (token-level): average over all tokens in the batch, so every token counts equally.
  • Dr. GRPO: divide by a fixed constant (the maximum length), and also drop the std division from the advantage (Lesson 8.3's 7-of-8 example).

Fix 2: clip-higher (DAPO)

🎮 Playground · How much can a rare good token grow per update?

Reminder: PPO's clip (Lesson 5.6) is a speed limit. A token's probability may grow by at most a factor of 1 + ε per update (ε = 0.2 → 20%). So a token at 1% can only reach 1.2% before its push stops. Rare tokens like "Wait," or "Let me re-check" can be exactly the useful ones, and symmetric clipping keeps them rare, which speeds up entropy collapse (8.7). DAPO raises only the upper bound (εlow = 0.2, εhigh = 0.28).

Same caveat as Lesson 8.3: the clip only bites on the 2nd+ update from the same rollouts, or on stale data from an asynchronous pipeline (8.11). With one update on fresh samples the ratio is exactly 1 and no clip, high or low, ever fires.

✏️ Worked example: knob ① on two groups of 8

Group A: 1 of 8 right (a hard prompt). Group B: 7 of 8 right (nearly solved). Group A's mean is 0.125, group B's is 0.875, and both have the same spread, std ≈ 0.33.

GRPO: (r − mean)/stdDr. GRPO: r − meanMaxRL: (r − mean)/mean
A: the lone success+0.875/0.33 ≈ +2.65+0.875+0.875/0.125 = +7
B: the lone failure−0.875/0.33 ≈ −2.65−0.875−0.875/0.875 = −1

GRPO treats both prompts symmetrically. Dr. GRPO shrinks both. MaxRL makes the rare success on the hard prompt count 7× more than the failure on the easy one. Same data, three different ideas of "what deserves a push".

Math refresher Token-level vs sequence-level importance ratios

The "importance ratio" in one line: ratio = (new probability of a token) ÷ (old probability when it was sampled). Ratio 1.3 means "the update made this token 30% more likely". PPO's clip limits this number.

Numbers first: a token went from 0.01 to 0.03 → ratio 3 (huge!), even though the change is tiny in absolute terms. Rare tokens produce wild ratios, and that noise has nothing to do with the reward, which is given once per whole answer.

GSPO's fix: compute one ratio for the whole answer, then take its "per-token average" so long answers aren't extreme: s = (πnew(y) / πold(y))1/|y|. Read it as: "how much more likely is this entire answer now, averaged per token" (|y| = number of tokens). One reward per answer → one ratio per answer.

🌍 Where it's used
Token-level loss + clip-higher + filtering 🏭 In production · 2025 Magistral (Mistral)

How: state = math or code problem · action = reasoning + answer · reward = correctness. Loss summed over all tokens ÷ total length, no KL, all-equal groups dropped.

Why this method: Mistral trained Magistral Medium with RL alone, no distilled traces. It nudged the upper clip between 0.26 and 0.28 during training to keep entropy stable.

CISPO (clip the weight, keep the gradient) 🏭 In production · 2025 MiniMax-M1

How: state = a problem or a sandboxed software task · reward = checks and tests. Rare tokens like “However”, “Recheck”, “Wait” get a capped weight, not a zeroed update.

Why this method: those rare tokens start reasoning forks, and PPO's clip kept silencing them. With CISPO, M1's full RL run took 512 H800 GPUs three weeks (about $534,700 rental).

The whole “emerging recipe” at once 🧪 Research · 2025 Olmo 3 (AI2), “OlmoRL”

How: GRPO plus: drop all-equal groups and refill the batch, token-level loss, no KL, clip-higher, a capped mismatch correction (Lesson 8.11), advantage = reward − group mean (no std).

Why this method: AI2 dropped the std because it inflates too-easy and too-hard questions. Together with infrastructure work, its RL changes made training about 4× faster.

🤯 Fun fact

Recurring findings across these papers: the critic isn't needed for LLM RL, whether and how to divide by the std is still contested (results differ between setups), and loss aggregation is not a minor detail. PPO's ε = 0.2, picked in 2017 for robots and Atari, remains a surprisingly good default, but the field hasn't settled on the "right" trust region for language models.

🌉 How to read new papers in this area
  • Ask which component changed: advantage, normalization, clipping, aggregation or data selection? (Which box of the 8.1 map?)
  • Check whether gains are in learning speed or final performance (ScaleRL, Lesson 8.12).
  • Check how many base models and seeds were tested (Phase 9).

Check yourself ✅

1. With sample-level loss averaging, why do wrong answers drift longer?
Per-token push ∝ 1/length, so rambling dilutes the punishment. Token-level or constant normalization fixes it.
2. A token has probability 0.02 and positive advantage. With εhigh = 0.28, how high can it go before its gradient is clipped this round?
0.02 × 1.28 = 0.0256 (vs 0.024 with ε = 0.2). Clip-higher gives rare tokens more room to grow.
3. DAPO's "dynamic sampling" solves which problem?
Every prompt in the update batch then carries signal (more generation cost, better step efficiency).
📝 TL;DR
  • Loss averaging matters: sample-level → length bias. Use token-level (DAPO) or a constant (Dr. GRPO).
  • Advantage scaling is debated: Dr. GRPO drops the std, MaxRL divides by the success rate instead. Filter or resample zero-variance groups (DAPO, ScaleRL).
  • Trust regions are being rethought: clip-higher (DAPO), clipped weights (CISPO), sequence ratios (GSPO), divergence estimates (DPPO). The clip only matters from the 2nd update on the same rollouts.
  • Emerging recipe: critic-free, careful aggregation, softer trust regions, explicit attention to data and compute.
