Phase 8B · Lesson 11

RL infrastructure: rollouts, async training & mismatches

At scale, LLM RL is mostly a systems problem: generating rollouts fast enough, keeping GPUs busy, and making sure "the same model" really gives the same probabilities everywhere.

🗓️ Last reviewed: Oct 2026. Frameworks and tricks here change fast.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • On-policy vs off-policy: learning from your own current behavior vs from someone else's (Lessons 3.3–3.4).
  • PPO's ratio = new probability ÷ old probability of a token (Lesson 5.6).
🎯 Hook

In 2025, researchers noticed something odd: the program that generates answers fast (an inference engine like vLLM) and the program that computes the training updates (a training engine like PyTorch FSDP) could give noticeably different token probabilities for the same weights, occasionally wildly different. Same model, two programs, two different answers to "how likely is this token?". "On-policy" RL (learning only from your own current behavior) was secretly off-policy (learning from slightly different behavior). Fixes ranged from importance-sampling corrections to simply switching number formats from BF16 to FP16.

💡 Big idea An RL training system has two engines: a fast inference engine that generates rollouts (most of the time goes here) and a training engine that updates weights, plus reward services (verifiers, sandboxes, judges) and a way to sync weights between them. Most systems work is about keeping every GPU busy without breaking the RL math.

Why two engines and not one? Training frameworks are built to compute gradients over whole sequences at once; generating text one token at a time with them is far slower. Inference engines like vLLM and SGLang are built for exactly that: they store each request's memory of past tokens (the "KV cache") in small pages, like an operating system's virtual memory, and keep swapping finished requests out and new ones in mid-batch ("continuous batching"). The vLLM paper reported 2–4× higher throughput than the best serving systems before it. The price: two copies of the model, in two programs, that must agree on every probability. They don't quite, which is the mismatch below.

🧠 Analogy: a restaurant kitchen

Cooks (inference engine) prepare dishes: generating answers is slow and takes most of the time. Tasters (reward services) score each dish. The head chef (training engine) adjusts the recipe based on the scores and hands the new recipe to the cooks (weight sync). The problems in this lesson: cooks standing idle while one slow dish finishes, cooks still using yesterday's recipe (staleness), and cooks and chef reading the same recipe slightly differently (the mismatch).

Synchronous vs asynchronous

🎮 Playground · Why GPUs sit idle

Each step needs a batch of responses. Response lengths have a long tail: most are short, a few are very long. Synchronous: generate the whole batch, wait for the slowest response, then train (the generators wait while training runs). Asynchronous: generators keep producing with slightly old weights while the trainer trains.

In synchronous mode, one 32k-token response holds up the whole batch. Async pipelines (ScaleRL picked one, and AReaL, slime and prime-rl are built around them) keep GPUs busy, but the data is now generated by a policy a step or two stale. That's off-policy: like the cooks using yesterday's recipe. The fix is to reweight each sample by "how likely is this answer under today's recipe vs yesterday's" (importance sampling, the same ratio PPO uses, 5.6), cap those weights so one weird sample can't dominate, and limit how stale data may get.

The training–inference mismatch

  • Problem: the two programs do arithmetic in slightly different orders and number formats, so πsampler(token) ≠ πlearner(token) for the same weights. Example: the sampler thinks a rare token had probability 0.001, the learner computes 0.0001. The ratio between them is 10, and the update treats that token as if it had become 10× more likely, which is pure noise.
  • Fixes reported in 2025:
    • Truncated importance sampling (TIS): correct each token by the ratio between the two programs' probabilities, but cap it (e.g. at 2) so a crazy ratio can't blow up the batch.
    • Extra precision in the last layer: the "LM head" is the final layer that turns the model's internal numbers into token probabilities. Computing just that layer in 32-bit floats (FP32) removes much of the mismatch (MiniMax-M1, adopted by ScaleRL).
    • Switch to FP16 instead of BF16 for both programs (explained below), reported in "Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16".
✏️ Worked example: truncated importance sampling on 3 tokens

The sampler and the learner each report a probability for the same three tokens:

tokensampler saidlearner saysratio = learner ÷ samplercapped at 2
"the"0.500.511.021.02
"Wait"0.0100.0121.21.2
"∴"0.0010.0044.02.0

Each token's gradient is multiplied by its capped ratio. Common tokens barely change. The rare token, where tiny rounding differences become huge relative errors, would have counted 4× without the cap; with it, at most 2×.

Math refresher BF16 vs FP16 in one line

Both use 16 bits. BF16 spends more bits on the exponent (huge range, low precision: about 3 significant digits). FP16 spends more on the mantissa (smaller range, higher precision). Pretraining prefers BF16's range, but RL's ratio πnew/πold divides two nearly equal numbers, so tiny rounding differences become big relative errors. Precision matters more than range there.

🦕

The ancestor: IMPALA (2018)

Async LLM RL is an old idea in new clothes. DeepMind's IMPALA had many "actors" playing games with slightly old weights while one "learner" trained, and corrected the staleness with capped importance weights (V-trace). That's the same trade and the same fix as above.

🪶

Sidebar: LoRA for RL

LoRA trains small add-on matrices instead of all the weights (rank = their size). Thinking Machines' "LoRA Without Regret" (2025) found that even rank-1 LoRA matched full fine-tuning for policy-gradient RL in their experiments, arguing RL gets only about 1 bit of information per episode, so it needs little capacity. It's not settled for every setting, so check before relying on it.

🌍 Where it's used
Sync for math, async for agents 🏭 In production · 2025 GLM-4.5 (Zhipu AI), trained with slime

How: math and code RL (reward = answer check or tests) runs synchronously, sharing GPUs. Software agents run asynchronously: Docker sandboxes keep producing episodes while training proceeds. Rollouts use FP8.

Why this method: short math answers keep GPUs busy in lockstep. Long agent episodes would stall a synchronous trainer, so the team decoupled them.

Partial rollouts for the long tail 🏭 In production · 2025 Kimi k1.5 (Moonshot AI)

How: with up to 128k-token answers, unfinished long rollouts are saved to a buffer and continued in a later iteration, instead of regenerated from scratch or waited on.

Why this method: a handful of very long answers would otherwise hold up every batch, which is the idle-GPU problem from this lesson.

Fixing a mismatch special to MoE models 🏭 In production · 2025 DeepSeek-V3.2: “Keep Routing”

How: a Mixture-of-Experts model sends each token to a few expert sub-networks. Record which experts inference used when sampling, and force the same ones in the training pass.

Why this method: the two engines can route the same token differently, so training would update experts that never produced the sample. DeepSeek found this crucial for stability.

🤯 Fun fact

In 2025, Prime Intellect's INTELLECT-2, a 32B reasoning model, was trained with RL on compute contributed by participants around the world, connected over the internet. Asynchronous RL tolerates slow, distant workers, which made globally distributed training practical.

🌉 Why researchers should care about systems

Many "algorithm" improvements are partly systems effects: a loss that tolerates stale data enables async pipelines, and a stable ratio survives precision noise. Understanding the infrastructure helps you read papers critically and run experiments that actually finish.

Check yourself ✅

1. Where does most wall-clock time go in LLM RL?
Autoregressive generation is slow, and long-tail lengths make it slower.
2. What's the cost of asynchronous RL?
Higher throughput in exchange for staleness. Importance weights and staleness caps manage it.
3. The training–inference mismatch means…
Numerical differences, worst for rare tokens. Fixed with TIS, an FP32 LM head, or FP16.
📝 TL;DR
  • Inference engine (rollouts) + reward services + training engine + weight sync.
  • Long-tail response lengths idle synchronous pipelines. Async boosts throughput but adds staleness.
  • Training–inference mismatch: fix with truncated IS, an FP32 LM head, or FP16.
  • Frameworks: verl, TRL, OpenRLHF, slime, AReaL, prime-rl, SkyRL, rLLM.
