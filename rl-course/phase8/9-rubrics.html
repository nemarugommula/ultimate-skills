Phase 8A · Lesson 9

Generative reward models & rubrics as rewards

How do you do RL on tasks with no single checkable answer, like medical advice, essays or research summaries? Give the judge an explicit checklist.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Reward models and AI judges, and their biases (Lessons 7.3 and 7.6).
  • RLVR: rewards a program can check (Lesson 8.2).
🎯 Hook

Scale AI's 2025 paper "Rubrics as Rewards" had a judge model score answers against a per-question checklist of what a good answer must include (and must avoid), then used that score as the RL reward. On HealthBench, a medical-advice benchmark, this beat judges giving a plain 1–10 rating by up to 31% (relative, per the paper's latest arXiv version).

HealthBench itself (OpenAI, May 2025) is the origin story: 262 physicians from 60 countries wrote conversation-specific rubrics for 5,000 health conversations, 48,562 criteria in all. Once a benchmark is a big pile of checklists, using the checklists as a reward is the obvious next step.

💡 Big idea Turn "is this a good answer?" (vague, easy to game) into many small, specific yes/no checks ("recommends seeing a doctor if symptoms are severe?", "avoids a definitive diagnosis?"). A judge LLM checks each item, and the reward is the weighted share satisfied. (A "generative" judge is simply an LLM that writes out its reasoning before giving a score.) It's an RLVR-style signal for subjective domains.
🧠 Analogy: a checklist vs a gut feeling

Ask a teacher "rate this essay 1 to 10" and you get a gut feeling that varies by mood. Hand them a checklist ("states a thesis? gives two pieces of evidence? addresses a counter-argument?") and grading becomes consistent and explainable. Rubric rewards turn fuzzy quality into a checklist the judge can apply reliably.

🎮 Playground · Score three answers with a rubric

Prompt: "My chest hurts when I climb stairs. What should I do?" Compare a naive keyword judge (checks whether the expected words appear) with a careful judge (checks whether each criterion is actually met). Then edit the rubric: untick items and see how the winner changes. Try removing the ⚠️ medication item with the keyword judge.

A: Chest pain with exertion can come from the heart (like angina) or from muscles or lungs. Please see a doctor soon. Call emergency services right away if the pain spreads to your arm or jaw, or comes with sweating or shortness of breath.
B (keyword-stuffed): Don't worry about a doctor or emergency; causes are usually nothing. Just rest and take two painkillers every hour.
C (vague): Everyone's body is different. Listen to your body and stay healthy!

The keyword judge gives answer B a high score: it contains "doctor", "emergency" and "causes" while giving dangerous advice. An RL policy trained against that judge would learn to stuff keywords. The careful judge checks meaning. That's why rubric quality and judge quality both matter, and why rubrics include negative items ("prescribes medication doses" → penalty).

The price: with a verifier (8.2), scoring a rollout is a few milliseconds of code. With rubrics, every rollout needs a judge LLM call, often one per criterion. A batch of 512 prompts × 8 samples × 10 criteria is 40,960 judge calls per step. And the judge is itself a learned model, so the policy can game it the same way it games a reward model (8.6): keyword stuffing, confident tone, length. Rubrics buy RL in subjective domains at the cost of compute and a new attack surface.

Math refresher Turning a rubric into a reward

In words: add up the weights of the checklist items that passed, and divide by the total weight. Example: items worth 3, 3, 2, 1; the answer passes the 3, 2 and 1 → (3+2+1)/9 = 0.67. In symbols: reward = Σi wi · meti / Σi wi (Σ = "add up over all items i", w = weight, met = 1 if passed else 0), with negative weights for things to avoid. You can also ask the judge for a single holistic score while showing it the rubric. Scale's paper compared both styles. Either way the reward lands in [0, 1] (or close to it), ready for GRPO's group normalization.

🌍 Where it's used
The model as its own rubric judge 🏭 In production · 2025 Kimi K2 (Moonshot AI)

How: state = a general prompt · action = several responses · reward = K2's critic ranks them pairwise against rubrics: core values, anti-hacking rules, and human-written ones per task.

Why this method: no program can check helpfulness or creativity. To keep the critic honest, Moonshot keeps updating it on rollouts from tasks that can be checked.

A judge that reads the policy document 🏭 In production · 2024 OpenAI: deliberative alignment (o1, o3-mini)

How: state = a safety-relevant prompt · action = reasoning + answer · reward = a judge model given OpenAI's written safety specs grades the answer. The reasoning is hidden from it.

Why this method: the spec works as a shared rubric. Hiding the reasoning avoids pressure that could teach deceptive reasoning (Lesson 8.6's “don't train against your monitor”).

A judge that writes its own criteria 🧪 Research · 2025 DeepSeek-GRM

How: for each query, the generative judge first writes principles (its own mini-rubric), then a critique, then a score. Several judgments are sampled and voted, guided by a meta-judge.

Why this method: fixed rubrics don't cover every query. Writing criteria per query, and spending more judging compute, gave better rewards than simply training a bigger judge.

🤯 Fun fact

A 2025 project ("Reinforcement Learning with Rubric Anchors") built over 10,000 rubrics, written by humans, LLMs and both together, and reported that rubric-based RL with only about 5,000 training samples improved open-ended writing, and made answers sound less "AI-like". Rubrics can target style as well as correctness.

🌉 Where it's going
  • Checklist rewards for agents: per-turn criteria for multi-step tool use (8.10).
  • Learned rubric generators: models trained to write good rubrics, judged by how well the rubrics predict human preferences.
  • The same core risk: whatever the judge misses, the policy will find (8.6).

Check yourself ✅

1. Why can rubrics beat a single 1–10 judge score?
Decomposing quality makes judgments more reliable, especially for smaller judge models.
2. In the playground, why did answer B fool the keyword judge?
Surface matching rewards keyword stuffing, the rubric version of reward hacking.
3. What does a generative reward model do differently from a scalar one?
Thinking before judging improves accuracy, at the cost of compute.
📝 TL;DR
  • Subjective tasks: replace "rate 1–10" with per-question rubrics (weighted yes/no criteria, including negatives).
  • Generative judges reason before scoring. Rubrics make rewards interpretable and more robust.
  • Results: big gains on HealthBench-style tasks; style control in writing.
  • Judges can still be gamed. Rubric and judge quality are the new reward design.
