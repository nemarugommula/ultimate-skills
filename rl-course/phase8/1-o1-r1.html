Phase 8A · Lesson 1

o1 & DeepSeek-R1: RL teaches models to think

Large-scale RL against simple correctness checks taught LLMs to write long chains of thought, check their work and backtrack. Nobody showed them how.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • An LLM is a policy: each token is an action, a whole answer is an episode (Lesson 7.1).
  • Policy gradient: make answers that earned high reward more likely (Lesson 5.2).
🎯 Hook

DeepSeek-R1-Zero started from a base model with no supervised fine-tuning, and was trained with GRPO (a simple RL method, explained in Lesson 8.3) using just two rules: is the final answer right? and did you put your reasoning between <think> tags? Its score on AIME 2024 (a competition exam for top high-school math students) went from 15.6% to 71.0% (86.7% if you ask it 64 times and take the most common answer). Its answers grew from hundreds to thousands of tokens on their own.

💡 Big idea Give a capable base model problems with checkable answers and a reward of 1 for correct, 0 for wrong. Run policy-gradient RL at scale. Behaviors that help get the answer right, like longer reasoning, self-verification and trying another approach, earn more reward, so they emerge.
🧠 Analogy: graded only on the final answer

Give a student thousands of math problems, scratch paper, and a grade based only on the final answer. Nobody teaches them to double-check, but students who happen to re-check their work score better, so that habit gets reinforced. Over time they write longer working, catch their own mistakes and try another approach when stuck. That's what RL did to R1-Zero.

Why didn't this work in 2020?

Numbers first: a model tries a problem 64 times. If it gets it right 0 times, every reward is 0, every answer is exactly as good as its siblings, and the update is zero: nothing to learn. If it gets it right 3 times, those 3 answers get pushed up, and next time it's right a bit more often.

RL can only reinforce what the model already samples. Older, weaker base models almost never got hard math right, so a right/wrong reward gave them almost no signal. RLVR took off once pretrained models were right sometimes: then the reward has something to amplify. (Lesson 8.8 comes back to this: if the right path has near-zero probability, RL alone won't find it.)

Milestones

🍓

OpenAI o1 (Sept 2024)

First widely used "reasoning model". OpenAI reported that performance improved smoothly with more RL training compute and with more thinking time at test time, a new scaling axis. The recipe was not published.

🐋

DeepSeek-R1 (Jan 2025)

An open-weight model and paper showing a working recipe: GRPO (Lesson 8.3) + rewards a program can check. R1-Zero showed pure RL works. R1 added a cold start and extra stages for readability (Lesson 7.2). Its release helped trigger a record one-day drop in Nvidia's market value (roughly $590 billion on 27 Jan 2025).

🥇

IMO gold (July 2025)

Google DeepMind's Gemini Deep Think scored 35/42 at the International Mathematical Olympiad, writing proofs in plain language within the 4.5-hour limit, officially graded by IMO coordinators. DeepMind credited "novel reinforcement learning techniques". OpenAI reported the same score for an experimental model, graded by its own panel of former medalists rather than certified by the IMO.

📐

AlphaProof (Nature, Nov 2025)

A different route: an AlphaZero-style agent that learns, by RL, to write proofs a computer can check line by line (in the Lean language). Its 2024 IMO system solved 3 of the 5 non-geometry problems. Planning by search like this is Lesson 6.3b.

What emerged during R1-Zero's training

  • Longer thinking: average response length rose steadily over training. The model "decided" to spend more tokens on harder problems.
  • Reflection: re-checking steps, "wait, let me reconsider…", and the famous "aha moment" line. Caveat: a later study ("Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training", 2025, the Dr. GRPO paper) found this kind of self-reflection already in the base model, DeepSeek-V3-Base. RL didn't invent it; it made it much more frequent.
  • Downsides: mixed languages and messy formatting, since nothing in the reward asked for readability.
🎮 Playground · Test-time compute: majority voting

One cheap way to spend more compute at inference: sample k answers and take the most common one (self-consistency). Each sample is correct with probability p. Wrong answers are spread over several different wrong values.

If the correct answer is the single most likely one (even at only 40%), voting over more samples pushes accuracy toward 100%. If a specific wrong answer is more likely than the right one (try 1 distinct wrong answer with p < 0.5), more samples make things worse. Test-time compute amplifies whatever the model's distribution already favors. RL training is what moves that distribution toward the right answers.

Math refresher Why "pass@1 with majority voting" ≠ pass@k

pass@k: at least one of k samples is correct (needs a checker to find it). maj@k (or cons@k): the most common answer among k is correct (no checker needed). R1-Zero's 86.7% on AIME was cons@64, the vote-of-64 number. When you read results, check which number is being reported.

🌍 Where it's used
RL for long reasoning 🏭 In production · 2025 Kimi k1.5 (Moonshot AI)

How: state = problem + reasoning so far · action = next token · reward = final answer correct, with a length penalty switched on later in training.

Why this method: a checkable answer plus up to 128k tokens of thinking was enough. Moonshot reported o1-level scores without tree search, a value function or step-by-step graders.

Pure RL from a base model (the R1-Zero idea) 🧪 Research · 2025 Open-Reasoner-Zero

How: state = question + tokens so far · action = next token · reward = 1 for an exact match with the reference answer, else 0. No warm-up on worked examples, no KL leash.

Why this method: it showed the recipe isn't secret: plain PPO (Lesson 5.6) on the same Qwen2.5-32B base beat R1-Zero's run there, using a tenth of the training steps.

Test-time compute (a thinking budget) 🏭 In production · 2025 Qwen3 (Alibaba)

How: state = question + thinking so far · action = keep thinking, until the user's token budget is hit and a “stop thinking, answer now” line is inserted. No reward at run time.

Why this method: one model serves quick chats and hard problems. Qwen reported scores rising smoothly with the budget, and the cut-off ability emerged without being trained directly.

🤯 Fun fact

The DeepSeek-R1 paper was later published in Nature (September 2025). Its supplementary material put the cost of R1's reasoning-RL training at roughly $294,000 of GPU time, on top of the base model's training. That's tiny compared with typical frontier-model budgets, and one reason the paper set off a wave of open replications.

✏️ Worked example: pass@k vs majority voting

Ask a model the same question 5 times. It answers 42, 17, 42, 9, 42, and the correct answer is 42.

  • maj@5 (majority vote, no checker needed): 42 appears 3 times → answer 42 → correct.
  • pass@5 (needs a checker to find the right one): at least one of the 5 is right → correct.

If the answers had been 17, 17, 42, 9, 17, voting would pick 17 (wrong), but pass@5 would still count it as solved. That's why pass@k numbers are always at least as high as voting numbers, and why papers must say which one they report.

Check yourself ✅

1. What reward did DeepSeek-R1-Zero use?
Simple, checkable rules. The paper explicitly avoided neural reward models for reasoning, citing reward hacking.
2. Response length grew during R1-Zero training. Why?
Emergent behavior: length wasn't rewarded, correctness was. (Lesson 8.4 shows part of this growth can also come from a loss-normalization bias.)
3. Majority voting can make accuracy worse with more samples when…
Voting converges to the mode of the model's distribution, right or wrong.
📝 TL;DR
  • o1: RL compute + thinking time = a new scaling axis. R1: an open recipe (GRPO + rule-based rewards).
  • R1-Zero: pure RL from a base model, AIME 15.6% → 71.0%. Long reasoning and reflection emerged.
  • Test-time compute (voting, verifier selection) amplifies the model's distribution. RL improves the distribution itself.
