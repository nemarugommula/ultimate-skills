Phase 3 · Lesson 2

Temporal Difference (TD) learning

Don't wait for the end. Update your guess after every single step, using your next guess.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Monte Carlo: wait until the episode ends, then move V(s) toward the real return G (last lesson).
  • Bellman's idea: a state's value = the reward you get now + γ × the value of where you land, on average (Lesson 2.4).
🎯 Hook

Sutton & Barto write that if you had to pick one idea central and novel to reinforcement learning, it would undoubtedly be TD learning. It's what TD-Gammon used to teach itself backgammon in 1992, and the same TD error turned out to match the signal dopamine neurons send in your brain (Lesson 1.2's fun fact, and all of Lesson 6.8).

🧸 Analogy · driving home (Sutton & Barto's example)

Leaving the office you predict "30 minutes home". Ten minutes in, you hit heavy traffic and now predict "35 more". You don't wait until you're home to realize your first guess was too optimistic: you can already correct it, because your new guess is better informed. That's TD. Monte Carlo would only update once you're parked in the driveway.

💡 Big idea After each step, move V(s) toward r + γV(s'): the reward you just got plus your current guess for the next state. Learning a guess from a guess is called bootstrapping.

✏️ Worked example: the same episode as last lesson, done the TD way

γ = 1, α = 0.5, all values start at 0. Episode: A → B → 💎 (rewards −1, then +10).

  • Step A → B (r = −1): target = −1 + V(B) = −1 + 0 = −1. V(A) = 0 + 0.5 × (−1 − 0) = −0.5. (TD updates immediately, but B's value is still unknown, so A looks bad for now.)
  • Step B → 💎 (r = +10): target = 10 + 0 (terminal). V(B) = 0 + 0.5 × 10 = 5.
  • Next visit, A → B again (r = −1): target = −1 + V(B) = −1 + 5 = 4. V(A) = −0.5 + 0.5 × (4 − (−0.5)) = 1.75. The good news about B has now flowed back to A.

MC vs TD on this example: Monte Carlo waited, then set V(A) = 4.5 in one jump (from one noisy episode). TD learned bit by bit, one step at a time, using B's estimate as a stepping stone. TD's updates are smaller and steadier, and they never need the episode to end.

It's literally the Bellman equation (Lesson 2.4) turned into a sampled update: instead of averaging over all next states with P, we use the one next state that actually happened, and the step size α does the averaging over time.

🎮 Playground · The random walk (Sutton & Barto, Example 6.2)

Start in C. Each step, move left or right with 50/50 chance. Exit right → reward 1; exit left → 0. True values: A = 1/6, B = 2/6, … E = 5/6 (dashed). Why sixths? With fair coin-flip steps, a cell's value is just its chance of eventually exiting right, and on this line that chance is "how far right you are" out of 6 positions (from the left exit at 0 to the right exit at 6). TD vs Monte Carlo, averaged over 100 runs

Press Step a few times: the moment you move, the cell you left is updated using its neighbor's guess. At first only the cells next to an exit change: exiting left pulls A toward 0, exiting right pulls E toward 1 (in the middle, 0.5 − 0.5 gives δ = 0). From there the changes spread inward. In the race, TD's error falls faster and lower. On this problem TD learns more efficiently than MC, as Sutton & Barto show (our Lab 3 measures 0.052 vs 0.095 after 100 episodes).

✏️ Worked example: "You are the predictor" (Sutton & Barto, Example 6.4)

Why should TD beat MC at all? Here's the cleanest answer. You watch 8 episodes (γ = 1):

  • once: A, reward 0, B, reward 0, end
  • six times: B, reward 1, end
  • once: B, reward 0, end

V(B)? Both methods agree: B was seen 8 times and paid 1 in six of them, so V(B) = 6/8 = 0.75.

V(A)? Monte Carlo: A appeared once and its return was 0, so V(A) = 0. TD (replaying these 8 episodes until the values settle): A always moved to B with reward 0, so V(A) = 0 + V(B) = 0.75.

MC matches the one episode it saw. TD reuses everything it learned about B from all 8 episodes. If the world really is Markov (what happens after B doesn't depend on how you got there), TD's 0.75 is the better guess for the future. TD exploits the Markov property; MC ignores it.

Math refresher Why can a guess-from-a-guess converge?

It sounds circular, but every TD target contains one piece of real information: the actual reward r. Real rewards keep flowing in at the edges (here, the +1 at the right exit), and bootstrapping spreads that truth backward one step per visit, like value iteration's ripples in Lesson 2.6, but driven by experience. With a small constant α, TD(0)'s estimates converge to Vπ on average, but keep hovering around it, because every noisy step nudges them a little. To converge exactly, the proofs need α to shrink over time (e.g. α = 1/N).

🌍 Where it's used
TD learning from logged trips 🏭 In production · 2018 DiDi: value-based ride dispatching

How: state = a driver's place and time of day · action = which ride request to take · reward = the trip fee · V learned with TD updates from historical driver trips.

Why this method: a ride's worth includes where it leaves the driver, so each match is scored roughly by fee + discounted V(drop-off) − V(here), a TD error. It ran in DiDi's production system.

The TD error in the brain 🧪 Research · 1997 Schultz, Dayan & Montague: dopamine as prediction error

How: monkey dopamine neurons fire at surprise juice (reward); after learning they fire at the light that predicts it, and dip when predicted juice doesn't come. That's δ: positive, earlier, negative.

Why this method: a plain “reward arrived” signal would keep firing at the juice. The one-step surprise δ explains all three patterns, including the burst moving to the earliest predictor.

🤯 Fun fact

TD-Gammon reached near world-champion level using TD learning and self-play, starting from no backgammon knowledge (the strongest versions added some hand-made features). It found opening moves that human experts had dismissed, and top players adopted them.

Where it ran first: Sutton & Barto trace TD's roots to Arthur Samuel's checkers program (1959), which improved its judgment of a board by nudging it toward its own judgment of positions a few moves later: a guess learned from a later guess.

🌉 LLM bridge · where TD shows up in LLM training

PPO-based RLHF trains a value model (the critic) with TD-style targets, and its advantage estimate, GAE (Phase 5), is a knob between TD (λ = 0) and Monte Carlo (λ = 1). Most LLM RL today sits near the MC end, because the reward only arrives at the end of a short answer. For long agentic tasks, researchers are revisiting value learning and TD ideas to get credit to the right steps.

Check yourself ✅

1. V(s) = 0.5, you step to s' with V(s') = 0.8, reward 0, γ = 1, α = 0.1. New V(s)?
δ = 0 + 0.8 − 0.5 = 0.3. V(s) = 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.53.
2. What does "bootstrapping" mean in TD learning?
TD's target r + γV(s') uses the current guess V(s'). MC doesn't bootstrap; it uses the real return.
3. Your task never ends (a thermostat controlling a building forever). MC or TD?
MC needs the episode to end to compute G. TD updates every step, so it works on never-ending (continuing) tasks.
📝 TL;DR
  • TD(0): V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s') − V(s)] after every step.
  • TD error δ = "surprise one step later". Bootstrapping = learning a guess from a guess.
  • MC: unbiased, high variance, waits. TD: a little biased, low variance, online. Often faster.
  • TD = sampled Bellman equation. It's the heart of Q-learning, DQN and actor-critic.
