Phase 0 · Lesson 4

RL's greatest hits & why LLMs need it

A quick history: from checkers to ChatGPT to reasoning models, and the reason RL became the key ingredient.

🎯 Hook

In game 2 against Lee Sedol (2016), AlphaGo played Move 37, a move its own model estimated a human would play only 1 time in 10,000. Commentators thought it was a mistake. It turned out to be brilliant. AlphaGo didn't find it by copying humans. It found it because RL had let it practice far more than any human ever could.

A timeline of RL wins

Click any event for the story. ● = LLM-era milestones.

1957Richard Bellman: dynamic programming & the Bellman equation
The math foundation of nearly all RL. He reportedly picked the name "dynamic programming" partly because it sounded impressive and hid the fact that he was doing mathematical research, which a powerful official in Washington disliked. → Phase 2
1959Arthur Samuel's checkers program learns by playing itself
One of the first self-learning programs. Samuel also helped popularize the phrase "machine learning".
1989Q-learning (Chris Watkins)
A way to learn the best actions purely from experience, without knowing the rules of the world. Still taught everywhere. → Phase 3
1992TD-Gammon (Gerald Tesauro) plays backgammon at near world-champion level
A neural network trained only by playing itself with TD learning. It found strategies that changed how human experts played. → Phase 3
1998Sutton & Barto publish Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction
The standard RL textbook. The 2nd edition (2018) is free online. Sutton and Barto later won the 2024 Turing Award for founding modern RL.
2013DQN learns Atari games from raw pixels (DeepMind)
The first big "deep RL" result. The 2013 preprint learned 7 Atari games from raw pixels. The 2015 Nature version played 49 games with one algorithm and one set of settings, scoring at least 75% of a professional human tester on 29 of them. Google bought DeepMind in between. → Phase 4
2016AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol 4–1
Go has more possible positions than there are atoms in the universe. Experts had predicted this would take another decade.
2017PPO (OpenAI) and AlphaGo Zero
PPO became the most widely used RL algorithm and, later, the engine of RLHF. AlphaGo Zero learned with no human data at all. → Phase 5
2017Deep RL from Human Preferences (Christiano et al.)
A simulated robot learned to do a backflip just from humans picking which of two video clips looked more like a backflip. This is the origin of RLHF. → Phase 7
2019OpenAI Five beats Dota 2 world champions; AlphaStar reaches Grandmaster in StarCraft II
Complex team games with hidden information and thousands of actions per game, learned largely through self-play. → Phase 6
2020MuZero masters games without being told the rules
It learns its own internal model of the world and plans inside it. → Phase 6
2020Loon balloons steered through the stratosphere by RL
Internet balloons can only move up or down to catch different winds. An RL controller trained in simulation beat Loon's hand-built controller, including a 39-day flight over the Pacific (Nature, 2020).
2022InstructGPT → ChatGPT: RLHF goes mainstream
People preferred answers from a 1.3B-parameter model trained with RLHF over the raw 175B GPT-3, a model more than 100× bigger. (Parameters = the numbers a model learns, like dial settings; more parameters = a bigger, more expensive model.) Months later ChatGPT launched. → Phase 7
2022Fusion plasma control (DeepMind + EPFL)
One RL agent set the voltages of the TCV tokamak's magnetic coils to hold the plasma in shape, a job that used to need many hand-tuned controllers (Nature, 2022).
2023DPO: "Your language model is secretly a reward model"
A clever bit of math that learns from human preferences without a separate RL loop. It became hugely popular because it's simple. → Phase 7
2023Swift drone beats three champion human pilots
Trained with PPO in simulation, it won 15 of its 25 races against world-class drone racers on a real track (Nature, 2023). → Phase 5
2023AlphaDev finds faster sorting code
RL played a "game" of writing assembly instructions and found routines that sort 3–5 items up to 70% faster. They were merged into LLVM's libc++, the C++ standard library used by millions of programs (Nature, 2023).
2024OpenAI o1: RL teaches a model to think before answering
OpenAI said large-scale RL taught the model to "think productively" in a long chain of thought before answering. Performance kept improving with more RL compute and more thinking time. (OpenAI kept the exact recipe secret. DeepSeek-R1 later showed an open version of it.) → Phase 8
2025DeepSeek-R1: open reasoning model trained with GRPO + verifiable rewards
Its "R1-Zero" version was trained with pure RL, and partway through training it began writing things like "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." and re-checking its own work. Nobody taught it that. It set off a wave of open research on RL for LLMs. → Phase 8
2025–26Agentic RL: coding agents, tool use, computer use
Models are trained with RL inside sandboxes (real repos, terminals, browsers) where rewards come from tests passing or tasks completing. That's part of why coding agents improved so quickly. → Phase 8

Why is it all games?

RL learns by trying, and it needs millions of tries. Games and simulators make those tries cheap, safe and fast, with a clear score built in, so RL wins there first. The real-world wins above (balloons, plasma, drone, sorting code) all did their millions of tries in a simulator or a code sandbox first. LLMs became a fit once we had rewards a program can check: unit tests passing, a math answer matching.

🔬 How an LLM writes, in 5 steps
  1. Your text is chopped into tokens (word pieces: "unbelievable" might become "un" + "believ" + "able").
  2. The model gives a score to every possible next token (about 100,000 of them).
  3. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities that add up to 1 (bigger score → bigger probability).
  4. It samples one token from those probabilities, like rolling a loaded die.
  5. It sticks that token on the end of the text and repeats until it writes a "stop" token.

Each sampled token is an action, so RL can nudge those probabilities. Phase 7.1 (An LLM is a policy) builds on this.

How an LLM is made: 3 stages

💡 Big idea Copying (pretraining, SFT) can only get a model as good as its examples. RL lets it practice, get scored, and go beyond them.
🧠 Analogy: recipes vs tasting

You can learn to cook by following recipes exactly (pretraining and SFT: copy good examples). You'll never be better than the recipe book. A chef also tastes and adjusts: tries something, judges the result, does more of what worked (RL). That's how you discover dishes no recipe book contains.

🎮 Playground · Generate vs Verify

Task A: Find two whole numbers (both bigger than 1) that multiply to 391.

× = 391
0.0s

Task B: Is 17 × 23 = 391? (Quick: 17×20 = 340, plus 17×3 = 51…)

Task A (generating an answer) was slow. Task B (checking an answer) took seconds. That gap is why RL works so well for LLMs.

4 reasons LLMs need RL

✅

1. Checking is easier than creating

Writing a perfect proof is hard. Checking a final answer, running unit tests, or picking the better of two replies is easy. RL only needs the check as its reward, not a perfect example.

🚀

2. It can go beyond the teacher

Imitation is capped at the quality of the examples. RL optimizes the score directly, so it can find better strategies (Move 37, the "aha moment").

🔁

3. It learns from its own mistakes

SFT only ever shows perfect paths, so the model never practices recovering when it goes wrong. RL trains on the model's own attempts, mistakes included.

🎯

4. It optimizes what we actually want

Pretraining teaches the model to sound like the internet. We want correct, helpful, safe. RL lets us optimize for that directly, as long as we can score it.

🌍 Where it's used
Checking is easier than creating 🧪 Research · 2024 Google DeepMind: AlphaProof at the Maths Olympiad

How: state = a maths statement and the proof so far, written in the Lean language · action = the next proof step · reward = Lean's checker accepts the finished proof.

Why this method: writing a proof is hard, but a program can check one perfectly. After practising on millions of problems, it solved 3 of the 6 problems at IMO 2024.

Going beyond the teacher 🧪 Research · 2022 Google DeepMind: AlphaTensor

How: a one-player game · state = what's left of a matrix-multiplication problem · action = add one multiplication step to the recipe · reward = finishing in as few steps as possible.

Why this method: it started with no knowledge of human methods, so it wasn't capped by them. For 4×5 by 5×5 matrices it found a recipe with 76 multiplications; the best human one needs 80.

Pretrain → SFT → RL 🏭 In production · 2023 Meta: Llama 2-Chat

How: state = the prompt plus the reply so far · action = the next token · reward = a score from a model trained on over a million human choices between two replies.

Why this method: SFT copied written examples first. RL then pushed toward replies people preferred, which only needs people to pick the better of two, not write perfect answers.

🤯 Fun fact

Sutton's 2019 essay "The Bitter Lesson" argues that across 70 years of AI, general methods that scale with compute (search and learning) have always beaten clever hand-built human knowledge in the end. RL on LLMs is a good example: let the model practice at huge scale instead of hand-writing how it should think. It's a 2-page read and worth it.

Check yourself ✅

1. Why is "checking is easier than creating" so important for RL on LLMs?
This is the idea behind RLVR (RL with Verifiable Rewards), used to train DeepSeek-R1 and most open reasoning models since. A checker gives a reward, and the model works out how to earn it.
2. Which training stage teaches an LLM to imitate example answers?
SFT = learn from demonstrations. Pretraining = predict the next word on internet text. RL = practice and get scored.
3. What was surprising about DeepSeek-R1-Zero's "aha moment"?
With only a "correct or not" reward, the model started to reflect, backtrack and re-verify on its own. The behavior emerged because it earned more reward.
📝 TL;DR
  • RL went from checkers (1959) to Go (2016) to ChatGPT (2022) to reasoning models (2024–25) and coding agents.
  • LLM pipeline: pretrain (read) → SFT (imitate) → RL (practice + score).
  • RL wins because checking is easier than creating, it can go beyond its examples, and it learns from its own mistakes.
