Phase 0 · Lesson 6

Is it an RL problem? When (not) to use RL

RL is a powerful tool and an expensive one. Three questions tell you when you need it, and a ladder of simpler tools tells you what to try first.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • The agent picks an action in a state, the environment answers with a reward and the next state; a policy is its rule for choosing (Lesson 0.2).
  • The return adds up the rewards of a whole episode, with later ones shrunk by γ (Lesson 0.3).
  • One run is luck: compare averages with error bars (Lesson 0.5).
🎯 Hook

Netflix picks which artwork to show you for each title. It tries different images, sees whether you press play, and gets better by trial and error. An agent, actions, rewards, learning from experience: that sounds exactly like RL.

Netflix's engineers chose something simpler, and they were right to. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to tell why in three questions, and you'll spot the same choice in a lot of systems that are sold as “RL”.

The 3-question test

Take a cooling plant: every 5 minutes you set the fans, and the temperature you face next depends on what you just did. Now take a spam filter: each email gets one yes/no, and flagging today's email doesn't change tomorrow's. The first is an RL problem; the second isn't. Three questions tell them apart:

🔗

1. Decisions in sequence?

Is it a chain of decisions working toward one goal (a game, a journey, a day of running a plant), not one stand-alone decision?

🔀

2. Does your choice change what comes next?

Does your action change the next state: what happens, or what you get to see, at the next step?

🏅

3. Is the feedback a score, not the answer?

Do you only learn how good the outcome was (a reward), never what you should have done?

Three yeses: it's an RL problem. A “no” anywhere points to a simpler tool, and simpler tools are cheaper to build, easier to test and less likely to surprise you.

💡 Big idea RL is for chains of decisions where each choice changes what comes next and you only get a score, never the right answer. If you have right answers, copy them. If your choices don't carry over, solve each one on its own. If you know exactly how the world works, compute the plan. Reach for full RL last.
🧠 Analogy: the kitchen

Making toast? Follow the recipe (rules). Learning knife skills? Copy the chef standing next to you (labelled answers). Cooking a big dinner where you know every oven time? Write the schedule down before you start (planning). Choosing tonight's special? Try a different one each night and keep the popular ones; tonight's pick doesn't change tomorrow's diners (a one-shot choice). Running the kitchen on a busy Saturday, where every order you start now decides which burners are free in ten minutes, and the only feedback is the reviews? That one needs RL.

The ladder: try these first

Climb only as far as the problem forces you to. Each rung is a real tool with real deployments.

  1. 📝 Plain rules / code

    If you can write down what to do, write it down. “Lock the account after 5 wrong passwords” needs no learning.

    Real example: Google's Rules of Machine Learning starts with Rule #1, “Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning”, and suggests rules like “filter out publishers that have sent spam before”.

  2. 🏷️ Supervised learning: you have labelled answers

    You have lots of examples with the right answer attached (this email is spam, this one isn't), and each decision stands alone. A model learns to copy the answers. That's “supervised” learning: a teacher marked every example.

    Real example: Gmail's spam filter. Google's 2019 post says its learned models, working with older rules, block more than 99.9% of spam, and a new model added about 100 million more blocked messages a day.

  3. 🗺️ Planning / optimisation: you know the model exactly

    Your choices do change what comes next, but you know exactly how: the map, the distances, the rules. Then you don't need trial and error; you compute the best plan, like a satnav computing a route before you drive.

    Real example: UPS's ORION software works out the order of each driver's stops from the road map. When it won the 2016 Edelman prize (the top award in operations research, the field of optimising plans), UPS expected it to cut about 100 million miles a year at full deployment. (Phase 2 teaches RL's own way to plan when you have the model.)

  4. 🎰 A one-shot choice: a “bandit”

    You only get a score, but nothing carries over: the next visitor is the same kind of visitor whatever you showed the last one. So there's no chain to plan, just one repeated question: “which option scores best on average?” The catch is that you only see the score of the option you picked, so you must keep trying options to find the best one while using the best one so far. This is called a bandit, after slot machines (“one-armed bandits”). Phase 1 is all about it.

    Real example: the Netflix artwork from the hook. Their 2017 engineering post says they use “contextual bandits”: a bandit that also looks at who you are before choosing.

  5. 🤖 Full RL

    All three questions say yes, and you can't simply compute the plan: you don't know the model, or the world is far too big to plan through. Now RL earns its cost.

    Real examples: AlphaZero learned chess by playing itself (2017). The rules are known, but there are far too many positions to plan through. Loon's balloons (below) rode the winds with an RL controller because the wind forecasts are too wrong to plan with.

✏️ Worked example: when “best right now” goes wrong

Why does question 2 matter so much? Two tiny problems, two decisions each.

The thumbnail (nothing carries over). Image A gets a press of play 3 times per 100 views, image B 5 times. Today's choice doesn't change tomorrow's visitors, so the best two-day plan is just “show B, then show B”: 5 + 5 = 10 plays per 100 visitors a day. Picking the best option each time is the best plan.

The cooling plant (choices carry over). Energy used is the cost, so the reward = −energy. Each hour you set the fans:

PlanHour 1What it does to hour 2Hour 2Total reward
Low, then forced high−1room warms up, so you must run high−5−1 + −5 = −6
Medium, medium−2room stays fine−2−2 + −2 = −4

Looking only at hour 1, “low” wins (−1 beats −2). Over both hours it loses (−6 vs −4). With discounting (γ = 0.9, Lesson 0.3) the answer is the same: −1 + 0.9 × (−5) = −5.5 versus −2 + 0.9 × (−2) = −3.8.

In words: when choices carry over, the best move now can be a bad move overall. That is the extra difficulty RL exists to handle. As equations (“average” = the long-run average over many tries):

one-shot choice: pick the a with the best average r “pick the option whose score is highest on average”
full RL: pick the π with the best average r₁ + γr₂ + γ²r₃ + … “pick the policy (the rule for every situation) whose whole return is highest on average, because each action changes the later rewards”
Math refresher Why “best each time” is the best plan only when nothing carries over

Say your total is day 1's score plus day 2's score. If day 2's score doesn't depend on what you did on day 1, the two parts are separate: making each part as big as possible makes the sum as big as possible. 5 + 5 beats any other pair.

If day 2's score depends on day 1's choice, the parts are tied together. Making part 1 bigger (−1 instead of −2) can shrink part 2 (−5 instead of −2). Now you have to judge each choice by everything it causes later, which is exactly what the return from Lesson 0.3 measures.

Programmer's version: a one-shot choice is a max over options. A chain of choices is a search over sequences, and the number of sequences grows like (options)steps: 4 actions for 20 steps is about a trillion sequences.

An honest note: many deployed “RL” systems are bandits

When a company says it uses reinforcement learning to personalise a page, it is very often the one-shot rung: a bandit. That's not cheating. Bandits are the simplest slice of RL, and when nothing carries over they're the right tool. Some documented examples:

  • Netflix artwork (2017): contextual bandits, as in the hook.
  • Microsoft Personalizer, a cloud service marketed as reinforcement learning: its own docs say it “uses contextual bandits”. It grew out of Microsoft's Decision Service, whose 2016 paper reports two live content-recommendation deployments with 25–30% more clicks. (Microsoft retired the service on 1 October 2026.)
  • Yahoo! news (research, 2010): a contextual-bandit news picker got 12.5% more clicks than a bandit that ignored who the reader was, tested on 33 million logged events.

The tell: one choice per visit, one click (or not), and the next visit starts fresh. Ask question 2. If showing today's item changes what the user wants tomorrow, the honest framing is full RL, and it gets much harder.

🌍 Where it's used
Labelled answers (supervised learning) 🏭 In production · 2019 Gmail: the spam filter

How: input = one email · answer = spam or not spam · a model learns from emails whose right answer is already known, alongside hand-written rules.

Why this method: every email has a right answer and each decision stands alone; flagging one email doesn't change the next. No chain, no score-only feedback, so no RL.

Planning with a known model 🏭 In production · 2016 UPS ORION: ordering delivery stops

How: state = where the van is and which stops are left · action = the next stop · cost = miles driven, computed from a known road map.

Why this method: the map and distances are known, so the best order can be computed in advance instead of learned by trial and error with real vans.

One-shot choice (a bandit) 🏭 In production · 2016 Microsoft Decision Service

How: state = the visitor and the page · action = which article to show · reward = a click.

Why this method: showing an article doesn't change who visits next, so there's nothing to plan ahead; it only has to keep trying articles while mostly showing the best one.

Full RL: all three questions say yes 🏭 In production · 2020 Loon: steering balloons with RL (Nature)

How: state = measured winds + position · action = go up, go down or stay · reward = staying within 50 km of a fixed ground station.

Why this method: each altitude change decides which winds the balloon rides next, and wind forecasts are too inaccurate to compute a plan from. Trial and error in simulation filled the gap.

🤯 Fun fact · Google's Rule #1 is “maybe don't”

Google's widely read Rules of Machine Learning open with Rule #1: “Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning.” Its reasoning: “If you think that machine learning will give you a 100% boost, then a heuristic will get you 50% of the way there.” The same goes double for RL: start at the bottom of the ladder, and keep the simple version as the baseline your RL has to beat (error bars included, Lesson 0.5).

🌉 LLM bridge

Training a chatbot climbs the ladder too. OpenAI's InstructGPT (2022) first used labelled answers: people wrote good replies, and the model was trained to copy them. Only then did it use RL, for what copying can't capture: there's no single right reply, but people can rank which of two replies is better. The result: people preferred replies from the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model 100× larger.

Why is it RL-shaped at all? Run the 3-question test on writing a reply word by word: a chain of decisions (yes), each word changes what the model sees next (yes), and the feedback is a score from people, not a correct reply (yes). Phase 7 builds this step by step.

Check yourself ✅

A hospital wants a model that flags pneumonia on chest X-rays. It has 100,000 X-rays, each already labelled by radiologists. What should it try first?
Question 3 says no: the feedback isn't just a score, it's the right answer. And each X-ray stands alone (question 2 is no too). When you have labelled answers, copy them; turning them into a reward would throw away information.
Which question best separates a one-shot choice (a bandit) from full RL?
Both get a numeric score, and both can use any kind of model. The difference is whether choices carry over. If today's pick changes tomorrow's situation, the best move now can be bad overall (the cooling example), and you need the return, not just the next reward.
A shop tests prices for one product each day as a bandit. Then it notices that a low price today makes customers stock up and buy less tomorrow. What has changed?
Question 2 flipped from no to yes. The state now has to include something like “how many customers already stocked up”, and a price should be judged by today's sales plus what it does to the next days.
📝 TL;DR
  • The 3-question test: decisions in sequence? Does your choice change what comes next? Is the feedback only a score? Three yeses = an RL problem.
  • The ladder, lowest first: rules → labelled answers (supervised) → planning (you know the model) → one-shot choice (a bandit, Phase 1) → full RL.
  • Many deployed “RL” systems are honest bandits. When choices carry over, “best right now” can be wrong overall: that's what the rest of the course is for.
