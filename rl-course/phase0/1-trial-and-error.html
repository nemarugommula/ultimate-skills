Phase 0 · Lesson 1

Learning by trial & error

You'll play a game where nobody tells you the rules. That's what being an RL agent is like.

🎯 Hook

In 2017, DeepMind's AlphaGo Zero learned the board game Go without seeing a single human game. It played against itself millions of times, and the only feedback it got was "you won" or "you lost". After 3 days it beat the version that had beaten world champion Lee Sedol, 100 games to 0.

No teacher and no examples, just trying things and checking the score. That's reinforcement learning.

Look at what you just did:

💡 Big idea Reinforcement Learning (RL) is learning what to do by trying actions and seeing which ones get rewarded.

The loop that runs all of RL

Every RL system, from a game-playing bot to RL training for ChatGPT, is this loop repeated over and over:

  • Agent: the learner and decision-maker (you, a robot, an LLM).
  • Environment: everything the agent interacts with (the game, the world, a user, a code sandbox).

It isn't only games. In 2022 DeepMind and EPFL used this loop to steer the super-hot plasma inside the TCV fusion reactor, with the agent's actions setting the voltages on its magnetic coils (Nature, 2022).

🧸 Analogy · learning to ride a bike

Nobody can hand you the "correct muscle movements" for riding a bike. You wobble and fall (bad), you stay up for 2 seconds (good), and slowly your body works out what to do. You had no correct answers to copy, only feedback on how it went. RL works the same way.

RL vs. the other ways machines learn

👩‍🏫

Supervised learning

A teacher gives the correct answer for every example: "this photo is a cat".

Like studying with an answer key.

🔍

Unsupervised learning

No answers at all. Find patterns in the data: "these photos look similar".

Like sorting your sock drawer.

🎮

Reinforcement learning

No answers, only a score after you act: "that went well / badly".

Like a video game or training a dog.

Three things make RL harder (and more interesting) than supervised learning:

🤷

Rewards are vague

A reward says how good the action was, not what the right action was. A −1 doesn't tell you which button to press instead.

⏳

Rewards come late

In chess you only find out if you won at the very end. Which of your 40 moves deserves the credit? This is called the credit assignment problem.

🔁

Your actions shape your data

You only learn about the buttons you actually press. If you never try B, you'll never find out B is great.

🌍 Where it's used
Learning by trial and error 🏭 In production · 2023 Sony AI: GT Sophy, the AI driver in Gran Turismo 7

How: state = positions and speeds of its car and the cars nearby · action = steer, accelerate or brake, 10 times a second · reward = progress around the track, minus penalties for leaving it or hitting walls.

Why this method: nobody can write down the perfect move for every corner and overtake. It practised instead: it could lap the track within hours, then kept improving over 45,000+ hours of driving (Nature, 2022).

The loop, inside an app you use 🏭 In production · 2018 Facebook: which push notifications to send

How: state = facts about you and one candidate notification · action = send it or drop it · reward = your activity on Facebook afterwards, minus a small penalty for every notification sent.

Why this method: there's no answer key for “should this person get this ping?”. The only feedback is what people do next. For some notification types it replaced a system that just predicted clicks.

Rewards come late (credit assignment) 🏭 In production · 2020 Google DeepMind: AlphaChip lays out chips

How: state = a chip grid with some circuit blocks already placed · action = where to put the next block · reward = how good the finished layout is, given only after the last block lands.

Why this method: half a chip can't be scored, so the agent must work out which early placements earned the final score. It has laid out every Google TPU generation since 2020.

🤯 Fun fact · RL started with cats

In 1898, psychologist Edward Thorndike put cats in "puzzle boxes" with a hidden lever. At first they escaped by luck. With each attempt they got faster, because the action that led to escape (and food) got "stamped in". He called this the Law of Effect: actions followed by good outcomes become more likely. More than a century later, that's still the core idea behind algorithms you'll build in Phase 5.

🌉 LLM bridge

When OpenAI turned GPT-3 into ChatGPT, nobody could write the perfect answer to every possible question. Instead, people looked at several answers and said which one was better. That judgment became a reward. RL then pushed the model toward answers people liked: answers people ranked higher got their probabilities raised, like pressing the winning button more often. This is RLHF (RL from Human Feedback), and you'll build it in Phase 7.

Reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (and, as far as we know, OpenAI's o1) take it further. The reward is simply "was the final math answer correct?" or "did the code pass the tests?", and the model works out on its own how to think step by step.

Check yourself ✅

1. What feedback does an RL agent get?
RL gets a score, not an answer key. Correct answers are supervised learning; patterns with no feedback are unsupervised learning.
2. You win a chess game after 40 moves. Which move gets the credit?
Working out which earlier actions led to a later reward is one of the central challenges in RL. Phases 2–5 are largely about clever ways to solve it.
3. In the Mystery Machine, why does a random agent score about 0?
Learning means changing behavior based on reward. The random agent ignores the rewards, so it stays at 50/50 and its +1s and −1s cancel out.
📝 TL;DR
  • RL = learning what to do from rewards, by trial and error.
  • The core loop: agent takes an action → environment returns a new state + reward → repeat.
  • What makes it hard: rewards are vague, delayed, and depend on what you chose to try.
  • RL is what turned GPT into ChatGPT, and LLMs into reasoning models.
