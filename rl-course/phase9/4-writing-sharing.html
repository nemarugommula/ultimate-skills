Phase 9 · Lesson 4

Writing it up & sharing your work

Research that nobody reads might as well not exist. Clear writing is a core research skill, and your best way to get noticed.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • 95% CI: the range where the true average probably lies; to compare two methods, bootstrap the difference B − A and check whether it excludes 0 (9.3).
  • Ablation: remove one component, re-run, and see how much of the gain disappears (9.3).
🎯 Hook

"The 37 Implementation Details of PPO" was a blog post (Shengyi Huang and colleagues, ICLR 2022 Blog Track). No new algorithm, just a careful, runnable account of what PPO's code really does. It became the standard reference people cite before reproducing PPO, and its lead author's single-file library, CleanRL, is widely used (9.2). A clear write-up of careful work can travel further than a flashy result.

💡 Big idea Every write-up answers four questions in order: What question? → What did you do? → What did you find (with uncertainty)? → What does it mean, and what doesn't it show? State claims no stronger than your evidence.
🧠 Analogy: a great PR description

The best pull-request descriptions say what changed, why, how it was tested, and what the risks are. A research write-up is the same: the question, what you did, what you found (with error bars), and its limitations. Reviewers, of code or of papers, trust authors who state the limits themselves.

A write-up template (blog post or short paper)

  1. TL;DR in 2–3 sentences, with the headline result and its error bar.
  2. Motivation: why should anyone care? Link to the open problem (8.12).
  3. Setup: model, environment and reward, algorithm and hyperparameters, compute, number of seeds.
  4. Results: one figure per claim. Clear axis labels, units, confidence bands, legend.
  5. Ablations: what happens when you remove each piece.
  6. Limitations: one model? small scale? one domain? Say it before a reviewer does.
  7. Code + logs: a repository someone can run in one command, plus training logs.

Practice: how strong a claim can you make?

🎮 Playground · Match the claim to the evidence

Tip Where to share
  • GitHub: clean, runnable code with a README showing results. This is your portfolio.
  • Blog (your own site, Substack, or similar): technical explainers, reproductions, negative results.
  • arXiv: for paper-style work (for your first submission in a category, an existing arXiv author has to vouch for you, called an "endorsement").
  • Workshops at NeurIPS, ICML and ICLR: lower barrier than main conferences, great for first papers.
  • Social media (X, LinkedIn, Bluesky): a short thread with your main figure gets researchers' attention.

🌍 Where it's used
Reporting guidelines 🏭 In production · 2025 CONSORT 2025 (first published 1996)

How: reports of randomised trials follow a 30-item checklist and a flow diagram that tracks every participant from enrolment to analysis, dropouts included.

Why this method: readers can only judge a claim if the failures are visible. The flow diagram is the ‘report every seed, including the crashed ones’ rule.

Model cards 🏭 In production · proposed 2019 Hugging Face: model cards

How: every Hugging Face model repo's README is a model card: intended uses, limitations and biases, training details, datasets and evaluation results, following Mitchell et al. (2018).

Why this method: a limitations section that ships with the model keeps claims scoped to where they were tested: ‘claims no stronger than the evidence’.

Negative results get published 🏭 In production · 2013 Center for Open Science: Registered Reports

How: reviewers judge the question and method before any data exist. A study that passes gets in-principle acceptance that isn't withdrawn because of its results. Over 300 journals offer it.

Why this method: when acceptance doesn't depend on a positive result, null results get written up. That's why your negative result deserves a post too.

🤯 Fun fact

In 2022, ICLR (one of ML's major conferences) opened a peer-reviewed Blog Track. Its call for posts cited "a reproducibility crisis" and aimed to reward researchers for reviewing and re-examining past work, exactly the kind of write-up this lesson is about. A blog post can now be a peer-reviewed publication.

Check yourself ✅

1. Which caption is best for a plot of held-out accuracy over training for GRPO and Dr. GRPO?
A caption says what's plotted, the setup, how many seeds, what the shading means, and the one takeaway. The first is long but says none of that; the third is a claim with no setup or uncertainty.
2. You tried clip-higher on Lab 8 with 5 seeds. The 95% CI of the difference is [−2, +3] points. What do you publish?
Negative results save others weeks of work, and several influential 2025 GRPO analyses (8.4) were careful "this common choice is biased" findings. But "no detectable effect at 135M with 5 seeds" is not "doesn't work": scope it to what you tested.
3. Where should a write-up's limitations go?
Stating limits yourself (one model, small scale, one domain) builds trust. Hiding them in an appendix, or leaving them for reviewers, does the opposite.
📝 TL;DR
  • Question → method → findings (with uncertainty) → meaning and limitations.
  • One figure per claim. Claims no stronger than the evidence.
  • Share code, logs and short blog posts. Negative results count.
