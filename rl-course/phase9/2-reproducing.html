Phase 9 · Lesson 2

Reproducing a paper end to end

The fastest way to really understand a method, and a strong signal to employers that you can do research.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Probe environment: a tiny made-up environment whose correct answer you know in advance (introduced in this lesson).
  • V(s): the expected return from state s; a reward one step later is worth γ × reward today (0.3, 2.3).
  • Clip fraction: the share of samples whose PPO probability ratio hit the clip limit, a gauge of how big each update is (5.6).
🎯 Hook

A 2020 MIT study (Engstrom et al., "Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients") found that much of PPO's advantage over TRPO came from code-level details not described as part of the algorithm, like reward scaling, learning-rate annealing and value clipping (limiting how much the critic's predictions may change per update). Reproducing papers is how you discover what really makes a method work.

💡 Big idea Reproduce small first: verify each component on tiny problems where you know the right answer, then scale. When it fails (it will), debug systematically: RL bugs rarely crash. They just quietly learn nothing.
🧠 Analogy: fixing a flaky production bug

You wouldn't debug a flaky distributed system by staring at the full production cluster. You build the smallest setup that reproduces the problem, add logging, and change one thing at a time. Reproducing an RL paper is the same craft: tiny environments with known answers, unit-tested pieces, lots of logging, one change at a time.

A reproduction plan that works

  1. Pick a target number from the paper (one table cell or curve) and the smallest setting that shows it.
  2. Find a reference implementation (official code, CleanRL's single-file implementations, TRL, verl) to compare against.
  3. Unit-test the pieces, like the labs did: returns, advantages, the loss on hand-computed examples, masks, log-probs.
  4. Probe environments: tiny problems with known answers (one state, constant reward → V should equal that reward). If it fails there, it'll fail everywhere.
  5. Log everything: reward, episode length, entropy, KL to the reference, value loss, gradient norm, clip fraction, response length (for LLMs).
  6. Multiple seeds before concluding anything (9.3).
  7. Write down every difference from the paper. That list often becomes the most interesting part of your report.
✏️ Worked example: Andy Jones' probe environments

From his post “Debugging RL, Without the Agonizing Pain” (2021). Each probe adds the smallest possible new piece, so the first one that fails points at the bug. Values below use γ = 0.9.

ProbeSetupShould learnIf it fails, suspect
1. Constant reward1 action, observation always 0, 1 step, reward +1V(0) = 1value loss / value network
2. Predictable reward1 action, observation +1 or −1 at random, 1 step, reward = observationV(+1) = +1, V(−1) = −1backprop: the network ignores its input
3. Delayed reward1 action, observation 0 then 1, 2 steps, reward 0 then +1V(1) = 1, V(0) = γ × 1 = 0.9discounting, done flags
4. Action choice2 actions, observation 0, 1 step, reward +1 for one action, −1 for the otherpolicy picks the +1 action, so V(0) → 1advantages, policy loss
5. Action + observation2 actions, observation ±1, the right action depends on the observationthe right action for each observation; V = +1 for bothbatching, policy and value together

Reading the numbers on probe 3: 0.9 is correct. 1.0 means you aren't discounting. 0 means the bootstrap is being cut off a step early (a misplaced done flag). One tiny run, three different bugs told apart.

🌍 Where it's used
Reproduction as a publication 🏭 In production · 2018 ML Reproducibility Challenge

How: every year since the ICLR 2018 edition, volunteers re-run recent ML papers and report what held up. Reports go through the TMLR journal; MLRC 2026 is a NeurIPS 2026 track.

Why this method: a reproduction becomes credited, peer-reviewed work, so careful re-runs get written up instead of staying in someone's notebook.

Replication at scale 📜 History · 2015 Open Science Collaboration, Science (2015)

How: re-ran 100 published psychology studies with high-powered designs, using the original materials where available. 97% of the originals were significant; 36% of the replications were, with effects about half as big.

Why this method: small samples plus publishing only the hits is the same trap as lucky seeds in RL. Only a systematic re-run could measure how big the problem was.

Reproducing an RL pipeline in the open 🧪 Research · 2025 Hugging Face: Open-R1

How: planned the DeepSeek-R1 pipeline as three steps, did the distillation step first, and compared its OpenR1-Distill-7B with DeepSeek's own 7B distill on the same benchmarks, publishing datasets along the way.

Why this method: starting with the step that has a published number to hit is the ‘pick one target number’ advice: you learn early whether your setup matches.

🤯 Fun fact

CleanRL (Huang et al., JMLR 2022) puts each algorithm in a single file (PPO in a few hundred lines), with every implementation detail visible and benchmarked. Its author also wrote "The 37 Implementation Details of PPO". Reading clean single-file code is one of the fastest ways to learn an algorithm properly.

🔍 Case study: the chip-floorplanning dispute

In 2021, Google researchers reported in Nature that an RL agent could lay out a chip's floorplan (where the big blocks go) in under six hours, matching or beating human designs. In 2023, Cheng, Kahng and colleagues at UC San Diego re-ran it on open benchmarks and found that human experts and a strengthened simulated-annealing baseline (an older search method) often matched or beat it. The original authors replied that the reproduction skipped pre-training, used about 20× fewer experience collectors and half the GPUs, and didn't train to convergence. In September 2024, Nature published an addendum from the authors describing more of the method. The argument continues.

The lesson: a result is only as strong as someone else's ability to reproduce it. When you publish, release the exact setup (pre-training, compute, benchmarks). When you reproduce, match that setup before calling a result wrong, and say precisely where you differed.

🌉 Why this matters for your career

"I reproduced paper X, found that detail Y matters more than the paper says, and here's the evidence" is one of the strongest things you can show in a research job application. It demonstrates understanding, engineering, rigor and communication in one artifact.

Check yourself ✅

1. Your PPO agent learns nothing. What's the best first step?
Most "RL doesn't learn" problems are bugs in data flow (signs, masks, shapes, done flags), not hyperparameters.
2. Why log entropy, KL and clip fraction, not just reward?
They're leading indicators. Reward is a lagging, noisy one. KL between the old and new policy is useful in any policy-gradient method, not just LLMs: a spike means an update moved the policy too far (5.6).
3. Your reproduction gets 80% of the paper's result. What next?
Systematic difference-hunting, which is the research skill itself. Tuning first changes things the paper didn't, so you can no longer tell which difference caused the gap. And 80% may be within seed noise: check variance before calling anything wrong (see the case study).
📝 TL;DR
  • Start small, unit-test pieces, use probe environments, log leading indicators, use multiple seeds.
  • Implementation details often matter as much as the headline idea.
  • A careful reproduction write-up is a strong portfolio piece.
