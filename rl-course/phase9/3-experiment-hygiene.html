Phase 9 · Lesson 3

Experiment hygiene: seeds, error bars, ablations

RL results are noisy. Rigor is what separates a finding from a fluke, and what reviewers and hiring managers look for.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Mean, standard deviation and standard error: what's typical, how much it varies, and how precise the mean is (Lesson 0.5).
  • Confidence bands: shaded regions showing where the true average probably lies (Phase 1 recap).
🎯 Hook

In "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters" (Henderson et al., 2018), researchers ran the same algorithm with the same hyperparameters and split 10 random seeds into two groups of 5. The two groups' learning curves looked like two different algorithms, one clearly "better". The only difference was the random seeds.

💡 Big idea Treat every RL result as a random variable. Run several seeds, report an aggregate with a confidence interval, pre-decide your comparisons, and use ablations to show which component causes the effect. The tools are the mean/std/standard-error ideas from Lesson 0.5.
🧠 Analogy: A/B testing a website

You wouldn't ship a new checkout page because 3 visitors bought more with it. You'd run the test on thousands of visitors, look at the confidence interval, and decide in advance what counts as a win. RL experiments need the same discipline: each random seed is one "visitor", and RL results vary a lot from visitor to visitor.

🎮 Playground · Is B really better than A?

Truth (hidden from the experimenter): Algorithm B is 5 points better on average (105 vs 100), but each seed varies with a standard deviation of 15. Run an experiment with n seeds per algorithm.

The second chart is the one that decides: the bootstrap 95% CI of the difference B − A (dashed line = the true +5). If its whiskers stay above 0, B's lead is established. With 1–3 seeds, you'll often see A "win", or B win by 20 points: noise dominates. With cherry-picking, B always looks great, which is how irreproducible results get published. Use the power button to see how many seeds a 5-point effect really needs at this noise level.

The rules

🎲

Seeds

At least 3–5 per configuration for quick checks, 10+ for claims. Seed everything (Python, NumPy, torch, environment, action space).

📏

Confidence intervals

Report mean ± 95% CI (bootstrap is simple and robust). To compare A and B, bootstrap the difference B − A: if its 95% CI excludes 0, the gap is established. Non-overlapping bars are a stricter shortcut: they imply a real gap, but overlapping bars don't rule one out.

🧮

Robust aggregates

IQM (interquartile mean: average of the middle 50% of runs) is less sensitive to outliers than the mean. It averages the middle half of the runs (5 of 10) instead of the median's middle one or two, so it typically wobbles less. Recommended by "rliable".

🔬

Ablations

Remove one component at a time (Rainbow, 4.4; Lab 4's no-target ablation). Shows why it works.

⚖️

Fair baselines

Equal tuning budget, same compute, same evaluation protocol. Report the hyperparameter sweeps.

📝

Decide in advance

Choose the metric, the number of seeds and the comparison before running. Otherwise you'll unconsciously pick the flattering view.

✏️ Worked example: bootstrap [2, 4, 6, 8] by hand

Four seeds scored 2, 4, 6 and 8 (mean 5). Draw 4 of them with replacement (the same seed may come up twice), take the mean, and do that 5 times:

ResampleDrawnMean
12, 4, 4, 84.5
26, 8, 8, 26.0
32, 2, 4, 63.5
48, 6, 4, 86.5
54, 6, 2, 64.5

Sorted: 3.5, 4.5, 4.5, 6.0, 6.5. The means scatter around 5, and that scatter is the uncertainty. Let a computer do 10,000 resamples and keep the middle 95%: it runs from 3 to 7, so the 95% CI is [3, 7]. Same trick for a comparison: resample A's seeds and B's seeds, record mean(B) − mean(A) each time, and read off the middle 95% of those differences.

Math refresher The bootstrap confidence interval in 5 lines

You have n run scores. Resample n scores with replacement, compute the mean, repeat 10,000 times. The middle 95% of those means is your 95% CI. No formulas or assumptions about bell curves needed.

def bootstrap_ci(scores, stat=np.mean, B=10_000):
    scores = np.asarray(scores)
    boots = [stat(np.random.choice(scores, len(scores), replace=True)) for _ in range(B)]
    return np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])

To compare two algorithms, bootstrap the gap itself. Each round, resample A's runs and B's runs separately and record the difference of means; Δ̂ ("delta-hat", the measured gap) is what you'd get from the real data:

Δ̂ = mean(B) − mean(A)    →    95% CI of Δ̂ from 10,000 resampled differences If the whole interval sits above 0, B's lead is established at the 95% level. If it straddles 0, the data can't tell them apart yet, even if B's bar is higher. 
def bootstrap_diff_ci(a, b, B=10_000):
    a, b = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(b)
    diffs = [np.random.choice(b, len(b)).mean() - np.random.choice(a, len(a)).mean() for _ in range(B)]
    return np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])

🚪 141 seeds is too many. The ways out
  • Paired seeds: run A and B on the same seeds (same environment randomness, same initial weights where possible) and bootstrap the per-seed differences. Noise both runs share cancels out.
  • More evaluation episodes per seed: averaging 100 test episodes instead of 10 shrinks the evaluation part of the noise (not the training-luck part), so the std goes down.
  • Test larger effects: the seeds needed grow with (std / effect)². Doubling the effect to 10 points cuts 141 to about 36. Look for changes big enough to matter.
⚠️ A finding from the lab

IQM is much steadier than the mean when failures are rare outliers (5% of seeds: spread ±16 vs ±24). When 25% of seeds fail, failing is part of the algorithm's behavior, and no single aggregate summarizes it well. Report the failure rate, or plot every seed.

🌍 Where it's used
Power and false positives in A/B tests 🏭 In production · 2021 Netflix: false negatives and power

How: action = show members version A or B · reward = a product metric · the false-positive rate is set at 5%, and each test is sized for 80% power at an effect worth detecting.

Why this method: people vary as much as seeds do. Choosing the sample size before the test stops you ‘winning’ by luck or missing a real gain.

Pre-registration 🏭 In production · 2005 ICMJE: clinical trial registration

How: major medical journals consider a clinical trial only if it was entered in a public registry, including its planned outcomes, at or before the first patient enrolled. The rule applies to trials starting after July 2005.

Why this method: fixing the comparison before seeing the data stops quietly switching to whichever outcome looked best. Same reason you fix seeds and metrics before running.

Ablations: remove one piece at a time 🧪 Research · 2017 Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2017)

How: combined six improvements to DQN, then trained versions with each one removed to measure what every piece contributes on the Atari benchmark.

Why this method: a combined system's score can't tell you which part matters. Leave-one-out runs can, and they're the table readers check first.

🤯 Fun fact

"Deep Reinforcement Learning at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" (Agarwal et al., 2021) won an outstanding paper award at NeurIPS 2021. Re-analyzing published Atari results with proper uncertainty estimates, it found that some claimed improvements weren't supported by the evidence. Its open-source library, rliable, implements IQM and bootstrap confidence intervals.

🌉 Especially for LLM RL

LLM RL runs are expensive, so people run one seed, but benchmarks like AIME have only 30 problems. One question is 3.3 points. Report pass@1 averaged over many samples per problem (e.g. 16–64), give confidence intervals over problems, and be humble about small differences.

Check yourself ✅

1. B beats A by 4 points with 5 seeds each. The bootstrap 95% CI of the difference B − A is [−6, +14]. Conclusion?
The difference's CI is the test: it straddles 0, so "no difference" is still plausible. Bar overlap is only a stricter shortcut: separate bars imply a real gap, but overlapping bars can still hide one.
2. Why prefer IQM over the mean for aggregating RL runs?
The mean is pulled by outliers, the median wastes data, and IQM is a good middle ground.
3. A paper reports "best of 5 seeds" for its method and "average" for baselines. Problem?
Compare like with like: same aggregation, same seeds budget.
📝 TL;DR
  • Seeds can fake algorithm differences. Run several, seed everything.
  • Report mean or IQM ± bootstrap 95% CI. To compare, bootstrap B − A: a CI that excludes 0 means the gap is established.
  • Ablations explain. Fair baselines and pre-registered comparisons protect you from fooling yourself.
