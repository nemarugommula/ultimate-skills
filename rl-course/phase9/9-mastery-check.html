Phase 9 · Lesson 9

Mastery check: explain, frame, solve

Twenty-five core ideas to explain out loud in a minute each, then one problem nobody has framed for you. This is how you find out what you really know before graduating.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • Is it an RL problem? The ladder: rules → labelled answers → planning with a model → a one-shot bandit → full RL; use the lowest rung that works (Lesson 0.6).
  • Seeds and error bars: one run is luck; report mean ± standard error over several seeds (Lessons 0.5, 9.3).
  • Reward hacking: an agent scores high without doing what you meant (Lesson 8.6).
🎯 Hook

Rate from 1 to 7 how well you understand how a zipper works. Now write, step by step, how the teeth lock together. Then rate yourself again. In a classic Yale study, people did exactly this for zippers, flush toilets and cylinder locks, and their ratings dropped once they tried to explain. RL is the same: rereading a TL;DR feels like knowing. Saying it out loud is the test.

💡 Big idea You've mastered RL when you can do three things without notes: explain each core idea in plain words in about a minute; frame a new problem as state, action, reward and horizon; and solve it honestly: pick the simplest method that fits, train it, evaluate it with seeds and error bars, and say how it could go wrong.
🧠 Analogy: rubber-duck debugging

Explain your code line by line to a rubber duck and the bug often jumps out, because explaining forces you through every step you were skipping in your head. The 60-second check is rubber-ducking your understanding: the sentence you can't finish is the bug. Mark it 🤔, reread that lesson, and try again tomorrow.

Phase 0 · What RL is

1What are the six words of RL, and how do they fit into one loop?Lesson 0.2 →
Model answer

An agent looks at the state of its environment, picks an action, and gets back a reward and a new state; then it repeats. What it learns is the policy π(a|s): the probability of each action in each state. For an LLM, the state is the text so far and the action is the next token.

2Why does an agent maximize the return rather than the next reward, and what does γ do?Lesson 0.3 →
Model answer

The return G = r₁ + γr₂ + γ²r₃ + … adds up all future rewards, so the agent can accept a small loss now for a bigger win later. γ (gamma, between 0 and 1) shrinks rewards that are further away; the agent effectively looks about 1/(1−γ) steps ahead, so γ = 0.9 means about 10 steps.

3Algorithm B beat A in one run. Why isn't that enough?Lesson 0.5 →
Model answer

RL results vary a lot from seed to seed, so one run is mostly luck. Run several seeds and report the mean with its standard error, σ/√n: how much the mean itself would wobble if you reran everything. A gap smaller than about 2 standard errors could be luck, and 4× more runs only halves the standard error.

4How do you tell whether a problem needs RL at all?Lesson 0.6 →
Model answer

Three questions: are there decisions in sequence, does your choice change what comes next, and is the feedback only a score? Three yeses make an RL problem. Even then, climb the ladder from the bottom: rules, then labelled answers (supervised learning), then planning if you know the model, then a one-shot bandit, and only then full RL.

Phase 1 · Bandits

5Explain the update Q ← Q + α(R − Q).Lesson 1.2 →
Model answer

Move your estimate Q a fraction α of the way toward what you just saw. R − Q is the surprise: positive means better than expected. With α = 1/n it's the exact running average; a fixed α counts recent rewards more, which helps when the world changes. The same surprise signal returns in TD learning, advantages and dopamine.

6What is the explore–exploit dilemma, and how do ε-greedy, UCB and Thompson handle it?Lesson 1.3 →
Model answer

Exploiting picks what looks best now; exploring tries other options in case they're better. Pure greedy gets stuck, because an option that looked bad early is never tried again. ε-greedy acts at random with probability ε (epsilon, a small number) and greedily otherwise; UCB and Thompson sampling explore smarter, by trying the options whose value is still most uncertain (Lesson 1.4).

Phase 2 · MDPs and Bellman

7What does “Markov” mean, and what do you do when your state isn't?Lesson 2.1 →
Model answer

The current state tells you everything about the future that the whole history could; the past adds nothing. If it doesn't (one frame of a ball shows where it is but not how fast it moves), put the missing information into the state: velocity, a few stacked frames, or memory. An LLM is Markov because its state is the whole context.

8What's the difference between V(s) and Q(s, a)?Lesson 2.3 →
Model answer

V(s) is the expected return from state s if you follow the policy: “how good is this place?” Q(s, a) is the expected return if you first take action a and then follow the policy: “how good is this move?” You act by picking the argmax of Q (the action with the highest Q), and V is the policy-weighted average of the Qs.

9State the Bellman equation in words.Lesson 2.4 →
Model answer

A state's value = the average, over what can happen next, of [reward now + γ × value of where you land]. It's a consistency rule: the true values make every state agree with its neighbours. The optimality version takes the best action instead of the policy's average, and that is the target behind Q-learning and DQN.

10What is policy iteration, and why does the idea matter far beyond gridworlds?Lesson 2.5 →
Model answer

Alternate two steps: evaluate the current policy (compute its values), then improve it by acting greedily on those values. An improvement step is never worse, and the loop reaches the optimal policy, usually in a few rounds. This evaluate ↔ improve loop is the skeleton of actor–critic, PPO, AlphaZero and LLM self-improvement.

Phase 3 · Learning from experience

11Monte Carlo vs TD learning: what's the trade-off?Lesson 3.2 →
Model answer

Monte Carlo waits until the episode ends and learns from the actual return: unbiased, but noisy and slow to update. TD updates after every step toward r + γV(s′), a guess built on a guess (bootstrapping): a little biased, but much less noisy and often faster. n-step returns and λ sit in between (Lesson 3.5).

12SARSA vs Q-learning: what do on-policy and off-policy mean?Lesson 3.4 →
Model answer

SARSA updates toward the action it will actually take next, exploration included, so it learns the value of how it really behaves and plays safe near the cliff. Q-learning updates toward the best next action (the max of Q), whatever it actually does next, so it learns the optimal values from any exploratory data. That off-policy property is what makes replay buffers possible.

Phase 4 · Deep RL

13Why replace value tables with function approximation?Lesson 4.1 →
Model answer

Real problems have far too many states to list (every camera image is a new state), and a table learns nothing about a state it has never visited. A function with weights, v̂(s, w) (“v-hat”, an estimate), shares what it learns across similar states: it generalizes. You then learn the weights instead of table cells, still driven by the TD error.

14Which two tricks make DQN work, and what are they fighting?Lesson 4.3 →
Model answer

Function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy data (the “deadly triad”) can make values blow up. A replay buffer trains on random, reusable minibatches of past experience, which breaks the correlation between consecutive steps; a target network keeps the TD target frozen for a while so the network isn't chasing its own moving guess. It works very well in practice, without a convergence guarantee.

Phase 5 · Policy gradients

15How does REINFORCE decide which actions to make more likely?Lesson 5.2 →
Model answer

Play an episode, then push up the log-probability of each action in proportion to the return that followed it: ∇J = 𝔼[G · ∇log π(a|s)] (“the gradient of the objective is the average of return times the gradient of log-probability”). High-return actions become more likely, low-return ones less. It's reward-weighted supervised learning on your own samples, and it's very noisy.

16Why subtract a baseline, and why doesn't it bias the gradient?Lesson 5.3 →
Model answer

Use the advantage A = G − b(s): was this better or worse than usual from here? Judging actions relative to normal cuts the noise a lot. It adds no bias because, on average, ∇log π times anything that doesn't depend on the action is zero. The best baseline is close to V(s), learned by a critic; GRPO uses the group's mean reward instead.

17What problem does PPO's clipping solve?Lesson 5.6 →
Model answer

One update that is too big can wreck the policy, and a bad policy then collects bad data. PPO reuses each batch for several updates, weighting by the ratio πnew/πold, but clips that ratio to 1 ± ε, so there is nothing to gain by moving the policy further than that. It's simple and stable, it's the RL inside classic RLHF, and GRPO is PPO without the critic.

Phase 6 · Advanced RL

18How do search and self-play work together in AlphaZero?Lesson 6.3b →
Model answer

At each move, tree search (MCTS) uses the policy network to suggest moves and the value network to judge positions, and it ends up with a better move distribution than the network alone. The network is then trained to imitate the search's visit counts and to predict who won, on games it played against itself. Better search makes a better network, which makes search better: policy iteration again.

19Why is learning a policy from a fixed log so hard?Lesson 6.4 →
Model answer

Offline, the agent can't try anything new, so values for actions the log never contains are pure guesses, and a maximizing agent seeks out exactly the guesses that are too high (distribution shift). Online, a wrong overestimate gets tried and corrected; offline it never does. Fixes: be pessimistic about unseen actions (CQL), stay close to the logged actions (IQL), and estimate a new policy with off-policy evaluation before shipping it.

Phase 7 · RL for LLMs

20In what sense is an LLM a policy in an MDP?Lesson 7.1 →
Model answer

State = the prompt plus the tokens written so far; action = the next token, from roughly 100k choices; the policy is the model's softmax over them. Transitions are deterministic (append the token), the reward usually comes at the end, and γ = 1. A response's log-probability is the sum of its tokens' log-probabilities.

21Why does RLHF need a KL leash?Lesson 7.4 →
Model answer

The reward model is only a learned stand-in for human preferences, and pushing hard on it finds its mistakes: the proxy score keeps rising while true quality rises, then falls (over-optimization). RLHF maximizes reward − β · KL(π ‖ πref), where KL measures how far the policy has drifted from the reference model, so it stays fluent and close to text the reward model understands. β (beta) sets how tight the leash is.

22What's the key trick behind DPO?Lesson 7.5 →
Model answer

The best policy for the KL-leashed RLHF objective has a formula, so the reward can be rewritten in terms of the policy itself: r = β log(π/πref) + a constant. Plugging that into the Bradley–Terry preference model gives a simple logistic loss on (chosen, rejected) pairs: no reward model and no RL loop. The catch is that it learns offline, from a fixed set of pairs.

Phase 8 · Reasoning and agents

23How does GRPO train a reasoning model without a critic?Lesson 8.3 →
Model answer

For each prompt, sample a group of G answers and score each with a checkable reward (is the final answer right, do the tests pass). Each answer's advantage is its reward minus the group's mean, divided by the group's spread, followed by a PPO-style clipped update. The group is the baseline, so no value network is needed; if every answer gets the same score, that prompt teaches nothing.

24What is reward hacking, and how do you defend against it?Lesson 8.6 →
Model answer

The agent scores high without doing what you intended: padding answers, flattering the user, special-casing the tests, even tampering with the scoring code. Stronger optimizers find more hacks. Defenses: a KL leash, several reward models, hardened environments with hidden tests, monitoring the reasoning (to detect, not to train against), and reading samples by hand.

25What changes when an LLM acts over many turns with tools?Lesson 8.10 →
Model answer

An episode becomes many rounds of think → tool call → observation, with an outcome reward at the end. Tool outputs land mid-episode, so they're masked out of the loss (the model didn't write them). Long-horizon credit assignment, slow rollouts, context limits and building environments are the hard parts; the context window (plus any notes) acts as the agent's memory of the situation.

🧩 Frame and solve an unseen problem

🧩 New problem: the electric-bus depot

A city's 40 electric buses return to the depot between 21:00 and 01:00, each with a different amount of charge left. Every bus must have enough energy for its morning route when it leaves around 05:00. The depot's grid connection can only deliver so much power at once, and the electricity price changes every 15 minutes. Today a simple rule plugs every bus in at full power as soon as it arrives.

The transit agency wants a smaller bill without ever stranding a morning route. Work through the five stages below in order. Before opening each one, write your own answer: that's the exercise.

Stage 1 of 5. Write down the state, the action, the reward, episodic or continuing (and γ), and one thing you'd need in the state to keep it Markov.

Reveal 1 Framing

State: for each bus, its charge level, the energy its route needs, its departure time and whether it's plugged in; plus the time of night, the current price, the price forecast, and the depot's total load. Without the departure times and forecast it isn't Markov: the same charge level means something different at 23:00 and at 04:30.

Action: every 15 minutes, a charging power for each plugged-in bus (0 up to the charger's maximum), with the total kept under the grid limit.

Reward: each step, −(electricity cost); at each departure, −(a large penalty × kWh missing). Optionally a small cost for battery wear or for peak demand.

Episodic? Yes: one night is one episode, 22:00 to 05:00 = 28 steps of 15 minutes. With a short episode that really ends, γ = 1 is fine.

rt = − pricet × energyt − penalty × shortfallt “this step's reward = minus what we paid for electricity, minus a big fine for every kWh a departing bus is missing”
✏️ Worked example: two buses, one night

Two buses each need 100 kWh by 05:00. The depot can deliver 80 kW in total. Price: $0.30/kWh from 22:00 to 01:00, $0.10/kWh from 01:00 to 05:00.

  • Today's rule (charge on arrival at 22:00): 40 kW each for 2.5 h = 200 kWh, all at $0.30 → $60.
  • Wait for the cheap price: 40 kW each from 01:00 to 03:30 = 200 kWh at $0.10 → $20. Saving: $40 a night (67%).
  • The trap: set the penalty to $0.05 per missing kWh. Not charging at all costs 200 × $0.05 = $10 in penalties, which beats the $20 bill. A reward-maximizing agent will learn to leave both buses empty.
  • The fix: make missing energy cost far more than any saving (say $5/kWh, like the cost of cancelling a route: leaving both empty now scores −$1,000), and also enforce the minimum charge as a hard rule outside the learned policy.

Stage 2 of 5. Which rung of the ladder would you try first, and which RL method (if any) after that? Why not the obvious alternatives?

Reveal 2 Method choice

Start low on the ladder (Lesson 0.6). First a better rule (“wait for the cheap hours, charge the earliest departures first”). If arrivals, energy needs and prices were known exactly in advance, this would be a pure planning problem: you have the model, so solve it directly (Phase 2 style) and skip RL entirely.

Why a learned policy can still help: late buses, batteries that arrive emptier than expected, and price spikes make the night uncertain, and a policy that reacts to the state can beat a fixed plan. A strong non-RL competitor is to replan every 15 minutes with the latest forecast; that's also how Google's cooling system works (see “Where it's used”).

If RL: actions are continuous and there are many buses, so a policy-gradient method such as PPO (5.6) or SAC (6.2) trained in a simulator. Not DQN: a max over every combination of 40 charging powers is hopeless (Lesson 6.1). Not pure offline RL from the depot's logs: the logs only show “charge on arrival”, never waiting, so values for waiting would be guesses (Lesson 6.4). Use the logs to build the simulator instead.

Safety layer: whatever the policy outputs, a simple checker cuts it back to the grid limit and forces full power when a bus would otherwise miss its departure.

Stage 3 of 5. Sketch the training plan: data, simulator, baselines, what you'd log, and two sanity checks.

Reveal 3 Training plan
  1. Simulator from a year of logs: arrival times, arrival charge, route energy, prices. Check it first: replaying the current rule in the simulator should reproduce last year's real bill closely.
  2. Baselines before training: today's rule, the better rule, and an oracle planner that knows the whole night in advance. Nothing can beat the oracle, so it tells you how much saving is possible at all.
  3. Reward checks by hand on tiny cases (the worked example): the penalty must outweigh any possible saving.
  4. Train PPO on many simulated nights in parallel; randomize arrivals, battery levels and prices (domain randomization, Lesson 6.3) so it doesn't memorize last year.
  5. Log the bill, the number of buses short at departure, entropy, and PPO's KL and clip fraction (Lab 5's diagnostics).
  6. Sanity checks: on a night with a flat price, the policy should do about as well as the rule; on a night with a huge price spike, it should avoid charging during the spike.

Stage 4 of 5. How will you show the saving is real? Name the test data, the number of seeds, the numbers you'd report, and how you'd go from simulator to real depot.

Reveal 4 Evaluation with seeds and error bars

Held-out nights: test on the last two months of data, never used for training, including a cold snap (batteries need more energy in the cold).

Seeds: train 5 seeds. Say their savings versus today's rule are 31%, 28%, 35%, 22% and 34%. Mean = 30%; standard deviation ≈ 5.24; standard error = 5.24/√5 ≈ 2.35; so about 30 ± 4.7, a range of roughly 25%–35%. Report it that way, not as “35%” from the best seed.

Compare fairly: against the better rule and the replanning baseline with the same test nights; look at the confidence interval of the difference (Lesson 9.3). Also report what fraction of the oracle's saving you capture.

Report the worst case, not just the average: buses short at departure across all test nights must be 0. An average bill saving can hide one stranded route.

Then the real depot, step by step: shadow mode first (the policy only recommends; operators compare with what the rule did), then a pilot with alternating nights or one depot as an A/B test, with error bars again.

Stage 5 of 5. List the ways this could go wrong (at least one reward hack, one distribution shift, one safety issue) and a guard for each.

Reveal 5 Risks: reward hacking, shift, safety
  • Reward hacking: a penalty that's too small strands buses (the worked example). Simulator bugs get exploited too, such as charging past 100% or negative prices. Guard: physics limits asserted in the simulator, and reading a few simulated nights by hand.
  • Proxy goals (Goodhart): optimizing only the bill might wear batteries out faster with sharp bursts of power. Guard: add a wear cost, or at least track it.
  • Distribution shift: winter, new bus models or a new electricity tariff make the training data stale. Guard: monitor results against the rule every week, retrain, and fall back to the rule when the state looks unlike anything in training.
  • Safety: grid limits, battery temperature and minimum charge are enforced by hard rules outside the learned policy, operators can override at any time, and the old rule is one switch away.
  • People: a stranded route hurts riders and drivers. Any shortfall triggers a blameless postmortem: what was the state, what did the policy do, and why did the guards miss it?
Math refresher Standard error and “about 2 standard errors”

Mean = the typical result: add the runs and divide by how many there are. Standard deviation (σ, sigma) = how far single runs typically land from the mean. Standard error = σ/√n = how much the mean would wobble if you repeated the whole experiment.

With 5 runs of 31, 28, 35, 22, 34: the mean is 150/5 = 30; the gaps from 30 are 1, −2, 5, −8, 4; their squares add to 110; divide by n − 1 = 4 to get 27.5; √27.5 ≈ 5.24 = σ; and 5.24/√5 ≈ 2.35. A difference smaller than about 2 standard errors could be luck (Lesson 0.5).

🌍 Where it's used
Blameless postmortems 🏭 In production · 2017 Google SRE book: Postmortem Culture

How: after an incident, the team writes down the state (what was known), the actions taken and the impact (the reward), then fixes the process so it doesn't recur.

Why this method: if people fear blame, they stop bringing issues to light. Assuming everyone acted well on the information they had points the fix at the system, not the person.

Safety review before handing over control 🏭 In production · 2018 DeepMind: safety-first data-centre cooling

How: state = a sensor snapshot every five minutes · action = cooling settings · reward = less energy, inside operator-defined safety limits; low-confidence actions are dropped and the local control system checks every action again.

Why this method: the system first only recommended actions to operators; direct control came later, after operator feedback, with eight safety mechanisms including two layers of checks on every action.

Log probabilities so you can evaluate offline 🏭 In production · 2016 Microsoft: the Decision Service

How: a loop of explore → log → learn → deploy for contextual bandits; every decision's probability is logged with its reward, so new policies can be scored on old data.

Why this method: incorrect data collection is a common source of hidden technical debt; logging the probabilities keeps the data unbiased, so a policy can be checked before it goes live.

🤯 Fun fact

The zipper test is real research. In “The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth” (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002), Yale students rated how well they understood devices such as a zipper, a flush toilet and a cylinder lock, wrote step-by-step explanations, and then rated themselves again. Their ratings dropped significantly. Warning people in advance that they would be tested made the drop smaller, but it was still there. The illusion is strongest for how things work, which is exactly what this page tests.

🌉 LLM connection

Your 🤔 list works like the prompt filtering in reasoning RL (Lesson 8.4): a prompt the model always gets right, or always gets wrong, gives GRPO no learning signal, so DAPO-style training drops it and spends compute on the ones in between. Spend your review time the same way: not on the ideas you already ace, but on the shaky ones. And the model answers here are a small rubric, the same idea as rubric rewards (Lesson 8.9).

Check yourself ✅

1. In simulation, the bus agent cuts the bill by 60%, but some buses leave at 80% of the charge they need. What's the most likely cause?
That's reward hacking (8.6): the agent optimizes exactly what you wrote. Longer training would make it hack better. Fix the reward (the penalty must outweigh any saving) and enforce the minimum charge as a hard rule.
2. Suppose every bus's arrival time, charge and route, and every price, were known exactly a day in advance. What should you use first?
The ladder (0.6): if you know the model, plan. RL earns its place when the night is uncertain and the policy has to react to what actually happens.
3. Your agent saves 30% ± 2.35 (standard error, 5 seeds). A colleague's rule saves 27% ± 2.5. What can you claim?
The standard error of a difference is √(2.35² + 2.5²) ≈ 3.4, so a real gap should be roughly 7 points or more before you trust it at this sample size (0.5, 9.3). Also check the worst case: zero stranded buses.
📝 TL;DR
  • Explain each core idea out loud in about a minute, then check the model answer; mark the shaky ones and review those first.
  • To frame a new problem: state (Markov?), action, reward (hackable?), episode length and γ; then climb the ladder from rules and planning before reaching for RL.
  • Solve honestly: baselines and an oracle, a validated simulator, several seeds with error bars, the worst case reported, shadow mode before control, and hard safety rules outside the learned policy.
