How to read an RL paper in 30 minutes

Hundreds of ML papers appear on arXiv every day. Researchers don't read them all. They triage fast and go deep on a few.

🧰 You'll need (quick reminders)
  • GRPO: sample a group of answers per prompt and score each one against the group's average (8.3).
  • pass@k: the chance that at least one of k tries is correct (8.8).
  • Random seed: the number that fixes all the randomness in a run; a different seed gives a different result (Lab 4).
🎯 Hook

S. Keshav's two-page note "How to Read a Paper" (2007) has been passed around by PhD students for years. Its core idea: read in three passes, each deeper than the last, and stop as soon as you know whether the paper deserves more of your time.

💡 Big idea Pass 1 (5–10 min): title, abstract, figures, conclusion. What's the claim? Pass 2 (~30 min): method and experiments, skipping proofs. Is the evidence convincing? Pass 3 (hours): re-derive it, or reproduce it. Only for papers you'll build on.
🧠 Analogy: reviewing a pull request

You don't review a 3,000-line PR by reading every line first. You read the description, look at the tests and the riskiest files, decide whether it deserves a deep review, and only then go line by line. Papers work the same way: abstract and figures first, method and experiments second, details only for the few papers you'll build on.

✏️ Worked example: pass 1 on the DeepSeekMath abstract (5 minutes)

The paper that introduced GRPO (arXiv 2402.03300, Feb 2024). The abstract's key sentences, trimmed:

  • “DeepSeekMath 7B … continues pre-training DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B with 120B math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl …”
  • “… 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without relying on external toolkits and voting techniques, approaching the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4.”
  • “Self-consistency over 64 samples … achieves 60.9% on MATH.” (Self-consistency = sample 64 answers and take the majority vote.)
  • “… attributed to two key factors”: a carefully engineered web-data selection pipeline, and GRPO, “a variant of PPO” that also cuts PPO's memory use.

Your turn: write three lines in your notes file: Claim (what they say), Evidence (what supports it, so far), Doubt (what you'd check in pass 2). Then open the sample.

Reveal a sample summary
  • Claim: a 7B open model reaches 51.7% on MATH with no tools and no voting, near Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4, thanks to 120B tokens of filtered web math plus GRPO.
  • Evidence: one headline benchmark number (plus 60.9% with a 64-sample majority vote).
  • Doubt: two causes are credited at once. Which one gives how much? In pass 2, look for an ablation that separates the data from GRPO, and for results on other benchmarks.

Verdict: worth a pass 2, because GRPO became one of the standard LLM-RL algorithms (8.3). Your lines don't need to match these; they need a claim, its evidence and one concrete doubt.

The RL-specific checklist

Use this on every paper during pass 2. Your progress is saved in your browser.

Tip A reading routine that works
  • Keep a single notes file: for each paper, write a 3-line summary (claim, evidence, doubts) and one idea it gives you.
  • Follow a few good sources: arXiv listings for cs.LG / cs.CL, curated newsletters, and researchers' blogs (like the "State of RL for reasoning LLMs" review used in Phase 8).
  • Read the code when it's released. The loss function and data pipeline often reveal more than the paper.
  • Every few weeks, write a short blog post comparing 3–5 related papers. That's how you build expertise and visibility (9.4).

🌍 Where it's used
A reading checklist, made mandatory 🏭 In production · 2019 Pineau et al.: the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program

How: NeurIPS added a reproducibility checklist to paper submission, alongside a code-submission policy and a community-wide challenge to reproduce accepted papers.

Why this method: the questions your 30-minute read asks are the ones reviewers need answered. Asking authors up front puts the answers in the paper, where readers can find them.

Check tuning parity 🧪 Research · 2017 Melis et al.: well-tuned LSTMs

How: re-ran several newer language-model designs and the plain LSTM with the same large automatic hyperparameter search, instead of comparing numbers from different code bases.

Why this method: with equal tuning, the plain LSTM beat the newer models. The earlier wins came from uneven code and tuning, exactly what your Doubt column checks.

Check it holds beyond one model 🧪 Research · 2025 Spurious Rewards (Shao et al., 2025)

How: GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B with rewards assigned at random still raised MATH-500 by 21.4 points (true rewards: 29.1). The same trick often failed on Llama 3 and OLMo 2.

Why this method: a result shown on one model family may be a property of that model, not of the method. Add ‘which models, and a random-reward control?’ to your checklist.

🤯 Fun fact

Many important RL ideas appeared first as blog posts or short notes rather than polished papers: "The Bitter Lesson" (Sutton, 2019), "The 37 Implementation Details of PPO" (ICLR blog track, 2022), and the analysis of training–inference mismatch (2025). Good research writing doesn't have to be long.

Check yourself ✅

1. "Our method beats GRPO by 3 points on AIME" (1 model, 1 seed, 1 compute budget). How much should you trust it?
Interesting, but it needs multiple seeds, models and compute scales (S-curves can cross, 8.12). AIME has only 30 problems per year, so 3 points can be a single question. Lesson 9.3 shows how to measure this luck.
2. The baseline in a paper uses default hyperparameters; the new method was tuned for weeks. What's wrong?
Equal training compute isn't enough: weeks of tuning are extra compute and extra chances to get lucky, spent on one side only. One of the most common problems in deep RL papers. Look for equal tuning budgets or published sweeps.
3. A paper reports pass@1 improvements only. What would you want to see as well?
RL can trade coverage for reliability (8.7–8.8). Length changes can hide in the averaging (8.4).
📝 TL;DR
  • Three passes: skim (claim) → method and experiments (evidence) → deep (reproduce).
  • RL checklist: environment and reward, baselines and tuning parity, seeds and error bars, compute, ablations, metrics beyond pass@1.
  • Keep notes, read code, write short comparisons.
