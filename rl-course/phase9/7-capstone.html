Capstone: train a reasoning model with RL, and study it

Everything comes together: an LLM policy, a verifier reward, GRPO-family training, rigorous evaluation, and a public write-up.

🎯 Hook

You started with a robot in a 5×5 grid and a slot machine. Now you'll train a language model to reason better with reinforcement learning, measure it like a researcher, and publish the result.

💡 The brief Pick a small open model (0.1B–1.5B), a task with a verifiable reward, and one research question from Phase 8. Train with GRPO (extend Lab 8, or use TRL / verl), compare against a baseline with ≥3 seeds, and publish code + a write-up.
🧠 Analogy: shipping your first feature end to end

The labs were unit tests: one function at a time, with checks. The capstone is a whole feature, from design doc to launch: a spec (your research question), an implementation, tests that prove it works (baseline, seeds, CIs), and release notes people can read (the write-up). As at work, most of the effort goes into the parts around the core code.

Measure your baseline first. Base accuracy and run time depend so much on prompt format, answer extraction and hardware that a quoted number would mislead you. Before training: (1) evaluate the base model on ~200 held-out problems with your exact prompt and verifier, greedy and sampled (16 samples each at temperature 1, giving pass@1 and pass@16); (2) time 5 training steps and multiply by your planned steps. For reference, Lab 8's SmolLM2-135M started at greedy 0.64 / sampled 0.28 on its arithmetic task. If the base model is near 0% or near 100%, pick an easier or harder task: GRPO learns nothing from groups that are all wrong or all right (8.3).

Pick one research question

  • Loss design: GRPO vs Dr. GRPO vs DAPO-style token-level loss. Effect on accuracy, length, entropy? (8.4)
  • Exploration: does clip-higher or a higher temperature preserve pass@k? (8.7–8.8)
  • Reward design: strict vs sloppy verifier; document the hacks and fixes (8.2, 8.6).
  • Sample efficiency: group size G = 4 vs 8 vs 16 at equal compute (8.3, 8.12).
  • Sharpen or expand? pass@1 vs pass@64 before and after RL, with the unbiased estimator (8.8).

🌍 Where it's used
Verifiable task + GRPO, at full scale 🧪 Research · 2025 DeepSeek-R1-Zero (2025)

How: state = a problem · action = a reasoning answer · reward = a rule checks accuracy and format · GRPO straight from a base model, no SFT. Your capstone is this recipe, shrunk.

Why this method: a rule-checked reward needs no reward model or human labels, so it's cheap to run and much harder to flatter than a learned scorer.

The same idea at hobby scale 🧪 Research · 2025 TinyZero (Jiayi Pan et al.)

How: R1-Zero-style RL on Countdown (reach a target number with given numbers): a 3B base model learned to check and search on its own; a 0.5B model didn't. The authors put the cost under $30.

Why this method: a task with an instant checker plus a model just big enough keeps runs cheap, so you can afford the seeds and ablations.

Report pass@k, not just pass@1 🧪 Research · 2025 Yue et al. (2025): does RL add new reasoning?

How: compared RL-trained models with their base models on maths, code and visual reasoning. RL won at k = 1, but the base models had higher pass@k at large k.

Why this method: one number hides whether RL taught new skills or sharpened old ones. The whole pass@k curve is what makes a capstone finding defensible.

🤯 Fun fact

Arthur Samuel's checkers program (1959), one of the first programs to learn from experience, was built on an IBM 701 with about 2,000 words of memory and still learned to beat its creator. You have far more compute in a free Colab notebook. What matters most is a good question and careful work.

Check yourself ✅

1. Before training, your base model solves 2% of held-out problems, and even pass@16 is near 0. What should you do?
GRPO scores each answer against its group's average. A group that is all wrong (or all right) gives every answer an advantage of 0, so there is no signal (8.3). Aim for a base model that sometimes succeeds.
2. One Dr. GRPO run beats one GRPO run by 3 points. Can you write “Dr. GRPO is better”?
One run per method is luck plus method, and you can't separate the two (0.5, 9.3). Several seeds, the same budget for both, and an interval on the difference make the claim defensible.
3. After RL, pass@1 rose from 0.40 to 0.55, but pass@64 fell from 0.90 to 0.84. What's the honest headline?
That's the “sharpen, don't expand” pattern from 8.8 and Yue et al.: probability moved onto answers the model already knew, and some rare correct answers disappeared. Reporting the whole pass@k curve is what makes the finding honest.
📝 TL;DR
