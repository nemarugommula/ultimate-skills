Graduation: Phase 9 cheat sheet, Lab 9 & what's next

How to do RL research on one page, the whole course on one screen, and where to go from here.

🧾 Cheat sheet

Reading papers (9.1)

Three passes: skim for the claim (5–10 min) → method and experiments (~30 min) → reproduce (hours, only for papers you build on). Notes: Claim / Evidence / Doubt. Check tuning parity, seeds, compute, ablations, pass@k.

Reproducing (9.2)

Pick one target number. Unit-test the pieces. Probe environments: constant reward → V = 1; delayed reward → V(first) = γ. Log entropy, KL, clip fraction. List every difference from the paper, then ablate them one at a time.

Seeds & CIs (9.3)

Treat every result as a random variable. Bootstrap: resample runs with replacement, keep the middle 95% of the means. To compare, bootstrap B − A: a CI that excludes 0 means the gap is established. Use IQM against outlier seeds.

Power (9.3)

A 5-point effect with std 15 needs ~141 seeds per method for 80% power. Ways out: paired seeds, more evaluation episodes per seed, bigger effects (10 points → ~36 seeds).

Writing (9.4)

Question → what you did → findings with uncertainty → limitations. One figure per claim. Claims as strong as the evidence, no stronger and no weaker. Negative results count.

Portfolio (9.5)

Clear question + correct code + seeds and ablations + honest write-up + one-command reproduction. One deep project beats ten shallow ones. Laptop and Colab are enough.

Interviews (9.6)

Concepts, derivations, coding, research discussion. Explain from the problem, not the formula. Have a 3-minute project story with error bars, a surprise and next steps.

Capstone (9.7)

Small model + verifiable task + one Phase 8 question + GRPO. Measure the baseline first, ≥3 seeds, CIs, pass@k, length and entropy. Publish code and a write-up.

🗂️ Case files: five real problems, one phase later

The same five problems follow you through the course (meet them). Here's what this phase changes for each, and where each one stands in 2026.

🌡️ The cooling plant

Now: to prove a saving, compare against a baseline under the same weather and load, over weeks, and put an interval on the difference.

✅ Where it stands: in production. Google's autonomous cooling went live in 2018, reporting about 30% average savings after a few months. A 2022 trial with Trane in two commercial buildings reported about 9% and 13%, and named evaluation as a core challenge.

📱 The feed

Now: every ranking change ships only after an A/B test sized for power in advance, judged by the interval on the difference (9.3).

✅ Where it stands: in production. Netflix chooses artwork with bandits, and YouTube runs a policy-gradient recommender with off-policy correction, tested in live experiments. The reward is still a proxy, so the A/B test stays the final judge.

🦾 The robot hand

Now: evaluate over many objects, rooms and seeds, and report failure rates, not the best video.

✅ Where it stands: open, but moving fast. QT-Opt reached 96% grasp success on unseen objects (2018) and a sim-trained hand solved a Rubik's cube (2019). In 2025 π*0.6 used RL on real-robot experience to more than double throughput on some of its hardest tasks. General dexterity isn't solved.

♟️ The board game

Now: measure strength over many games with Elo-style ratings, and test against opponents trained to find weaknesses.

✅ Where it stands: solved for perfect-information games by AlphaZero (2017) and MuZero (2019, which wasn't even given the rules). Yet adversarial policies beat the superhuman Go AI KataGo over 97% of the time by tricking it into blunders: strong on average isn't the same as robust.

🧑‍💻 The coding agent

Now: report solve rates with intervals over enough tasks, keep tests hidden, and check whether the model saw the benchmark in training.

✅ Where it stands: coding agents are trained with RL on real repositories (Phase 8); SWE-RL reached 41.0% on SWE-bench Verified in 2025. By 2026 the benchmark was the problem: OpenAI stopped reporting it after finding flawed tests and signs of contamination. Measuring progress is now as hard as making it.

🧩 Frame it yourself

🧩 New problem (capstone-sized)

A city wants to retime the traffic lights at 12 connected intersections to cut waiting time. Sensors count the cars queued in every lane each few seconds. You have a traffic simulator calibrated on last year's counts, and a year of logs from the current fixed-time plan. An ambulance-priority rule must never be broken, and the city will only roll it out if you can prove it works.

Before you open the answer, write down: the state, the action, the reward, episodic or continuing (and γ), which method from this phase (or earlier) you'd try first, and how you'd prove it worked.

Reveal One good framing + the classic mistakes

State: queue length and waiting time per lane, the current light phase and how long it has been on, and the neighbours' phases. Action: every few seconds, keep the current phase or switch to the next. Reward: minus the total waiting time across all lanes this step. Episodic? No: traffic never stops, so it's continuing, γ ≈ 0.99.

Method, using the whole course: the fixed-time plan is your baseline (P9). Quick win: a bandit choosing among a few timing plans per time of day (P1). In the simulator, DQN or PPO with one shared network for all intersections (P4–5), randomizing arrival rates so it survives the move to real roads (P6). Before going live, off-policy evaluation on the logs, and ambulance priority as a hard constraint, not a reward term (P6). No LLM needed: the reward is measured, not judged (P7–8). To prove it (P9): pre-register the metric, run a pilot on some intersections and days against controls, compare paired days, and report a bootstrap interval on the difference.

Classic mistakes: reward = cars through per minute (the main road gets a permanent green and side streets starve: add a maximum-wait penalty); leaving the phase timer out of the state (not Markov); trusting the simulator blindly; comparing against a different week (holidays, weather); one seed; switching the metric after seeing the results.

⚠️ What the reference run shows
  • Splitting one algorithm's seeds 5 vs 5 looks "significant" in 7.2% of splits, more than the 5% a 95% test promises: 1.96 assumes many seeds, and failed seeds make results heavy-tailed.
  • With 5% failing seeds, IQM wobbles ±16 across repeated studies vs ±24 for the mean. With 25%, no single number summarizes it: report the failure rate.
  • A 5-point effect (std 15) needs 141 seeds per method for 80% power.
  • Sharpening on the same problems: pass@1 0.40 → 0.58, but pass@16 0.88 → 0.80. More reliable on problems it could already solve, less coverage on hard ones (8.8).

Boss quiz 👾

1. A vs B, 10 seeds each. Their 95% bars overlap a little, but the bootstrap 95% CI of B − A is [+1.2, +7.5]. Conclusion?
The difference's CI is the test. Overlapping bars don't rule out a real gap; separate bars are only a stricter shortcut. And "this setting" matters: one task, one scale.
2. Probe 3 (two steps, reward 0 then +1, γ = 0.9): your value net learns V(first state) = 1.0. What's wrong?
A reward one step away is worth γ × reward. A misplaced done flag would give 0, not 1.0. Each wrong number points to a different bug.
3. After GRPO, on the same problems: pass@1 0.40 → 0.58, pass@16 0.88 → 0.80. Best reading?
This is the pass@k debate from 8.8. Report both numbers, and look at which problems were lost.
4. "Walk me through your project." Which order works best?
The same order as a good write-up (9.4). Expect "how do you know it isn't noise?" next, and answer with seeds and a CI of the difference.
